## Verbal analogies

:::definizione{#analogia-verbale titolo="Verbal analogy"}
A **verbal analogy** asks a question of the form “$A$ is to $B$ as $C$ is to …”. The goal is to find the word $D$ that stands in the same relation to $C$ as $B$ does to $A$. The analogy can also be written as $A : B = C : D$, like a proportion between numbers. Only the relationship between the words determines the answer, so the two pairs can concern very different subjects.

In “Fish is to swimming as bird is to …”, the relationship between a fish and swimming is that between an animal and the way it moves. A bird moves by flying, so the missing word is *flying*.
:::

:::teorema{#frase-ponte titolo="The connecting sentence method"}
To solve an analogy, write a short sentence linking the two words in the complete pair. Then repeat the sentence, replacing $A$ with $C$. The word that completes the second sentence is the answer. This method works because the sentence makes the relationship explicit, so each option in turn can be tested against it.

In “Oven is to cook as refrigerator is to …”, the connecting sentence is “An oven is used to cook food.” With refrigerator it becomes “A refrigerator is used to … food,” which is completed by *preserve*. The option “kitchen” can be ruled out because “A refrigerator is used to kitchen food” is ungrammatical, just as it is in the Italian example.
:::

:::osservazione{#tipi-di-relazione titolo="Types of relationships between pairs"}
The table lists the relationships that appear most often in quizzes, with a connecting sentence for each.

| relationship | example | connecting sentence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| synonymy | glad : happy | “glad” has the same meaning as “happy” |
| opposition | full : empty | “full” has the opposite meaning to “empty” |
| part and whole | wheel : bicycle | a wheel is part of a bicycle |
| member and category | rose : flower | a rose is a type of flower |
| tool and function | scissors : cut | scissors are used to cut |
| cause and effect | rain : puddle | rain produces puddles |
| place and activity | gym : exercise | a gym is a place where people exercise |
| degree of intensity | lukewarm : boiling | “boiling” describes a much higher temperature than “lukewarm” |

In a given analogy, the two pairs always have the same type of relationship. An option that forms a different type of relationship with $C$ can therefore be ruled out immediately, even if the words are related. In “Wheel is to bicycle as … is to house,” the option “live” is related to a house, but the relationship is place and activity, whereas the complete pair expresses part and whole.
:::

:::attenzione{#verso-della-relazione titolo="The direction of the relationship"}
The same type of relationship is *not* enough if its direction is reversed. In “Wheel is to bicycle as … is to house,” the first word is the part and the second is the whole. The missing word must therefore be part of a house, such as a *window*. The option “neighborhood” forms a part–whole pair with “house,” but the house is part of the neighborhood, so the part and the whole have switched places. Substituting “neighborhood” into the connecting sentence “A wheel is part of a bicycle” gives “A neighborhood is part of a house,” which is false.
:::

:::osservazione{#frase-precisa titolo="When the connecting sentence is too general"}
If the connecting sentence is too general, several options will complete it. In that case, write a more precise sentence that describes the full relationship. In “Painter is to brush as writer is to …”, the sentence “A painter uses a brush” can be completed with either *pen* or *dictionary* in the writer’s case. In contrast, “A painter paints with a brush” becomes “A writer writes with a pen.” A dictionary does not complete this sentence because the writer consults it and does not write with it.
:::

## Classification and the odd one out

:::definizione{#termine-estraneo titolo="The odd one out"}
Questions about the **odd one out** give a group of words or pairs of words and ask which one does not belong. The answer must be justified by a **criterion**, a property that all but one of the items share. In a group of five, for example, four must have the property.

In the group *rose*, *tulip*, *daisy*, *oak*, *violet*, the criterion is “is a flower.” The rose, tulip, daisy, and violet are flowers, whereas the oak is a tree. The odd one out is therefore *oak*.
:::

:::osservazione{#criterio-comune titolo="Common criteria"}
A few types of criteria distinguish the odd one out from the rest of a group.

- **Category**: four words belong to the same category, such as means of transportation or musical instruments, and the fifth does not.
- **Part and whole**: four words denote parts of a single object, and the fifth denotes the whole object.
- **Meaning**: four words are synonyms, and the fifth has a different meaning.
- **Relationship within a pair**: of five pairs, four express the same relationship and the fifth expresses a different one. Write a connecting sentence for each pair, as in an analogy.

In the pairs *high – low*, *wide – narrow*, *long – short*, *heavy – light*, and *big – enormous*, the first four consist of words with opposite meanings. In the fifth, “enormous” expresses a greater degree of size than “big,” so *big – enormous* is the odd pair out.
:::

:::attenzione{#criterio-unico titolo="A criterion must single out one item"}
A criterion that separates a group into three items and two items does *not* identify an odd one out. In the group *steam train*, *motor ship*, *airliner*, *bus*, *pedal bicycle*, the criterion “has an engine” applies to the first four but not to the bicycle, so it singles out one item. In contrast, “travels on roads” applies to the bus and the bicycle but not to the train, ship, or airplane, so it identifies no odd one out. If two criteria seem to single out different items, check which one answers the question and describes the other four as a group. If both meet those conditions, the question is ambiguous without further guidance.
:::

## Sentence completion and connectives

:::definizione{#connettivi-testuali titolo="Textual connectives"}
A **textual connective** is a word or expression that links two parts of a sentence, or two sentences, and identifies the logical relationship between them. The table lists the relationships most frequently tested in quizzes.

| relationship | what the connective introduces | connectives |
| --- | --- | --- |
| consequence | a fact that follows from what was said earlier | so, therefore, thus, consequently |
| cause | the reason for a fact | because, since, given that, as |
| explanation | confirmation or clarification of what was said earlier | in fact, that is |
| contrast | a fact contrary to what the first part states or leads us to expect | but, yet, however, by contrast |
| concession | a fact that would lead us to expect the opposite of the main clause | although, though, even though |

“The train was late, so Paolo missed his connection” and “Paolo missed his connection because the train was late” describe the same event. In the first sentence, *so* introduces the effect. In the second, *because* introduces the cause.
:::

:::teorema{#scelta-del-connettivo titolo="Choosing a connective"}
To choose a connective, identify the relationship between the two parts of the sentence before looking at the options, then look for a connective that expresses it.

- If the second part follows from the first, the connective expresses a consequence.
- If the second part gives the reason for the first, the connective expresses cause or explanation.
- If the second part runs counter to what the first leads us to expect, the connective expresses contrast.
- If the part introduced by the connective would lead us to expect the opposite of the main clause, yet the main clause remains true, the connective expresses concession.

In “The restaurant was almost empty, ___ the service was slow,” a nearly empty restaurant leads us to expect fast service, yet the service was slow. The relationship is contrast, so the blank can be filled with *but*, or with *however* if the punctuation is adjusted. A concession expresses the same idea in “*Although* the restaurant was almost empty, the service was slow.”
:::

:::attenzione{#perche-e-infatti titolo="“So,” “because,” and “in fact”"}
The connectives *so* and *because* are *not* interchangeable, since they point in opposite directions. “It is raining, so the streets are wet” makes sense, whereas “It is raining because the streets are wet” presents the effect as the cause. In these examples, *in fact* introduces supporting information in a new sentence or after a semicolon. “Luca stayed home because he had a fever” and “Luca stayed home. In fact, he had a fever” both link the fever to Luca’s staying home. The first states the cause explicitly. In the second, the reader infers that the fever explains why Luca stayed home.
:::

:::osservazione{#parola-mancante titolo="The missing word"}
In questions with a missing word, look for the word that fits the rest of the sentence. The connective already given indicates the relationship between the two parts, and that relationship determines the meaning of the missing word.

In “Although the application guidelines were ___, many candidates made mistakes when filling out the application,” the part introduced by *although* must lead us to expect the opposite of the main clause. Since the main clause says that many candidates made mistakes, the missing word must lead us to expect few mistakes, so it is *clear*. The word *confusing* makes the sentence incoherent, because confusing guidelines are a reason for the mistakes, and a reason is introduced by *since*.

The verb mood is another clue in Italian. *Benché* and *sebbene* (both meaning “although”) take the subjunctive, as in “benché fosse” (“although it was”), whereas *poiché* (“since”) and *anche se* (“even though”) take the indicative when introducing a fact, as in “poiché era” (“since it was”). In a hypothetical concession, *anche se* can instead take the subjunctive, as in “anche se fosse” (“even if it were”). When two words are missing, an option is correct only if both words make the sentence coherent.
:::

## Passages and statements

:::definizione{#vero-falso-non-deducibile titolo="True, false, or cannot be inferred"}
In these questions, read a short passage and judge a statement *on the basis of the text alone*. The statement is

- **true** if the passage states it or if it follows from one or more pieces of information in the passage,
- **false** if it contradicts at least one piece of information in the passage,
- classified as **cannot be inferred** if the passage does not establish either that it is true or that it is false.

Consider the passage “The Borgonuovo recycling center accepts only paper, glass, plastic, and small appliances. It is open only on Wednesdays and Saturdays, from 8:00 to 13:00. Furniture and large appliances, such as refrigerators and washing machines, are collected from homes by appointment.” The statement “The center is closed on Fridays” is true because the center is open only on Wednesdays and Saturdays. “You can take a washing machine to the center on Saturday morning” is false because a washing machine is a large appliance and the center accepts only small ones. “The center receives more plastic than glass” cannot be inferred because the passage gives no quantities.
:::

:::attenzione{#conoscenze-personali titolo="The reader’s prior knowledge is irrelevant"}
Readers’ knowledge and assumptions do *not* make a statement true or false. If a passage says “The night bus route connects the station with the hospital,” the statement “The night bus mainly serves hospital shift workers” is plausible, but the passage does not say who uses the route, so the statement cannot be inferred. Likewise, a statement the reader knows to be true in the real world cannot be inferred if the passage says nothing about it.
:::

:::osservazione{#parole-spia titolo="Words that change the assessment"}
Many incorrect statements reproduce the passage but change a single word.

- **Quantity**: the passage says “some” or “many,” and the statement says “all” or “most.”
- **Frequency**: the passage says “often,” and the statement says “always.”
- **Certainty**: the passage says “may cause” or “is expected,” and the statement says “causes” or “will happen.”
- **Cause**: the passage says that two events occurred during the same period, and the statement says that one caused the other.

If the passage says “Some employees at the office cycle to work,” the statement “All employees cycle to work” cannot be inferred, because “some” means *at least one*, possibly all. In contrast, “No employee cycles to work” is false. The logical meaning of “some” is discussed in the chapter on syllogisms and quantifiers.
:::

## The structure of an argument

:::definizione{#premesse-e-conclusione titolo="Premises and conclusion"}
An **argument** is a text in which some statements, called the **premises**, are presented as reasons supporting another statement, the **conclusion**. The conclusion is what the author wants the reader to accept. The premises are the reasons the author gives for accepting it. The conclusion is often introduced by *so*, *therefore*, or *it follows that*, whereas premises are introduced by *since*, *given that*, or *in fact*.

The conclusion can appear at the beginning, in the middle, or at the end of the text. In the argument “The gym should stay open until 22:00. In fact, many members finish work at 19:00, and at 20:00 the rooms are still full,” the first sentence is the conclusion: the two statements introduced by *in fact* explain why the gym should stay open. A quick check is to put *therefore* in front of each statement. The text remains coherent when *therefore* precedes the conclusion. In a longer argument, a statement supported by some premises can itself serve as a premise for another statement. The **main conclusion** is then the statement that supports no other statement in the text.
:::

:::definizione{#assunzione-implicita titolo="Unstated assumption"}
An argument’s **unstated assumption** is a premise the author does not write down, but without which the conclusion does not follow from the stated premises. The assumption is therefore a *necessary* condition for the argument to hold.

In “Marco is on the professional register of engineers, so he can sign off on the project,” the only stated premise concerns his registration. The inference assumes that Marco’s registration authorizes him to sign off on this project. “Anyone on the professional register of engineers can sign off on the project” would be sufficient, but it is stronger than necessary because the conclusion concerns Marco alone. In many quizzes, the unstated assumption is precisely the implication “if the premise holds, then the conclusion holds” that the author takes for granted.
:::

:::teorema{#prova-della-negazione titolo="The negation test"}
To identify the assumption among the options, negate each option and check whether the conclusion still follows from the premises. If the conclusion no longer follows when an option is negated, the option is an assumption of the argument. If the conclusion still has support, the option is not an assumption. This test works because an assumption is a necessary condition for the argument, and when a necessary condition is false the argument no longer holds.

In “The Via Roma playground is closed for construction, so children in the neighborhood will play at the Via Verdi park,” consider the option “The Via Verdi park is open.” Its negation is “The Via Verdi park is closed.” The conclusion then no longer follows, so the option is an assumption. The negation of “The Via Verdi park has more play equipment than the Via Roma playground” is “The Via Verdi park does not have more play equipment.” The conclusion can still be supported, so the option is not an assumption.
:::

:::attenzione{#assunzione-troppo-forte titolo="Overly strong assumptions"}
An option that says more than is necessary is *not* an assumption of the argument, even if it makes the argument more convincing. In the parks example, the negation of “All children in the neighborhood prefer the Via Verdi park” is “At least one child does not prefer the Via Verdi park.” The conclusion can still be supported, so the option is not an assumption. Likewise, an option that repeats a stated premise is not an unstated assumption, since an unstated assumption is, by definition, a premise absent from the text.
:::

## Strengthening and weakening an argument

:::definizione{#rafforzare-indebolire titolo="Strengthening and weakening"}
A statement **strengthens** an argument if adding it to the premises makes the conclusion better supported, and it **weakens** the argument if it makes the conclusion less well supported. In these questions, assume that the statement is true even if the passage does not include it.

Consider the argument “Since the station café set up outdoor tables, its June revenue has risen compared with its May revenue. Therefore, the outdoor tables caused revenue to rise.”

- “Many more tourists came to the city in June than in May” weakens the argument because it offers another possible cause of the increase.
- “On rainy days in June, when the outdoor tables could not be used, revenue was similar to that in May” strengthens the argument because it links the increase specifically to the days when the tables were in use.
:::

:::teorema{#legame-con-le-condizioni titolo="Connection with necessary and sufficient conditions"}
Many arguments move from “$A$ is true” to “$B$ is true,” assuming that $A$ is a **sufficient condition** for $B$, that is, that “if $A$, then $B$” holds. The rules in the chapter on necessary and sufficient conditions identify which statements affect the conclusion.

- A statement that makes $A$ sufficient for $B$ completes an instance of modus ponens, so the conclusion becomes necessarily true.
- A statement that makes $A$ merely necessary for $B$, that is, “if $B$, then $A$,” does not suffice. Using this implication to go from $A$ to $B$ is the fallacy of affirming the consequent.
- When the conclusion itself claims that $A$ is sufficient for $B$, an instance in which $A$ is true and $B$ is false is a **counterexample** and makes that conclusion false. An instance in which $B$ is true and $A$ is false does not contradict the conclusion, since it shows only that $A$ is not necessary.

In the argument “Sara passed the written test, so she will be admitted to the oral exam,” the statement “All candidates who pass the written test are admitted to the oral exam” makes the conclusion necessarily true. In contrast, “Only candidates who pass the written test are admitted to the oral exam” says that passing the written test is necessary, so it does not establish that Sara will be admitted.
:::

:::attenzione{#affermazioni-irrilevanti titolo="Irrelevant statements"}
Discussing the same topic as an argument does *not* in itself make a statement strengthen or weaken it. In the café example, “The station café opens at 6:00” concerns the café but does not affect the link between outdoor tables and revenue, so it is irrelevant. Many irrelevant quiz options contain the same words as the passage, such as café, revenue, or June. A statement that repeats a premise does not strengthen an argument either, since it adds no information.
:::

## Quick methods for quizzes

:::osservazione{#metodi-rapidi titolo="How to approach multiple-choice questions"}
Verbal reasoning questions can be solved in a few steps, almost all of which can be taken before reading the options.

- **Analogies**: write the connecting sentence for the complete pair, then repeat it with each option. Rule out options with a different kind of relationship and those that reverse its direction. When two words are missing, check each proposed pair in full, since the first word often fits even when the second does not.
- **Odd one out**: find a criterion that applies to all but one item and check it against every item. In a group of five, it must apply to four.
- **Connectives**: identify the relationship between the two parts (consequence, cause, explanation, contrast, or concession) before reading the options. When the relationship is concession, a subjunctive verb after the blank in Italian can point to *benché* or *sebbene* (“although”).
- **Passages**: for each statement, look for information in the passage that, alone or in combination, confirms or contradicts it. If the passage establishes neither outcome, the statement cannot be inferred. Compare words expressing quantity, frequency, and certainty with the text one by one.
- **Assumptions**: apply the negation test to each option. Rule out options that repeat a premise and those that are too strong.
- **Strengthening and weakening**: write down the link that the argument assumes, usually “if $A$, then $B$” or “$A$ causes $B$.” Another possible cause or a counterexample weakens the argument, whereas a statement that rules out other causes or makes $A$ sufficient for $B$ strengthens it.
:::
