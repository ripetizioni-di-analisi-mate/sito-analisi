## The protein amino acids

:::definizione{#amminoacidi titolo="Amino acid structure"}
An **α-amino acid** has an amino group and a carboxyl group attached to the same carbon, called the α-carbon. For an amino acid with a primary amine, the formula with uncharged groups is $\mathrm{H_2N{-}CH(R){-}COOH}$; $R$ represents the side chain, which distinguishes the amino acids. Proline has a side chain that closes back onto the nitrogen, forming a secondary amine.

In water, protonation depends on pH. For an amino acid with a primary amine and no ionisable side-chain groups, the form $\mathrm{{}^{+}H_3N{-}CH(R){-}COO^{-}}$ predominates near neutral pH. In protonated proline, the nitrogen instead bears two hydrogens and a positive charge. Identifying the backbone and side chain separately helps predict charge, polarity and interactions. For example, alanine and serine differ by the replacement of one side-chain hydrogen with a hydroxyl group.

![Side chains of aspartate, asparagine, cysteine and methionine, highlighting their different functional groups.](/figure/chimica-semestre-filtro/biomolecole/sidechains-en.svg)
:::

:::definizione{#essenziali titolo="Essential amino acids and endogenous synthesis"}
In healthy adults, an **essential amino acid** must be supplied in the diet because endogenous synthesis is absent or insufficient to meet requirements. The nine essential amino acids are histidine, isoleucine, leucine, lysine, methionine, phenylalanine, threonine, tryptophan and valine.

The others can be synthesised by the body under ordinary conditions; some become *conditionally essential* at particular stages or under particular metabolic conditions. For example, tyrosine is derived from phenylalanine. This classification concerns requirements, whereas polar and nonpolar describe chemical structure.
:::

## Stereochemistry, charges and isoelectric point

:::definizione{#stereochimica-amminoacidi titolo="L configuration and Fischer projections"}
Standard amino acids other than glycine have a stereogenic α-carbon. In a **Fischer projection**, with the carboxyl group at the top and the side chain at the bottom, an amino group on the left identifies the L configuration; on the right it identifies D. Horizontal bonds point towards the observer and vertical bonds point away.

Proteins synthesised by ribosomes normally use L amino acids. The designation L describes a relative configuration, and the sign of optical rotation is determined experimentally. Most L amino acids have the α S configuration; L-cysteine has the α R configuration because sulfur changes the side-chain priority order. Glycine, with two hydrogens on the α-carbon, is achiral. Isoleucine and threonine also have a second stereocentre in their side chains.

![Fischer projections of L-alanine and L-cysteine: the same L series, different absolute configurations.](/figure/chimica-semestre-filtro/biomolecole/fischer-aa-en.svg)
:::

:::definizione{#acido-base-pi titolo="Zwitterions and isoelectric point"}
A **zwitterion** contains both positively and negatively charged groups. For glycine, the predominant forms as pH increases are
$$
\mathrm{{}^{+}H_3NCH_2COOH}
\rightleftharpoons
\mathrm{{}^{+}H_3NCH_2COO^{-}}
\rightleftharpoons
\mathrm{H_2NCH_2COO^{-}}.
$$
Their net charges are $+1$, $0$ and $-1$ respectively. The **isoelectric point**, denoted by $pI$, is the pH at which the molecule's mean charge is zero. In a dilute-solution model, for an amino acid with a primary amine and no ionisable side chain,
$$
pI=\dfrac{pK_{a,\mathrm{COOH}}+pK_{a,\mathrm{NH_3^+}}}{2}.
$$
With $pK_a=2.30$ and $9.60$, this gives $pI=5.95$. For an amino acid with an ionisable side chain, the result is approximated using the two $pK_a$ values bracketing the form with zero net charge. For example, for aspartate with values $2.10$, $3.90$ and $9.80$, $pI=3.00$.

A pH below the $pI$ corresponds to a positive mean charge; a pH above it corresponds to a negative mean charge. This property helps interpret electrophoretic separation. The zero-net-charge form retains its internally charged groups and can interact with water.

![Mean glycine charge as a function of pH, with the isoelectric point between the two pKa values.](/figure/chimica-semestre-filtro/biomolecole/pi-en.svg)
:::

## Peptide bonds and protein structure

:::definizione{#legame-peptidico titolo="Formation and orientation of peptide bonds"}
A **peptide bond** is an amide bond between the carboxyl carbon of one residue and the nitrogen of the next. To illustrate formation by acyl substitution, consider an esterified amino acid and the free amino group of a second amino acid; the scheme shows the groups involved and omits any protection of other reactive groups:
$$
\begin{aligned}
&\mathrm{H_2N{-}CH(R_1){-}COOCH_3}
+\mathrm{H_2N{-}CH(R_2){-}COOH}\\[4pt]
&\quad\longrightarrow
\mathrm{H_2N{-}CH(R_1){-}CO{-}NH{-}CH(R_2){-}COOH}
+\mathrm{CH_3OH}.
\end{aligned}
$$
The amine attacks the ester carbonyl and, after proton transfers, the methoxy group is replaced. This scheme illustrates reactivity; in ribosomal synthesis the acyl group is activated as an ester with tRNA.

Sequences are written from the **N-terminus** to the C-terminus. For example, Gly-Ala means *glycylalanine*, since glycine supplies the first residue and alanine retains the terminal carboxyl group. A linear chain with $n$ residues has $n-1$ peptide bonds. The formal composition balance starting from free amino acids subtracts one water molecule for each bond, regardless of the activation mechanism used.
:::

:::osservazione{#geometria-peptidica titolo="Resonance and planarity"}
The amide nitrogen lone pair is delocalised towards the carbonyl; the C–N bond therefore has partial double-bond character. The six atoms of the peptide unit are approximately coplanar, and rotation around this C–N bond is restricted. Rotation around the bonds adjacent to the α-carbon allows chain folding. The trans peptide-bond configuration is usually favoured because it reduces steric crowding between residues; bonds preceding proline have a larger cis fraction than other peptide bonds.

![Six atoms of the peptide plane and partial double-bond character of C–N.](/figure/chimica-semestre-filtro/biomolecole/peptide-en.svg)
:::

:::definizione{#struttura-proteine titolo="Levels of protein structure"}
**Primary structure** is the amino acid sequence. Secondary structure describes local arrangements of the backbone, such as α-helices and β-sheets, stabilised by hydrogen bonds between backbone carbonyls and N–H groups. In an α-helix, the carbonyl of residue $i$ typically interacts with N–H of residue $i+4$.

Tertiary structure is the overall three-dimensional organisation of one chain. Contributions include the hydrophobic effect, hydrogen bonds, ionic interactions, van der Waals forces and, where present, disulfide bonds. Quaternary structure describes the association of several chains in a complex; a single-chain protein already has tertiary structure.

Two cysteines can be oxidised to form a **disulfide bond**:
$$
2\,\mathrm{RSH}\longrightarrow\mathrm{RSSR}+2\,\mathrm{H^+}+2\,e^-.
$$
The half-reaction shows the loss of two electrons; a complete reaction requires an acceptor. This bond is covalent and may connect parts of the same chain or different chains. Denaturation alters spatial organisation; under conditions leaving peptide bonds intact, the sequence is preserved.
:::

## Monosaccharides and stereoisomerism

:::definizione{#monosaccaridi titolo="Aldoses, ketoses and the D/L series"}
A **monosaccharide** is a carbohydrate that cannot be hydrolysed to simpler sugars. In its open-chain form, it contains a carbonyl group and several hydroxyl groups. An aldose contains an aldehyde, whereas a ketose contains a ketone. Glucose is an aldohexose, fructose a ketohexose and ribose an aldopentose.

In a Fischer projection with the carbonyl towards the top, an OH group on the right at the stereocentre furthest from the carbonyl identifies the D series. Two enantiomers have opposite configurations at every stereocentre; two diastereomers differ at some but not all. **Epimers** differ at a single stereocentre. For example, D-glucose and D-mannose are C2 epimers; D-glucose and D-galactose are C4 epimers. Open-chain glucose and fructose are constitutional isomers, since the position and nature of the carbonyl change.

![Fischer projections of D-glucose and D-mannose: only the C2 centre changes.](/figure/chimica-semestre-filtro/biomolecole/fischer-sugar-en.svg)
:::

:::definizione{#anomeri titolo="Cyclisation and anomers"}
The carbonyl of a monosaccharide can react with one of its own hydroxyl groups. An aldose forms a cyclic hemiacetal; a ketose forms a cyclic hemiketal. In glucose, attack of the C5 OH on the C1 carbonyl produces a six-membered ring, glucopyranose. In fructose, attack of the C5 OH on C2 can produce five-membered fructofuranose; other cyclic forms are also possible.

Cyclisation generates a new stereogenic centre at the former carbonyl carbon, called the **anomeric carbon**. The α and β anomers differ at this centre. In the conventional Haworth projection of D-glucopyranose, the anomeric OH lies on the opposite side from $\mathrm{CH_2OH}$ in the α anomer and on the same side in the β anomer. The criterion is relative and is not generally equivalent to simply saying up or down.

**Mutarotation** is the change in optical rotation as a solution reaches equilibrium between anomers through ring opening and closure. In glucose, the free hemiacetal allows opening. A glycoside whose anomeric carbon is locked as an acetal instead requires bond hydrolysis to return to the carbonyl form.

![α and β D-glucopyranose anomers, with the anomeric OH opposite to or on the same side as CH2OH.](/figure/chimica-semestre-filtro/biomolecole/haworth-en.svg)
:::

## Monosaccharide reactions and glycosidic bonds

:::osservazione{#reazioni-carboidrati titolo="Oxidation, reduction and condensation"}
Selective oxidation of the glucose aldehyde group produces gluconic acid; oxidation of the primary alcohol at C6 produces glucuronic acid. Oxidation of both ends produces an aldaric acid. Reduction of the glucose carbonyl gives the polyol sorbitol; reduction of fructose can produce sorbitol and mannitol because it generates a new stereocentre at C2.

A sugar with a free anomeric carbon can form a carbonyl species and act as a **reducing sugar** under appropriate test conditions. Fructose also gives reducing tests in alkaline conditions because it can isomerise to aldoses through enediol intermediates. Aldose–ketose tautomerism involves proton transfers and movement of a double bond, whereas anomerism concerns the configuration of the anomeric carbon in the cyclic form.

The anomeric carbon can react with an OH of another molecule, forming an acetal or ketal with an **O-glycosidic bond**. The formal condensation balance eliminates water. For example, an α$(1\to4)$ bond joins the α C1 of one glucose unit to the C4 OH oxygen of another. Hydrolysis reverses this balance by consuming water.
:::

:::definizione{#oligosaccaridi titolo="Disaccharides and amino sugars"}
An **oligosaccharide** contains a small number of monosaccharide residues joined by glycosidic bonds; a disaccharide contains two. Its properties depend on the identity of the units, the positions connected and the anomeric configuration.

| Disaccharide | Units and bond | Reducing character |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Maltose | Glucose α$(1\to4)$ glucose | Reducing, one free anomeric centre |
| Lactose | Galactose β$(1\to4)$ glucose | Reducing, one free anomeric centre |
| Sucrose | Glucose α$(1\leftrightarrow2)$β fructose | Nonreducing, both anomeric centres involved |

Hydrolysis of lactose gives one glucose and one galactose per molecule; hydrolysis of sucrose gives one glucose and one fructose. Counting free hydroxyl groups alone does not determine reducing character: the state of the anomeric carbon matters.

In **amino sugars**, an OH group is replaced by an amino group. In glucosamine this occurs at C2; acetylation of the nitrogen gives N-acetylglucosamine. These residues occur in surface oligosaccharides and extracellular-matrix polysaccharides, so changing the functional group changes the molecule's bonding possibilities.
:::

## Polysaccharides and extracellular matrix

:::definizione{#polisaccaridi titolo="Starch, glycogen and cellulose"}
A **homopolysaccharide** contains a single type of monosaccharide unit; a heteropolysaccharide contains several types. Starch, glycogen and cellulose are glucose homopolymers, but they differ in their bonds and branching.

| Polymer | Main bonds | Organisation and function |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Amylose, a component of starch | α$(1\to4)$ | Mainly linear chains; plant storage |
| Amylopectin, a component of starch | α$(1\to4)$ and α$(1\to6)$ branches | Branched chains; plant storage |
| Glycogen | α$(1\to4)$ and α$(1\to6)$ branches | More branched than amylopectin; animal storage |
| Cellulose | β$(1\to4)$ | Linear chains associated into fibres; plant structural role |

Different bond stereochemistry changes chain shape and enzyme recognition. Human digestive enzymes hydrolyse starch bonds, whereas humans do not produce a cellulase that cleaves cellulose β$(1\to4)$ bonds.

![α(1→4) links along a chain and α(1→6) at a glycogen branch.](/figure/chimica-semestre-filtro/biomolecole/branch-en.svg)
:::

:::definizione{#glicosamminoglicani titolo="Glycosaminoglycans"}
**Glycosaminoglycans**, abbreviated GAGs, are heteropolysaccharides with repeating disaccharide units that usually contain an amino sugar and a uronic acid. Keratan sulfate contains galactose instead of uronic acid. Carboxylate groups and, in many GAGs, sulfate groups confer negative charges that favour hydration and interaction with cations and proteins.

Hyaluronic acid alternates glucuronic acid and N-acetylglucosamine and has no sulfate groups. Chondroitin sulfate does contain sulfate groups. Different substitution distinguishes their chemical composition while retaining the repeating structure typical of GAGs. Many GAGs attach to proteins to form proteoglycans; hyaluronan is a significant exception to covalent attachment to a core protein.
:::

## Fatty acids and triacylglycerols

:::definizione{#acidi-grassi titolo="Saturated and unsaturated chains"}
A **fatty acid** is a carboxylic acid with a hydrocarbon chain. The notation $18{:}1\,\Delta^9$ indicates eighteen carbons and one double bond between C9 and C10, counting from the carboxyl group. The term ω-9 counts the first double bond from the methyl end. Oleic acid is cis-$18{:}1\,\Delta^9$, whereas stearic acid is $18{:}0$.

Saturated chains pack more regularly. A cis double bond introduces a bend in the chain, so, other features being equal, it makes packing less efficient and tends to lower the melting temperature. A longer chain generally increases dispersion interactions. The trans configuration allows more regular packing than the corresponding cis configuration.

Fatty acids form salts by deprotonation and esters through reaction of their carboxyl group. Linoleic acid $18{:}2\,\Delta^{9,12}$ belongs to the ω-6 series; α-linolenic acid $18{:}3\,\Delta^{9,12,15}$ belongs to ω-3. The first double bond from the methyl end is found by subtracting the highest Δ position from the carbon count. Both are essential in the human diet because the body cannot introduce these double bonds at the required positions.

Hydrogenation reduces double bonds; oxidation can affect unsaturated chains. These reactions connect the degree of unsaturation to lipid properties.

![Cis and trans geometry around the C9=C10 double bond of a fatty acid.](/figure/chimica-semestre-filtro/biomolecole/cis-trans-en.svg)
:::

:::definizione{#triacilgliceroli titolo="Glycerol esters and saponification"}
A **triacylglycerol**, or triglyceride, is a triester of glycerol with three fatty acids. The three acyl residues may be identical or different. Triacylglycerols are mainly hydrophobic and provide an energy reserve; fats and oils are mixtures with different compositions and consistencies at the temperature considered.

Basic hydrolysis, called **saponification**, produces glycerol and three carboxylates:
$$
\mathrm{C_3H_5(OCOR)_3}+3\,\mathrm{OH^-}
\longrightarrow\mathrm{C_3H_5(OH)_3}+3\,\mathrm{RCOO^-},
$$
for three identical chains. For example, one mole of tripalmitin consumes three moles of NaOH and forms three moles of sodium palmitate. A carboxylate has an ionic head and a hydrophobic chain, so it can contribute to aggregate formation in water.
:::

## Complex lipids and cholesterol

:::definizione{#lipidi-complessi titolo="Glycerophospholipids, sphingolipids and glycolipids"}
A **glycerophospholipid** contains a glycerol backbone, generally two acyl residues and a phosphate group attached to a polar group. In phosphatidylcholine, the head includes choline; in phosphatidate, phosphate provides the simplest head. The hydrophobic chains and polar region make these molecules amphipathic and favour bilayer formation.

**Sphingolipids** derive from sphingosine, a long-chain amino alcohol. A fatty acid joined by an amide bond to its amino group forms a ceramide. Sphingomyelin contains a phosphocholine head and belongs to both sphingolipids and phospholipids. A glycosphingolipid instead attaches one or more sugars to ceramide; cerebrosides and gangliosides are examples, and the latter contain sialic acid.

The term **glycolipid** denotes a lipid with a carbohydrate portion. Classes are therefore recognised by identifying the backbone first, then the fatty-acid bond type and finally the polar head. A fatty acid joined by an amide bond to sphingosine identifies a ceramide, whereas three esters on glycerol identify a triacylglycerol.

![Glycerol esters and the ceramide amide: comparison of backbones and bonds.](/figure/chimica-semestre-filtro/biomolecole/lipids-en.svg)
:::

:::osservazione{#colesterolo titolo="Steroid structure and derivatives"}
**Cholesterol** has four fused rings, a small polar region with an OH at C3 and an extended hydrocarbon portion. It inserts into membranes with its OH near the polar heads and its rings among the chains; it changes membrane order and permeability. Its effect on fluidity depends on temperature and lipid composition.

Cholesterol gives rise to steroid hormones, such as cortisol and testosterone, and bile acids, which have additional polar groups. Vitamin D derives from a sterol precursor, 7-dehydrocholesterol, through photochemical opening of a ring and subsequent transformations. Recognising the steroid skeleton connects molecules with different functional groups and functions.
:::

## Nucleotides, coenzymes and nucleic acids

:::definizione{#nucleotidi titolo="Nitrogenous bases, nucleosides and nucleotides"}
Adenine and guanine are **purine bases**, with two fused rings; cytosine, thymine and uracil are pyrimidine bases, with one ring. A **nucleoside** joins a base to a pentose through an N-glycosidic bond between sugar C1′ and purine N9 or pyrimidine N1. A nucleotide is a nucleoside with one or more phosphates.

Ribose has an OH at C2′; 2-deoxyribose has an H in the same position. Adenosine is the nucleoside of adenine and ribose; AMP is a nucleotide with one phosphate. In ATP, the phosphate closest to ribose forms a phosphoester, while two phosphoanhydride bonds link the phosphates.

Hydrolysis of ATP to ADP and inorganic phosphate can couple to processes with a positive free-energy change, provided the sum of the changes is negative and a shared mechanism connects the transformations, for example through phosphoryl-group transfer or a common intermediate. Simply placing two reactions together does not couple them. Spontaneity concerns the balance of the complete reaction; breaking a bond alone requires energy. ATP, ADP and phosphate concentrations and the medium conditions determine the actual value of $\Delta G$.
:::

:::definizione{#coenzimi-redox titolo="NAD and FAD in electron transfer"}
NAD is a dinucleotide containing a nicotinamide portion and an adenine portion. The **NAD⁺/NADH** pair transfers two electrons and one proton to the coenzyme, with a second proton potentially released from the substrate into the medium. The reduction half-reaction is
$$
\mathrm{NAD^+}+\mathrm{H^+}+2e^-\longrightarrow\mathrm{NADH}.
$$
FAD contains a flavin portion linked to an adenine portion. The **FAD/FADH₂** pair can acquire two electrons and two protons:
$$
\mathrm{FAD}+2\,\mathrm{H^+}+2e^-\longrightarrow\mathrm{FADH_2}.
$$
The flavin centre also allows one-electron intermediate states. These equations allow comparison of redox balances without confusing electron transport with ATP phosphoryl-group transfer.
:::

:::definizione{#acidi-nucleici titolo="Phosphodiester bonds and strand orientation"}
In polynucleotides, a **3′–5′ phosphodiester linkage** joins the oxygen of the C3′ group of one residue to the phosphate attached to C5′ of the next. The strand has a direction, and its sequence is written from 5′ to 3′. A linear strand of $n$ nucleotides contains $n-1$ phosphodiester links between adjacent residues; each link includes two ester bonds of the same phosphate.

DNA contains deoxyribose and, normally, the bases A, G, C and T. In the double helix the strands are antiparallel; A pairs with T through two hydrogen bonds and G with C through three. Base stacking contributes to stability. For example, sequence 5′-AGTC-3′ has complementary strand 3′-TCAG-5′, which becomes 5′-GACT-3′ when written from 5′ to 3′.

RNA contains ribose and normally U instead of T. It often consists of a single chain that can fold to form paired regions. The C2′ OH promotes backbone hydrolysis in alkaline conditions because it can attack a nearby phosphate. This difference connects a sugar modification to the polymer's chemical stability.

![A negatively charged 3′–5′ phosphodiester bridge and the C2′ difference between DNA and RNA.](/figure/chimica-semestre-filtro/biomolecole/nucleotide-en.svg)
:::

## Nonenzymatic modifications and protection against oxidation

:::osservazione{#modificazioni-non-enzimatiche titolo="Glycation and deamination"}
**Glycation** is the nonenzymatic reaction between a carbonyl, for example the open-chain form of a reducing sugar, and an amino group. Condensation initially forms a Schiff base; when the sugar is an aldose, it can rearrange to an Amadori product, a ketoamine. Further transformations can produce advanced glycation products. This set of transformations is part of the Maillard reaction. Enzymatic glycosylation instead attaches sugar residues through enzyme-controlled reactions.

**Deamination** of cytosine replaces its amino group with a carbonyl group and forms uracil. In DNA, a cytosine paired with guanine therefore becomes a uracil mismatched with guanine; if the lesion remains unrepaired before replication, it can lead to replacement of the G–C pair by A–T. Deamination of 5-methylcytosine instead forms thymine. The identity of the starting base therefore determines the modification product.

![From an aldose and amine to a Schiff base and an Amadori product: the carbonyl moves to C2.](/figure/chimica-semestre-filtro/biomolecole/glycation-en.svg)
:::

:::osservazione{#radicali-antiossidanti titolo="Hydroxyl radicals, glutathione and tocopherol"}
The hydroxyl radical $\mathrm{{}^{\bullet}OH}$ is highly reactive. It can abstract hydrogen or add to double bonds, modifying protein side chains and the sugars and bases of nucleic acids. In unsaturated lipids it can initiate a peroxidation chain reaction; reaction products can in turn alter other biomolecules.

**Reduced glutathione**, GSH, is the tripeptide γ-glutamyl-cysteinyl-glycine. The bond between glutamate and cysteine uses glutamate's side-chain carboxyl group. The cysteine thiol participates in redox processes; two GSH molecules can form the disulfide GSSG. An overall balance for peroxide reduction is
$$
2\,\mathrm{GSH}+\mathrm{ROOH}
\longrightarrow\mathrm{GSSG}+\mathrm{ROH}+\mathrm{H_2O}.
$$
In biological systems this transformation is often enzyme-catalysed. The thiol is the molecular group enabling reducing transfer. GSH can also participate directly in nonenzymatic reactions with oxidising species. The rate depends on the oxidant and medium: the presence of a thiol does not imply that every reaction is rapid. The thiolate form $\mathrm{GS^-}$ is more nucleophilic than the protonated form; oxidation can involve radical intermediates and other products in addition to the disulfide of the simplified balance.

**Tocopherol** contains a phenolic portion and a hydrophobic portion that localises it in lipid regions. It can donate a hydrogen to a peroxyl radical, forming a tocopheroxyl radical stabilised by delocalisation. In this way it interrupts a propagation step in lipid peroxidation. **Carotenoids**, such as β-carotene and lycopene, have an extended system of conjugated double bonds. They can dissipate singlet-oxygen energy through energy transfer and participate in radical scavenging; the effect depends on concentration, oxygenation and environment. Tocopherol stabilises its radical through its aromatic system, whereas carotenoids have a conjugated polyene chain.

In peroxidation, initiation can be distinguished from propagation. A radical abstracts H from a lipid $\mathrm{LH}$ to form $\mathrm{L^\bullet}$; oxygen addition produces $\mathrm{LOO^\bullet}$. This radical can abstract H from another $\mathrm{LH}$ molecule, producing $\mathrm{LOOH}$ and a new $\mathrm{L^\bullet}$. The reaction continues because it regenerates a radical: the hydroperoxide product and the peroxyl radical are distinct species.
:::
