## The protein amino acids

:::definizione{#amminoacidi titolo="Amino acid structure"}
Proteins are made of **amino acids**. An α-amino acid is a molecule in which an amino group $\mathrm{NH_2}$ and a carboxyl group COOH are bonded to the same carbon, called the **α carbon**. A hydrogen and a fourth group, the **side chain**, written R, are also bonded to the α carbon. The general formula is $\mathrm{H_2N{-}CH(R){-}COOH}$. The part common to all amino acids is the backbone; the side chain is different in each and determines its properties. In proline the side chain closes back onto the nitrogen and forms a ring, so the amino group is a secondary amine.

In water the two groups of the backbone are ionised. At a pH close to 7 the carboxyl group has given up its proton and is $\mathrm{COO^-}$, while the amino group has gained one and is $\mathrm{NH_3^+}$. The prevailing form is therefore $\mathrm{{}^{+}H_3N{-}CH(R){-}COO^{-}}$, with one positive and one negative charge. The properties of an amino acid are found by looking at the side chain: whether it is non-polar, polar or charged. For example, alanine has a $\mathrm{CH_3}$ group as side chain, non-polar; serine has a $\mathrm{CH_2OH}$ group, polar because it contains an OH.

![Side chains of aspartate, asparagine, cysteine and methionine, highlighting their different functional groups.](/figure/chimica-semestre-filtro/biomolecole/sidechains-en.svg)
:::

:::

:::definizione{#essenziali titolo="Essential amino acids and endogenous synthesis"}
An **essential amino acid** is an amino acid that the human body is unable to produce, or produces in insufficient amounts, and that must therefore be taken in with food. In adults there are nine: histidine, isoleucine, leucine, lysine, methionine, phenylalanine, threonine, tryptophan and valine.

The other eleven are produced by the body from other molecules. For example, tyrosine is obtained from phenylalanine. Some of them become necessary in the diet in particular situations, such as growth or illness, and are called *conditionally essential*. The distinction between essential and non-essential concerns nutrition and has no relation to the chemical classification into polar and non-polar.
:::

## Stereochemistry, charges and isoelectric point

:::definizione{#stereochimica-amminoacidi titolo="L configuration and Fischer projections"}
In all amino acids except glycine the α carbon is bonded to four different groups, so it is a stereogenic centre and there are two enantiomers, called L and D. They are distinguished with the **Fischer projection**: the carboxyl group is drawn at the top and the side chain at the bottom; if the amino group is on the left the amino acid is L, if it is on the right it is D. In this drawing the horizontal bonds come towards the viewer and the vertical ones go away.

Proteins contain only L-amino acids. The letter L shows the arrangement of the groups and not the direction in which the substance rotates polarised light, which is obtained only by measurement. In the R/S system almost all L-amino acids have the S configuration. L-Cysteine is an exception and is R: the arrangement in space is the same, but the sulfur of the side chain changes its priority in the CIP rules. Glycine has two hydrogens on the α carbon and is not chiral. Isoleucine and threonine have a second stereogenic centre in the side chain.

![Fischer projections of L-alanine and L-cysteine: the same L series, different absolute configurations.](/figure/chimica-semestre-filtro/biomolecole/fischer-aa-en.svg)
:::

:::definizione{#acido-base-pi titolo="Zwitterions and isoelectric point"}
A **zwitterion** is a molecule that carries both a positive and a negative charge, and is neutral overall. An amino acid has two groups that give up or gain protons, so its charge depends on the pH. For glycine, going from acidic to basic pH, three forms follow one another.
$$
\mathrm{{}^{+}H_3NCH_2COOH}
\rightleftharpoons
\mathrm{{}^{+}H_3NCH_2COO^{-}}
\rightleftharpoons
\mathrm{H_2NCH_2COO^{-}}.
$$
At very acidic pH both groups have their proton and the charge is $+1$. At intermediate pH the carboxyl group has lost its proton: this is the zwitterion, with charge 0. At basic pH the amino group has also lost its proton and the charge is $-1$. The **isoelectric point** $pI$ is the pH at which the average charge of the molecule is zero. For an amino acid with no ionisable groups in the side chain it is the average of the two $pK_a$ values.
$$
pI=\dfrac{pK_{a,\mathrm{COOH}}+pK_{a,\mathrm{NH_3^+}}}{2}.
$$
For example, for glycine with $pK_a=2.30$ and $9.60$ one gets $pI=\dfrac{2.30+9.60}{2}=5.95$. If the side chain also has an ionisable group, there are three $pK_a$ values, and the average is taken of the two on either side of the form with zero charge. For aspartic acid the values are $2.10$ (backbone carboxyl), $3.90$ (side-chain carboxyl) and $9.80$ (amino group). The form with zero charge lies between the first two, so $pI=\dfrac{2.10+3.90}{2}=3.00$. Acidic amino acids have a low $pI$ and basic ones, such as lysine, a high $pI$.

Comparing the pH with the $pI$ gives the charge of the molecule. If the pH is lower than the $pI$ the molecule has a positive charge; if it is higher it has a negative charge; if it is equal the charge is zero. **Electrophoresis** is based on this property; it is a technique that separates amino acids and proteins in an electric field: positive molecules migrate towards the negative pole, negative ones towards the positive pole and those at the isoelectric point stay still. The zwitterion has zero total charge but contains two charged groups, so it is very soluble in water.

![Mean glycine charge as a function of pH, with the isoelectric point between the two pKa values.](/figure/chimica-semestre-filtro/biomolecole/pi-en.svg)
:::

## Peptide bonds and protein structure

:::definizione{#legame-peptidico titolo="Formation and orientation of peptide bonds"}
Amino acids are joined together by the **peptide bond**, which is an amide bond between the carboxyl group of one amino acid and the amino group of the next. The formation of the bond is a nucleophilic acyl substitution. A carboxyl group as such reacts poorly, so it must first be activated. The scheme below shows the reaction with the first amino acid in the form of a methyl ester: the amino group of the second amino acid attacks the carbonyl of the ester and takes the place of the $\mathrm{OCH_3}$ group.
$$
\begin{aligned}
&\mathrm{H_2N{-}CH(R_1){-}COOCH_3}
+\mathrm{H_2N{-}CH(R_2){-}COOH}\\[4pt]
&\quad\longrightarrow
\mathrm{H_2N{-}CH(R_1){-}CO{-}NH{-}CH(R_2){-}COOH}
+\mathrm{CH_3OH}.
\end{aligned}
$$
A **dipeptide** is obtained and methanol is released. In cells the principle is the same: during protein synthesis on the ribosome each amino acid is bound as an ester to a tRNA molecule, which acts as the leaving group.

A chain of amino acids has two different ends: the **N-terminal** end, with the free amino group, and the **C-terminal** end, with the free carboxyl group. The sequence is always written from the N-terminus to the C-terminus. For example, Gly-Ala is the dipeptide in which glycine has the free amino group and alanine the free carboxyl group; Ala-Gly is a different dipeptide. The amino acids inserted in a chain are called *residues*. A chain of $n$ residues contains $n-1$ peptide bonds, and for each bond formed one molecule of water is lost. A short chain is called a peptide; a long chain, a polypeptide or protein.
:::

:::osservazione{#geometria-peptidica titolo="Resonance and planarity"}
The peptide bond has a particular geometry. The lone pair of the nitrogen is spread towards the carbonyl by resonance, so the C–N bond has partly the character of a double bond. Two consequences follow. The six atoms of the peptide group (the carbon and oxygen of the carbonyl, the nitrogen with its hydrogen and the two neighbouring α carbons) lie in the same plane. In addition there is no rotation around the C–N bond. The chain can rotate only around the two bonds of the α carbon, and this limits the shapes a protein can take. The two α carbons are almost always on opposite sides of the peptide bond (*trans* configuration), because in this way the side chains are further apart; the cis configuration is sometimes found before a proline.

![Six atoms of the peptide plane and partial double-bond character of C–N.](/figure/chimica-semestre-filtro/biomolecole/peptide-en.svg)
:::

:::definizione{#struttura-proteine titolo="Levels of protein structure"}
The structure of a protein is described on four levels.

The **primary structure** is the sequence of amino acids, that is, the order in which they are joined by peptide bonds.

The **secondary structure** is the regular shape taken by stretches of the chain. It is held together by hydrogen bonds between the C=O group of one peptide bond and the N–H group of another. The two main forms are the **α-helix**, in which the chain coils into a spiral and each C=O is bonded to the N–H of the residue four positions further along, and the **β-sheet**, in which extended stretches of the chain lie side by side.

The **tertiary structure** is the complete three-dimensional shape of a chain. It is held together by interactions between the side chains: the hydrophobic effect, which brings non-polar chains inside the protein away from water; hydrogen bonds; attractions between opposite charges; van der Waals forces; disulfide bridges.

The **quaternary structure** exists only in proteins made of several chains and is the way the chains associate. Haemoglobin, for example, is made of four chains.

The **disulfide bridge** is the only covalent bond between side chains. It forms by oxidation of the SH groups of two cysteines.
$$
2\,\mathrm{RSH}\longrightarrow\mathrm{RSSR}+2\,\mathrm{H^+}+2\,e^-.
$$
Each cysteine loses one electron and one proton, so the reaction requires an oxidant to receive the two electrons. The bridge can join two points of the same chain or two different chains. **Denaturation** is the loss of the secondary, tertiary and quaternary structure, caused for example by heat or by an extreme pH. The denatured protein loses its function. The peptide bonds do not break, so the primary structure stays intact.
:::

## Monosaccharides and stereoisomerism

:::definizione{#monosaccaridi titolo="Aldoses, ketoses and the D/L series"}
**Carbohydrates**, or sugars, are compounds with a carbonyl group and several OH groups. A **monosaccharide** is a simple sugar, which cannot be split into smaller sugars. Monosaccharides are classified in two ways.

- According to the carbonyl group: an **aldose** contains an aldehyde group and a **ketose** a ketone group.
- According to the number of carbons: a pentose has five and a hexose six.

Glucose is an aldose with six carbons (aldohexose); fructose is a ketose with six carbons (ketohexose); ribose is an aldose with five carbons (aldopentose).

Monosaccharides have several stereogenic centres and are drawn in Fischer projection, with the carbonyl group at the top. They belong to the D series if the OH of the stereogenic centre farthest from the carbonyl, that is, the second-to-last carbon, is on the right; to the L series if it is on the left. Natural sugars almost all belong to the D series. Between two sugars with the same formula three relationships are possible.

- They are **enantiomers** if all the stereogenic centres have the opposite configuration, like D-glucose and L-glucose.
- They are **diastereomers** if they differ only at some centres. If they differ at a single centre they are called **epimers**: D-glucose and D-mannose are epimers at carbon 2, D-glucose and D-galactose at carbon 4.
- They are **constitutional isomers** if they have different groups, like glucose and fructose, which both have the formula $\mathrm{C_6H_{12}O_6}$ but one is an aldose and the other a ketose.

![Fischer projections of D-glucose and D-mannose: only the C2 centre changes.](/figure/chimica-semestre-filtro/biomolecole/fischer-sugar-en.svg)
:::

:::definizione{#anomeri titolo="Cyclisation and anomers"}
In water monosaccharides with five or six carbons are almost entirely in ring form. An OH group of the molecule attacks the carbonyl of the same molecule and forms a cyclic hemiacetal (a hemiketal, if the sugar is a ketose). In glucose the OH of carbon 5 attacks carbon 1 and a six-membered ring forms, called a **pyranose**. In fructose the OH of carbon 5 attacks carbon 2 and a five-membered ring forms, called a **furanose**.

The carbon that was the carbonyl becomes a new stereogenic centre and is called the **anomeric carbon**. Its OH can be in two positions, so there are two cyclic forms, the α and β **anomers**. The rings are drawn with the **Haworth projection**, in which the ring is seen edge-on and the groups lie above or below. For D-glucose, in the α anomer the OH of the anomeric carbon is on the opposite side from the $\mathrm{CH_2OH}$ group (down in the usual drawing); in the β anomer it is on the same side (up).

In solution the ring opens and closes continuously, and the two anomers turn into each other through the open form until an equilibrium is reached. The two anomers rotate polarised light to a different extent, so during this process the optical rotation of the solution changes: the phenomenon is called **mutarotation**. It takes place only if the anomeric carbon has a free OH. If the anomeric carbon is engaged in a glycosidic bond, described in the next section, the ring cannot open.

![α and β D-glucopyranose anomers, with the anomeric OH opposite to or on the same side as CH2OH.](/figure/chimica-semestre-filtro/biomolecole/haworth-en.svg)
:::

## Monosaccharide reactions and glycosidic bonds

:::osservazione{#reazioni-carboidrati titolo="Oxidation, reduction and condensation"}
Monosaccharides give the reactions of their functional groups.

- **Oxidation**. If the aldehyde group of glucose is oxidised to a carboxyl group, gluconic acid is obtained. If the primary alcohol of carbon 6 is oxidised, glucuronic acid is obtained. If both ends are oxidised, an aldaric acid is obtained.
- **Reduction**. If the carbonyl group is reduced to an alcohol, a polyol is obtained. Glucose gives sorbitol. Fructose gives sorbitol and mannitol, because the reduction creates a new stereogenic centre at carbon 2.

A **reducing sugar** is a sugar that can be oxidised, reducing another reagent; it is recognised with tests such as Fehling's. Every sugar with a free anomeric carbon is reducing, because the ring can open and release the aldehyde group. Fructose, which is a ketose, also turns out to be reducing: in a basic environment it is partly converted into glucose and mannose through an intermediate called an enediol. This conversion between an aldose and a ketose is a tautomerism, in which a hydrogen and a double bond change position.

The anomeric carbon of a sugar can react with an OH group of another molecule. A molecule of water is eliminated and the hemiacetal becomes an acetal: the bond that forms is called an **O-glycosidic bond**. Monosaccharides are joined to each other by this bond. The name of the bond gives the anomer and the carbons involved. For example, in the α$(1\to4)$ bond carbon 1 of one glucose, in the α configuration, is bonded to the oxygen of carbon 4 of another glucose. Hydrolysis, that is, the reaction with water, breaks the bond and gives back the two sugars.
:::

:::definizione{#oligosaccaridi titolo="Disaccharides and amino sugars"}
A **disaccharide** consists of two monosaccharides joined by a glycosidic bond; an **oligosaccharide** contains from three to about ten. The three most important disaccharides are shown in the table.

| Disaccharide | Units and bond | Reducing character |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Maltose | Glucose α$(1\to4)$ glucose | Reducing, one free anomeric centre |
| Lactose | Galactose β$(1\to4)$ glucose | Reducing, one free anomeric centre |
| Sucrose | Glucose α$(1\leftrightarrow2)$β fructose | Nonreducing, both anomeric centres involved |

On hydrolysis maltose gives two molecules of glucose, lactose one glucose and one galactose, sucrose one glucose and one fructose. Maltose and lactose are reducing, because one of the two units still has a free anomeric carbon. In sucrose, table sugar, the bond joins the anomeric carbons of both units: no ring can open and the sugar is not reducing.

An **amino sugar** is a sugar in which an OH group is replaced by an amino group. In glucosamine the $\mathrm{NH_2}$ group is on carbon 2 of glucose. If the amino group is bonded to an acetyl group the result is N-acetylglucosamine. Amino sugars are found in the sugars attached to cell-surface proteins and in the polysaccharides of connective tissue.
:::

## Polysaccharides and extracellular matrix

:::definizione{#polisaccaridi titolo="Starch, glycogen and cellulose"}
A **polysaccharide** consists of hundreds or thousands of monosaccharides. It is called a **homopolysaccharide** if it contains a single kind of monosaccharide and a **heteropolysaccharide** if it contains several kinds. Starch, glycogen and cellulose are all made of glucose only. They differ in the type of glycosidic bond and in the presence of branches, and this is enough to give them very different properties and functions.

| Polymer | Main bonds | Organisation and function |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Amylose, a component of starch | α$(1\to4)$ | Mainly linear chains; plant storage |
| Amylopectin, a component of starch | α$(1\to4)$ and α$(1\to6)$ branches | Branched chains; plant storage |
| Glycogen | α$(1\to4)$ and α$(1\to6)$ branches | More branched than amylopectin; animal storage |
| Cellulose | β$(1\to4)$ | Linear chains associated into fibres; plant structural role |

The α$(1\to4)$ bond makes the chain coil into a helix; the β$(1\to4)$ bond gives straight chains, which lie side by side and bind to each other with hydrogen bonds, forming strong fibres. The α$(1\to6)$ branches of glycogen create many ends, from which enzymes quickly detach glucose when it is needed. Human digestive enzymes, the amylases, hydrolyse the α bonds of starch but not the β bonds of cellulose. This is why starch is a food and cellulose is an indigestible fibre.

![α(1→4) links along a chain and α(1→6) at a glycogen branch.](/figure/chimica-semestre-filtro/biomolecole/branch-en.svg)
:::

:::definizione{#glicosamminoglicani titolo="Glycosaminoglycans"}
**Glycosaminoglycans** (GAGs) are long, unbranched heteropolysaccharides, formed by the repetition of the same unit of two sugars. Usually one of the two is an amino sugar and the other a uronic acid, that is, a sugar with a carboxyl group. They have many negative charges, due to carboxylate groups and, in many GAGs, to sulfate groups. The charges repel each other and attract a lot of water, so GAGs form gels that occupy a large volume and resist compression. They are found in connective tissue, in cartilage and in joint fluid.

**Hyaluronic acid** consists of alternating glucuronic acid and N-acetylglucosamine, and contains no sulfate. **Chondroitin sulfate**, abundant in cartilage, contains sulfate groups. In keratan sulfate galactose takes the place of the uronic acid. Almost all GAGs are covalently bonded to a protein and form **proteoglycans**; hyaluronic acid is an exception and is not bonded to a protein.
:::

## Fatty acids and triacylglycerols

:::definizione{#acidi-grassi titolo="Saturated and unsaturated chains"}
**Lipids** are biological molecules insoluble in water. The simplest are **fatty acids**, carboxylic acids with a long hydrocarbon chain, usually from 12 to 20 carbons. A fatty acid is **saturated** if the chain has only single bonds and **unsaturated** if it has one or more double bonds.

A fatty acid is denoted by two numbers: the number of carbons and the number of double bonds. Stearic acid is $18{:}0$, with 18 carbons and no double bond. Oleic acid is $18{:}1\,\Delta^9$: the symbol $\Delta^9$ shows that the double bond lies between carbons 9 and 10, counting from the carbon of the carboxyl group. In the ω (omega) notation one counts instead from the other end, from the $\mathrm{CH_3}$ group: oleic acid is an ω-9.

The shape of the chain determines the melting temperature. Saturated chains are straight, pack in an orderly way next to one another and attract each other strongly: saturated fats, such as butter, are solid at room temperature. In natural unsaturated fatty acids the double bonds are almost always cis. A cis double bond bends the chain, the molecules pack in a less orderly way and the melting temperature falls: oils are liquid. A trans double bond leaves the chain almost straight, and trans fatty acids behave in a similar way to saturated ones. For the same number of double bonds, the melting temperature increases with the length of the chain.

Linoleic acid, $18{:}2\,\Delta^{9,12}$, is an ω-6; α-linolenic acid, $18{:}3\,\Delta^{9,12,15}$, is an ω-3. The ω number is obtained by subtracting the highest Δ position from the number of carbons: $18-12=6$ and $18-15=3$. These two fatty acids are **essential**: the human body cannot insert double bonds in those positions, so they must be taken in with food.

Fatty acids give the reactions of the carboxyl group: they form salts with bases and esters with alcohols. The double bonds give two reactions. **Hydrogenation** adds hydrogen and turns them into single bonds; in this way a liquid oil becomes a solid fat, as in the production of margarine. **Oxidation** by the oxygen in air alters unsaturated fats and makes them go rancid.

![Cis and trans geometry around the C9=C10 double bond of a fatty acid.](/figure/chimica-semestre-filtro/biomolecole/cis-trans-en.svg)
:::

:::definizione{#triacilgliceroli titolo="Glycerol esters and saponification"}
A **triacylglycerol**, or triglyceride, consists of a molecule of glycerol bonded to three fatty acids. Glycerol has three OH groups, and each forms an ester bond with the carboxyl group of a fatty acid. The three fatty acids can be the same or different. Triglycerides are entirely non-polar and insoluble in water. They are the main energy reserve of the body and accumulate in adipose tissue. The fats and oils in food are mixtures of triglycerides.

The hydrolysis of a triglyceride with a strong base is called **saponification**. The three ester bonds break and glycerol and three fatty acid salts are obtained. For three identical fatty acids the reaction is
$$
\mathrm{C_3H_5(OCOR)_3}+3\,\mathrm{OH^-}
\longrightarrow\mathrm{C_3H_5(OH)_3}+3\,\mathrm{RCOO^-},
$$
where R stands for the chain of the fatty acid. For example, one mole of tripalmitin consumes three moles of NaOH and forms one mole of glycerol and three moles of sodium palmitate. The salts of fatty acids are **soaps**. They have a charged head, with an affinity for water, and a long non-polar tail, so they are amphipathic. In water they form micelles: the tails gather inside and enclose fatty substances, and this explains their cleaning action.
:::

## Complex lipids and cholesterol

:::definizione{#lipidi-complessi titolo="Glycerophospholipids, sphingolipids and glycolipids"}
The lipids of cell membranes are amphipathic: they have a **polar head** and two **non-polar tails**. In water they spontaneously arrange themselves in a bilayer, with the tails inside and the heads towards the water, which is the basic structure of every membrane.

**Glycerophospholipids** are the most abundant. They consist of a molecule of glycerol to which two fatty acids, through ester bonds, and a phosphate group are attached. Another polar group is usually bonded to the phosphate. If this group is choline the result is phosphatidylcholine (lecithin). The simplest is phosphatidic acid, in which nothing is bonded to the phosphate.

**Sphingolipids** are based on sphingosine instead of glycerol. Sphingosine is a molecule with a long hydrocarbon chain, an amino group and two OH groups. A fatty acid bonds to the amino group through an amide bond and a **ceramide** is obtained, which therefore has two non-polar tails. A polar head, of two kinds, is then attached to the ceramide.

- If the head is phosphate and choline the result is **sphingomyelin**, abundant in the myelin sheath of nerves. It is both a sphingolipid and a phospholipid.
- If the head consists of one or more sugars the result is a **glycosphingolipid**. Cerebrosides have a single sugar; gangliosides have a chain of sugars that contains sialic acid.

Any lipid that contains sugars is called a **glycolipid**. To recognise a lipid three elements are examined in order: the base molecule (glycerol or sphingosine), the bond by which the fatty acids are attached (ester on glycerol, amide on sphingosine) and the polar head. Three fatty acids bonded to glycerol indicate a triglyceride; two fatty acids and a phosphate on glycerol indicate a glycerophospholipid; a fatty acid bonded to sphingosine through an amide indicates a ceramide.

![Glycerol esters and the ceramide amide: comparison of backbones and bonds.](/figure/chimica-semestre-filtro/biomolecole/lipids-en.svg)
:::

:::osservazione{#colesterolo titolo="Steroid structure and derivatives"}
**Cholesterol** belongs to the steroids, lipids whose structure consists of four fused carbon rings. It has a single polar group, an OH on carbon 3, and a hydrocarbon tail; the rest of the molecule is non-polar and rigid. It fits into membranes with the OH near the polar heads of the phospholipids and the rings between the tails. Its presence regulates the fluidity of the membrane: at high temperature it hinders the movement of the tails and makes the membrane less fluid; at low temperature it prevents the tails from packing tightly and keeps it fluid.

From cholesterol the body produces other molecules that keep the four rings: **steroid hormones**, such as cortisol, testosterone and oestrogens; **bile acids**, which have more polar groups and serve to emulsify fats in the intestine; **vitamin D**, which forms in the skin from a derivative of cholesterol, 7-dehydrocholesterol, when ultraviolet light opens one of the rings.
:::

## Nucleotides, coenzymes and nucleic acids

:::definizione{#nucleotidi titolo="Nitrogenous bases, nucleosides and nucleotides"}
Nucleic acids, DNA and RNA, are made of **nucleotides**. A nucleotide has three components: a nitrogenous base, a five-carbon sugar and one or more phosphate groups.

The **nitrogenous bases** are of two kinds. The **purines**, adenine (A) and guanine (G), have two fused rings. The **pyrimidines**, cytosine (C), thymine (T) and uracil (U), have a single ring.

A **nucleoside** consists of a base and the sugar, without phosphate. The base is bonded to carbon 1′ of the sugar by an N-glycosidic bond, through nitrogen 9 in purines and nitrogen 1 in pyrimidines. The carbons of the sugar are marked with a prime (1′, 2′ and so on) to distinguish them from the atoms of the base. A **nucleotide** is a nucleoside with one or more phosphates bonded to carbon 5′.

The sugar of RNA is **ribose**, which has an OH group on carbon 2′. The sugar of DNA is **2-deoxyribose**, which has a hydrogen in that position. For example, adenosine is the nucleoside made of adenine and ribose. Adding one phosphate gives AMP (adenosine monophosphate), with two ADP and with three ATP. In ATP the first phosphate is bonded to ribose by an ester bond; the three phosphates are joined to each other by two phosphoanhydride bonds, which are the bonds from which the cell obtains energy.

**ATP** is the molecule with which the cell transfers energy. Its hydrolysis to ADP and phosphate has a very negative $\Delta G$. Cells use this reaction to drive reactions that on their own would have a positive $\Delta G$: the two reactions are *coupled*, and the overall process takes place if the sum of the two $\Delta G$ values is negative. Coupling requires the two reactions to have an intermediate in common. Usually an enzyme transfers a phosphate group from ATP to a reactant, which thus becomes more reactive. The energy does not come from the breaking of the bond in itself, which actually requires energy: it comes from the fact that the products, ADP and phosphate, are more stable than ATP. The actual value of $\Delta G$ depends on the concentrations of ATP, ADP and phosphate in the cell.
:::

:::definizione{#coenzimi-redox titolo="NAD and FAD in electron transfer"}
In the redox reactions of metabolism electrons are carried by two coenzymes, which are also derived from nucleotides.

**NAD** consists of two joined nucleotides: one contains adenine and the other nicotinamide, which derives from vitamin B3 (niacin). Nicotinamide is the part that receives the electrons. The oxidised form is $\mathrm{NAD^+}$ and the reduced form is NADH. When a substrate is oxidised it loses two electrons and two protons: $\mathrm{NAD^+}$ receives the two electrons and one proton, that is, a hydride ion $\mathrm{H^-}$, and the second proton passes into the solution.
$$
\mathrm{NAD^+}+\mathrm{H^+}+2e^-\longrightarrow\mathrm{NADH}.
$$
**FAD** contains a flavin, which derives from vitamin B2 (riboflavin), bonded to a nucleotide with adenine. The oxidised form is FAD and the reduced form is $\mathrm{FADH_2}$. FAD receives two electrons and two protons, that is, two hydrogen atoms.
$$
\mathrm{FAD}+2\,\mathrm{H^+}+2e^-\longrightarrow\mathrm{FADH_2}.
$$
Unlike NAD, the flavin can also receive a single electron at a time. NADH and $\mathrm{FADH_2}$ then pass their electrons to the respiratory chain of the mitochondria. The two kinds of coenzyme have different tasks: NAD and FAD carry electrons, whereas ATP carries phosphate groups.
:::

:::definizione{#acidi-nucleici titolo="Phosphodiester bonds and strand orientation"}
In nucleic acids the nucleotides are joined into a chain by the **phosphodiester bond**. The phosphate group, bonded to carbon 5′ of one nucleotide, also bonds to the OH of carbon 3′ of the previous nucleotide. The phosphate therefore forms two ester bonds, hence the name. The chain has two different ends: the 5′ end, with a free phosphate, and the 3′ end, with a free OH. The sequence of bases is always written from the 5′ end to the 3′ end. A chain of $n$ nucleotides contains $n-1$ phosphodiester bonds. Each phosphate carries a negative charge, so nucleic acids are strongly charged molecules.

**DNA** contains deoxyribose and the bases A, G, C and T. It consists of two chains wound around each other in a **double helix**. The two chains are *antiparallel*, that is, oriented in opposite directions: one runs from 5′ to 3′ and the other from 3′ to 5′. The sugars and phosphates are on the outside and the bases on the inside, where each base of one chain is paired with a base of the other by hydrogen bonds. The pairing is always the same: A with T, with two hydrogen bonds, and G with C, with three. The two chains are therefore *complementary*: the sequence of one determines that of the other. The double helix is also stabilised by the attraction between the bases stacked on top of one another. For example, the sequence 5′-AGTC-3′ corresponds on the other chain to 3′-TCAG-5′, which read from 5′ to 3′ is 5′-GACT-3′.

**RNA** contains ribose and has uracil (U) in place of thymine. It usually consists of a single chain, which can fold back on itself and form short paired stretches. RNA is less stable than DNA: the OH on carbon 2′ of ribose can attack the neighbouring phosphate and break the chain, especially in a basic environment. DNA does not have this OH, and this is one of the reasons why it is suited to storing genetic information.

:::

## Nonenzymatic modifications and protection against oxidation

:::osservazione{#modificazioni-non-enzimatiche titolo="Glycation and deamination"}
Some reactions modify biomolecules without the action of enzymes, and over time alter their function.

**Glycation** is the reaction between the carbonyl group of a reducing sugar, in its open form, and an amino group of a protein. It proceeds in three phases.

1. The amino group adds to the carbonyl and an imine, or Schiff base, forms.
2. If the sugar is an aldose, the Schiff base turns into a more stable compound, the **Amadori product**, in which the carbonyl has moved to carbon 2.
3. Over time advanced glycation end products (AGEs) form, which link proteins to each other permanently. These reactions as a whole are known as the Maillard reaction.

Glycation increases when the concentration of glucose in the blood is high. Glycated haemoglobin, measured in blood tests, shows the average glucose level of the previous months. *Glycosylation*, on the other hand, is the addition of sugars to a protein by enzymes, at precise positions, and is a normal process.

**Deamination** is the loss of an amino group. In cytosine the $\mathrm{NH_2}$ group is replaced by a C=O group, and cytosine becomes uracil. In DNA this is damage: cytosine was paired with a guanine, whereas uracil pairs with adenine. If the damage is not repaired before replication, the G–C pair becomes an A–T pair, that is, a mutation. Since uracil is not a normal base of DNA, repair enzymes recognise it and remove it. If the cytosine carries a methyl group (5-methylcytosine), deamination produces thymine, which is a normal base of DNA and is recognised with more difficulty.

:::

:::osservazione{#radicali-antiossidanti titolo="Hydroxyl radicals, glutathione and tocopherol"}
The **hydroxyl radical** $\mathrm{{}^{\bullet}OH}$ is the most reactive of the reactive oxygen species. It reacts with almost any molecule it meets: it removes a hydrogen atom or adds to a double bond. In this way it damages the side chains of proteins, sugars and the bases of DNA. In the unsaturated fatty acids of membranes it starts **lipid peroxidation**, a chain reaction. The cell defends itself with **antioxidant** molecules, which react with radicals and neutralise them.

**Glutathione** (GSH) is the most abundant antioxidant inside cells. It is a tripeptide made of glutamate, cysteine and glycine. It has one peculiarity: glutamate is bonded to cysteine through the carboxyl group of its side chain (γ bond) instead of that of the backbone. The active part is the SH group of cysteine. Two molecules of glutathione each give up a hydrogen atom and join through a disulfide bridge, forming oxidised glutathione (GSSG). In this way they reduce a peroxide ROOH to an alcohol.
$$
2\,\mathrm{GSH}+\mathrm{ROOH}
\longrightarrow\mathrm{GSSG}+\mathrm{ROH}+\mathrm{H_2O}.
$$
In cells the reaction is catalysed by the enzyme glutathione peroxidase. Another enzyme, glutathione reductase, then brings GSSG back to GSH using NADPH, so glutathione is available again. Glutathione also reacts directly with some oxidising species, without enzymes; the form that reacts fastest is the thiolate ion $\mathrm{GS^-}$.

In membranes, which are non-polar, fat-soluble antioxidants act.

**Tocopherol** (vitamin E) has a phenolic ring and a long hydrocarbon tail, which keeps it in the membrane. It gives the hydrogen of its OH to a peroxyl radical and neutralises it. Tocopherol in turn becomes a radical, but the unpaired electron is spread over the aromatic ring by resonance: this radical is not very reactive and does not continue the chain.

**Carotenoids**, such as β-carotene and lycopene, have a long chain of conjugated double bonds. They neutralise radicals and singlet oxygen, a very reactive form of oxygen, whose excess energy they absorb.

Lipid peroxidation proceeds in three steps, in which LH stands for an unsaturated fatty acid.

1. **Initiation**. A radical removes a hydrogen atom from LH and forms a lipid radical $\mathrm{L^\bullet}$.
2. **Propagation**. The radical $\mathrm{L^\bullet}$ reacts with oxygen and forms a peroxyl radical $\mathrm{LOO^\bullet}$. This removes a hydrogen from another LH molecule: a hydroperoxide LOOH and a new radical $\mathrm{L^\bullet}$ form, and the latter repeats the cycle.
3. **Termination**. The chain stops when two radicals combine or when an antioxidant, such as tocopherol, gives a hydrogen to the peroxyl radical.

Since each cycle regenerates a radical, a single initial radical damages many lipid molecules.
:::
