## Mixtures, phases and solutions

:::definizione{#miscele titolo="Homogeneous and heterogeneous mixtures"}
A **mixture** is a set of two or more substances mixed together. A mixture is *homogeneous* if it has the same appearance and the same composition at every point: the components cannot be told apart even under a microscope and form a single **phase**. A mixture is *heterogeneous* if different parts can be distinguished, that is, several phases separated by a surface. Water and dissolved sugar form a homogeneous mixture; water and oil form a heterogeneous mixture.

A **solution** is a homogeneous mixture. The component in which the others are dissolved is called the **solvent** and the dissolved components are called **solutes**. The solvent is usually the component present in the largest amount; in any case it is the one that has the same physical state as the solution. In the solution the solute is divided into single molecules or single ions, spread evenly through the solvent. Solutions exist in all physical states: salt water is a liquid solution, air is a solution of gases and bronze is a solid solution of copper and tin.

For example, a teaspoon of salt dissolved in a glass of water gives a single liquid phase. If more salt keeps being added, at some point the salt no longer dissolves and settles at the bottom. The liquid is then a *saturated* solution and the system, made of the liquid and the solid salt, is heterogeneous.
:::

:::definizione{#dispersioni titolo="Suspensions, colloids and aerosols"}
Between solutions and heterogeneous mixtures visible to the naked eye there are two intermediate cases, distinguished by the size of the dispersed particles.

- In a **suspension** the particles are large, more than about one micrometre. Over time they settle at the bottom and can be separated with a filter. Fine sand in water is an example.
- In a **colloid** the particles have sizes roughly between one nanometre and one micrometre. They are too small to settle or to be held back by an ordinary filter, and large enough to scatter light: a beam of light passing through a colloid becomes visible (Tyndall effect), whereas in a solution it cannot be seen.

Milk is a colloid made of fat droplets dispersed in water; a colloid of one liquid in another liquid is called an *emulsion*. Blood is a more complex mixture: the cells are suspended in the plasma, and the plasma contains salts in solution and proteins in colloidal form. An **aerosol** is a dispersion of liquid droplets or solid particles in a gas, such as fog and smoke. The laws of solutions presented in this chapter hold for true solutions and not for suspensions and colloids.
:::

:::esempio{titolo="Phases and dispersed particles"}
A solution of salt in water contains three kinds of particles (sodium ions, chloride ions and water molecules) in a single phase. The number of substances and the number of phases are therefore two different things. Whole blood, on the other hand, is not a solution, because the cells are particles much larger than molecules and form a phase distinct from the plasma. A clear liquid is often a solution; to be certain, one observes whether a deposit forms over time and whether a beam of light passing through it becomes visible.

:::

## Water, dissolution and electrolytes

:::definizione{#solubilita titolo="Solvation and solubility"}
The water molecule is polar: the oxygen has a partial negative charge and the hydrogens a partial positive charge. This is why water dissolves substances made of ions or of polar molecules well.

When a substance dissolves three things happen: the solute particles separate from one another, some solvent molecules move apart to make room, and the solute particles become surrounded by solvent molecules. This last process is called **solvation**; when the solvent is water it is called **hydration**. An ion is surrounded by water molecules oriented according to its charge. Molecules such as glucose and ethanol, which contain OH groups, form hydrogen bonds with water.

The **solubility** of a substance is the maximum amount that can be dissolved in a given amount of solvent, at a given temperature. A solution that contains this maximum amount is called **saturated**: any further solute added remains undissolved. Solubility depends on three factors.

- The nature of the solute and of the solvent. Polar substances dissolve in polar solvents and non-polar ones in non-polar solvents.
- The temperature. The solubility of most solids in water increases with temperature; that of gases decreases, and indeed a warm fizzy drink loses its gas more quickly.
- The pressure. It matters only for gases, whose solubility increases with pressure.

Non-polar molecules, such as fats, dissolve very little in water. An **amphipathic** molecule has a polar part, which stays in contact with water, and a non-polar part, which moves away from it. Soaps and phospholipids are amphipathic. In water these molecules gather into ordered structures, with the non-polar parts inside and the polar parts outside: **micelles**, which are spherical, and the bilayers of cell membranes. These dispersions are not true solutions, so the laws of this chapter do not apply to them.
:::

:::definizione{#elettroliti titolo="Dissociation and particle number"}
An **electrolyte** is a substance that separates into ions in water. The solution then conducts electric current, because the ions carry charge. Three cases are distinguished.

- A **strong electrolyte** separates into ions completely. Salts, such as NaCl, and strong acids and bases are strong electrolytes.
- A **weak electrolyte** separates into ions only to a small extent. Acetic acid is an example.
- A **non-electrolyte** dissolves while remaining as whole molecules. Glucose is an example.

The two reactions below show the dissociation of two salts.
$$
\mathrm{NaCl}\longrightarrow\mathrm{Na^+}+\mathrm{Cl^-},\qquad
\mathrm{CaCl_2}\longrightarrow\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}+2\mathrm{Cl^-}.
$$
One mole of sodium chloride therefore produces two moles of particles (one of $\mathrm{Na^+}$ and one of $\mathrm{Cl^-}$), and one mole of calcium chloride produces three (one of $\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}$ and two of $\mathrm{Cl^-}$). As a consequence the concentration of the salt and that of the individual ions are different: a $0.10\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$ solution of $\mathrm{CaCl_2}$ contains $0.10\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$ of calcium ions and $0.20\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$ of chloride ions. The main ions of body fluids are sodium, potassium, calcium, chloride and bicarbonate.
:::

:::osservazione{titolo="Hydration, solubility and ionisation"}
Two pairs of terms denote different properties.

- *Soluble* and *electrolyte*. Glucose is very soluble in water and is not an electrolyte, because it dissolves without forming ions.
- *Strong* and *concentrated*. "Strong" means that the dissolved substance is entirely separated into ions; "concentrated" means that one litre of solution contains a lot of solute. A dilute solution of HCl contains a strong acid in a small amount.

Around a cation the water molecules turn their oxygen towards the ion; around an anion they turn their hydrogens. The water molecules next to the ion are continuously replaced by others.

## Expressing concentration

:::definizione{#concentrazioni titolo="Percentages, molarity and molality"}
The **concentration** shows how much solute is present in a certain amount of solution or of solvent. There are several ways of expressing it, collected in the table. Here $m$ denotes mass, $V$ volume and $n$ the number of moles; the subscript $s$ refers to the solute, $w$ to the solvent and $\mathrm{tot}$ to the whole solution.

| Expression | Definition | Meaning of a value of 5 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mass percentage | $100\dfrac{m_{s}}{m_{\mathrm{tot}}}$ | 5 g solute in 100 g solution |
| Mass–volume percentage | Grams of solute in 100 mL solution | 5 g solute in a final volume of 100 mL |
| Volume percentage | $100\dfrac{V_{s}}{V_{\mathrm{tot}}}$ | 5 mL liquid component per final 100 mL |
| Molarity $c$ | $\dfrac{n_{s}}{V_{\mathrm{tot}}}$ | $5\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$ |
| Molality $b$ | $\dfrac{n_{s}}{m_{w}}$ | $5\,\mathrm{mol\,kg^{-1}}$ solvent |

The two most used quantities are molarity and molality, and they differ in the denominator.

- In **molarity** one divides by the volume *of the whole solution*, in litres. For example, $0.10\,\mathrm{mol}$ of solute in $0.50\,\mathrm L$ of solution give $c=\dfrac{0.10}{0.50}=0.20\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$.
- In **molality** one divides by the mass *of the solvent alone*, in kilograms. The same amount of solute dissolved in $0.50\,\mathrm{kg}$ of water gives $b=\dfrac{0.10}{0.50}=0.20\,\mathrm{mol\,kg^{-1}}$.

The mass-volume percentage is a ratio between grams and millilitres, so the units are always stated.

The volume of a liquid changes with temperature, so the molarity also changes slightly when the solution warms up or cools down. The mass does not change, so the molality stays constant; this is why it is used in the laws concerning boiling and freezing temperatures. To go from the mass percentage to the molarity two more data are needed: the density of the solution, which converts mass into volume, and the molar mass of the solute, which converts grams into moles.
:::

:::osservazione{titolo="Identifying the denominator"}
A concentration calculation is carried out in two steps. First one establishes what the amount of solute is referred to: the whole solution, the solvent alone or the total moles. Then the units are converted, that is, grams into moles and millilitres into litres. A percentage is complete only if its type is known (by mass, mass-volume or by volume). For example, a 10% by mass solution contains $10\,\mathrm g$ of solute in every $100\,\mathrm g$ of solution. One litre of this solution contains $100\,\mathrm g$ of solute only if it has a mass of exactly one kilogram, that is, if the density is $1\,\mathrm{kg\,L^{-1}}$; in general the density is different.

![Molarity using final volume and molality using water mass alone](/figure/chimica-semestre-filtro/soluzioni-proprieta-colligative/denominatori-en.svg)
:::

:::definizione{#frazione-molare titolo="Mole fraction"}
Component $j$'s **mole fraction** is its amount divided by the total amount in the mixture:
$$
x_j=\dfrac{n_j}{\sum_k n_k},\qquad \sum_jx_j=1.
$$
It is a number without units, between 0 and 1, and the sum of the mole fractions of all the components is 1. For example, a solution with $1\,\mathrm{mol}$ of ethanol and $5\,\mathrm{mol}$ of water contains 6 moles in all, so the mole fraction of ethanol is $\dfrac16$ and that of water is $\dfrac56$. The mole fraction is used to calculate the partial pressures of gases and the vapour pressure of solutions.
:::

## Dilutions, equivalents and real solutions

:::osservazione{#diluizioni titolo="Solute balance in dilution"}
Diluting a solution means adding solvent. The volume increases and the concentration decreases, while the number of moles of solute stays the same. The moles are the product of concentration and volume, so this product is the same before and after dilution. Writing $c_1$ and $V_1$ for the concentration and volume of the starting solution taken, and $c_2$ and $V_2$ for those of the final solution, one has
$$
c_1V_1=c_2V_2.
$$
For example, $100\,\mathrm{mL}$ of a $0.20\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$ solution are to be prepared starting from a $1.0\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$ solution. The volume to take is $V_1=\dfrac{c_2V_2}{c_1}=\dfrac{0.20\cdot100}{1.0}=20\,\mathrm{mL}$. One takes $20\,\mathrm{mL}$ of the concentrated solution and adds water until the total reaches $100\,\mathrm{mL}$. The final volume is measured, and not the water added, because when two liquids are mixed the total volume can be slightly different from the sum of the two volumes.

When two solutions of the same solute are mixed, the total moles are the sum of the moles contained in the two solutions. The final concentration is obtained by dividing the total moles by the final volume. In exercises the final volume is usually assumed equal to the sum of the two volumes.
:::

:::osservazione{titolo="Solute balance during mixing"}
For example, $100\,\mathrm{mL}$ of a $1.0\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$ solution are mixed with $300\,\mathrm{mL}$ of a $0.20\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$ solution of the same solute. The moles are $0.100\cdot1.0+0.300\cdot0.20=0.16\,\mathrm{mol}$ and the final volume is $0.400\,\mathrm L$, so the final concentration is $\dfrac{0.16}{0.400}=0.40\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$. The result differs from the average of the two concentrations, which would be $0.60\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$, because the two volumes are not equal. This calculation holds if all the solute stays in solution; if part of it reacts or precipitates, the dissolved moles decrease and have to be recalculated.

![Dilution retaining four particles before and after: volume increases, solute amount does not](/figure/chimica-semestre-filtro/soluzioni-proprieta-colligative/diluizione-en.svg)
:::

:::definizione{#equivalenti titolo="Chemical equivalents and ionic charge"}
The **equivalent** is a unit that counts the charges, protons or electrons that a substance can exchange, instead of its particles. One equivalent is the amount of substance that exchanges one mole of charges (for an ion), one mole of $\mathrm{H^+}$ (for an acid or a base) or one mole of electrons (in a redox reaction). If each mole of substance exchanges $z$ moles of these units, the number of equivalents is $n_{\mathrm{eq}}=z\,n$ and the concentration in equivalents per litre is $c_{\mathrm{eq}}=z\,c$.

For an ion, $z$ is the value of the charge, without sign. The ion $\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}$ has charge 2, so $1\,\mathrm{mmol}$ of calcium corresponds to $2\,\mathrm{mEq}$, and a concentration of $2.5\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}$ corresponds to $5.0\,\mathrm{mEq\,L^{-1}}$. For $\mathrm{Na^+}$, which has charge 1, millimoles and milliequivalents coincide. In blood tests ions are often expressed in $\mathrm{mEq\,L^{-1}}$. In a solution the total equivalents of the cations equal those of the anions, because the solution is electrically neutral.

For an acid, $z$ is the number of $\mathrm{H^+}$ given up in the reaction considered. Sulfuric acid $\mathrm{H_2SO_4}$ giving up both protons has $z=2$. For an acid with several protons the value depends on how many it gives up in that reaction, so the reaction has to be specified. In a redox reaction, $z$ is the number of electrons exchanged by each particle. When the iron(III) ion becomes iron(II) it gains a single electron, so $z=1$, even though the charge of the ion is 3.
:::

:::definizione{#attivita titolo="Activity and activity coefficient"}
In a very dilute solution the solute particles are far apart and behave independently. In a more concentrated solution the particles attract and repel each other, and the solution behaves as if the concentration were different from the real one. The **activity** $a_j$ of a substance is the concentration corrected to take these interactions into account. It is calculated with the formula below.
$$
a_j=\gamma_j\dfrac{c_j}{c^\circ},\qquad c^\circ=1\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}.
$$
In this formula $c_j$ is the concentration of the substance, $c^\circ=1\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$ is a reference concentration used to remove the units, and $\gamma_j$ is the **activity coefficient**. The activity and the coefficient are numbers without units. In a very dilute solution $\gamma_j$ is about 1 and the activity coincides with the numerical value of the concentration. For example, if $c_j=0.050\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$ and $\gamma_j=0.80$, then $a_j=0.80\cdot0.050=0.040$.

The activity coefficient depends on the temperature and on all the substances present in the solution. The inside of a cell contains many ions and many macromolecules, so it is far from the conditions of a dilute solution, and there activities differ from concentrations. In calculations with dilute solutions concentrations are normally used, that is, $\gamma_j=1$ is assumed.

Activity appears in the laws of equilibrium. The **chemical potential** $\mu_j$ of a substance, that is, its free energy per mole in that solution, is $\mu_j=\mu_j^\circ+RT\ln a_j$, where $\mu_j^\circ$ is the value in the reference state, $R$ the gas constant and $T$ the absolute temperature. Two solutions with the same concentration of a substance can have different activities, if the other substances present are different.
:::

## Gas mixtures and dissolved gases

:::definizione{#dalton titolo="Partial pressures and Dalton's law"}
In a mixture of gases each gas contributes to the total pressure. The **partial pressure** $p_j$ of a gas is the pressure that gas would exert if it occupied the whole container alone, at the same temperature. **Dalton's law** states that the total pressure is the sum of the partial pressures, and that the partial pressure of a gas is obtained by multiplying the total pressure by the mole fraction $x_j$ of the gas.
$$
p_{\mathrm{tot}}=\sum_jp_j,\qquad p_j=x_jp_{\mathrm{tot}}.
$$
For example, dry air contains about $78\%$ nitrogen, $21\%$ oxygen and small amounts of argon and carbon dioxide. These percentages are mole fractions. At a pressure of $1\,\mathrm{atm}$ the partial pressure of oxygen is therefore $0.21\cdot1\,\mathrm{atm}=0.21\,\mathrm{atm}$ and that of nitrogen is $0.78\,\mathrm{atm}$.

Moist air also contains water vapour, which has its own partial pressure $p_{\mathrm{H_2O}}$. The other gases then share the remaining pressure, $p_{\mathrm{tot}}-p_{\mathrm{H_2O}}$. If $x_{\mathrm{O_2,dry}}$ is the fraction of oxygen in dry air, the partial pressure of oxygen is $p_{\mathrm{O_2}}=x_{\mathrm{O_2,dry}}(p_{\mathrm{tot}}-p_{\mathrm{H_2O}})$. In the lungs the air is saturated with water vapour, with $p_{\mathrm{H_2O}}\simeq0.062\,\mathrm{atm}$ at $37\,^\circ\mathrm C$, so the partial pressure of inhaled oxygen is $0.21\cdot(1-0.062)\simeq0.20\,\mathrm{atm}$. Exhaled air contains less oxygen, more carbon dioxide and more water vapour than inhaled air.
:::

:::definizione{#henry titolo="Gas solubility and Henry's law"}
A gas in contact with a liquid partly dissolves in the liquid. **Henry's law** states that, at constant temperature, the concentration of the dissolved gas is proportional to the partial pressure of the gas above the liquid. The law holds for sparingly soluble gases that do not react with the solvent, when gas and liquid have reached equilibrium.
$$
c=k_Hp.
$$
In this formula $c$ is the concentration of the dissolved gas, $p$ its partial pressure above the liquid and $k_H$ the **Henry constant**, which depends on the gas, the solvent and the temperature. With $c$ in $\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$ and $p$ in atmospheres, $k_H$ is measured in $\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}\,atm^{-1}}$. Some texts write the law in the inverse form, with the pressure on the left; the units of the constant show which form is used.

If the partial pressure doubles, the concentration of the dissolved gas also doubles. This is why a fizzy drink, bottled under $\mathrm{CO_2}$ pressure, loses its gas when the bottle is opened and the pressure falls. In blood only a small part of the oxygen is dissolved in the plasma, and Henry's law applies to this part; most of it is bound to haemoglobin. Carbon dioxide reacts with water and forms bicarbonate, so its total amount in blood also depends on these equilibria.
:::

:::osservazione{titolo="Henry law conventions"}
In the inverse form the law is written $p=k'_Hc$, and the new constant is the reciprocal of the previous one, $k'_H=\dfrac{1}{k_H}$. In the form $c=k_Hp$ a large constant indicates a very soluble gas; in the inverse form a large constant indicates a sparingly soluble gas.

The law contains the partial pressure of the gas considered, and not the total pressure. If another gas is added to a closed container, the total pressure increases and the partial pressure of the first gas stays the same, so its concentration in the liquid does not change.

:::

## Solution vapour pressure

:::definizione{#raoult titolo="Raoult's law"}
**Colligative properties** are the properties of a solution that depend only on the number of dissolved solute particles, and not on their nature. There are four: the lowering of the vapour pressure, the elevation of the boiling temperature, the depression of the freezing temperature and the osmotic pressure. The laws that describe them hold for dilute solutions.

The first property is described by **Raoult's law**. The vapour pressure of a liquid in a solution equals the vapour pressure of the pure liquid multiplied by the mole fraction of the liquid in the solution.
$$
p_j=x_jp_j^\ast.
$$
In this formula $p_j$ is the vapour pressure of component $j$ above the solution, $x_j$ its mole fraction in the liquid and $p_j^\ast$ the vapour pressure of the same pure component, at the same temperature. The most common case is a solvent (subscript $w$) in which a non-volatile solute (subscript $s$) is dissolved, that is, a solute that does not evaporate, such as a salt or a sugar. The vapour then consists of the solvent alone and the vapour pressure of the solution is $p=x_{w}p^\ast$. The lowering with respect to the pure solvent is
$$
\Delta p=p^\ast-p=x_{s}p^\ast.
$$
The lowering is therefore proportional to the mole fraction of the solute. For example, if 2% of the particles are solute, $x_w=0.98$ and the vapour pressure is 98% of that of the pure solvent, that is, $p=0.98\,p^\ast$. If the solute is also volatile, as in a mixture of water and ethanol, each component follows Raoult's law and the total pressure is the sum of the two partial pressures. The vapour is richer in the more volatile component than the liquid is.
:::

:::osservazione{titolo="Liquid–vapour equilibrium"}
The vapour pressure falls because mixing with the solute makes the solvent more stable in the liquid; in thermodynamic terms, the solute lowers the chemical potential of the solvent. The solvent therefore has less tendency to pass into the vapour state, and the equilibrium between liquid and vapour is established at a lower pressure. The effect depends on the mole fraction of the solute throughout the liquid, and not only on the particles at the surface. In concentrated solutions the law is corrected by using the activity of the solvent in place of the mole fraction.

:::

## Boiling and freezing

:::definizione{#ebullioscopia-crioscopia titolo="Boiling-point elevation and freezing-point depression"}
A solution boils at a higher temperature and freezes at a lower temperature than the pure solvent. The first effect is called **boiling-point elevation** and the second **freezing-point depression**. Both are proportional to the molality of the solute. For a non-volatile solute that does not dissociate into ions the formulas below hold.
$$
\Delta T_b=K_b b,\qquad \Delta T_f=K_f b.
$$
In these formulas $\Delta T_b$ is the increase in the boiling temperature, $\Delta T_f$ the decrease in the freezing temperature and $b$ the molality of the solute. The two changes are written as positive numbers. $K_b$ is the ebullioscopic constant and $K_f$ the cryoscopic constant: they depend only on the solvent and not on the solute.

For water $K_b=0.512\,\mathrm{K\,kg\,mol^{-1}}$ and $K_f=1.86\,\mathrm{K\,kg\,mol^{-1}}$. For example, a $0.10\,\mathrm{mol\,kg^{-1}}$ glucose solution has $\Delta T_b=0.512\cdot0.10=0.0512\,\mathrm K$ and $\Delta T_f=1.86\cdot0.10=0.186\,\mathrm K$. At a pressure of $1\,\mathrm{atm}$ it therefore boils at about $100.05\,^\circ\mathrm C$ and freezes at about $-0.19\,^\circ\mathrm C$. A temperature difference has the same value in kelvin and in degrees Celsius.

Both effects derive from the lowering of the vapour pressure. A liquid boils when its vapour pressure reaches the external pressure. The solution has a lower vapour pressure than the pure solvent, so it has to be heated more to reach that value. For freezing it is assumed that the solid that forms is pure solvent, as happens for the ice that separates from a dilute aqueous solution. This is why salt is spread on icy roads and antifreeze is added to the water in radiators. At equal molality, different solutes produce the same change, because what counts is the number of particles and not their kind.
:::

:::definizione{#vant-hoff titolo="The van’t Hoff factor"}
Colligative properties depend on the number of particles in solution. An electrolyte separates into ions, so it produces more particles than the units of solute dissolved. The **van't Hoff factor** $i$ is the number of particles produced by each unit of solute. If dissociation is complete, $i=1$ for glucose, which does not dissociate, $i=2$ for $\mathrm{NaCl}$ and $i=3$ for $\mathrm{CaCl_2}$. In the formulas of the colligative properties the molality is multiplied by $i$.
$$
\Delta T_b=iK_b b,\qquad \Delta T_f=iK_f b.
$$
For example, a $0.10\,\mathrm{mol\,kg^{-1}}$ solution of $\mathrm{NaCl}$ in water has $\Delta T_f=2\cdot1.86\cdot0.10=0.372\,\mathrm K$, twice that of a glucose solution of the same molality.

For a weak electrolyte dissociation is partial. If each unit can give $\nu$ ions and the dissociated fraction is $\alpha$ (degree of dissociation, between 0 and 1), the factor is $i=1+\alpha(\nu-1)$. With $\alpha=1$ one recovers $i=\nu$ and with $\alpha=0$ one recovers $i=1$. In real salt solutions the measured value of $i$ is slightly smaller than the theoretical one, because ions of opposite charge partly stay close together. If the calculation already uses the total concentration of particles, the factor $i$ is already included and is not multiplied again.
:::

## Diffusion, osmosis and osmotic concentration

:::definizione{#diffusione titolo="Diffusion and Fick's first law"}
**Diffusion** is the spontaneous movement of solute particles from regions where the concentration is higher towards regions where it is lower. It is due to the random motion of the particles and continues until the concentration is the same everywhere. **Fick's first law** states that the flux of solute is proportional to how rapidly the concentration varies in space. Along a direction $x$ it is written
$$
J=-D\dfrac{\mathrm dc}{\mathrm dx}.
$$
In this formula $J$ is the **flux**, that is, the amount of solute crossing a unit area in one second; $\dfrac{\mathrm dc}{\mathrm dx}$ is the **concentration gradient**, that is, how much the concentration changes for each metre travelled; $D$ is the **diffusion coefficient**, which depends on the solute, the solvent and the temperature. The minus sign shows that the solute moves towards the region where the concentration is lower. With $c$ in $\mathrm{mol\,m^{-3}}$ and $x$ in metres, $J$ is measured in $\mathrm{mol\,m^{-2}\,s^{-1}}$ and $D$ in $\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}$. The amount crossing a surface of area $A$ in one second is $JA$. Transport is therefore greater if the concentration difference is large, if the distance is small and if the area is large, as happens between the pulmonary alveoli and the blood.

A membrane lets some solutes through and holds others back; in this case it is said to be *selectively permeable*. For ions, which carry a charge, the movement also depends on the electric potential difference between the two sides of the membrane, as well as on the concentration difference.
:::

:::definizione{#osmosi titolo="Osmosis and osmotic pressure"}
**Osmosis** is the passage of solvent through a membrane that lets the solvent through and holds back the solute (*semipermeable* membrane). If the membrane separates two solutions of different concentration, water passes from the more dilute solution to the more concentrated one. The passage tends to make the two concentrations equal. For example, if a membrane separates pure water from a sugar solution, water enters the solution and the level of the liquid on that side rises.

The **osmotic pressure** $\pi$ of a solution is the pressure that has to be applied to the solution to prevent pure water from entering through the membrane. For a dilute solution it is calculated with the formula below.
$$
\pi=icRT=c_{\mathrm{osm}}RT.
$$
In this formula $c$ is the molarity of the solute, $i$ the van't Hoff factor, $R$ the gas constant and $T$ the absolute temperature. The product $ic$ is the total concentration of particles, written $c_{\mathrm{osm}}$. With $c$ in $\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$ and $R=0.082057\,\mathrm{L\,atm\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}}$ the pressure comes out in atmospheres. For example, a $0.10\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$ glucose solution at $298\,\mathrm K$ has $\pi=0.10\cdot0.082057\cdot298\simeq2.4\,\mathrm{atm}$. If the membrane separates two solutions, what counts is the difference between the two particle concentrations, and the difference in osmotic pressure is $\Delta\pi=RT\,\Delta c_{\mathrm{osm}}$.
:::

:::osservazione{titolo="Net flow and osmotic pressure"}
Osmotic pressure is a property of the solution: it gives the pressure that would be needed to stop osmosis, and a solution in a glass does not exert this pressure on the walls. The net passage of water stops when the pressure difference between the two sides of the membrane equals the difference in osmotic pressure. At equilibrium water molecules keep crossing the membrane in both directions, in equal numbers. If a pressure greater than the osmotic pressure is applied to the concentrated solution, water passes in the opposite direction, from the concentrated solution to the dilute one. This process is called **reverse osmosis** and is used to make sea water drinkable.

:::

:::definizione{#osmolarita titolo="Osmolarity and osmolality"}
The **osmolarity** is the total concentration of all the solute particles, expressed in moles of particles per litre of solution. One mole of particles is called an *osmole* (Osm). The **osmolality** is the same quantity referred to one kilogram of solvent. They are calculated by adding, for each solute $j$, the concentration multiplied by the van't Hoff factor $i_j$.
$$
c_{\mathrm{osm}}=\sum_j i_jc_j,\qquad b_{\mathrm{osm}}=\sum_j i_jb_j.
$$
Osmolarity is measured in $\mathrm{Osm\,L^{-1}}$ and osmolality in $\mathrm{Osm\,kg^{-1}}$. For example, a $0.10\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$ solution of $\mathrm{NaCl}$, with $i=2$, has an osmolarity of $0.20\,\mathrm{Osm\,L^{-1}}$. A solution containing $0.10\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$ of $\mathrm{NaCl}$ and $0.05\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$ of glucose has an osmolarity of $0.20+0.05=0.25\,\mathrm{Osm\,L^{-1}}$. Blood plasma has an osmolarity of about $0.29\,\mathrm{Osm\,L^{-1}}$. In dilute aqueous solutions one litre of solution contains about one kilogram of water, so osmolarity and osmolality have almost equal values.
:::

## Tonicity and biological fluids

:::definizione{#tonicita titolo="Isotonic, hypotonic and hypertonic solutions"}
**Tonicity** describes the effect of a solution on the volume of a cell immersed in it. It depends on the solutes that do not cross the cell membrane, because only these maintain a concentration difference between inside and outside over time.

- In an **isotonic** solution the concentration of these solutes equals the internal one. Water enters and leaves to the same extent and the volume of the cell stays constant.
- In a **hypotonic** solution the external concentration is lower. Water enters the cell, which swells.
- In a **hypertonic** solution the external concentration is higher. Water leaves the cell, which shrinks.

Two solutions with the same total osmolarity are called **isoosmotic**. A solution that is isoosmotic with the cell is also isotonic only if its solutes do not cross the membrane. Urea, for example, crosses the membrane of many cells. In an isoosmotic urea solution urea enters the cell, the internal concentration of particles increases and water follows it. The cell swells, so the solution behaves as hypotonic.

A red blood cell placed in a strongly hypotonic solution swells until it bursts; the bursting is called **haemolysis**. In a hypertonic solution it loses water and shrinks. This is why fluids injected into a vein are isotonic with blood.

**Oedema** is an accumulation of fluid in the spaces between the cells of a tissue. The exchange of fluid between capillaries and tissues depends on several factors: the blood pressure in the capillaries, which pushes fluid outwards; the plasma proteins, which draw it back in by osmosis; the permeability of the capillary wall; the drainage of the lymphatic vessels.
:::

:::osservazione{titolo="Assumptions of the cell model"}
The final volume of a cell can be calculated with a simple model. It is assumed that the amount of solutes inside stays constant, that the solutes do not cross the membrane and that the external solution is much larger than the cell. Water enters or leaves until the internal concentration becomes equal to the external one. For example, a cell with internal concentration $0.30\,\mathrm{Osm\,L^{-1}}$ placed in a $0.15\,\mathrm{Osm\,L^{-1}}$ solution doubles its volume, because the same amount of solutes ends up in twice the volume. In a real cell the result is approximate, because part of the volume is not water and because over time the cell regulates its own solute content.

:::

:::esempio{#fisiologica-glucosata titolo="Saline and glucose solutions"}
Physiological saline is a $0.9\%$ mass-volume solution of $\mathrm{NaCl}$, that is, it contains $0.9\,\mathrm g$ of salt in $100\,\mathrm{mL}$, equal to $9.0\,\mathrm{g\,L^{-1}}$. The molarity is calculated by dividing by the molar mass, $58.5\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}$, and then the osmolarity by multiplying by $i=2$.
$$
c=\dfrac{9.0}{58.5}\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}
\simeq0.154\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}},\qquad
c_{\mathrm{osm,id}}\simeq2c=0.308\,\mathrm{Osm\,L^{-1}}.
$$
The osmolarity obtained, about $0.31\,\mathrm{Osm\,L^{-1}}$, is close to that of plasma. The ions $\mathrm{Na^+}$ and $\mathrm{Cl^-}$ cross the cell membrane very little, so physiological saline is isotonic and does not alter the volume of red blood cells. The measured value is slightly smaller than the calculated one, because the ions interact with each other.

The $5\%$ mass-volume glucose solution contains $50\,\mathrm{g\,L^{-1}}$ of glucose. The molar mass of glucose is $180\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}$ and $i=1$, so the osmolarity is $\dfrac{50}{180}\simeq0.278\,\mathrm{Osm\,L^{-1}}$, also close to that of plasma. At the moment of infusion the solution is isoosmotic. Glucose, however, enters the cells and is consumed, so after a certain time only the water remains and the final effect is that of a hypotonic solution.
:::
