## Passage structure

:::definizione{#struttura-brano titolo="Subject and function of sentences"}
The **subject** is what the passage is about. A sentence's **function** is the contribution it makes to the passage. A sentence can introduce a phenomenon, supply a figure, report an objection or state the conditions under which a conclusion holds. To identify its function, consider how omitting it would change your understanding of the passage.
:::

:::esempio{titolo="An example after a general claim"}
> Neighbourhood libraries can give people access to tools they use infrequently. The Harbour Street branch, for example, lends drills and ladders. In the first quarter, some users borrowed tools after attending a maintenance course.

The first sentence makes a claim about more than one library. The second names a branch and two tools, so it gives an instance of the general claim. The third describes how some of the loans at that branch took place. If asked what the second sentence does, the answer should mention that it **gives an example of a lending service**. The number of users and the value of the maintenance course concern a different question.
:::

:::esempio{titolo="A conclusion at the end"}
> Both routes take twenty minutes under normal conditions. The first crosses a movable bridge and may be delayed when ships pass. The second takes just as long when the bridge is open to shipping. The company therefore chooses the second route for deliveries that have to arrive at a fixed time.

The opening establishes what the routes have in common. The next two sentences introduce the key difference, which is the reliability of the travel time. The final sentence applies the comparison to an operational choice. A summary must include the condition *for deliveries that have to arrive at a fixed time*, because that condition connects the reasoning to the decision.
:::

:::osservazione
A question about the role of a sentence requires reading what comes before and after it. The meaning of the sentence tells you *what it says*. Its relationship to the surrounding sentences explains *why it appears at that point*. In a passage with several paragraphs, a short mental label for each paragraph, such as *problem*, *example* or *limitation*, makes it quicker to find the part you need.
:::

## Explicit information

:::definizione{#informazioni-esplicite titolo="Explicit information and paraphrase"}
Information is **explicit** when the passage states it. The answer can use different wording as long as it preserves the subject, action, time and conditions. Restating information in this way is called **paraphrasing**. To compare an answer with the passage, check who is said to have done what and whether the answer preserves every condition in the question.
:::

:::esempio{titolo="The days and times refer to different activities"}
> The laboratory takes deliveries of supplies from Monday to Thursday. On Friday morning, it opens to students who have booked a visit. On the other days, students can use the room next to the laboratory.

The second sentence answers the question *When can a student with a booking enter the laboratory?* The answer *on Friday morning* preserves both the day and the time of day. The first sentence concerns deliveries. The third gives students access to a different space. All the days appear in the passage, so the answer depends on which ones are linked to *entering the laboratory*.
:::

:::esempio{titolo="The condition applies to refunds"}
> An exhibition ticket can be changed to another date free of charge up to the day before the visit. A refund is available in exactly one situation, when the organisers cancel the opening. In either case, the request goes through the office that issued the ticket.

To establish when a visitor is entitled to a refund, find the word *refund* and read the condition attached to it. Visitors are entitled to a refund **if the organisers cancel the opening**. Changing the date free of charge has a separate time condition. The last sentence specifies where to make the request for both procedures.
:::

:::osservazione
Each part of a two-part answer needs support. Suppose a notice says that a gym opens on Tuesdays and that it runs classes for adults. The statement *the adult classes take place on Tuesdays* combines two pieces of information. The answer is supported only when the passage also makes that connection. Words that an answer shares with the passage can help you locate the relevant part.
:::

## Cohesion and meaning in context

:::definizione{#coesione-e-lessico titolo="What an expression refers to"}
A **referent** is the person, object, group or event that a word in the passage points to. Expressions such as *the latter*, *that intervention* and *this* refer back to things mentioned earlier. A pronoun can refer to a whole situation. Replacing the expression with a possible referent lets you check the grammar and meaning of the sentence.
:::

:::esempio{titolo="A pronoun refers to an event"}
> The museum sent the reproductions to the schools. The originals stayed in storage while awaiting restoration. This allowed the classes to prepare for their visit before the museum reopened.

The pronoun *this* can refer to a preceding event. Replacing it with *sending the reproductions to the schools* links the materials the schools received to the classes' preparation. Replacing it with *keeping the originals in storage* leaves unexplained how waiting for the restoration provided materials to the schools. The intended referent is **sending the reproductions**.
:::

:::definizione{titolo="Meaning in context"}
A word's **meaning in context** is the sense it has in the part of the passage where it appears. Look for a paraphrase that preserves its relationship to the surrounding words. In *a programme absorbs half of the budget*, *absorbs* indicates how much of the available funding the programme uses. The word *budget* limits the possible meanings.
:::

:::esempio{titolo="A word with a technical meaning"}
> To cushion pressure fluctuations, the technician fitted a chamber between the pump and the outlet pipe. The chamber contains a volume of air that is compressed when the pressure fluctuates.

*Chamber* can refer to a room, a political body or an enclosed part of a device. The phrase *between the pump and the outlet pipe* identifies a component. The next sentence specifies what it contains. The correct paraphrase is therefore **a space inside the device that contains air**.
:::

:::osservazione
When a pronoun is grammatically compatible with two possible referents, its function in the sentence helps settle the question. If a passage discusses a regulation and a form, *fill it in* normally refers to the form, while *apply it* can refer to the regulation. The verb indicates what must be possible with the candidate referent.
:::

## Main idea and purpose

:::definizione{#idea-e-scopo titolo="Main idea and purpose of a passage"}
The **main idea** is what the passage as a whole says about its subject. Its **purpose** is what the writer does in presenting that idea, such as describing a procedure, comparing two solutions or explaining a result. The distinction matters when two alternatives concern the same subject and only one captures what the passage actually does.
:::

:::esempio{titolo="An observed result and its scope"}
> A shop opened half an hour earlier for one month. On market days the extra half-hour attracted customers, while on other days the staff had very few visitors. The management will therefore keep the earlier opening time on market days only.

The subject is the new opening time. The **main idea** includes both the different outcomes on different days and the decision to adopt the change on some days. The **purpose** is to explain why the earlier opening time will remain in place for part of the week. A summary centred on staff reports one detail. A summary about earlier opening in all shops extends the finding to businesses outside the passage.
:::

:::esempio{titolo="A comparison without a general recommendation"}
> Aluminium trays cool quickly, while ceramic trays retain heat for longer. In a canteen, quick cooling makes washing easier at the end of the shift. For deliveries, keeping the food at a steady temperature during the journey matters more.

The passage describes a difference and links it to two situations. A statement that recommends ceramic trays everywhere adds a general recommendation. A statement that simply lists the materials loses the link to their uses. The full main idea is that **the suitability of either material depends on how it will be used**.
:::

:::osservazione
A proposed summary can be checked against each part of a passage. If it covers only the final example, it is too narrow. If it applies the result to new people or situations, it is too broad. When the passage presents the views of two people, a summary may report their disagreement. Attributing one view to the writer requires a cue in the passage that supports that attribution.
:::

## Relationships between sentences and paragraphs

:::definizione{#relazioni-logiche titolo="Links in a passage"}
A connective makes a **relationship** between two statements explicit. *Because* often introduces a cause or a justification. *Therefore* introduces a consequence. *However* marks a limitation or an outcome that differs from what one would expect. *For example* narrows attention to a case. When there is no connective, the relationship can be identified by considering what the second sentence adds to the first.
:::

:::esempio{titolo="A qualification limits the estimate"}
> The estimated maintenance cost is 8,000 euros. The estimate covers the parts that are routinely replaced. Any work identified by the electrical inspection remains to be costed.

The last sentence identifies an expense that is still to be determined. The relationship between the first and last sentences is therefore a **qualification of the estimate's scope**. The passage supports a figure of 8,000 euros for the work already estimated. A figure for the full cost would also require pricing the work identified after the inspection.
:::

:::esempio{titolo="Two events in sequence"}
> In March, the association moved its archives to a larger room. In May, it began digitising the historic records with funding from the local council.

The move preceded the digitisation. The second sentence explicitly names the funding as what made digitisation possible. The move serves a different purpose in the passage, which is to describe what happened in March. An answer linking the work to the extra space would require a cause that the passage leaves unspecified.
:::

:::osservazione
Check the direction of the relationship. In *requests increased, so the service extended its opening hours*, the increase in requests comes first and is given as the reason for the decision. The statement *the longer opening hours led to the increase* reverses cause and effect. A similar check applies to conditions. If a pass is valid **only with** identification, identification is necessary for its validity. The passage may also specify further requirements.
:::

:::osservazione
*Also* adds a case. *Only* limits the cases. *So far* confines an observation to the period that has already passed. These short expressions change the scope of an entire sentence. To choose between two close paraphrases, check which one preserves the time limit, group or condition in the original.
:::

## Inferences supported by the passage

:::definizione{#inferenze titolo="Inference and scope of a conclusion"}
An **inference** is information obtained by linking elements of the passage. The support may be decisive, when those elements make the answer follow, or limited, when the passage describes a possibility or puts forward a hypothesis. A question about what the passage *suggests* can call for a qualified conclusion. A question about what *necessarily follows* requires a conclusion that holds under every reading consistent with the information given.
:::

:::esempio{titolo="Linking two sentences"}
> Only forms received by the last day of the month are considered at the first meeting. A grant application received on the first day of the following month concerns a project that the same committee has funded before.

The rule in the first sentence reserves consideration at the first meeting for forms that arrived on time. The date in the second sentence falls after the deadline. The application **can be considered only at a different time from the first meeting**, if the procedure allows it to be considered at all. The previous grant is consistent with the rule and leaves the outcome of the application open.
:::

:::esempio{titolo="A possible explanation remains a hypothesis"}
> In a trial with several grape varieties, the vines grown next to the wall began to bud several days before the others. The person in charge suggests reduced exposure to wind as the explanation, although that exposure was not measured.

The person in charge puts forward a possible cause. The passage supports the observation about budding and reports the explanation offered. Concluding that wind **was** the cause would require further evidence. The answer must therefore keep the original qualification, for example *reduced exposure to wind could explain the earlier budding*.
:::

:::attenzione
From *some subscribers also use the service on Sundays*, it follows that at least one subscriber uses it on Sundays. The passage leaves open both the total number of Sunday users and whether the remaining subscribers use the service on that day. Adding *only some*, *most* or *all* changes what was asserted.
:::

:::osservazione
To test a strong inference, look for a different situation in which the facts in the passage still hold. If an article reports that course enrolment rose and tuition fees stayed the same, fee income rises if the amount actually paid per student stays the same. Scholarships or fee waivers could change that result. An unconditional conclusion about income would therefore take an extra assumption.
:::

## Figures and percentages in a passage

:::definizione{#dati-nel-testo titolo="Numerator, base and unit of comparison"}
A percentage compares a part with its **base**. In *30% of complete applications*, the part described corresponds to 30 out of every 100 complete applications. The words after the percentage identify the reference group. When the passage compares two periods, the base may change. The difference between two percentages is expressed in **percentage points**.
:::

:::esempio{titolo="Equal shares and different quantities"}
> In the first session, 60 people responded, and in the second, 90 people responded. In both sessions, 20% of the respondents said they preferred home delivery.

| Session | Respondents | Share in favour |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| First | 60 | 20% |
| Second | 90 | 20% |

The percentage applies to the respondents in each session. Since $20\%$ is equal to $\dfrac{1}{5}$, the number in favour is $60\cdot\dfrac{1}{5}=12$ in the first session and $90\cdot\dfrac{1}{5}=18$ in the second. The passage reports **the same share with two different quantities**. The total number of respondents is what allows those quantities to be distinguished.
:::

:::esempio{titolo="Percentage points and relative increase"}
> The subscription renewal rate went from 40% to 50% after the service changed. In the report, the manager asks for the difference between the two rates to be described without attributing it to the change in service.

The difference is $50\%-40\%=10$ **percentage points**. If the question asks for the relative increase in the rate, the base is the initial rate, so the calculation is $\dfrac{50-40}{40}\cdot 100\%=25\%$. The results answer different questions. The sequence in time leaves the cause of the change open.
:::

:::osservazione
A table and its accompanying text can give different levels of detail about a figure. A total relates to the whole group, while a table may break down only selected categories. If the passage describes *survey respondents*, the interpretation remains limited to that group even when the question refers to customers in general. A correct calculation for the wrong group answers a different question.
:::

## Choosing an answer under time pressure

:::definizione{#metodo-a-tempo titolo="Checking the answer against the passage"}
An answer is supported when the passage contains the **specific evidence** needed to justify it. Under time pressure, one approach is to start with the question, find the relevant sentence and formulate a word or short phrase before looking at the options. For a question about the passage as a whole, the evidence may come from two paragraphs together. For a local question, one sentence and its context are often enough.
:::

:::esempio{titolo="Four ways to alter the meaning"}
> In the first six weeks, the laboratory lent sensors to three classes at a school. The teachers reported that the loan made outdoor practical work easier. The school will decide in June whether to expand the scheme.

An accurate answer reports that **the teachers involved found outdoor practical work easier**. *All the teachers at the school* changes the group. *It improved grades* changes the outcome observed. *The laboratory decided to expand the scheme* turns a future decision into a completed one and changes who makes it. *The classes used the sensors for six consecutive weeks* extends the period of use beyond what the passage supplies.
:::

:::esempio{titolo="Checking a long answer in parts"}
> An appointment-based repair service was introduced at two branches. At both branches, weekday waiting times fell. On Saturdays the service continues to see customers in order of arrival.

The statement *appointments reduced weekday waiting times and replaced the first-come, first-served system on Saturdays* has a first part that is consistent with the account and a second part that is inconsistent. The third sentence settles the difference. In a test, finding that second part is enough to rule out the whole option. The supported answer limits the shorter waiting times to **weekdays**.
:::

:::osservazione
The standard test allows 75 minutes for 50 questions. That is an average of $90$ seconds per question across the test as a whole. The time a particular question takes can vary with the length of the passage and the task. For a detail, it may be enough to find the exact sentence. A comparison between several sentences may call for a second reading. Before advancing to the next screen, check the answers still on the current one. When reviewing a mock test, classifying errors by the type of reading involved helps identify what to practise.
:::
