Earth science studies the planet: what it is made of, how it moves in space and which processes change its interior and its surface. In TOLC-S the questions ask you to recognize a process from a description, to put past events in order and to do short calculations with balances and percentages. Each section presents a topic, the terms it needs and a concrete case.

## Earth in space

:::definizione{#terra-spazio titolo="Earth's motions and astronomical scales"}
A **star** is a celestial body that produces energy in its interior through nuclear reactions and emits it as light. The Sun is a star. A **planet** is a body that orbits a star and produces no light of its own: it is visible because it reflects the light of the star. Earth is one of the eight planets that orbit the Sun, held by the force of gravity. The Sun, the planets and the minor bodies form the **Solar System**. A **galaxy** is a collection of billions of stars; the Solar System lies in the galaxy called the Milky Way.

Earth has two motions.

- **Rotation** is the turning of Earth about its axis, the line through the North Pole and the South Pole. It takes about 24 hours and produces the alternation of day and night, because at any moment only half of the planet faces the Sun. Earth rotates from west to east, so from the surface the Sun is seen moving the opposite way: it rises in the east and sets in the west.
- **Revolution** is the journey of Earth around the Sun. It takes about one year.

Earth's axis is tilted by about $23.4^\circ$ from the perpendicular to the plane of the orbit, and it always points in the same direction while Earth travels along the orbit. The **seasons** depend on this tilt. For half of the year the northern hemisphere is tilted towards the Sun: it receives rays closer to the vertical and its days are longer than its nights, so in that hemisphere it is summer. In the same period the southern hemisphere is tilted the other way and has winter. Six months later the situation is reversed.

A year has four reference dates. At the two **solstices**, around 21 June and 21 December, the difference between the length of day and the length of night is greatest. At the two **equinoxes**, around 21 March and 23 September, day and night last about 12 hours each everywhere on Earth.
:::

:::attenzione
The seasons depend on the tilt of the axis and not on the distance between Earth and the Sun. Earth passes through the point of its orbit closest to the Sun at the beginning of January, when it is winter in the northern hemisphere. Moreover, on the same day the two hemispheres are at the same distance from the Sun and have opposite seasons.
:::

:::osservazione{#distanze titolo="Distances, light, and alignments"}
Astronomical distances are measured with two units.

- The **astronomical unit** is the mean distance between Earth and the Sun, about 150 million kilometers. It is used for distances inside the Solar System.
- The **light-year** is the distance that light travels in a vacuum in one year, about 9500 billion kilometers. It is used for distances between stars. It is a length, even though its name contains the word "year".

Light takes time to arrive, so a distant object is seen as it was when the light left it. A star 4 light-years away is seen as it was 4 years ago.

The **Moon** is Earth's satellite, that is, a body that orbits it; it completes one orbit in about a month. It emits no light of its own and reflects that of the Sun. Half of the Moon is always lit, but from Earth a different part of it is seen according to the position of the Moon along its orbit: these are the **phases of the Moon**. At new moon the Moon lies between Earth and the Sun and turns its dark face towards Earth. At full moon Earth lies between the Sun and the Moon, and the whole lit face is seen from Earth.

An **eclipse** occurs when the Sun, Earth and the Moon are precisely aligned.

- In a **solar eclipse** the Moon lies between the Sun and Earth and covers the Sun. It occurs only at new moon.
- In a **lunar eclipse** Earth lies between the Sun and the Moon, and the Moon enters Earth's shadow. It occurs only at full moon.

The Moon's orbit is tilted by about $5^\circ$ with respect to Earth's. In almost every month, therefore, at new moon and at full moon the Moon passes a little above or below the line joining the Sun and Earth, and no eclipse occurs.

**Tides** are the periodic rise and fall of sea level, caused by the attraction of the Moon and, to a lesser extent, of the Sun. At new moon and at full moon the two attractions act along the same line and add up, so the difference between high and low tide is larger.
:::

:::esempio{titolo="The time sunlight takes to arrive"}
The distance between the Sun and Earth is $d = 150$ million kilometers, that is, $1.5 \cdot 10^{8}\,\mathrm{km}$. Light travels in a vacuum at the speed $c = 3.0 \cdot 10^{5}\,\mathrm{km/s}$. The time is the ratio of the distance to the speed:
$$
t = \frac{d}{c} = \frac{1.5 \cdot 10^{8}\,\mathrm{km}}{3.0 \cdot 10^{5}\,\mathrm{km/s}} = 500\,\mathrm{s} .
$$
Since $500\,\mathrm{s}$ is 8 minutes and 20 seconds, the Sun is seen from Earth as it was about 8 minutes earlier.
:::

## The spheres of the Earth system

:::definizione{#struttura titolo="Composition and mechanical behavior"}
The interior of Earth is divided into concentric layers. The layers are described in two ways, according to the property considered.

The first way considers the material the layer is made of.

- The **crust** is the outer layer, thin and rocky. Under the continents it is on average $35\,\mathrm{km}$ thick (continental crust), under the oceans about $7\,\mathrm{km}$ (oceanic crust). Oceanic crust is thinner and denser than continental crust.
- The **mantle** extends below the crust to a depth of about $2900\,\mathrm{km}$. It is made of rocks rich in silicon, oxygen, iron and magnesium.
- The **core** occupies the center of Earth and is made mostly of iron. The outer core is liquid, the inner core is solid.

The second way considers how the layer behaves when a force acts on it.

- The **lithosphere** is the rigid outer layer. It includes the whole crust and the uppermost part of the mantle, and is on average $100\,\mathrm{km}$ thick. It is divided into large blocks, the plates.
- The **asthenosphere** is the part of the mantle below the lithosphere. It is solid, but hot enough to deform slowly, like a plastic material. The plates of the lithosphere move over it.

The two subdivisions have different boundaries, because the lithosphere contains the crust and also part of the mantle.

The inner core is hotter than the outer core, and yet it is solid. The state of a material depends on temperature and also on pressure: at the center of Earth the pressure is so high that it keeps iron in the solid state.
:::

:::esempio{titolo="The uplift of Scandinavia"}
During the last glaciation Scandinavia was covered by a layer of ice up to $3\,\mathrm{km}$ thick. The weight of the ice made the lithosphere sink into the asthenosphere, as a loaded boat sinks deeper into water. The ice melted about 10,000 years ago, and since then the region has been rising slowly: in some areas the ground still rises by about one centimeter per year. The uplift shows that the asthenosphere, although solid, flows slowly. The floating equilibrium of the lithosphere on the asthenosphere is called **isostasy**.
:::

:::definizione{#acqua titolo="Water reservoirs and fluxes"}
The **hydrosphere** is all the water of Earth: oceans, rivers, lakes, groundwater, ice and vapor. About $97\%$ of the water is salty and lies in the oceans. The frozen part is also called the **cryosphere**.

Water passes continuously from one reservoir to another, along a path called the **water cycle**. It has four stages.

1. **Evaporation**. The heat of the Sun turns the liquid water of seas and lakes into vapor, which rises into the air. Plants also release vapor from their leaves, by **transpiration**.
2. **Condensation**. As it rises, the air cools and the vapor becomes liquid again as tiny droplets, which form clouds.
3. **Precipitation**. The enlarged drops fall as rain, snow or hail.
4. **Runoff and infiltration**. Part of the fallen water flows over the surface towards rivers and the sea. Another part enters the ground and feeds the groundwater.

Groundwater fills the empty spaces between the grains of rocks. Two quantities describe a rock from this point of view.

- **Porosity** is the fraction of the volume of the rock occupied by empty spaces, called pores. A sand with a porosity of $30\%$ contains 300 liters of pores in every cubic meter.
- **Permeability** is the ability of the rock to let water pass through it. It depends on how well the pores are connected to one another.

The two quantities are independent. Clay has high porosity, but its pores are very small and poorly connected, so it is almost impermeable. Gravel is porous and also very permeable.

An **aquifer** is a layer of porous and permeable rock that contains water and lets it flow, for example towards a well. The water contained in an aquifer is called **groundwater**. The amount of water in an aquifer changes over time according to the balance
$$
\text{change} = \text{inputs} - \text{outputs} .
$$
The main input is the rainwater that infiltrates, called recharge. The outputs are springs and withdrawals from wells. A balance of this kind is worked out in the section on georesources.
:::

:::definizione{#atmosfera titolo="Atmosphere and energy balance"}
The **atmosphere** is the envelope of gases that surrounds Earth. Dry air near the ground is $78\%$ nitrogen and $21\%$ oxygen; the remaining $1\%$ includes argon, carbon dioxide and other gases. To these is added water vapor, in an amount that varies from place to place.

The atmosphere is divided into layers. The two lowest are the following.

- The **troposphere** extends from the ground to an altitude of about $12\,\mathrm{km}$. It contains almost all the water vapor, and clouds, winds and rain form in it. Temperature decreases with height.
- The **stratosphere** lies above the troposphere and reaches about $50\,\mathrm{km}$. It contains the **ozone layer**, a gas that absorbs most of the Sun's ultraviolet rays, which are harmful to organisms.

**Atmospheric pressure** is the weight of the column of air above a surface, divided by the area of the surface. At higher altitude the column of air above is shorter, so the pressure decreases: at $5500\,\mathrm{m}$ it is about half of the pressure at sea level.

The Sun heats the regions near the equator more, where the rays arrive almost vertically, and the regions near the poles less. Warm air is less dense and rises; cold air sinks. Differences in pressure arise in this way, and air moves from areas of high pressure towards areas of low pressure. This movement is **wind**.

**Weather** is the state of the atmosphere in a place at a given moment: today's temperature, rain and wind. **Climate** is the set of average conditions of a place, calculated over at least 30 years. A very cold week concerns the weather; to establish whether the climate is changing, the averages of decades are compared.

Two quantities regulate the temperature of the surface.

- **Albedo** is the fraction of sunlight that a surface reflects. Fresh snow has an albedo of about $0.8$, that is, it reflects $80\%$ of the light and absorbs $20\%$. The sea has an albedo of about $0.06$ and absorbs almost all the light. A surface with low albedo heats up more.
- The **greenhouse effect** is the warming caused by some gases of the atmosphere, called greenhouse gases: water vapor, carbon dioxide, methane. Earth's surface, heated by the Sun, emits infrared radiation. Greenhouse gases absorb part of it and re-emit it also downwards, so the surface stays warmer. Without the greenhouse effect the mean temperature of Earth would be about $-18\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ instead of about $+15\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$.

Air holds vapor up to a maximum amount, which is smaller when the air is colder. Air that holds the maximum amount of vapor is said to be **saturated**. If moist air cools until it is saturated, the excess vapor condenses into droplets: this is how fog, dew and clouds form.

The atmosphere, water, rocks and organisms also exchange matter. In photosynthesis, for example, plants take carbon dioxide from the air and use its carbon to build their own substances.
:::

:::esempio{titolo="Energy absorbed by two surfaces"}
Two equal surfaces each receive $500\,\mathrm{J}$ of sunlight. The first is covered with fresh snow, with albedo $0.8$; the second is asphalt, with albedo about $0.1$.

The snow reflects $0.8 \cdot 500\,\mathrm{J} = 400\,\mathrm{J}$ and absorbs the remaining $100\,\mathrm{J}$.

The asphalt reflects $0.1 \cdot 500\,\mathrm{J} = 50\,\mathrm{J}$ and absorbs the remaining $450\,\mathrm{J}$.

For the same light received the asphalt absorbs 4.5 times as much energy, so it heats up much more.
:::

## Plates and mountain building

:::definizione{#placche titolo="Three types of boundary"}
The lithosphere is divided into about a dozen major **plates** and many minor ones, which move relative to one another by a few centimeters per year. A single plate carries both continents and ocean floor: the African plate, for example, includes Africa and part of the floor of the Atlantic Ocean. The edge of a plate often lies in the middle of an ocean, far from the coasts.

The border between two plates is called a **boundary**. There are three types, according to the motion of one plate relative to the other.

- **Divergent boundary**. The plates move apart. Hot material from the mantle rises into the space that opens and partly melts, because the pressure decreases as it rises. The melt solidifies and forms new oceanic crust. Divergent boundaries run along **mid-ocean ridges**, underwater mountain chains. The sea floor is older the farther it is from the ridge. An example is the Mid-Atlantic Ridge.
- **Convergent boundary**. The plates move towards each other. If one of the two carries oceanic crust, which is denser, it sinks into the mantle beneath the other plate. The process is called **subduction**. An **ocean trench** forms along the line of descent, and a chain of volcanoes lines up on the plate that stays above. An example is the Andes. If both plates carry continents, neither sinks, because continental crust has low density: the crust shortens, thickens and forms a mountain chain. An example is the Himalaya, born from the collision between India and Asia.
- **Transform boundary**. The plates slide past each other horizontally. Along the boundary no new crust forms and none is consumed. An example is the San Andreas Fault, in California.
:::

:::esempio{titolo="Opening rate of the Atlantic"}
South America and Africa began to separate about 130 million years ago, and today they are about $5000\,\mathrm{km}$ apart. The mean rate of separation is the ratio of the distance to the time. The distance is expressed in centimeters: $5000\,\mathrm{km} = 5 \cdot 10^{8}\,\mathrm{cm}$.
$$
v = \frac{5 \cdot 10^{8}\,\mathrm{cm}}{1.3 \cdot 10^{8}\,\text{years}} \approx 3.8\,\frac{\mathrm{cm}}{\text{year}} .
$$
The result, a few centimeters per year, is the typical speed of plate motion.
:::

:::osservazione{#prove-placche titolo="From observations to a model"}
The theory of plate motion, called plate tectonics, rests on observations of different kinds.

- **Matching coasts, fossils and rocks**. At the beginning of the twentieth century Alfred Wegener noticed that the coasts of Africa and South America have complementary outlines, and that the same fossils and the same rock formations are found on the two continents. He deduced that the continents were once joined. His hypothesis, called continental drift, did not say which force moved them, however, and for decades it was not accepted.
- **Magnetic stripes of the sea floor**. The lava that comes out of a ridge contains iron minerals. While the lava solidifies, these minerals align with Earth's magnetic field of that moment and keep that alignment. Earth's magnetic field reverses its polarity at irregular intervals, on average of a few hundred thousand years. On the two sides of a ridge, therefore, there are bands of rock with alternating polarity, arranged symmetrically. The symmetry shows that the sea floor forms along the ridge and moves away on both sides.
- **Distribution of earthquakes and volcanoes**. Most earthquakes and volcanoes are concentrated along narrow belts, which correspond to plate boundaries.

Some volcanoes lie inside a plate, far from the boundaries. They are explained by a **hot spot**, that is, a fixed zone of the mantle from which very hot material rises. The plate slides over the hot spot, and the active volcano is found on a new stretch of plate as time passes. A line of extinct volcanoes remains, older the farther they are from the hot spot. The Hawaiian Islands formed in this way: the youngest island, with the active volcanoes, is in the south-east, and the islands are older going north-west.

## Minerals and the rock cycle

:::definizione{#rocce titolo="Recognizing a rock's origin"}
A **mineral** is a natural solid substance with a definite chemical composition and with its atoms arranged in an ordered, repeating way, that is, in a crystal lattice. Quartz, for example, is a mineral made of silicon and oxygen. A **rock** is a natural aggregate of one or more minerals. Granite is a rock made of quartz, feldspars and micas.

A mineral is recognized by some properties: **hardness**, that is, resistance to being scratched; **cleavage**, that is, the tendency to break along flat surfaces; density; reaction with acids. Color alone is not enough, because the same mineral occurs in different colors. Hardness is expressed with the **Mohs scale**, which ranks ten reference minerals from 1 (talc) to 10 (diamond): each mineral scratches those with a lower number. The scale gives only an order. The number 10 of diamond, therefore, does not mean a hardness ten times that of talc.

Rocks are divided into three families, according to the way they formed.

- **Igneous rocks** form by solidification of a **magma**, that is, of molten rock. If the magma solidifies at depth, cooling is slow and the crystals have time to grow until they are visible to the naked eye: this is the case of granite. If the magma reaches the surface, where it is called **lava**, cooling is rapid and the crystals stay microscopic, as in basalt. With very rapid cooling no crystals form and a glass results, such as obsidian.
- **Sedimentary rocks** form from the accumulation of **sediments**, that is, fragments of other rocks, remains of organisms or salts deposited by water. The deeper layers are squeezed by the weight of those above (compaction), and the minerals carried by water bind the grains together (cementation). In this way sand becomes sandstone. Other examples are many limestones, formed by the accumulation of shells and skeletons of marine organisms, and rock salt, deposited by the evaporation of salt water.
- **Metamorphic rocks** form when an existing rock is subjected to high temperatures and pressures, and its minerals change while remaining solid. Limestone becomes marble; mudstone becomes slate. A pressure that is stronger in one direction arranges the minerals in parallel planes, and this sheet-like structure is called **foliation**. If the rock goes as far as melting, the melt is a magma, and the rock that derives from it is igneous.
:::

:::esempio{titolo="Recognizing three rocks from a description"}
- A light-colored rock is made of crystals a few millimeters across, all clearly visible and interlocked. Large crystals indicate slow cooling at depth, so the rock is **igneous**. It is a granite.
- A rock is made of cemented sand grains, is arranged in layers and contains imprints of shells. Grains, layers and fossils indicate a deposit of sediments, so the rock is **sedimentary**. It is a sandstone.
- A rock has its minerals aligned in thin sheets and splits into slabs. Foliation indicates a transformation under pressure, so the rock is **metamorphic**. It is a slate.
:::

:::osservazione{#ciclo-rocce titolo="A cycle with different pathways"}
Over long times every rock turns into a rock of another family. The set of these transformations is called the **rock cycle**. There are three steps.

- A rock exposed at the surface is broken up and eroded. The fragments are deposited and form a sedimentary rock.
- A rock carried to depth, at high temperature and pressure, becomes a metamorphic rock.
- A rock that melts becomes magma, and the magma that solidifies forms an igneous rock.

Each rock follows any one of the three steps, according to what happens to it. A granite, for example, has three paths ahead. If it is exposed at the surface it is eroded, and its grains end up in a sandstone. If it sinks it becomes a metamorphic rock, gneiss. If it sinks even deeper it melts and becomes magma again.
:::

## Surface processes

:::definizione{#superficie titolo="Weathering, erosion, and deposition"}
Earth's surface is shaped by four processes, which take place in succession.

1. **Weathering** breaks the rock up in place. It is **mechanical** when the rock breaks into fragments without changing composition: water that freezes in fractures increases in volume and widens them. It is **chemical** when the minerals react with water and change composition: rainwater, slightly acidic because of the carbon dioxide it contains, dissolves limestone and carves out caves.
2. **Erosion** detaches the fragments and carries them away.
3. **Transport** moves them. It is carried out by the water of rivers, by wind, by glaciers and by the force of gravity along slopes.
4. **Sedimentation** deposits them, when the agent that carries them loses energy.

Each agent of transport leaves recognisable shapes.

- A **river** cuts valleys with a V-shaped profile. Where the current slows down, for example at its mouth, it deposits first pebbles and gravel, which are heavier, then sand and finally mud, which stays in suspension longer.
- A **glacier** cuts valleys with a U-shaped profile, with a wide floor and steep sides.
- **Wind** carries sand and piles it up in dunes.

From the type of sediment and from the fossils it contains, the environment in which a sedimentary rock formed is reconstructed: a river, a lake, a desert, a beach, a sea floor.
:::

:::esempio{titolo="Shells on top of a mountain"}
In the Dolomites, at more than $2000\,\mathrm{m}$ of altitude, there are rocks with fossils of corals and marine shells. The fossils show that those rocks formed on the floor of a sea. Later the movements of the plates lifted them to their present altitude. Sea level, therefore, never reached $2000\,\mathrm{m}$: it is the rock that rose.
:::

:::osservazione{#biosfera titolo="Energy and interactions with organisms"}
Surface processes receive energy from two sources. The **Sun** evaporates water and sets the atmosphere in motion, so it produces rain and wind. **Gravity** makes water flow downwards, makes sediments fall to the bottom and makes slopes slide.

Organisms take part too. Roots widen the fractures of rocks and, together with the remains of plants and animals, help to form **soil**, the surface layer in which plants grow. Roots also hold the ground: a slope covered by a forest is eroded much less than a deforested slope.

A **drainage basin** is the land from which all rainwater flows towards the same river. The line that separates two neighboring basins, usually a ridge, is called a **watershed**. A drop that falls on one side of the ridge ends up in one river, a drop that falls on the other side ends up in a different river.

A surface covered with asphalt or concrete is impermeable. Rainwater does not infiltrate and all of it flows over the surface, so it reaches watercourses faster and in larger amounts.
:::

:::esempio{titolo="The same rain on a meadow and on a paved yard"}
A meadow and a paved yard of the same area both receive $20\,\mathrm{mm}$ of rain. Suppose that the soil of the meadow absorbs $15\,\mathrm{mm}$ of it.

On the meadow $20\,\mathrm{mm} - 15\,\mathrm{mm} = 5\,\mathrm{mm}$ of rain flows over the surface.

On the yard the asphalt absorbs no water, so all $20\,\mathrm{mm}$ flows over the surface.

The runoff from the yard is 4 times that from the meadow. For this reason, for the same rain, a heavily built-up area sends more water to rivers than an area covered with vegetation.
:::

## Internal heat, earthquakes, and volcanoes

:::definizione{#calore titolo="Heat sources and transfer"}
The interior of Earth is hot. In the crust the temperature increases on average by about $30\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ for every kilometer of depth, and at the center of the planet it exceeds $5000\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$. This heat has two origins.

- **Primordial heat** dates back to the formation of the planet, about 4.5 billion years ago. The impacts between the bodies that were clumping together and the sinking of iron towards the center released energy, and part of it is still stored in the interior.
- **Radiogenic heat** is produced continuously by the decay of radioactive elements contained in rocks, such as uranium, thorium and potassium.

Internal heat is independent of the heat that the surface receives from the Sun.

Heat is transferred in three ways.

- **Conduction**. Heat passes through a material at rest, from the hot zone to the cold one. This is how the handle of a spoon dipped in tea warms up. It takes place in the lithosphere, which is rigid.
- **Convection**. Heat is carried by material that moves. Hot material, less dense, rises; cold material, denser, sinks, and a circulation forms. This is what happens to water in a pan on the stove. It takes place in the mantle, where solid rock flows by a few centimeters per year, and in the outer core, which is liquid.
- **Radiation**. Heat is emitted as electromagnetic radiation, which travels even through a vacuum. This is how the Sun heats Earth and how Earth loses heat to space.

When heat is carried by a fluid moving in one direction, such as a rising magma, the term **advection** is also used.
:::

:::definizione{#sismi titolo="Source, waves, and effects"}
A **fault** is a fracture of the crust along which two blocks of rock slide relative to each other. Plate motion deforms the rocks on the two sides of the fault, which store energy like a compressed spring. When the deformation exceeds the strength of the rock, the two blocks slip suddenly and the energy is released in the form of waves. This event is an **earthquake**.

- The **hypocenter** is the point at depth where the rupture begins.
- The **epicenter** is the point of the surface vertically above the hypocenter.

An earthquake is measured with two different quantities.

- **Magnitude** measures the energy released at the source. Each earthquake has a single magnitude. One more unit of magnitude corresponds to about 30 times as much energy.
- **Intensity** describes the effects on people and buildings in a place, and is expressed with the Mercalli scale. The same earthquake has different intensities in different places: it is higher near the epicenter and where the ground and the buildings are less resistant.

The waves that leave the hypocenter are of two main types.

- **P waves** (primary) compress and stretch the rock in the direction in which they advance. They are the fastest, about $6\,\mathrm{km/s}$ in the crust, and pass through both solids and liquids.
- **S waves** (secondary) make the rock oscillate perpendicular to the direction in which they advance. They are slower, about $3.5\,\mathrm{km/s}$ in the crust, and pass only through solids.

P waves reach a measuring station before S waves. The delay between the two arrivals grows with the distance of the epicenter, so it allows that distance to be calculated.

S waves stop at the boundary of the outer core. Since they pass only through solids, from this observation it was deduced that the outer core is liquid.

A **tsunami** is a series of sea waves produced by a sudden vertical displacement of the sea floor, for example during a strong undersea earthquake.
:::

:::esempio{titolo="Distance of the epicenter from the delay between the waves"}
A station records the S waves $10\,\mathrm{s}$ after the P waves. The speeds are $6\,\mathrm{km/s}$ for P waves and $3.5\,\mathrm{km/s}$ for S waves. Let $d$ be the distance of the epicenter in kilometers. Each type of wave takes a time equal to the distance divided by the speed, so the delay is
$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{d}{3.5} - \frac{d}{6} &= 10 \\[6pt]
\frac{6d - 3.5\,d}{21} &= 10 \\[6pt]
\frac{2.5\,d}{21} &= 10 \\[6pt]
d &= \frac{210}{2.5} = 84 .
\end{aligned}
$$
The epicenter is $84\,\mathrm{km}$ away. Check: P waves take $\dfrac{84}{6} = 14\,\mathrm{s}$ and S waves take $\dfrac{84}{3.5} = 24\,\mathrm{s}$, a difference of $10\,\mathrm{s}$.
:::

:::osservazione{#vulcani titolo="Magma, gases, and eruptive products"}
A **volcano** is an opening in the crust from which magma and gases come out. The type of eruption depends on two properties of the magma: **viscosity**, that is, resistance to flow, and the amount of dissolved gases.

- A fluid magma lets gases escape easily. The eruption is **effusive**: lava comes out and flows down the sides in streams. This is the case of the Hawaiian volcanoes.
- A viscous magma traps the gases, whose pressure increases until the magma is shattered and thrown into the air. The eruption is **explosive**. This is the case of the eruption of Vesuvius in AD 79, which destroyed Pompeii.

An explosive eruption produces ash and fragments, called **pyroclasts**. A **pyroclastic flow** is a mixture of very hot gases and pyroclasts that runs down the sides of the volcano at hundreds of kilometers per hour. It is the most dangerous volcanic phenomenon.

In subduction zones the descending plate carries water with it. At depth the water is released and passes into the mantle above, whose melting temperature it lowers. Part of the mantle melts, and the magma that forms rises and feeds the volcanoes.
:::

## Geological time

:::definizione{#cronologia titolo="Ordering events"}
Earth is about $4.54$ billion years old. This time is divided into ever shorter intervals: eons, eras, periods, epochs and ages.

Past events are dated in two ways.

- **Relative dating** establishes which of two events happened first, without saying how many years ago.
- **Absolute** (or numerical) **dating** assigns an age in years.

Relative dating uses three rules.

1. **Superposition**. In a series of sedimentary layers that has not been overturned, each layer is older than the one above it, because sediments are deposited one on top of another.
2. **Cross-cutting**. A fault or an intrusion of magma is younger than the rocks it cuts through, because the rocks had to exist already in order to be cut.
3. **Inclusion**. A fragment contained in a rock is older than the rock that contains it.

**Index fossils** are remains of organisms that lived for a short time and over very wide areas. Two layers that contain the same index fossil, even on different continents, have the same age.

An **angular unconformity** is a surface that separates tilted layers, below, from horizontal layers, above. It records four events in succession: the deposition of the first layers, their tilting, the erosion of the upper part, the deposition of the new layers.

The principle of **uniformitarianism** states that the processes observable today also acted in the past. The ripples preserved in an ancient sandstone, for example, are interpreted by comparing them with those that waves form today on the sand of a beach.
:::

:::esempio{titolo="Putting four events in order"}
In a rock face three layers A, B and C are observed, from bottom to top. A fault F cuts layers A and B and stops below layer C.

By the rule of superposition the order of the layers is A, then B, then C. By the cross-cutting rule the fault F is younger than A and B, because it cuts them. Layer C is not cut, so it was deposited after the movement of the fault.

The order of the events, from the oldest to the most recent, is A, B, F, C.
:::

:::definizione{#datazioni titolo="Decay and conditions for validity"}
Absolute dating is based on **radioactive isotopes**, unstable atoms that over time turn into atoms of another element. The transformation is called decay. The starting atom is called the parent and the atom produced is called the daughter.

The **half-life** $T$ is the time in which half of the parent atoms present decay. It is a constant characteristic of each isotope: it is 5730 years for carbon-14 and 1.25 billion years for potassium-40.

At each half-life the remaining amount is halved again. After a time $T$, $\dfrac{1}{2}$ of the initial atoms remains, after $2T$, $\dfrac{1}{4}$ remains, after $3T$, $\dfrac{1}{8}$ remains. In general, if $N_0$ is the initial number of parent atoms, after a time $t$ the number remaining is
$$
N(t)=N_0\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{\frac{t}{T}} .
$$
By measuring in a mineral the fraction of parent atoms remaining, the number of half-lives that have passed is obtained, and hence the age of the mineral.

The age is correct if, since the moment it formed, the mineral has not exchanged atoms with its surroundings, that is, it has neither lost nor gained parent or daughter atoms. The age measured is that of the moment at which the mineral crystallized.

::grafico{f="100*2^(-x/20)" xmin="0" xmax="60" ymin="0" ymax="100" nomi="t,p" punti="0,100;20,50;40,25;60,12.5" titolo="Remaining fraction p as a percentage; time t in millions of years, with a half-life of 20 million years"}
:::

:::esempio{titolo="Age of a mineral"}
In a mineral, $\dfrac{1}{8}$ of the initial atoms of an isotope with a half-life of 20 million years remains. Since
$$
\frac{1}{8} = \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{3} ,
$$
3 half-lives have passed. The age of the mineral is $3 \cdot 20 = 60$ million years. In the graph this case is the point with abscissa 60 and ordinate $12.5$, because $\dfrac{1}{8}$ corresponds to $12.5\%$.
:::

:::attenzione
At each half-life, half of the remaining atoms decay, not half of the initial ones. With 800 initial atoms, after $T$ 400 remain, after $2T$ 200 remain and after $3T$ 100 remain. If 400 atoms decayed at each step, none would remain after $2T$.
:::

## Georesources and sustainability

:::definizione{#risorse titolo="Resources, reserves, and renewability"}
A **georesource** is a material or a form of energy obtained from Earth: metal ores, building stone, groundwater, oil, heat from underground.

Two quantities are distinguished.

- The **resource** is the whole amount present underground, discovered or estimated.
- The **reserve** is the part of the resource already identified and worth extracting with present technologies and prices.

The reserve is therefore a part of the resource, and it changes over time even if nothing changes underground. If the price of a metal rises, deposits that were previously too expensive to exploit become profitable, and the reserve increases.

The **grade** of a deposit is the percentage of useful material contained in the extracted rock.

A resource is **renewable** if it re-forms in times comparable with those in which it is consumed, and **non-renewable** otherwise.

- Coal, oil and natural gas are **fossil fuels**: they derive from remains of organisms transformed over millions of years. They are consumed in a few decades, so they are non-renewable.
- The energy of the Sun and that of the wind are renewable. **Geothermal energy**, which exploits Earth's internal heat, is also considered renewable.
- The water of an aquifer is renewable as long as withdrawals stay below recharge.
:::

:::esempio{titolo="Metal obtained from a deposit"}
From a deposit, $100\,\mathrm{t}$ of rock with a copper grade of $2\%$ is extracted. The copper contained is
$$
100\,\mathrm{t} \cdot \frac{2}{100} = 2\,\mathrm{t} .
$$
The processing plant recovers $80\%$ of the copper contained:
$$
2\,\mathrm{t} \cdot \frac{80}{100} = 1.6\,\mathrm{t} .
$$
From $100\,\mathrm{t}$ of rock, $1.6\,\mathrm{t}$ of copper is obtained. The remaining $98.4\,\mathrm{t}$ is waste material.
:::

:::osservazione{#bilanci titolo="Sustainable withdrawal depends on fluxes"}
A withdrawal is sustainable if the stored amount does not decrease over time. To establish this, the balance between inputs and outputs is written.

An aquifer receives 4 million cubic meters of water each year from infiltrating rain, and wells withdraw 6 million. The annual balance is
$$
4 - 6 = -2 \ \text{million cubic meters} .
$$
The negative sign shows that the aquifer loses 2 million cubic meters per year. If it contains 50 million cubic meters, with these withdrawals it is exhausted in $\dfrac{50}{2} = 25$ years.

The withdrawal becomes sustainable if it falls below the recharge of 4 million cubic meters. Part of the recharge also feeds the springs and rivers connected to the aquifer, so the sustainable withdrawal is less than 4 million cubic meters per year.
:::

## Natural hazards and land use

:::definizione{#rischi titolo="Hazard, exposure, and vulnerability"}
Natural **risk** is the damage expected from a phenomenon such as an earthquake, a flood, a landslide or an eruption. It depends on three factors.

- **Hazard** is the probability that in an area, in a given interval of time, a phenomenon of a given strength occurs. It depends on the nature of the place.
- **Exposure** is the set of people and assets present in the area.
- **Vulnerability** is how easily people and assets suffer damage. A building built to earthquake-resistant standards has low vulnerability.

Risk is high only if all three factors are high. In symbolic form it is written
$$
\text{risk}=\text{hazard}\times\text{exposure}\times\text{vulnerability} .
$$
A desert area with strong earthquakes has high hazard and almost no exposure, so low risk. A city built without earthquake-resistant standards in an area with moderate earthquakes has high risk.

The hazard of a place stays what it is. Risk is reduced by acting on the other two factors: strengthening buildings reduces vulnerability; leaving free the areas that a river floods reduces exposure.
:::

:::esempio{titolo="Expected annual loss"}
In an area, a flood that causes damage of 2 million euros has probability $\dfrac{1}{50}$ of occurring in a year. The expected annual loss is the product of the probability and the damage:
$$
\frac{1}{50} \cdot 2{,}000{,}000 = 40{,}000 \ \text{euros} .
$$
Half of the assets are moved out of the floodable area. The damage falls to 1 million euros and the expected annual loss becomes
$$
\frac{1}{50} \cdot 1{,}000{,}000 = 20{,}000 \ \text{euros} .
$$
The probability of the flood is still $\dfrac{1}{50}$. Exposure has decreased, and risk with it.
:::

:::osservazione{#prevenzione titolo="Connecting mechanisms and measures"}
Each phenomenon has its own mechanism, and the prevention measure acts on that mechanism.

- **Landslides**. Prolonged rain fills the pores of the ground of a slope with water. The water reduces the friction that holds the grains in place, so the ground slides. Slope, type of ground and the presence of vegetation matter. The measures are draining the water and keeping the forest.
- **Floods**. They occur when a river receives more water than its bed can hold. The measures are leaving free the areas where the river spreads during high water and limiting impermeable surfaces.
- **Earthquakes**. The measure is building and strengthening buildings to earthquake-resistant standards.
- **Eruptions**. Lava flows, ash fall and pyroclastic flows reach different areas around the volcano. The measure is evacuation plans for the exposed areas.

**Monitoring** instruments record the signals that precede some phenomena, such as small tremors and the swelling of the ground before an eruption. They make it possible to give early warning of eruptions, floods and tsunamis. For earthquakes, today the areas where they are likely are identified, but not the day and time at which they will occur.
:::
