Earth science connects observations, processes, and timescales. A rock preserves evidence of its history; a measurement describes a system only alongside the conditions in which it was obtained. This chapter distinguishes materials, energy sources, transfers, and consequences to reconstruct these connections.

## Earth in space

:::definizione{#terra-spazio titolo="Earth's motions and astronomical scales"}
Earth is a planet in the Solar System, bound to the Sun by gravity. The Sun is a star: it produces energy through nuclear reactions in its interior. Planets and satellites are observed mainly through reflected light. The Solar System belongs to the Milky Way, a galaxy containing many stars; a galaxy is not a single planetary system.

Earth's **rotation** about its axis takes about one day and produces the alternation of day and night. Viewed from the North Pole, it is counterclockwise; the Sun's apparent daily motion runs in the opposite direction, from east to west. For orientation in the Northern Hemisphere, Polaris indicates approximately north. At an equinox the Sun rises approximately due east, on an unobstructed horizon at nonpolar latitudes.

Earth's **revolution** around the Sun takes about one year. Its axis is tilted by about $23.4^\circ$ from the perpendicular to the orbital plane and keeps almost the same direction throughout an orbit. This changes daylight duration and the Sun's altitude: when a hemisphere receives more direct sunlight and longer days, it experiences summer. The other experiences winter. Distance from the Sun does not explain opposite seasons at the same time. At equinoxes, day and night are approximately equal in length; solstices mark the annual extremes of daylight duration.
:::

:::osservazione{#distanze titolo="Distances, light, and alignments"}
The astronomical unit is approximately the mean Earth-Sun distance. A **light-year** is the distance light travels through a vacuum in one year. From $d=ct$, observing an object four light-years away means receiving light emitted about four years earlier, neglecting relative motion.

The Moon reflects sunlight; its phases depend on the illuminated portion visible from Earth. During a lunar eclipse, Earth lies between the Sun and Moon; during a solar eclipse, the Moon lies between the Sun and Earth. Eclipses do not occur every month because the Moon's orbital plane is tilted relative to Earth's. Alignments at new and full moon favour larger tidal ranges without requiring an eclipse.
:::

## The spheres of the Earth system

:::definizione{#struttura titolo="Composition and mechanical behaviour"}
Classification by composition distinguishes **crust**, **mantle**, and **core**. Continental crust is on average thicker and less dense than oceanic crust. The mantle consists mainly of silicate rocks; the core is rich in iron. The outer core is liquid and the inner core solid: physical state depends on both temperature and pressure, so greater depth does not automatically imply melting.

Mechanical classification instead uses the behaviour of materials. The **lithosphere** includes the crust and rigid uppermost mantle; it is divided into plates. The underlying asthenosphere, which is mostly solid, can deform slowly. The crust-mantle boundary therefore does not coincide with the lithosphere-asthenosphere boundary. A solid can transmit seismic waves and deform over millions of years: these behaviours are observed on different timescales. Removing a large ice sheet can allow slow land uplift through isostatic readjustment after unloading.
:::

:::definizione{#acqua titolo="Water reservoirs and fluxes"}
The hydrosphere includes oceanic, continental, and underground water; the frozen component is also called the cryosphere. Evaporation turns liquid water into vapour, condensation forms droplets, and precipitation returns water towards the surface. Wind carries moisture between regions. Some water runs off into rivers, while some infiltrates and can recharge groundwater; plants return water to the atmosphere through transpiration.

**Porosity** is the fraction of volume occupied by voids. **Permeability** describes the ability to transmit water: many isolated pores do not guarantee good flow. An aquifer stores water and transmits significant quantities of it. In its water budget, the change in stored water equals inputs minus outputs, including recharge, withdrawals, and any natural exchanges specified in the problem.
:::

:::definizione{#atmosfera titolo="Atmosphere and energy balance"}
Dry air near the surface contains about $78\%$ nitrogen and $21\%$ oxygen; water vapour is variable. The troposphere contains much of the water vapour and hosts most weather. Above it lies the stratosphere, which contains the ozone layer that absorbs ultraviolet radiation. Pressure generally falls with altitude because the weight of the overlying air column decreases.

Uneven heating produces pressure differences and promotes atmospheric circulation. Weather describes short-term local conditions; climate describes their distributions, averages, and variability over long periods. One cold episode alone does not establish a climatic trend.

**Albedo** is the fraction of incident radiation reflected: with the same incoming energy, a darker surface absorbs more. Greenhouse gases absorb and re-emit some terrestrial infrared radiation, altering the energy balance. Cooling moist air can bring it to saturation: beyond that condition, some vapour may condense. Air, water, rocks, and the biosphere also exchange matter: photosynthesis, for example, transfers carbon from carbon dioxide into organic matter.
:::

## Plates and mountain building

:::definizione{#placche titolo="Three types of boundary"}
Lithospheric plates move by a few centimetres per year. They may contain both oceans and continents; a continental edge is not necessarily a plate boundary. At **divergent** boundaries, plates move apart. At ridges, material rises, partially melts as pressure decreases, and produces new oceanic crust. The youngest seafloor lies near the axis; its age generally increases away from it.

At **convergent** boundaries, oceanic lithosphere may descend into the mantle in a subduction zone. A trench, a dipping zone of increasingly deep earthquakes, and a volcanic arc are consistent evidence of this process. In continental collision, crustal shortening and thickening dominate, producing mountain ranges. At **transform** boundaries, plates slide past one another: lithosphere is not systematically created or consumed along the active segment.
:::

:::osservazione{#prove-placche titolo="From observations to a model"}
Wegener used matching fossils, rocks, and structures on continents now separated as evidence of a former connection. Similar coastlines alone do not demonstrate the mechanism of movement. Symmetrical magnetic stripes beside ridges provide further evidence: basalts record the field's polarity as they cool and are then carried away on both sides.

A volcanic chain with ages increasing in one direction may record a plate moving over a relatively persistent source. This hotspot model requires assumptions about source mobility: not every volcano is at a plate boundary. Earthquake and volcano distributions overlap only partly.

## Minerals and the rock cycle

:::definizione{#rocce titolo="Recognising a rock's origin"}
A **mineral** is a natural solid with an ordered internal structure and a composition within defined limits. A rock is a natural aggregate of minerals or other materials. Volcanic glass, which lacks long-range crystalline order, is not a crystalline mineral. Hardness, cleavage, density, and chemical reactions help identification; colour alone is often insufficient. The Mohs scale ranks resistance to scratching: it is a relative scale, not a linear measure of resistance to breaking.

**Igneous** rocks form from a melt. Slow cooling at depth favours visible crystals; rapid cooling may produce small crystals or glass. Large crystals in a fine groundmass may indicate two cooling stages. Magma is molten material below ground; lava is molten material erupted at the surface.

**Sedimentary** rocks form through the accumulation and transformation of sediments or precipitation of dissolved substances. Compaction and cementation turn sand into sandstone. Evaporation can bring a solution to saturation and cause salts to precipitate, forming evaporites. Biological remains can contribute to carbonate sediments: sedimentary origin does not always imply fragments carried by a river.

**Metamorphic** rocks change in the solid state through changes in temperature, pressure, and interaction with fluids. Limestone may recrystallise into marble. Directed stress can favour foliation; near an intrusion, the thermal effect of contact metamorphism dominates. If a rock melts and the melt solidifies, the new product is classified as igneous.
:::

:::osservazione{#ciclo-rocce titolo="A cycle with different pathways"}
The rock cycle is not a compulsory three-stage sequence. Granite may be weathered, eroded, and converted into sediment; it may undergo metamorphism or melt. Reconstructing the pathway requires identifying the documented transformations. The initial rock's name does not by itself determine the final rock's name: melting, deposition, recrystallisation, and environmental conditions matter.
:::

## Surface processes

:::definizione{#superficie titolo="Weathering, erosion, and deposition"}
**Weathering** changes rocks in place. It may be mechanical, such as fragmentation by repeated freezing and thawing in cracks, or chemical, such as carbonate dissolution in weakly acidic water. Erosion removes material; transport moves it; sedimentation deposits it. A fractured rock that remains in place has undergone weathering without necessarily being transported.

Rivers, wind, ice, and gravity sort and shape material in different ways. When a river enters a lake and slows, it tends to deposit coarser grains first; fine particles can remain suspended longer. Dunes indicate wind deposition; a U-shaped valley may record glacial erosion, whereas river incision tends to produce V-shaped valleys. The present shape may combine several processes from different periods.

Sedimentary environments include continental rivers, lakes, and deserts, coastal beaches and deltas, and marine shelves and basins. Grain size, sedimentary structures, and fossils help reconstruct them together. A marine fossil in a rock now high in a mountain indicates a past environment followed by uplift: it does not require the sea to have reached the mountain's present altitude.
:::

:::osservazione{#biosfera titolo="Energy and interactions with organisms"}
The Sun powers evaporation and atmospheric circulation; gravity promotes runoff, sedimentation, and slope movements. Organisms contribute to soil formation and rock weathering. Roots can hold soil and reduce surface erosion: removing vegetation changes the water-soil system.

A drainage basin directs runoff towards the same outlet. A drainage divide separates adjacent basins; crossing a ridge may change the destination of rainwater. Paving a surface often reduces infiltration and increases rapid runoff, with rainfall and the other conditions held equal.
:::

## Internal heat, earthquakes, and volcanoes

:::definizione{#calore titolo="Heat sources and transfer"}
Earth's heat includes residual energy from planetary accretion and differentiation, as well as heat produced by radioactive decay. Dense materials sinking during differentiation convert gravitational energy into internal energy. These sources differ from solar heating of the surface.

**Conduction** transfers energy without bulk movement of matter. **Convection** involves movement of material, often associated with density differences. The mostly solid mantle can undergo slow motions. **Advection** is heat transport by moving material, for example rising magma. **Radiation** transfers energy through electromagnetic waves and enables Earth to lose energy to space. Identifying the process requires distinguishing energy flow from matter flow.
:::

:::definizione{#sismi titolo="Source, waves, and effects"}
An earthquake releases energy when rapid slip occurs along a fault. The **hypocentre** is the point at which rupture starts at depth; the **epicentre** is its projection onto the surface. Magnitude characterises the source according to the scale used. Intensity describes effects at a location and varies with distance, ground conditions, and buildings: one earthquake can have different intensities in different places.

P waves are compressional and travel through solids and liquids; S waves are shear waves and do not propagate through liquids. In the same ordinary solid, P waves travel faster than S waves. The S-P delay can therefore provide distance information using a velocity model. The absence of S waves crossing the outer core is evidence of its liquid state. Rapid vertical displacement of a large seafloor area may generate a tsunami; not every underwater earthquake does so.
:::

:::osservazione{#vulcani titolo="Magma, gases, and eruptive products"}
Volcanic behaviour depends on viscosity, gases, and system conditions. Viscous magma can hinder gas escape and favour explosive behaviour. A lava flow differs from a pyroclastic density current, a hot mixture of gas and fragments that can move rapidly down slopes. In subduction zones, fluids released by the slab promote partial melting of the overlying mantle: the entire slab need not melt.
:::

## Geological time

:::definizione{#cronologia titolo="Ordering events"}
Earth is about $4.54$ billion years old. Geological time is organised into successively shorter units: eons, eras, periods, epochs, and ages. **Relative dating** establishes event order; numerical dating assigns ages with uncertainties. A correct order does not automatically give durations or rates.

In a sedimentary sequence that has not been overturned, lower layers are older than upper ones. A fault or intrusion is younger than the rocks it cuts; an included fragment is older than the rock containing it. An angular unconformity requires deposition, deformation, erosion, and renewed deposition. Index fossils allow correlation when they are geographically widespread and restricted to a relatively short time interval.

Actualism uses observable processes to interpret evidence of the past. Ripples in sandstone can be compared with those formed today by waves or currents. This does not imply constant rates or exclude rapid, catastrophic events.
:::

:::definizione{#datazioni titolo="Decay and conditions for validity"}
The half-life $T$ is the time required for half the initial radioactive nuclei to remain. In a closed system, after time $t$,
$$
N(t)=N_0\left(\frac12\right)^{t/T}.
$$
After $T$, $2T$, and $3T$, the remaining fractions are respectively $\dfrac12$, $\dfrac14$, and $\dfrac18$ of the initial nuclei. The remaining amount halves; half the initial amount is not subtracted each time. Determining an age requires an appropriate isotopic system, information about the initial daughter product, and no later exchange that alters the measured ratio. A radiometric age may refer to crystallisation or system closure, not always to formation of the entire rock.

## Georesources and sustainability

:::definizione{#risorse titolo="Resources, reserves, and renewability"}
A **georesource** is a geological material or energy source that can potentially be used. A **reserve** is the identified portion that can be exploited under applicable technical, economic, and other criteria. Geological discovery and economic viability are different issues. A higher price or more efficient technology can change reserves without creating new minerals underground.

Ore grade indicates how much useful material the extracted ore contains. From $100\,\mathrm{t}$ of ore at $2\%$, the theoretical useful component is $2\,\mathrm{t}$; an $80\%$ recovery makes $1.6\,\mathrm{t}$ available. Extracted material therefore differs from recovered product. Exploitation also requires consideration of energy, water, waste, and environmental impacts.

Coal, oil, and natural gas form over times far longer than human consumption and are nonrenewable on that timescale. Geothermal energy uses Earth's heat; solar energy depends on incoming radiation. Renewability compares replenishment with use and does not imply the absence of local limits.
:::

:::osservazione{#bilanci titolo="Sustainable withdrawal depends on fluxes"}
An aquifer may receive $4$ million cubic metres per year and lose $6$ through withdrawals, neglecting other fluxes. The annual balance is $4-6=-2$ million cubic metres: storage decreases. Even reducing withdrawals to match recharge does not by itself demonstrate overall sustainability, because ecologically necessary discharge and water-quality issues may exist. The model must specify which inputs and outputs it includes.
:::

## Natural hazards and land use

:::definizione{#rischi titolo="Hazard, exposure, and vulnerability"}
**Hazard** concerns the possibility of a damaging phenomenon of a given intensity occurring in an area over a time interval. **Exposure** concerns the people and assets present; **vulnerability** indicates how susceptible they are to damage. Risk combines these components. An uninhabited area may have high hazard but low human exposure; constructing new buildings can increase risk without changing the natural process.

A map of expected shaking does not by itself describe economic losses. Resistant buildings can reduce seismic vulnerability; moving facilities outside a flood-prone area reduces exposure. The probability of the phenomenon is not automatically eliminated. In a simplified model, an event with annual probability $p$ and loss $D$ contributes $pD$ to expected annual loss; events of different intensities require a more detailed model.
:::

:::osservazione{#prevenzione titolo="Connecting mechanisms and measures"}
Prolonged rain can raise pore-water pressure in a slope and reduce resistance to sliding. Landslide assessment considers slope, materials, water, and land modifications. Flooding depends on rainfall, runoff, channel capacity, and occupation of flood-prone areas. For volcanoes, lava flows, ash fall, and pyroclastic density currents must be distinguished because their distributions differ.

Monitoring and precursor signals improve assessment but do not amount to an exact prediction of the location, time, and intensity of every event. A preventive measure must address the relevant component: strengthening a school, preserving space for floodwater, and planning evacuation address different problems. Outcomes also depend on implementation and local context.
:::
