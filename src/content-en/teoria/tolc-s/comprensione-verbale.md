## What understanding a passage means

:::definizione{#comprensione titolo="Text, question and answer"}
A **reading comprehension** question has three parts: a **passage** to read, a **question** and five **answers**, only one of which is correct. The correct answer is the one that the passage supports, that is, the one that can be obtained from what the passage says. Personal knowledge of the topic does not count: a sentence that is true in reality is wrong as an answer if the passage does not contain it and does not allow it to be deduced.

The question says what to look for in the passage. There are four common wordings.

- "According to the author…" asks for the opinion of the writer.
- "Which fact is reported…" asks for a piece of information written in the passage.
- "Which conclusion follows…" asks for a deduction, that is, a piece of information obtained by connecting several sentences.
- "Which statement contradicts the text…" asks for the sentence that conflicts with what the passage says.

The same passage has different answers for different questions, so the question is read as carefully as the passage.
:::

:::esempio{titolo="A survey and what it measures"}
> In October the town library recorded 1200 evening entries, against 900 in September. The time each visitor spent reading was not measured.

First question: "Which fact is reported in the passage?" The answer is that evening entries rose from 900 to 1200. The fact is written in the first sentence.

Second question: "Does it follow from the passage that in October visitors read for longer?" The answer is no. The passage counts entries and states that reading time was not measured. A larger number of entries is also obtained with more people who come in for a few minutes.

The two questions use the same passage and have different answers, because the first asks for a written fact and the second asks for a deduction that the passage does not allow.
:::

:::osservazione
The passages in this chapter are original and are not official questions from CISIA, the consortium of universities that runs the TOLC tests. Institutions, data and situations serve the reading exercise. Only the information in the passage is used to answer: the real rules of a library, a museum or a laboratory are not added to the text.
:::

## Explicit, implicit and absent information

:::definizione{#informazioni titolo="Three relationships with the text"}
With respect to a passage, a piece of information is in one of three situations.

- It is **explicit** if the passage states it directly. The answer that reports it often uses words different from those of the passage.
- It is **implicit** if the passage does not write it, but it can be obtained with certainty by connecting pieces of information that the passage contains.
- It is **absent** if the passage does not write it and does not allow it to be obtained. Absent information is possible, but the passage neither confirms nor denies it.

Consider the passage "The museum is open from Tuesday to Sunday and is closed on Mondays. The class visit is set for Monday."

- "The museum is closed on Mondays" is explicit information, because it is written in the first sentence.
- "On the day set for the visit the museum is closed" is implicit information. No sentence says so, but it follows from the two sentences read together.
- "25 students will take part in the visit" is absent information, because the passage says nothing about the number of participants.
:::

:::esempio{titolo="A deduction joining two facts"}
> The archive has digitised all the maps published after 1950 and some maps published earlier.

One map in the catalogue is from 1962. Since 1962 comes after 1950, the map belongs to the group of which the passage says "all". The map has therefore been digitised, and the information is implicit.

Another map is from 1930. The passage says that "some" maps earlier than 1950 have been digitised, without saying which. For the 1930 map the correct answer is "the passage does not allow this to be established", and the information is absent.
:::

:::attenzione
"The passage does not show that the procedure works" and "the passage shows that the procedure does not work" are two different statements. The first leaves the question open, the second gives a negative result. For example, a trial carried out on only two samples and successful in both is too small to conclude that the procedure always works, but it does not indicate that the procedure fails.
:::

## Thesis, structure and examples

:::definizione{#tesi titolo="Topic and thesis"}
The **topic** of a passage is what the passage is about. The **thesis** is what the author claims about that topic. The topic is expressed with a noun or a few words, for example "maps". The thesis is expressed with a whole sentence, for example "the details of a map should be chosen according to the use that will be made of it". Two passages with the same topic often have different theses.

A passage is built in parts, and each part has a function. There are three common patterns.

- The passage presents a problem, compares two solutions and prefers one of them.
- The passage describes a phenomenon and then states within which limits the description holds.
- The passage tells an episode and uses it as an example of a more general change.

Recognising the pattern is useful in questions that ask for the thesis, a title or a summary: the correct answer takes account of all the parts of the passage and not of one only.
:::

:::definizione{#esempi titolo="The function of examples"}
In a passage an **example** is a particular case that the author reports in support of what is said. It has one of three functions: it makes an abstract idea concrete, it shows that something is possible, or it refutes a rule that is too general. In this last case it is called a counterexample.

An example illustrates the thesis with one case. It does not prove it for all cases.

> The landscape of a city also includes the sounds and the habits of those who live there. In a factory district, after night shifts were abolished, residents described the streets as "another place", although no building had changed.

The thesis is in the first sentence. The second sentence is the example: it shows a district perceived differently without the buildings having changed. It does not follow from the passage that the same happens in all factory districts, because the author describes a single case.
:::

:::esempio{titolo="A proportionate title"}
> A map for pedestrians highlights accessible routes and crossings. A map of the same area for maintenance workers highlights pipes and installations. Neither is more correct than the other: each selects what its user needs.

Three titles are compared.

- "The pipes of the district" concerns a detail of the second sentence. It is too narrow.
- "Maps are always incomplete and useless" contains a judgement that the passage does not express. It is too general.
- "Maps change with their purpose" summarises all three sentences. It is the adequate title.

An adequate title says as much as the thesis says, without removing a part of it and without adding claims.
:::

## Connectives and logical relationships

:::definizione{#connettivi titolo="Causes, consequences and contrasts"}
A **connective** is a word or expression that links two sentences and says how they are related. The most common connectives indicate five relationships.

| Relationship | Connectives | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cause | since, because, given that | The train is late *because* there is a fault. |
| Consequence | so, therefore, hence | There is a fault, *so* the train is late. |
| Contrast | but, however, yet | The train is late; *however*, the connection waits. |
| Addition | moreover, also | The train is late; *moreover*, it is full. |
| Explanation | that is, namely | The train is cancelled, *that is*, it does not leave. |

Changing the connective changes the meaning. In the sentence "The project arrived on time; however, a signature is missing", the connective "however" presents the missing signature as an obstacle that remains in spite of the punctuality. With "therefore" in place of "however" the sentence would say that the signature is missing because of the punctuality, which is something else.
:::

:::esempio{titolo="Concession and contrast"}
"Although the hall is smaller, the voices are easier to distinguish." The connective "although" introduces a **concession**: it admits a fact, namely that the hall is small, and states a result that would not be expected, namely that the voices are easier to distinguish. The sentence confirms that the hall is small. It does not say that the voices are easier to distinguish *because* the hall is small.

The word "while" has two meanings, and the sentence shows which of the two applies.

- "While in winter the boat leaves only in the morning, in summer it also makes an evening trip." Here "while" compares two situations.
- "While the boat was leaving, the caretaker was closing the gate." Here "while" indicates two actions that happen at the same moment.
:::

:::definizione{#condizioni titolo="Conditions and exceptions"}
A **condition** is a fact on which another fact depends. It is introduced by "if", "provided that", "as long as", "only if". In the notice "Access to the courtyard is allowed provided that the side passage is used", access depends on the use of the side passage: anyone who enters another way is not authorised.

An **exception** removes some cases from a general rule. It is introduced by "except", "apart from", "with the exception of". In the sentence "The plaster copies stay in the room, except those sent for restoration", the rule holds for all the copies minus those under restoration.

A condition is of two types, and the two sentences below say different things.

- "The application is accepted **only if** it is signed." The signature is a **necessary** condition: without a signature the application is rejected. A signed application, however, is still rejected if another requirement is missing.
- "**If** the application is signed, it is accepted." The signature is a **sufficient** condition: the signature is enough for the application to be accepted.

From the first sentence one knows what happens to unsigned applications. From the second one knows what happens to signed applications.
:::

## References, words and viewpoint

:::definizione{#riferimenti titolo="Pronouns and textual references"}
To avoid repeating a word, a passage recalls it with another expression: a pronoun, such as "this", "those", "it", or a phrase, such as "the former", "the latter", "in this way". The expression that recalls is called a **reference**, and the element recalled is called the **referent**. The referent is a noun or a whole sentence.

The referent is found in two steps. First, the elements that agree with the reference in number and, where it applies, in gender are sought in the preceding sentences. Then each candidate is substituted for the reference, and the one with which the sentence makes sense is kept.

- "The tower houses two bells and three clocks; the latter have been synchronised." The expression "the latter" indicates the last element named, so the referent is "three clocks".
- "The original code is kept in a separate field; in this way the starting image can be recognised." The expression "in this way" recalls the whole action of keeping the code in a separate field.
:::

:::definizione{#lessico titolo="Meaning in context"}
Many words have several meanings. The **contextual meaning** is the one the word has in the sentence in which it occurs. It is recognised by substituting each of its meanings for the word and keeping the one with which the sentence stays coherent with the rest of the passage.

In the sentence "The exhibition shows an unpublished photograph from 1950", the word "unpublished" means "never published before". It concerns publication and not the date of the shot: the photograph is decades old and is being shown for the first time.

Two words with the same root sometimes have different meanings. A "recycled" material is made with matter recovered from products already used. A "recyclable" material can be recovered after use. A passage that describes a "recyclable" package does not say that the package is made of recycled material.

If the passage gives the definition of a term, that definition holds, even when it differs from common usage.
:::

:::osservazione
The **viewpoint** is the person to whom an opinion or a memory reported in the passage belongs. Several voices often speak in a passage: the author, a narrator, the people the author quotes.

Consider the sentence "A colleague objects that the method is too slow; the data collected so far, however, show normal times". The opinion "the method is too slow" belongs to the colleague. The author reports it and then disputes it with the data. The answer "according to the author the method is too slow" attributes to the author the opinion opposite to the author's own, so it is wrong.

A reported opinion belongs to the author only if the author states agreement with it.
:::

## Quantities, times and the scope of claims

:::definizione{#ambito titolo="The scope of a conclusion"}
The **scope** of a statement is the set of cases to which the statement refers. It is indicated by quantity words.

| Word | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| all, every | the whole set, without exceptions |
| some | at least one case, without saying how many |
| none | zero cases |
| only | excludes what lies outside the group indicated |
| often | in many cases, not in all |
| may, can | it is possible, it is not certain |

The correct answer keeps the quantity word of the passage. If the passage says "some students arrive late", the answer "students arrive late" turns it into "all", and it is wrong.

Time also limits the scope. A survey carried out in August describes preferences in the summer months, and on its own says nothing about winter preferences. A comparison that lasted one week does not ensure the same result for a year.
:::

:::esempio{titolo="Sample and population"}
> On Monday morning a newsroom interviewed 80 people entering the campus through the north gate. Among those interviewed, the most used means of transport was the bus.

The people interviewed form the **sample**. All the people who attend the campus form the **population**. The passage describes the sample. Three answers are assessed.

- "Among those interviewed the bus prevails." It keeps the group and the quantity of the passage. It is correct.
- "All the students of the campus use the bus." It moves from those interviewed to all students, and from "the most used" to "all". It is wrong, because those who enter through the other gates or on other days were not interviewed.
- "Nobody uses other means." The expression "the most used" means that the other means are used less, and not that nobody uses them. It is wrong.
:::

:::definizione{#cronologia titolo="Temporal order and duration"}
In a passage that describes a series of events, time expressions give three types of information.

- **Order**, with "before", "after", "later". "After the check" means that the check has already taken place.
- **Deadline**, with "by". "By Friday" means Friday or an earlier day.
- **Duration**, with "for" and "in". "For three months" indicates how long an activity lasts, without saying when it begins.

To reconstruct a procedure, the steps are written in the order in which they happen. Consider the passage "The office receives the application and checks it. If data are missing it requests them; once the supplement is received, it checks the application again". There are five steps: receipt, check, request for data, supplement, second check. An answer that stops at the supplement omits the last step, so it is incomplete.
:::

## Data, tables and graphs

:::definizione{#dati titolo="Labels and units"}
In a **table** each row corresponds to an item, for example a person, a team or a day, and each column to a feature of that item. The meaning of a number depends on three things: the **heading** of the column, the **unit of measurement** and the **notes** under the table. These three things are read before the numbers.

In a train timetable, for example, a column headed "Return" indicates the departure time of the return train, and not the arrival time at the destination, if the note says so.

A cell with a zero and an empty cell have different meanings. In a table of checks, "0 anomalies" means that the check was carried out and found no anomalies. The empty cell means that the check was not carried out.
:::

:::esempio{titolo="Applying two thresholds together"}
A team is admitted to the final if it scores **at least** $15$ points **and** takes **at most** $30$ minutes. "At least 15" includes 15, and "at most 30" includes 30. The two conditions must hold together.

| Team | Points | Minutes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Iris | 15 | 30 |
| Cedar | 16 | 31 |
| Pine | 14 | 27 |

- Iris has 15 points and 30 minutes. Both conditions are satisfied, so it is admitted.
- Cedar has 16 points and 31 minutes. The condition on points is satisfied, the one on time is not, because 31 exceeds 30. It is not admitted.
- Pine has 14 points and 27 minutes. The condition on points is not satisfied, because 14 is less than 15. It is not admitted, even though the time is within the limit.

Only Iris is admitted.
:::

:::osservazione
Two groups are compared in two ways: with **absolute numbers** or with **percentages**. The two comparisons sometimes give opposite results.

Class A has 18 passes out of 20 students. Class B has 24 passes out of 30 students.

- In absolute numbers class B has more passes: 24 against 18.
- As a percentage class A has more passes:
$$
\frac{18}{20} = 90\% , \qquad \frac{24}{30} = 80\% .
$$

The question indicates which comparison to use. "Which class has more passes" asks for absolute numbers. "In which class is the share of passes larger" asks for percentages.

The percentage for the two classes together is calculated on the totals, and not by adding $90\%$ and $80\%$ nor by averaging them:
$$
\frac{18 + 24}{20 + 30} = \frac{42}{50} = 84\% .
$$
:::

:::esempio{titolo="Reading a comparison between two costs"}
A transport service has two fares. The Free fare costs 2 euros per journey. The Plan fare costs a fixed 20 euros plus 1 euro per journey. With $n$ journeys the total cost $C$, in euros, is $L = 2n$ for the Free fare and $P = 20 + n$ for the Plan fare.

::grafico{f="2*x;20+x" xmin="0" xmax="40" ymin="0" ymax="85" nomi="n,C" punti="20,40" legenda="L=2n;P=20+n" titolo="At twenty journeys the lines intersect: both fares cost forty euros."}

The number of journeys is read on the horizontal axis, the cost on the vertical one. The two lines meet at the point with $n = 20$, where both fares cost 40 euros, because $2 \cdot 20 = 40$ and $20 + 20 = 40$.

- With fewer than 20 journeys the Free line is lower, so Free costs less. With 10 journeys Free costs 20 euros and Plan 30 euros.
- With more than 20 journeys the Plan line is lower, so Plan costs less. With 30 journeys Free costs 60 euros and Plan 50 euros.

The sentence "Plan costs less" is true only beyond 20 journeys. An answer that reports it without this condition is wrong.
:::

## Comparisons, causes and limits

:::definizione{#causalita titolo="Association and causal explanation"}
Two phenomena are **associated** when they occur together in the data: where there is more of one there is also more of the other. One of the two is the **cause** of the other when it produces it. An association has three possible explanations, so on its own it does not establish a cause.

- The first phenomenon causes the second.
- The second phenomenon causes the first.
- A third factor causes both.

> Among the users of an exercise app, those who turned on reminders completed more exercises. Each user chose freely whether to turn them on.

The two associated phenomena are turning on reminders and the number of exercises completed. One explanation is that reminders help to complete the exercises. Another is that the more motivated users turn on reminders and, being more motivated, also complete more exercises: in this case motivation is the third factor. The passage does not allow a choice between the two explanations.

To the question "what does the study show" the correct answer is "those who turned on reminders completed more exercises". The answer "reminders make users complete more exercises" states a cause that the passage does not establish.
:::

:::esempio{titolo="Changing one condition"}
The effect of a factor is established by comparing two groups that differ only in that factor.

Two groups of 20 seeds are grown in different conditions.

| Group | Light | Water | Seeds germinated |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | much | much | 18 |
| 2 | little | little | 9 |

More seeds germinate in group 1. The two groups differ in light and also in water, however, so the comparison does not say which of the two factors produced the difference.

To the question "which experiment is needed to study the effect of light" the answer is: two groups with the same amount of water and different amounts of light.

The answer is obtained from the structure of the comparison and requires no knowledge of botany.
:::

:::osservazione
A **mean** describes a group as a whole and not each of its members. When the mean decreases, the value of some members may increase.

Three users each waited 10 minutes at the counter, so the mean wait was 10 minutes. After a reorganisation they wait 2, 9 and 13 minutes, and the mean wait is
$$
\frac{2 + 9 + 13}{3} = \frac{24}{3} = 8 \ \text{minutes} .
$$
The mean wait has decreased by 2 minutes, and yet the third user waits 3 minutes longer.

The same holds for a total. One hundred street lamps of $50\,\mathrm{W}$ consume $5000\,\mathrm{W}$. If they are replaced by 125 lamps of $40\,\mathrm{W}$, each lamp consumes less and the total consumption is still $125 \cdot 40\,\mathrm{W} = 5000\,\mathrm{W}$.
:::

## Summaries, alternatives and checking answers

:::definizione{#sintesi titolo="Faithful paraphrases and summaries"}
A **paraphrase** repeats the content of a sentence in different words. A **summary** condenses a passage into a few words and keeps its thesis. Many correct answers are paraphrases or summaries of the passage.

A paraphrase or a summary is faithful if it keeps five elements of the passage: who performs the action, the quantity, the time, the conditions and the degree of certainty.

> The mobile library will be trialled for three months starting in spring; the final decision will depend on the number of visits and on costs.

- "The service will be tested for a quarter and then assessed on visits and costs" is a faithful summary, because it keeps the trial, the duration and the criteria.
- "The mobile library will become a permanent service" turns a trial into a decision already taken, so it changes the degree of certainty.
- "The mobile library will start in spring" reports a true fact and leaves out the trial and the criteria. To the question "what is the content of the passage" it is an incomplete answer.
:::

:::definizione{#distrattori titolo="Why an option fails to answer"}
The four wrong answers of a question are called **distractors**. They are built by starting from the passage and altering it, so they often contain its very words. There are six common alterations.

1. **Contradiction**. The answer says the opposite of the passage.
2. **Addition**. The answer contains a piece of information that the passage does not give.
3. **Generalisation**. The answer extends to all what the passage says of some.
4. **Reversal of a condition**. The answer swaps "only if" with "if".
5. **Swap of cause and effect**. The answer presents the consequence as the cause.
6. **Detail in place of the thesis**. The answer reports a true detail when the question asks for the main idea.

The same words in a different order give a different meaning: "the procedure reduces errors" and "errors reduce the procedure" use the same terms and state different things. An answer is therefore assessed by comparing its meaning with that of the passage, sentence by sentence.
:::

:::esempio{titolo="A complete check"}
> The ceramics workshop will start in June if the material arrives by May and if the room is available. The material arrived on 20 May.

The question is "What follows from the passage?"

The start depends on two conditions that must hold together. The first is satisfied, because the material arrived by May. On the second, the availability of the room, the passage says nothing. Three answers are assessed.

- "The workshop will start in June." It takes the second condition as satisfied too, so it is an addition.
- "The workshop has been cancelled." It treats the lack of information as a negative outcome, so it is an addition in the opposite direction.
- "For the start, confirmation that the room is available is still missing." It reports what the passage allows one to say. It is the correct answer.
:::
