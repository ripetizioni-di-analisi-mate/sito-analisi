## Sentences: subjects, verbs and complements

:::definizione{#struttura titolo="Word order and function"}
The ordinary order in an English statement is **subject, verb, complement**: “The students read the instructions.” The subject identifies who performs the action or whose state is described; the verb expresses the action or state; the complement completes the meaning. English normally requires an explicit subject: “It is raining,” with impersonal *it*, or “There are two chairs,” to introduce the existence of something.

With *be*, subject number determines the form: “The chair is free,” “The chairs are free.” Formal existential constructions therefore distinguish singular *there is* from plural *there are*. The word nearest the verb is not always the subject: in “The box of pencils is open,” the head of the subject is singular *box*.

Pronouns change according to function: *I, he, she, we, they* are subject forms; *me, him, her, us, them* are object forms. “They helped us” assigns different roles to the two groups. A possessive determiner precedes a noun, as in *their notes*; a possessive pronoun replaces it, as in *theirs*. A reflexive shows that subject and object coincide: “Leo cut himself.” *By himself* instead means without help or alone.
:::

:::osservazione{#ordine-parole titolo="Positions that change interpretation"}
Frequency adverbs often occur before the main verb, “Mia usually walks,” and after *be*, “Mia is usually early.” An adjective describes a noun or follows a verb such as *be*; an adverb describes how an action occurs: “a careful driver,” “She drives carefully.” Not every word ending in *-ly* is an adverb, and some adverbs, such as *fast*, match the adjective. Sentence function comes before word ending.
:::

## Present forms, questions and negatives

:::definizione{#presente titolo="Habits, ongoing actions and states"}
The **present simple** describes habits, general facts and situations viewed as stable. With *he, she, it*, the verb normally takes *-s*: “She works here.” *Go* becomes *goes*; a consonant followed by *y* often gives *-ies*, as in *studies*. The **present continuous** combines *am/is/are* with the *-ing* form: “They are studying now.” It presents the activity as ongoing or temporary.

Context allows a comparison between “I work in Rome” and “I am working in Rome this month.” The second sentence emphasizes the temporary nature of the assignment. An expression such as *now* helps, but does not make every verb progressive. State verbs such as *know*, in the sense of possessing knowledge, normally use the simple form: “I know the answer.” Some verbs change construction when their meaning changes: *think* can express an opinion or an activity of reflection.
:::

:::definizione{#domande titolo="Auxiliaries and the base form"}
With ordinary verbs, present simple questions require *do/does*: “Do they study?”, “Does she study?” Negatives use *do not/does not*. After the auxiliary, the verb remains in the base form: “She doesn't study,” without a second third-person ending. With *be*, inversion is direct: “Is she ready?” *Do* is not added to this construction.

Question words specify the information required: *who* for a person, *where* for a place, *when* for a time, *why* for a reason, *whose* for the possessor. When *who* is the subject, the ordinary auxiliary is unnecessary: “Who called you?” When asking about the object, the auxiliary reappears: “Who did you call?” The two sentences give *you* opposite roles.
:::

:::osservazione{#risposte-brevi titolo="Short answers and agreement"}
An auxiliary can also appear in a short answer. The question “Does Nora work here?” is answered “Yes, she does” or “No, she doesn't.” The main verb need not be repeated, but subject and auxiliary must remain consistent with the question. With “Are they ready?”, the answers become “Yes, they are” and “No, they aren't.” This comparison distinguishes the role of *be* from that of ordinary verbs and shows why an answer cannot be built by translating only “yes” or “no.”
:::

:::osservazione{#contrazioni titolo="Reconstructing a contracted form"}
Contractions preserve grammatical structure: *doesn't* equals *does not*, and *isn't* equals *is not*. The form *'s*, however, can represent either *is* or *has*: in “She's waiting” it means *she is*, whereas in “She's finished the work” it means *she has*. The following word helps reconstruct the auxiliary. A participle can also function as an adjective, so the complete context must be read before automatically assigning a tense to a contraction.
:::

:::osservazione{#imperativo titolo="Affirmative and negative instructions"}
In an imperative, the addressee subject normally remains implicit: “Read the label before opening the package.” The verb is in the base form; *please* makes the request polite without introducing *to*. Negation uses *don't + base form*: “Don't open the package yet.” Its instructional force distinguishes this use from the statement “You read the label,” which may describe behavior rather than request it.
:::

## Past events, experience and duration

:::definizione{#passato titolo="Completed events and background"}
The **past simple** locates an event in a finished past time: “We visited the museum yesterday.” Regular verbs end in *-ed*; irregular verbs have their own forms, such as *take/took* and *give/gave*. Questions and negatives use *did* with the base form: “Did you leave?”, “We didn't leave.” Writing *did left* would mark the past twice.

The **past continuous**, *was/were + -ing*, describes an activity in progress at a past reference point: “I was reading when the phone rang.” Reading provides the background; the ring is the event occurring within it. The **past perfect**, *had + past participle*, places an event before another past moment: “The train had left when we arrived.” Verb order within the sentence does not necessarily match event order.

*Used to + base form* describes past habits or states that no longer apply: “We used to live near the station, but now we live elsewhere.” It must not be confused with *be used to + noun/-ing*, meaning to be accustomed to something. *Ago* follows a duration measured backwards from the present: “two weeks ago.”
:::

:::definizione{#perfect titolo="Connections with the present"}
The **present perfect** uses *have/has + past participle*: “I have lost my key.” The focus may be on a present consequence, experience up to today, or the duration of a situation that still holds. An event explicitly located in *yesterday* normally requires the past simple. *Been to* can describe a completed visit followed by a return; *gone to* often indicates that the person is still away.

*Since* introduces the starting point, “since April”; *for* introduces duration, “for three months.” *Ever* asks whether an experience has occurred; *never* denies it. *Yet* often occurs at the end of questions and negatives, while *already* signals that something has already happened. The **present perfect continuous**, *have/has been + -ing*, focuses on activity duration: “I have been waiting for an hour.” In several contexts, simple and progressive forms are both possible with different shades of meaning; no single word automatically determines the choice.
:::

## Future forms, modals and conditions

:::definizione{#futuro-modali titolo="Intention, possibility and obligation"}
*Will + base form* can express predictions, promises and decisions made at the moment of speaking: “I'll carry that bag for you.” *Be going to* can indicate an already formed intention or a prediction based on present evidence. The present continuous can also describe an already organized future arrangement. These forms overlap: a precise context is needed to distinguish the intended meaning.

The modals *can, could, may, might, must, should, would* are followed by the base form without *to* and do not take third-person *-s*. *Can* expresses ability or permission; *could* can express past ability or make a request polite. *Should* often introduces advice. *Must* can indicate obligation or a strong deduction: “The lights are on; someone must be inside.”

Negation distinguishes different meanings. *Mustn't* expresses prohibition; *don't have to* expresses lack of obligation. “You don't have to print it” allows someone to avoid printing, while “You mustn't print it” forbids printing. *Can't* can express impossibility or prohibition depending on context. A correct answer must respect both form and this difference in meaning.
:::

:::definizione{#condizionali titolo="Real and hypothetical conditions"}
A common **first conditional** pattern is *if + present simple*, followed by *will + base form*: “If it rains tomorrow, we will stay indoors.” The subordinate clause expresses the condition; the main clause expresses the predicted consequence. The **second conditional** typically uses *if + past simple* and *would + base form*: “If I had a car, I would drive.” Here the past expresses distance from reality, not necessarily a past event.

*Unless* means *if not*: “You cannot enter unless you have a pass” requires a pass for entry. *Provided that* introduces an explicit condition. Recognizing *if* alone is insufficient: also check whether the sentence concerns a general fact, a future possibility or an imagined situation.
:::

## Nouns, articles and quantities

:::definizione{#nomi-articoli titolo="Countability and identification"}
A countable noun can identify separate units, such as *book/books*. Nouns such as *information, advice, luggage* are normally uncountable: use *some information*, or *a piece of information* to identify one unit. The indefinite article *a/an* introduces a singular countable referent not yet identified. The choice depends on sound: *an hour*, with silent *h*, but *a university*, beginning with the consonant sound of *you*.

*The* identifies a recognizable referent, for example one already mentioned: “I bought a book. The book is on my desk.” A general plural may have no article: “Books can be expensive.” Saying *the books* instead restricts the reference to an identified set. A singular profession normally uses the indefinite article: “She is an engineer.”
:::

:::definizione{#quantita titolo="Amounts, numbers and sufficiency"}
*Many* and *a few* accompany plural countable nouns; *much* and *a little* accompany uncountable nouns. *Few* and *little*, without the article, often emphasize scarcity; *a few* and *a little* emphasize a small amount that is nevertheless available. “We have few chairs” highlights possible insufficiency, while “We have a few chairs” presents limited availability.

*Some* is common in affirmative sentences and offers, such as “Would you like some tea?” *Any* is common in questions and negatives, but can also mean whichever in an affirmative sentence. *Enough* precedes a noun, “enough time,” and follows an adjective, “warm enough.” *Neither* excludes both members of a pair; *none* indicates zero members of a set. *Another* means one additional singular item; *other* often precedes a plural, while *the other* can identify the second of two items.
:::

## Comparisons, prepositions and connections

:::definizione{#confronti titolo="Degree and comparison"}
The comparative compares two terms: *tall/taller*, *easy/easier*, *interesting/more interesting*. The superlative identifies an extreme within a group: *the tallest*, *the most interesting*. *Good* has the irregular forms *better* and *best*. The construction *as + adjective + as* expresses equality; *not as ... as* denies equality in the indicated direction.

For countable quantities, *fewer seats* indicates a smaller number; for uncountable quantities, *less water* indicates a smaller amount. *Too + adjective + to* expresses a degree that prevents the result: “The box is too heavy to lift.” The person can be specified with *for*: “too heavy for me to lift.” *Enough* instead expresses a reached threshold: “light enough to carry.”
:::

:::definizione{#preposizioni titolo="Time, place and lexical combinations"}
Time expressions normally use *at eight*, *on Monday*, *in July*. *By Friday* sets a deadline for completing something; *until Friday* indicates continuation up to that limit. “Send it by Friday” concerns the sending deadline; “Keep it until Friday” concerns storage duration. *During* precedes a noun, *while* a clause: “during the lesson,” “while we were studying.”

Prepositions often belong to combinations to learn together: *interested in*, *good at*, *depend on*, *different from*. In movement, *across* describes crossing; *towards* indicates direction without guaranteeing arrival. In the notice “The bus stops here on request,” *on request* indicates that the stop must be requested. Translating an Italian preposition with the same English word every time does not preserve these relationships.
:::

## Clauses, the passive and reported speech

:::definizione{#relative titolo="Linking a noun to a description"}
A relative clause specifies or describes a noun. *Who* refers to people; *which* to things; *that* can appear in restrictive relative clauses of both kinds. *Whose* expresses possession: “the student whose notebook is missing.” *Where* introduces a location relationship: “the school where I studied.” The choice depends on the gap's function: *whose* is followed by the possessed noun, whereas a subject pronoun is followed by the relative clause's verb.

In the **passive**, the subject receives the action: “The windows were cleaned yesterday.” The structure is a form of *be* followed by the past participle; number and tense are expressed in the auxiliary. The agent may be introduced with *by*. A participle alone is insufficient to form the sentence's finite verb.
:::

:::definizione{#discorso-indiretto titolo="Reporting words and questions"}
Reported speech adapts pronouns, time references and, when context requires it, verb tenses. Reporting “I am tired” from a past viewpoint can give “She said that she was tired.” Tense change is not compulsory in every situation: a still-valid truth may retain the present. An exercise must specify the necessary temporal reference.

Indirect questions use statement order: “Could you tell me where the station is?” The subordinate clause does not repeat the inversion in “Where is the station?” A yes/no question can use *if* or *whether*: “I asked whether it was open.” A request can become *ask + person + to + verb*: “She asked us to wait.” *Say* and *tell* have different constructions: *tell someone something*, but *say something to someone*.
:::

## Verbs, connectors and vocabulary in context

:::definizione{#costruzioni-verbali titolo="The form following the verb"}
Some verbs take a *to*-infinitive, such as *decide to leave* and *manage to finish*; others take an *-ing* form, such as *enjoy reading* and *suggest waiting*. The pattern belongs to the verb, so a word-for-word translation may produce an unnatural form. *Stop doing* means interrupting the activity; *stop to do* means stopping in order to perform another one. In “We stopped walking to drink,” walking is interrupted and drinking is the purpose.

Adjectives in *-ed* often describe whoever experiences an emotion; those in *-ing* describe what causes it: “The lecture was interesting; I was interested.” Word families change category: *success* is a noun, *successful* an adjective, *successfully* an adverb. The position to be completed determines the category required.
:::

:::osservazione{#connettivi-lessico titolo="Relationships and meanings"}
*Because* introduces a cause, *so* a consequence, *but* a contrast. *Although* takes a clause; *despite* takes a noun or an *-ing* form. “Although it rained” and “despite the rain” have different structures. Purpose can be expressed by *in order to + verb*. *However* often connects two sentences by contrasting the second with the first.

In vocabulary, *library* means Italian *biblioteca*, while *bookshop* means a commercial book store; *eventually* means in the end, not possibly. *Borrow* concerns the temporary recipient; *lend* concerns the provider. Among phrasal verbs, *hand in* means submit, *put off* means postpone, and *look into* means investigate. These meanings must be recognized in the whole situation: investigating a problem does not guarantee solving it.
:::

## Reading messages and instructions

:::osservazione{#lettura titolo="Stated information and its limits"}
A short notice may contain a rule, an exception and a required action. “Visitors may enter after registering” permits entry after registration: it does not say that all visitors are already registered. *Only* restricts the allowed category; *still* signals continuity; *instead* introduces a replacement. Update messages require a distinction between what changes and what is explicitly confirmed as unchanged.

Pronouns and substitute words refer back to earlier items. In “Leave the small bottles here; take the large ones,” *ones* refers back to *bottles*, while *large* selects a subgroup. Two instructions may have different destinations: assigning one object the place intended for the other changes the message even if all the words occur in the text.

For a time, distinguish the event from the required early arrival. “The train leaves at 8\:15; arrive ten minutes early” indicates 8\:05. *Half past two* means 2\:30 and *a quarter to three* means 2\:45. A possible statement is not automatically information contained in the text: if a seminar moves online, a change of teacher, date or topic cannot be inferred without further information.
:::

:::osservazione{#condizioni-avvisi titolo="Conditions and obligations in notices"}
A notice can also connect several conditions. “Bring a printed copy only if the upload fails” limits the copy requirement to upload failure. If uploading succeeds, the message does not require a copy. This reading preserves the force of *only if* without turning it into a stronger claim absent from the text, such as printing always being forbidden. Similarly, *may* expresses permission or possibility, while *must* presents an obligation or deduction.
:::

:::osservazione{#sequenze titolo="Tracking objects and operations"}
When understanding a sequence, connectors establish operation order. “Weigh the empty container, then add the powder and weigh it again” requires two different weighings. The pronoun *it* refers back to the container, but its contents have changed at the second weighing. Finding the repeated verb is insufficient: each action must be associated with its object and the moment when it occurs. These links explain why an answer can contain words present in the passage yet still describe an incorrect instruction.
:::
