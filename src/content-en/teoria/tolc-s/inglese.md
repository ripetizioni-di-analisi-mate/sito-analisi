## Sentences: subjects, verbs and complements

:::definizione{#struttura titolo="Word order and function"}
In an English statement the parts stand in a fixed order: **subject, verb, complement**. The subject is the person or thing that performs the action, the verb expresses the action and the complement completes its meaning. In “The students read the instructions” the three parts are *the students*, *read* and *the instructions*.

Italian leaves the subject out when the verb ending identifies it: in “Leggo” the ending *-o* stands for “io” (I). An English verb has almost the same form for every person, so the subject is stated even where Italian leaves it out: “I read”. When the action has no author, the subject is *it*: “It is raining”. The existence of something is expressed by *there is* before a singular noun and by *there are* before a plural noun: “There are two chairs”.

In the present the verb *be* has three forms: *am* with *I*, *is* with a singular subject, *are* with *you* and with a plural subject. The form depends on the main noun of the subject. In “The box of pencils is open” the main noun is *box*, which is singular, so the verb is *is*, even though the word nearest the verb is the plural *pencils*.

A **pronoun** is a word that replaces a noun. English personal pronouns change form according to their function.

| Subject | Object | Possessive determiner | Possessive pronoun | Reflexive |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| I | me | my | mine | myself |
| you | you | your | yours | yourself |
| he | him | his | his | himself |
| she | her | her | hers | herself |
| it | it | its | | itself |
| we | us | our | ours | ourselves |
| they | them | their | theirs | themselves |

*It* refers to a thing or an animal; its possessive pronoun is not in use.

- In “They helped us”, *they* is the subject and *us* is the object, that is, the people who receive the help.
- A **possessive determiner** stands before a noun, “their notes”. A **possessive pronoun** replaces the determiner and the noun: “These notes are theirs”.
- A **reflexive pronoun** is used when the subject and the object are the same person: “Leo cut himself”. *By himself* means “alone, without help”.
- Possession referring to a noun is expressed by adding *'s* to the owner: “Anna's bag”.
:::

:::osservazione{#ordine-parole titolo="Positions that change interpretation"}
An **adjective** describes a noun and stands before the noun or after the verb *be*: “a careful driver”. An **adverb** describes a verb, that is, it says how the action takes place: “She drives carefully”. Many adverbs are formed by adding *-ly* to the adjective, as *carefully* from *careful*.

Some words ending in *-ly* are adjectives, such as *friendly*. *Fast* is both an adjective and an adverb, with the same form: “a fast car”, “She runs fast”. The category follows from the word described: a noun requires an adjective, a verb requires an adverb.

**Frequency adverbs** say how often an action takes place: *always*, *usually*, *never*. They stand before the main verb, “Mia usually walks”, and after the verb *be*, “Mia is usually early”.
:::

## Present forms, questions and negatives

:::definizione{#presente titolo="Habits, ongoing actions and states"}
The **present simple** is the present tense of habits and of facts that are always true. It is formed with the **base form** of the verb, that is, the form listed in the dictionary, such as *work*: “I work here”. With *he*, *she*, *it*, that is, in the third person singular, the verb takes *-s*: “She works here”. The spelling changes in two cases: *go* becomes *goes*, and a verb ending in a consonant followed by *y* takes *-ies*, as *study* becomes *studies*.

The **present continuous** is the present tense of actions in progress at the moment of speaking and of temporary situations. It is formed with *am*, *is* or *are* followed by the verb with the ending *-ing*: “They are studying now”.

- “I work in Rome” describes a stable situation.
- “I am working in Rome this month” describes a temporary assignment.

**State verbs** describe a condition and not an action, such as *know*, *like*, *believe* and *understand*. They are used in the present simple even when they refer to the moment of speaking: “I know the answer now”. *Think* has two meanings: in the present simple it expresses an opinion, “I think it is right”, and in the present continuous a process of reasoning in progress, “I am thinking about it”.
:::

:::definizione{#domande titolo="Auxiliaries and the base form"}
An **auxiliary** is a verb that accompanies the main verb to form questions, negatives and compound tenses, that is, tenses formed by two words such as *has finished*. In the present simple the auxiliary is *do*, which becomes *does* with *he*, *she*, *it*.

| Sentence | With *they* | With *she* |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Statement | They study. | She studies. |
| Question | Do they study? | Does she study? |
| Negative | They do not study. | She does not study. |

In a question the auxiliary comes before the subject; in a negative it is followed by *not*. The third-person *-s* appears only once, in *does*, so the main verb stays in the base form *study*.

The verb *be* forms questions and negatives without *do*. In a question it moves before the subject, “Is she ready?”, and in a negative it is followed by *not*, “She is not ready”.

**Question words** stand at the beginning of the question and identify the information required: *who* for a person, *what* for a thing, *which* for a choice, *where* for a place, *when* for a time, *why* for a reason, *how* for a manner, *whose* for the owner. They are followed by the auxiliary and the subject: “Where do they study?”. With *who*, the construction depends on the role of the person asked about.

- “Who called you?” asks about the subject, that is, the person who made the call. *Who* occupies the position of the subject and there is no auxiliary.
- “Who did you call?” asks about the object, that is, the person who received the call. The subject is *you*, so the auxiliary *did*, the past of *do*, appears.
:::

:::attenzione
“Does she studies?” is wrong, because the third-person *-s* is already in *does*. The correct form is “Does she study?”.
:::

:::osservazione{#risposte-brevi titolo="Short answers and agreement"}
A **short answer** consists of *yes* or *no*, the subject pronoun and the auxiliary of the question. The main verb is not repeated.

- The question “Does Nora work here?” is answered “Yes, she does” or “No, she doesn't”, where *doesn't* is the short form of *does not*.
- The question “Are they ready?” is answered “Yes, they are” or “No, they aren't”.
:::

:::osservazione{#contrazioni titolo="Reconstructing a contracted form"}
A **contraction** is the shortened form of two words, written with an apostrophe in place of the missing letters: *doesn't* equals *does not*, *isn't* equals *is not*, *can't* equals *cannot*, *mustn't* equals *must not* and *I'll* equals *I will*.

The contraction *'s* equals *is* or *has*, according to the word that follows.

- In “She's waiting” an *-ing* form follows, so *'s* equals *is*.
- In “She's finished the work” the participle *finished* follows with its object *the work*, so *'s* equals *has*. The participle is the verb form used in compound tenses.
- In “She's tired”, *tired* is an adjective, so *'s* equals *is*.
:::

:::osservazione{#imperativo titolo="Affirmative and negative instructions"}
The **imperative** is the verb form that gives an instruction. In English it is the base form without a subject: “Read the label before opening the package”. The negative is formed with *don't* followed by the base form: “Don't open the package yet”. *Please* makes the request polite and leaves the verb in the base form: “Please wait here”.

With a subject the sentence becomes a description: “You read the label” says what the person does, whereas “Read the label” asks the person to do it.
:::

## Past events, experience and duration

:::definizione{#passato titolo="Completed events and background"}
The **past simple** is the tense of events completed in the past: “We visited the museum yesterday”. Regular verbs add *-ed* to the base form, as in *visited*. Irregular verbs have their own form, which is learnt by heart: *take* becomes *took*, *leave* becomes *left*, *go* becomes *went*. The past simple of *be* is *was* in the singular and *were* in the plural and with *you*.

Questions and negatives use the auxiliary *did* followed by the base form: “Did you leave?”, “We didn't leave”. The past is already expressed by *did*, so the main verb stays in the base form *leave*.

The **past continuous** describes an action that was in progress at a moment in the past. It is formed with *was* or *were* followed by the *-ing* form. In “I was reading when the phone rang” the reading was in progress and the ring, in the past simple, is the short event that happens in the meantime.

The **past perfect** indicates an event that happened before another past event. It is formed with *had* followed by the **past participle**, which in regular verbs is the same as the past simple and in irregular verbs is the third form in verb lists: *take, took, taken*; *go, went, gone*; *be, was/were, been*. In “The train had left when we arrived” the departure happens before the arrival. The order of events is shown by the tense, so “When we arrived, the train had left” also places the departure before the arrival.

*Used to* followed by the base form describes a past habit or situation that has now ceased: “We used to live near the station”. *Ago* follows a period of time and counts it backwards from today: “two weeks ago”.
:::

:::attenzione
“Did you left?” is wrong, because the past is already expressed by *did*. The correct form is “Did you leave?”.

“I used to live alone” means that the speaker lived alone in the past and no longer does. “I am used to living alone” means that the speaker is accustomed to living alone. This second construction contains *be* and is followed by a noun or an *-ing* form.
:::

:::definizione{#perfect titolo="Connections with the present"}
The **present perfect** connects a past fact with the present. It is formed with *have*, or *has* with *he*, *she*, *it*, followed by the past participle. It has three uses.

- A past fact with a present consequence: “I have lost my key” communicates that the speaker has no key now.
- An experience at an unspecified time: “I have visited London twice”.
- A situation that began in the past and still continues: “She has lived here for three years”.

Italian uses the *passato prossimo* in “Ieri ho perso la chiave” as well. In English, when the sentence states a finished time, such as *yesterday* or *last week*, the tense is the past simple: “I lost my key yesterday”.

Three pairs of words accompany the present perfect.

- *Since* introduces the starting point and *for* the duration: “since April”, “for three months”.
- *Ever* is used in questions and *never* has a negative meaning: “Have you ever visited Paris?”, “I have never visited Paris”.
- *Already* is used in affirmative sentences and *yet* at the end of negatives and questions: “She has already finished”, “She hasn't finished yet”, “Has she finished yet?”.

“He has been to Paris” says that he went and came back. “He has gone to Paris” says that he is still there.

The **present perfect continuous** highlights the duration of an activity that began in the past and is still in progress. It is formed with *have been* or *has been* followed by the *-ing* form: “I have been waiting for an hour”.
:::

:::attenzione
“I have seen him yesterday” is wrong, because *yesterday* states a finished time. The correct form is “I saw him yesterday”.
:::

## Future forms, modals and conditions

:::definizione{#futuro-modali titolo="Intention, possibility and obligation"}
The English verb has no ending for the future, which is expressed by three constructions.

- *Will* followed by the base form expresses a prediction, a promise or a decision made at the moment of speaking: “I'll carry that bag for you”.
- *Be going to* followed by the base form expresses an intention already formed or a prediction based on what can be seen: “I am going to study tonight”, “It is going to rain”.
- The present continuous expresses an arrangement already fixed: “We are meeting the teacher at five”.

**Modal verbs** are auxiliaries that add to the main verb an idea of ability, permission, obligation or probability. They are followed by the base form without *to*, take no *-s* in the third person and form questions and negatives without *do*: “She can swim”, “Can she swim?”, “She cannot swim”.

- *Can* expresses ability or permission; *could* a past ability or a polite request: “Could you open the window?”.
- *May* expresses permission and, like *might*, a possibility: “It might rain”.
- *Should* expresses advice and *must* an obligation: “You should rest”, “You must wear a helmet”.
- *Would* expresses a hypothesis or a polite request: “I would like some tea”.

*Can't* expresses inability or prohibition: “I can't read this notice”, “You can't park here”.

*Must* also expresses a deduction of which the speaker is certain: “The lights are on; someone must be inside”.

Obligation is also expressed by *have to*: “I have to go”. In the negative the two verbs have different meanings.

- “You mustn't print it” expresses a prohibition.
- “You don't have to print it” says that there is no obligation. Printing is allowed, without being required.
:::

:::definizione{#condizionali titolo="Real and hypothetical conditions"}
A **conditional** sentence consists of two clauses, that is, two parts of the sentence each with its own verb. The clause introduced by *if* expresses the condition; the other, called the main clause, expresses the consequence.

- The **first conditional** concerns a condition that is possible in the future. It has the present simple after *if* and *will* in the main clause: “If it rains tomorrow, we will stay indoors”.
- The **second conditional** concerns an imagined situation. It has the past simple after *if* and *would* in the main clause: “If I had a car, I would drive”. The speaker has no car. Here the past simple *had* corresponds to the Italian subjunctive “avessi” and refers to the present.

*Unless* means “if not”: “You cannot enter unless you have a pass”. *Provided that* means “on condition that”: “You can borrow the book provided that you return it by Friday”.
:::

:::attenzione
“If it will rain, we will stay indoors” is wrong, because after *if* the future is expressed by the present simple. The correct form is “If it rains, we will stay indoors”.
:::

## Nouns, articles and quantities

:::definizione{#nomi-articoli titolo="Countability and identification"}
A noun is **countable** if it refers to things that are counted one by one; it has a singular and a plural, such as *a book*, *two books*. The plural is formed with *-s*, but some nouns are irregular: *child* becomes *children* and *person* becomes *people*.

A noun is **uncountable** if it refers to a substance or a whole that English does not divide into units: *water*, *information*, *advice*, *luggage*. An uncountable noun has no plural, does not take *a/an* and requires a singular verb, even where Italian uses a plural: “The information is correct” (Italian “Le informazioni sono corrette”). A single unit is expressed by *a piece of*: “a piece of advice”.

The **indefinite article** is *a* or *an*. It accompanies a singular countable noun mentioned for the first time. *A* is used before a consonant sound and *an* before a vowel sound, according to the pronunciation of the word.

- In *an hour* the *h* is silent, so the first sound is a vowel.
- In *a university* the *u* is pronounced like *you*, which begins with a consonant sound.

The **definite article** is *the*. It accompanies something already known to the listener, for example because it has just been mentioned: “I bought a book. The book is on my desk”.

For a category taken in general English omits the article, whereas Italian uses it: “Books are expensive”. “The books are expensive” refers instead to particular books. Before the name of a profession English uses *a/an*: “She is an engineer”.
:::

:::attenzione
“Informations” and “an advice” are wrong forms, because *information* and *advice* are uncountable. The correct forms are “some information” and “a piece of advice”.
:::

:::definizione{#quantita titolo="Amounts, numbers and sufficiency"}
Words that express a quantity are chosen according to the type of noun.

| Meaning | With plural countable nouns | With uncountable nouns |
| --- | --- | --- |
| a large quantity | many books | much time |
| a small quantity, available | a few chairs | a little water |
| a small quantity, insufficient | few chairs | little water |

With the article *a* the quantity is presented as small but available: “We have a few chairs”. Without the article it is presented as insufficient: “We have few chairs”.

A question about quantity begins with *how many* before a plural countable noun and with *how much* before an uncountable noun: “How many students are there?”, “How much time do we have?”.

*Some* and *any* express an unspecified quantity.

- *Some* is used in affirmative sentences and in offers: “Would you like some tea?”.
- *Any* is used in questions and negatives: “There isn't any milk”.
- In an affirmative sentence *any* means “whichever”: “Take any seat”.

*Enough* comes before a noun and after an adjective: “enough time”, “warm enough”.

- *Both* is followed by a plural noun: “Both answers are correct”.
- *Neither* excludes both members of a pair and is followed by a singular noun: “Neither answer is correct”.
- *None* indicates zero members of a group: “None of the answers is correct”.
- *Another* comes before a singular noun and means “one more”: “another exercise”. *Other* comes before a plural noun: “other questions”. *The other* identifies the second of two items.
:::

## Comparisons, prepositions and connections

:::definizione{#confronti titolo="Degree and comparison"}
The **comparative** compares two items. The **superlative** identifies the item that exceeds all the others in a group. The form depends on the length of the adjective.

| Adjective | Comparative | Superlative |
| --- | --- | --- |
| short, such as *tall* | taller | the tallest |
| ending in a consonant and *y*, such as *easy* | easier | the easiest |
| long, such as *interesting* | more interesting | the most interesting |
| irregular, *good* | better | the best |

The second term of the comparison is introduced by *than*: “Anna is taller than Leo”.

*As* + adjective + *as* expresses equality: “Anna is as tall as Mia”. The negative form says that the first item has the quality to a lower degree: “Leo is not as tall as Mia” means that Leo is shorter than Mia.

A smaller quantity is expressed by *fewer* before a plural countable noun and by *less* before an uncountable noun: “fewer seats”, “less water”.

- *Too* + adjective + *to* + base form expresses an excessive degree, which prevents the action: “The box is too heavy to lift”. The person is added with *for*: “too heavy for me to lift”.
- Adjective + *enough* + *to* + base form expresses a sufficient degree, which allows the action: “The box is light enough to carry”.
:::

:::attenzione
“Anna is more taller than Leo” forms the comparative twice, with *more* and with *-er*. “Anna is taller of Leo” has *of* in place of *than*. The correct form is “Anna is taller than Leo”.
:::

:::definizione{#preposizioni titolo="Time, place and lexical combinations"}
A **preposition** is a short word that links a noun to the rest of the sentence and expresses a relationship of time or place, such as *at*, *on*, *in*. In time expressions *at* is used with a clock time, *on* with a day and *in* with a month or a year: “at eight”, “on Monday”, “in July”.

*By* and *until* both express a time limit.

- “Send it by Friday” concerns an action that takes place on Friday or earlier.
- “Keep it until Friday” concerns an action that continues and ends on Friday.

*During* is followed by a noun: “during the lesson”. *While* is followed by a clause with a subject and a verb: “while we were studying”.

In expressions of movement *across* means “from one side to the other” and *towards* means “in the direction of”. “She walked towards the station” gives the direction, without specifying whether the person arrived.

Many adjectives and verbs are followed by a fixed preposition, which is learnt together with the word.

| English | Italian |
| --- | --- |
| interested in | interessato a |
| good at | bravo in |
| depend on | dipendere da |
| different from | diverso da |

In the first three rows the English preposition differs from the literal translation of the Italian one.

In the notice “The bus stops here on request” the expression *on request* means “when asked”, that is, the bus stops only when a passenger asks for the stop.
:::

## Clauses, the passive and reported speech

:::definizione{#relative titolo="Linking a noun to a description"}
A **relative clause** follows a noun and specifies which person or thing is meant. It is introduced by a relative pronoun, which corresponds to the Italian “che”, “il cui”, “dove”.

- *Who* refers to people: “the teacher who explains physics”.
- *Which* refers to things and *that* to people and things: “the book which is on the desk”, “the book that I bought”.
- *Whose* indicates the owner: “the student whose notebook is missing”.
- *Where* indicates a place: “the school where I studied”.

*Whose* is always followed by a noun, that is, the thing owned, such as *notebook*. *Who* as a subject is followed directly by the verb, such as *explains*.

In the **passive** the subject of the sentence is whoever receives the action. It is formed with *be* followed by the past participle: “The windows were cleaned yesterday”. Tense and number are expressed by *be*: *were* is past and plural, because the action took place yesterday and *windows* is plural. In the present *be* is *is* or *are*: “Card payments are accepted”. Whoever performs the action is called the **agent** and is added with *by*: “The windows were cleaned by the caretaker”.
:::

:::definizione{#discorso-indiretto titolo="Reporting words and questions"}
**Reported speech** conveys another person's words without quoting them. It is introduced by a verb such as *say* followed by *that*.

Anna says: “I am tired”. Someone who reports her words later says: “She said that she was tired”.

- The pronoun *I* becomes *she*, because the person reporting is talking about Anna.
- The verb moves to a past tense, because *said* is in the past: *am* becomes *was*. In the same way *will* becomes *would* and *can* becomes *could*.

When the fact reported is still true, the present is also allowed: “She said that Rome is in Italy”.

An **indirect question** is a question placed inside another sentence. It has the word order of a statement, that is, subject and then verb, without the auxiliary *do*.

- “Where is the station?” becomes “Could you tell me where the station is?”.
- “Where does she live?” becomes “I don't know where she lives”.
- A yes/no question is introduced by *if* or *whether*: “Is it open?” becomes “I asked whether it was open”.

A request is reported with *ask*, the person, *to* and the base form: “Please wait here” becomes “She asked us to wait here”.

*Say* and *tell* have similar meanings. *Tell* is followed by the person: “She told me that she was tired”. *Say* is followed directly by the thing said and the person, when expressed, is added with *to*: “She said goodbye to me”.
:::

:::attenzione
“Could you tell me where is the station?” is wrong, because an indirect question has the subject before the verb. The correct form is “Could you tell me where the station is?”.
:::

## Verbs, connectors and vocabulary in context

:::definizione{#costruzioni-verbali titolo="The form following the verb"}
When a non-modal verb is followed by a second verb, the second takes one of two forms: the infinitive with *to*, such as *to leave*, or the *-ing* form, such as *leaving*. The form depends on the first verb and is learnt together with it, because Italian uses the infinitive in both cases.

- *Decide* and *manage* are followed by *to* and the base form: “decide to leave”, “manage to finish”.
- *Enjoy*, *suggest* and *avoid* are followed by the *-ing* form: “enjoy reading”, “suggest waiting”.

The verb *stop* takes both forms, with different meanings.

- In “We stopped walking” the *-ing* form indicates the activity that is interrupted.
- In “We stopped to drink” the infinitive with *to* indicates the purpose of the stop.
- In “We stopped walking to drink some water” both forms appear: *walking* is the activity interrupted and *to drink* the purpose.

Many verbs give rise to two adjectives. The one in *-ed* describes the person who experiences a feeling; the one in *-ing* describes the thing that causes it: “The lecture was interesting”, so “I was interested”. In the same way *boring* describes what causes boredom and *bored* the person who feels it.

A **word family** is the set of words that derive from the same root and belong to different categories: *succeed* is a verb, *success* is a noun, *successful* is an adjective, *successfully* is an adverb. In a gap-fill question the category required follows from the position of the gap.

- In “a ___ student” the gap comes before a noun, so it requires the adjective *successful*.
- In “She passed the test ___” the gap describes how the action takes place, so it requires the adverb *successfully*.
- In “The project was a great ___” the gap follows the article *a* and the adjective *great*, so it requires the noun *success*.
:::

:::osservazione{#connettivi-lessico titolo="Relationships and meanings"}
A **connector** is a word that links two sentences and expresses the relationship between them.

- *Because* introduces a cause: “We stayed in because it was raining”.
- *So* introduces a consequence: “It was raining, so we stayed in”.
- *But* introduces a contrast: “It was raining, but we went out”. *However* has the same value and begins a new sentence.
- *In order to* introduces a purpose and is followed by the base form: “We left early in order to catch the train”.

*Although* and *despite* introduce a fact that does not prevent the action. *Although* is followed by a clause, like *even though*, which has the same meaning: “Although it was raining, we went out”. *Despite* is followed by a noun or an *-ing* form: “Despite the rain, we went out”.

**False friends** are English words that resemble an Italian word and have a different meaning. *Library* means Italian “biblioteca”, whereas Italian “libreria” is *bookshop*. *Eventually* means “in the end”, whereas Italian “eventualmente” is *possibly*.

*Borrow* and *lend* describe the same loan from two points of view. *Borrow* means “take on loan”: “I borrowed a pen from Anna”. *Lend* means “give on loan”: “Anna lent me a pen”.

A **phrasal verb** is a verb followed by a short word, a preposition or an adverb, with which it forms a unit whose meaning differs from that of the verb alone.

- *Hand in* means “submit”: “Hand in your essay by Friday”.
- *Put off* means “postpone”: “They put off the meeting until Friday”.
- *Look into* means “examine, investigate”: “We will look into the problem”. With this sentence the speaker undertakes to examine the problem and does not state that it will be solved.
:::

## Reading messages and instructions

:::osservazione{#lettura titolo="Stated information and its limits"}
Some questions present a short message, such as a notice or an instruction. The correct answer reports what the message states, without additions.

In the notice “Visitors may enter after registering”, *may* expresses permission and *after registering* gives the order: registration first, then entry. The notice does not say how many visitors have already registered, so the answer “All visitors are registered” adds information that is absent.

Three short words change the meaning of a message.

- *Only* restricts the group: “Only students may enter” excludes anyone who is not a student.
- *Still* indicates that a situation continues: “The office is still closed”.
- *Instead* indicates a replacement: “The lesson is on Tuesday instead of Monday”.

The word *one* replaces a singular noun already written and *ones* a plural noun. In “Leave the small bottles here; take the large ones”, *ones* replaces *bottles*.

In clock times *past* counts the minutes elapsed since the hour named and *to* the minutes remaining before the hour named: *half past two* means 2\:30 and *a quarter to three* means 2\:45. The message “The train leaves at 8\:15; arrive ten minutes early” contains two times: the train leaves at 8\:15 and arrival at the station is required ten minutes earlier, that is, at 8\:05.

A message communicates only the changes it names. “The seminar will take place online” changes the format of the seminar. Teacher, date and topic are not named, so an answer announcing a change in any of them adds information that is absent.
:::

:::osservazione{#condizioni-avvisi titolo="Conditions and obligations in notices"}
*Only if* introduces the only case in which an instruction applies. Consider the notice “Bring a printed copy only if the upload fails”.

- The printed copy is required in one case only, that is, when the upload fails.
- If the upload succeeds, the printed copy is not required.

The notice concerns a requirement and contains no prohibition, so the answer “Printing is forbidden” states a prohibition that is absent from the text.

In notices *may* expresses permission and *must* an obligation: “Students may use a calculator”, “Students must show their card”.
:::

:::osservazione{#sequenze titolo="Tracking objects and operations"}
The order of the operations in an instruction is shown by words such as *first*, *then*, *finally*.

The instruction “Weigh the empty container, then add the powder and weigh it again” contains three operations.

1. The empty container is weighed.
2. The powder is added.
3. The container is weighed again.

The pronoun *it* replaces *container*. At the second weighing the container holds the powder, so the two weighings give different values.

The answer “Weigh the powder, then the container” uses the same words as the instruction and describes a different procedure, because both the object weighed and the order change.
:::
