## Molecules of life

:::definizione{#biomolecole titolo="Water, sugars and lipids"}
Water is a polar molecule: its charge distribution favors hydrogen bonds between molecules and interaction with many ions. A **hydrophilic** substance interacts favorably with water; a nonpolar region is **hydrophobic**. These properties explain both the dissolution of many salts and membrane organization. Water's high specific heat helps limit temperature changes in organisms.

Carbohydrates include monosaccharides such as glucose, fructose, ribose and deoxyribose. Sucrose joins glucose and fructose; lactose joins glucose and galactose. Starch and glycogen store glucose units mainly in plants and animals, respectively; cellulose supports the plant cell wall. Different bond configurations explain why human digestive enzymes break down starch but not cellulose.

Triglycerides contain glycerol and three fatty acids and serve as energy stores. Phospholipids have a polar head and nonpolar tails: this dual affinity favors bilayers. Cholesterol is a lipid with a ring structure, present in animal membranes and a precursor of several hormones. Lipids are not all polymers made of a single repeated unit.
:::

:::definizione{#proteine titolo="From sequence to function"}
Proteins are chains of amino acids joined by peptide bonds. **Primary structure** is the sequence; secondary structure includes local arrangements such as helices and sheets; tertiary structure is the overall folding of one chain; quaternary structure concerns the association of several chains. Not every protein has quaternary structure. Shape and chemical properties enable catalysis, transport, recognition and movement.

Denaturation changes conformation and can abolish function without breaking the sequence of peptide bonds. An enzyme speeds up a reaction by lowering its activation energy; it is not consumed overall and does not change the final equilibrium. Temperature, pH and substrate availability influence activity: a lower rate alone does not show that the amount of enzyme has decreased.
:::

:::osservazione{titolo="Recognizing monomers"}
A hydrolyzed protein yields amino acids, a polysaccharide monosaccharides, and a nucleic acid nucleotides or their components. Each nucleotide contains a sugar, a phosphate group and a nitrogenous base. Confusing a base with the whole nucleotide leads to an incomplete description of DNA and RNA.
:::

## Membranes and exchange

:::definizione{#membrane titolo="Selective permeability"}
The plasma membrane bounds every cell. In the bilayer, hydrophilic phospholipid heads face the aqueous environments; hydrophobic tails face inward. Proteins embedded in or associated with the membrane provide transport, communication and adhesion. The bilayer does not allow every substance through equally easily: small nonpolar gases cross more readily than ions.

Simple diffusion follows the concentration gradient through the lipids. Facilitated diffusion uses channels or carriers but remains passive. For ions, the **electrochemical** gradient includes concentration and electrical potential difference. Active transport moves substances against this gradient using energy, either directly from ATP or by coupling to other gradients. Endocytosis and exocytosis transport material using vesicles.
:::

:::esempio{#osmosi titolo="Water and tonicity"}
Osmosis is the net movement of water across a selectively permeable membrane. At equal initial pressure, if the solute cannot cross, water tends toward the more concentrated compartment. An animal cell in a hypertonic solution loses water; in a hypotonic solution it gains water and may lyse. In an isotonic solution its volume does not change persistently, although molecular exchange continues.

In a plant cell the wall resists expansion and enables turgor. It does not replace the selective permeability of the membrane. A higher total concentration outside is insufficient to predict the outcome if the solute crosses the membrane rapidly: its permeability and the observation interval must be known.
:::

## Cell organization and metabolism

:::definizione{#cellula titolo="Prokaryotes and eukaryotes"}
Bacteria and archaea have prokaryotic organization: DNA is not enclosed in a membrane-bound nucleus. Eukaryotic cells typically have a nucleus and internal compartments; some specialized cells, such as mature mammalian red blood cells, lose their nucleus during differentiation. Both types have a membrane, cytoplasm, genetic material and ribosomes. A bacterium may have a wall, but a wall alone does not identify a prokaryote: eukaryotic plants and fungi also have one.

The genome is the complete genetic material. Bacteria often have a circular chromosome, possibly accompanied by plasmids; in eukaryotes nuclear DNA is organized into several linear chromosomes associated with proteins. These typical descriptions do not imply that every organism has the same chromosome number or shape.
:::

:::definizione{#organelli titolo="Compartments and functions"}
The nucleus contains most eukaryotic DNA. Ribosomes assemble proteins; those associated with rough endoplasmic reticulum produce many proteins destined for secretion or membranes. Smooth endoplasmic reticulum participates in lipid synthesis. The Golgi apparatus modifies and sorts material; lysosomes provide intracellular digestion. The cytoskeleton supports shape, movement and internal transport.

Plant cells have a cellulose wall, plastids and often a large central vacuole. Chloroplasts are photosynthetic plastids; root cells need not contain them. The vacuole contributes to storage and turgor. Mitochondria and chloroplasts have their own DNA and ribosomes: together with other similarities to bacteria, this supports their endosymbiotic origin.
:::

:::osservazione{#metabolismo titolo="Usable energy"}
Metabolism includes breakdown and synthesis reactions. ATP links many energy-releasing reactions to processes that require energy. In aerobic respiration glucose is oxidized and oxygen is the final electron acceptor; in eukaryotes mitochondria host important stages of the process, whereas glycolysis occurs in the cytosol. Plants also respire in darkness by consuming reserves. The presence of chloroplasts does not remove the need for mitochondria.
:::

## DNA, RNA and gene expression

:::definizione{#dna-rna titolo="Information and complementarity"}
DNA contains deoxyribose and bases A, T, G, C; RNA contains ribose and uses U instead of T. In double-stranded DNA, A pairs with T and G with C. Strands are antiparallel: the $5'\to3'$ direction of one corresponds to $3'\to5'$ of the other. The percentage of A equals that of T in the entire duplex, not necessarily in each individual strand.

Replication is semiconservative: each new double helix retains one preexisting strand and contains one newly synthesized strand. DNA polymerases build the new strand in the $5'\to3'$ direction. A gene is a DNA region whose expression produces a functional RNA; for protein-coding genes this RNA enables protein production.
:::

:::definizione{#espressione titolo="Transcription and translation"}
Transcription synthesizes RNA using one DNA strand as a template. In eukaryotes, nuclear messenger RNA normally undergoes processing, including intron removal by splicing, before translation. The ribosome reads mRNA in triplets called **codons**, from $5'$ toward $3'$. tRNAs recognize codons through anticodons and carry the corresponding amino acids.

::grafico{assi=0 griglia=0 xmin=0 xmax=12 ymin=0 ymax=4 vettori="2.6,2,4.7,2;8,2,9.7,2" etichette="1.1,2,DNA;5.2,2,mRNA;10,2,protein;2.5,3,transcription;7.6,3,translation" titolo="The first step retains a nucleotide language; the second produces an amino acid sequence"}

The standard genetic code contains $4^3=64$ codons, three of which are stop codons. Different codons can specify the same amino acid: the code is degenerate. However, a codon has a definite meaning within that code. A substitution can be synonymous, change an amino acid or introduce a stop; inserting one nucleotide into a coding region can shift the reading frame. Mutation and functional effect are not synonyms: the effect depends on position and context.
:::

## Inheritance and crosses

:::definizione{#genetica-mendeliana titolo="Genes, alleles and segregation"}
An allele is a variant of a gene. In a diploid organism, at an autosomal locus, an $AA$ individual is homozygous and an $Aa$ individual heterozygous. **Genotype** describes the alleles; **phenotype** is the observed trait, also influenced by the environment. Under complete dominance, $AA$ and $Aa$ have the same dominant phenotype. Dominant does not mean more frequent, better or bound to increase.

During gamete formation, alleles at a locus segregate: a regular heterozygote produces $A$ and $a$ gametes with equal probabilities. In the cross $Aa\times Aa$, independently combining the two parents' gametes gives:

| Gametes | $A$ | $a$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $A$ | $AA$ | $Aa$ |
| $a$ | $Aa$ | $aa$ |

Expected genotypes have ratio $1:2:1$; under complete dominance phenotypes have ratio $3:1$. These are probabilities for each conception, not a compulsory distribution in every group of four offspring. Among dominant offspring only, the probability of being $AA$ becomes $\dfrac13$, because the set under consideration is restricted.
:::

:::osservazione{titolo="When predictions change"}
Two loci assort independently if this assumption is justified, for example because they are on different chromosomes. An $AaBb$ individual then produces $AB,Ab,aB,ab$ with probability $\dfrac14$ each. Nearby genes on the same chromosome can be linked: that distribution cannot be applied automatically.

Under incomplete dominance the heterozygote has an intermediate phenotype; under codominance both allelic products are observed. The environment can modify a phenotype without changing the inherited sequence. A pedigree allows models to be compared, but a small number of offspring alone cannot demonstrate an exact theoretical ratio.
:::

## Cell cycle, mitosis and meiosis

:::definizione{#ciclo-cellulare titolo="Counting chromosomes and DNA"}
Eukaryotic interphase comprises G1, S and G2. Replication occurs in S: each chromosome becomes composed of two sister chromatids. While the chromatids remain joined, chromosome number does not double. The amount of DNA does double. A diploid cell remains diploid after S because it has not acquired a new set of homologs.

Mitosis includes chromosome condensation, alignment in metaphase, sister chromatid separation in anaphase and nucleus formation in telophase. Cytokinesis divides the cytoplasm: if it fails, two nuclei can remain in one cell. Bacteria divide by binary fission, without nuclear mitosis.
:::

:::definizione{#meiosi titolo="Two divisions and one replication"}
In prophase I homologs pair; crossing-over exchanges segments between nonsister chromatids of homologous chromosomes. Meiosis I separates homologs, reducing ploidy. Meiosis II separates sister chromatids, without a new S phase between the two divisions. The orientation of homologous pairs and crossing-over contribute to diversity among products.

For an initial cell with $2n=4$, the counts per cell are:

| Stage | Chromosomes | Chromatids | Ploidy |
| --- | ---: | ---: | --- |
| G1 | 4 | 4 | diploid |
| After S | 4 | 8 | diploid |
| After meiosis I | 2 | 4 | haploid |
| After meiosis II | 2 | 2 | haploid |

The table considers cells after division, not the still undivided anaphase cell. Fertilization joins two haploid nuclei and restores diploidy. In animals meiosis is linked to gamete formation; in plants it produces spores from which the haploid generation develops. The resulting cells are not always four equivalent functional gametes: in oogenesis cytoplasmic divisions are unequal.
:::

## Tissues, organ systems and regulation

:::definizione{#tessuti titolo="From cell to organism"}
Organized cells form tissues; several tissues cooperate in organs; coordinated organs form organ systems. Epithelia line surfaces and form glands. Connective tissue supports and connects through an extracellular matrix: it includes bone, cartilage and blood. Muscle tissue contracts; nervous tissue receives and transmits signals. An organ normally contains several tissue types.

The skin contributes to protection and thermoregulation. Skeleton, joints and muscles enable support and movement; muscle shortening exerts tension on connected structures. The nervous system coordinates responses through impulses and synapses; in chemical synapses a neurotransmitter binds receptors on the target cell. The reproductive system produces gametes and enables processes associated with sexual reproduction.
:::

:::definizione{#apparati titolo="Exchange, transport and excretion"}
Digestion breaks food down into absorbable molecules; absorption transfers them across the epithelium. Intestinal villi and microvilli increase surface area. Alveoli have thin walls and an extensive capillary network: oxygen and carbon dioxide diffuse along their respective partial-pressure gradients. Hemoglobin in red blood cells carries much of the oxygen.

Arteries conduct blood away from the heart; veins return it to the heart. Direction is the criterion, so pulmonary arteries can carry oxygen-poor blood. Pulmonary circulation connects heart and lungs; systemic circulation connects heart and tissues. Capillaries are the main exchange sites.

In kidneys, glomeruli filter part of the plasma; tubules selectively reabsorb substances and secrete others into the filtrate. Filtrate and final urine therefore have different compositions. Excretion removes metabolic products and helps regulate water and salts; expelling undigested intestinal residues is a distinct process.
:::

:::esempio{titolo="A control response"}
Homeostasis keeps internal variables within ranges compatible with life. After a meal, increased blood glucose stimulates responses, including insulin release, that promote glucose use and storage. The response reduces the initial deviation: this is negative feedback. It does not mean that the variable remains perfectly constant, or that every cell requires insulin to take up glucose.
:::

## Plants: organs, transport and photosynthesis

:::definizione{#piante titolo="Structures linked to function"}
The root anchors the plant and absorbs water and minerals; root hairs increase contact with the soil. The stem supports aerial organs and contains conducting tissues. Apical meristems near root and shoot tips produce new cells through mitosis. Elongation of the resulting cells then contributes to length growth: division and expansion are distinct processes. Leaves are generally specialized for exchange and photosynthesis. In angiosperms the flower houses reproductive structures; after fertilization the ovule forms the seed and the ovary contributes to the fruit. The seed contains the embryo, reserves and protective coverings.

Xylem mainly transports water and minerals from roots; phloem distributes sugars from source organs to organs that consume or store them. An illuminated leaf can be a source and a root a sink, but roles vary with development. Transpiration is loss of water as vapor, mainly through stomata. Stomatal closure limits water loss and can also reduce carbon dioxide entry.
:::

:::definizione{#fotosintesi titolo="Light and carbon"}
Oxygenic photosynthesis transforms light energy into chemical energy. In chloroplasts, light reactions occur in thylakoid membranes; carbon fixation occurs in the stroma. Released oxygen comes from water, whereas the carbon in sugars comes from carbon dioxide. A simplified overall balance is
$$
6\,\mathrm{CO_2}+6\,\mathrm{H_2O}+\text{light}
\longrightarrow \mathrm{C_6H_{12}O_6}+6\,\mathrm{O_2}.
$$
The balance does not describe a single reaction and does not by itself identify the origin of oxygen atoms. Light, carbon dioxide and temperature can limit the process. Increasing one resource has little effect when another is lacking. A plant also respires in light: the observed net gas exchange reflects photosynthesis and respiration simultaneously. At the compensation point the flows balance, without both processes stopping.
:::

## Biodiversity, classification and evolution

:::definizione{#classificazione titolo="Relationships and names"}
The three-domain model distinguishes Bacteria, Archaea and Eukarya. The first two include prokaryotic organisms; Eukarya includes unicellular and multicellular forms. Animals, plants and fungi are eukaryotic groups. Viruses are acellular entities with genetic material and a protein coat, sometimes also a lipid envelope; they depend on host cells to multiply and do not form a fourth cellular domain.

In binomial nomenclature, *Homo sapiens* specifies the genus and specific name. Two species in the same genus share that level without being the same population. A phylogenetic tree represents hypotheses of relationships: two branches are closer if they share a more recent common ancestor. Placement on the right or left does not indicate progress. Homologous structures derive from a common origin, even with different functions; functional similarities can instead evolve independently.
:::

:::definizione{#evoluzione titolo="Variation and reproductive success"}
Mutation and recombination generate or rearrange variation. Natural selection changes the frequency of inherited variants when they affect reproductive success in an environment. Antibiotic resistance may already be present before treatment: exposure favors reproduction by resistant organisms. It does not necessarily produce the specific mutation the organism needs.

Genetic drift changes frequencies randomly, especially in small populations; a sharp numerical reduction or colonization by a few founders can accentuate it. Gene flow transfers alleles between populations. An adaptation is relative to the environment, not universal perfection. Reproductive isolation can promote speciation: geographic barriers or differences in reproductive timing reduce gene flow. Extinction eliminates a species; disappearance from one territory is local extinction, not necessarily global extinction.
:::

## Ecology: populations, interactions and energy

:::definizione{#ecologia titolo="Levels of organization"}
An individual belongs to a population of organisms of the same species in a given area. Populations of different species form a community; the ecosystem includes the community, physical environment and interactions. Species richness counts the species present; abundance counts individuals. Communities with equal richness can have very different abundance distributions.

Population size changes through births, deaths, immigration and emigration. Limited resources and interactions can slow growth. Carrying capacity is a sustainable population size in the context considered, not an unchanging constant of a species: it changes with conditions and resources. A closed population without reproduction decreases as individuals die.
:::

:::definizione{#flusso-energia titolo="Production and trophic levels"}
Autotrophic producers build organic substances from inorganic carbon. Consumers are heterotrophs; decomposers transform residues and return nutrients to matter cycles. Gross primary production includes the energy fixed by producers; subtracting their respiration gives net primary production, available for growth and other levels. Secondary production concerns new heterotroph biomass.

::grafico{assi=0 griglia=0 xmin=0 xmax=12 ymin=0 ymax=4 vettori="2.6,2,4.7,2;8.8,2,9.7,2" etichette="1.1,2,grass;5.1,2,grasshopper;10,2,lizard;4.5,3.3,energy and matter" titolo="Arrows follow transfer from food to consumer"}

Matter is recycled, whereas energy flows and some becomes heat at each transfer. If a problem specifies $10\%$ efficiency, each step multiplies energy by $0.10$: this value is a model assumption, not a universal law. A food web includes more connections than a single chain.
:::

:::osservazione{#interazioni titolo="Effects on both partners"}
In competition both partners are affected by using a limited resource. Predation and parasitism benefit one partner at the other's expense, in different ways. In mutualism both benefit; in commensalism one benefits while the other experiences no appreciable effect. Classifying a case requires the observed effects, not merely the fact that two species live close together.
:::
