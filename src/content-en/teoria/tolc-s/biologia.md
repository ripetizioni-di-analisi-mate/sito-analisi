## Molecules of life

:::definizione{#biomolecole titolo="Water, sugars and lipids"}
In the **water** molecule the oxygen carries a small negative charge and the two hydrogens a small positive charge. A molecule with charges distributed in this way is called **polar**. The hydrogen of one molecule is attracted to the oxygen of another, and this attraction is the **hydrogen bond**. A **hydrophilic** substance, such as salt, has charges and dissolves in water. A **hydrophobic** substance, such as oil, is non-polar, that is, without charges, and does not dissolve. Water has a high **specific heat**, that is, it needs a lot of heat to warm up, so an animal, made largely of water, changes temperature slowly.

**Carbohydrates**, or sugars, are made of carbon, hydrogen and oxygen.

- A **monosaccharide** is a single unit, such as glucose, fructose, galactose, ribose and deoxyribose.
- A **disaccharide** is made of two units. Sucrose, table sugar, joins glucose and fructose; lactose, in milk, joins glucose and galactose.
- A **polysaccharide** is a long chain of units. It is a **polymer**, that is, a molecule made of many similar units, the **monomers**, linked in a row.

Starch, glycogen and cellulose are polysaccharides made of glucose only. Starch is the reserve of plants, glycogen that of animals, and cellulose forms the wall of plant cells; the cell is the smallest unit of a living being. In cellulose the units are joined by a different bond, which human digestion does not break.

**Lipids** are hydrophobic substances, such as fats and oils.

- A **triglyceride**, an energy reserve, is made of glycerol and three **fatty acids**, long chains of carbon and hydrogen.
- A **phospholipid** has a polar head, which is hydrophilic, and two fatty-acid tails, which are hydrophobic. In water, phospholipids therefore form a **bilayer**, with the heads towards the water and the tails inside.
- **Cholesterol** is a lipid made of rings, found in animal membranes, from which the body produces some hormones, molecules that carry a signal through the blood.

Lipids are not polymers, because they are not chains of one repeated monomer.
:::

:::definizione{#proteine titolo="From sequence to function"}
A **protein** is a chain of **amino acids**, small molecules of twenty types, joined by **peptide bonds**. Proteins speed up reactions, transport substances, recognise other molecules and produce movement. The task of each protein depends on its shape, described at four levels.

- **Primary structure** is the sequence of amino acids.
- **Secondary structure** is the shape of short stretches, coiled into a helix or lying side by side in sheets.
- **Tertiary structure** is the folding of the whole chain.
- **Quaternary structure** is the union of several chains, in proteins that have more than one. Haemoglobin, the protein that carries oxygen in the blood, has four.

**Denaturation** is the loss of folding, caused for example by heat, as in egg white when it cooks. The sequence stays intact, but without its shape the protein no longer carries out its task.

An **enzyme** is a protein that speeds up a chemical reaction. The molecule it acts on is called the **substrate**. A reaction takes place only if the molecules receive a certain initial energy, the **activation energy**. The enzyme lowers it, so more molecules react in the same time. At the end the enzyme is intact, and the final amounts of the substances, that is, the equilibrium of the reaction, are the same as without the enzyme. Lactase splits lactose into glucose and galactose.

The rate of the reaction depends on the amount of enzyme and of substrate, on temperature and on **pH** (short for 'potential of hydrogen'), the number that measures the acidity of a liquid: 7 in pure water, less than 7 in acidic liquids. A slower reaction therefore does not indicate, by itself, a smaller amount of enzyme, because the same amount works more slowly with less substrate or at a different pH.
:::

:::osservazione{titolo="Recognizing monomers"}
The **nucleic acids** are two polymers. **DNA** (deoxyribonucleic acid) stores hereditary information. **RNA** (ribonucleic acid) is a similar molecule, into which the cell copies stretches of DNA in order to use their information. Their monomer, the **nucleotide**, is made of a sugar, a **phosphate group** (phosphorus and oxygen) and a **nitrogenous base**, a ring-shaped molecule that contains nitrogen. The base is therefore only one part of the nucleotide.

**Hydrolysis** is the reaction in which water breaks the bonds of a polymer and releases the monomers: amino acids from a protein, monosaccharides from a polysaccharide, nucleotides, or their three parts, from a nucleic acid.
:::

## Membranes and exchange

:::definizione{#membrane titolo="Selective permeability"}
The **plasma membrane** is the covering that separates the inside of every cell from the outside. It is a phospholipid bilayer, with the heads towards the water on both sides and the tails in the middle. The proteins embedded in it transport substances, receive signals and join neighbouring cells. The membrane has **selective permeability**, that is, it lets some substances through and not others. Small non-polar molecules, such as oxygen, cross the lipids. **Ions** (atoms with an electric charge, such as sodium) and large polar molecules, such as glucose, pass only through the proteins.

The concentration of a substance is its amount in a given volume. The difference in concentration between two regions is the **concentration gradient**. A substance moves spontaneously from the region where it is more concentrated to the one where it is less concentrated, and this movement is called **diffusion**.

- **Simple diffusion**. The substance passes between the lipids, from the higher concentration to the lower. Oxygen enters cells in this way.
- **Facilitated diffusion**. The substance passes through a protein, a channel or a carrier, in the same direction. Glucose enters many cells in this way. The two kinds of diffusion are **passive** transport, that is, without energy consumption.
- **Active transport**. A protein moves the substance from the lower concentration to the higher and consumes energy. The energy is supplied by **ATP** (adenosine triphosphate), the molecule that cells use as their immediate source of energy. The sodium-potassium pump uses ATP to carry sodium ions out of the cell and potassium ions in. In other cases the energy comes from the gradient of a second ion.

For an ion the difference in electrical potential, that is, in charge, between the two sides of the membrane also matters; the two effects together form the **electrochemical** gradient.

Larger material crosses the membrane inside **vesicles**, small spheres of membrane. In **endocytosis** the membrane folds inwards and encloses external material: a white blood cell, a blood cell that defends the body, engulfs in this way a bacterium, that is, an organism made of a single cell. In **exocytosis** a vesicle fuses with the membrane and pours its contents outside: the pancreas releases the hormone insulin in this way.
:::

:::esempio{#osmosi titolo="Water and tonicity"}
A **solution** is a liquid with a dissolved substance, the **solute**: in salt water the solute is the salt. **Osmosis** is the passage of water across a membrane that holds back the solute. If the pressure is the same on both sides, water passes from the more dilute solution to the more concentrated one. In the figure there are two solute particles on the left and six on the right, so water passes from left to right.

::grafico{assi=0 griglia=0 xmin=0 xmax=10 ymin=0 ymax=5 linee="5,0.7 5,4.3" punti="1,1.5,pieno;3,3.5,pieno;6,1,pieno;7,2,pieno;8,3,pieno;9,4,pieno;7,4,pieno;9,1.5,pieno" vettori="3.5,2.5,6.5,2.5" etichette="2,4.7,dilute;8,4.7,concentrated;5,0.2,membrane;4.7,3.1,water" titolo="Dots represent an impermeant solute; the arrow shows the initial net water flow"}

The **tonicity** of a solution is its concentration relative to the inside of the cell immersed in it.

- A **hypertonic** solution is more concentrated than the inside. The cell loses water and shrivels, like, in sea water, a red blood cell, the blood cell that carries oxygen.
- A **hypotonic** solution is less concentrated. The cell gains water, swells and, if the difference is large, bursts, like a red blood cell in distilled water. The bursting is called **lysis**.
- An **isotonic** solution has the same concentration. Water enters and leaves in equal amounts, and the volume of the cell stays constant.

A plant cell has, outside the membrane, a rigid wall. In a hypotonic solution it presses against the wall and does not burst. This pressure, **turgor**, keeps the leaves firm. The wall lets water and solutes through, so selecting substances remains the task of the membrane.

These predictions hold for a solute that does not cross the membrane. A solute that crosses it rapidly, such as urea (a waste molecule) in red blood cells, reaches the same concentration on both sides and does not hold water back, so the outcome also depends on the permeability of the membrane and on the time elapsed.
:::

## Cell organization and metabolism

:::definizione{#cellula titolo="Prokaryotes and eukaryotes"}
The **cell** is the smallest living unit. Every cell has a plasma membrane, a **cytoplasm**, DNA and **ribosomes**, the particles that build proteins. The cytoplasm is the contents of the cell: a liquid, the **cytosol**, with the structures immersed in it.

- In a **prokaryotic** cell the DNA is free in the cytoplasm. Bacteria and archaea, single-celled organisms, are prokaryotes.
- In a **eukaryotic** cell the DNA is enclosed in the **nucleus**, a compartment bounded by a double membrane, and the cytoplasm, around the nucleus, contains other compartments. Animals, plants and fungi are eukaryotes.

The two types are distinguished by the nucleus and not by the wall outside the membrane, which is found in many bacteria, in plants and in fungi. The red blood cells of mammals are eukaryotic cells that lose their nucleus as they mature.

The **genome** is all the DNA of an organism. A **chromosome** is a DNA molecule with its proteins. A bacterium usually has one circular chromosome and, often, **plasmids**, small rings of DNA. A eukaryote has several linear chromosomes, in a number that differs from organism to organism (46 in humans).
:::

:::definizione{#organelli titolo="Compartments and functions"}
An **organelle** is a structure of the eukaryotic cell with a task of its own.

- The **nucleus** contains almost all the DNA.
- **Ribosomes** join amino acids into proteins.
- The **endoplasmic reticulum** is a network of membranes. The rough reticulum is covered with ribosomes and produces the proteins destined for the membrane or for the outside; the smooth reticulum produces lipids.
- The **Golgi apparatus** modifies the proteins arriving from the reticulum and sorts them into vesicles.
- **Lysosomes** are vesicles of enzymes that break down molecules that are no longer useful.
- **Mitochondria** obtain energy from glucose.
- The **cytoskeleton**, a network of protein filaments, gives the cell its shape, moves it and transports vesicles.

Plant cells also have a cellulose wall, plastids, organelles found only in plants, and often a large vacuole. **Chloroplasts** are the plastids that carry out photosynthesis, that is, produce sugars with the energy of light, and contain chlorophyll, the green pigment that absorbs it; they are absent from the cells of roots, which live in the dark. The **vacuole** is a large sac of water and dissolved substances, which stores reserves and maintains turgor.

Mitochondria and chloroplasts have their own DNA and ribosomes, similar to those of bacteria. According to the **endosymbiotic theory** they derive from bacteria engulfed by a larger cell.
:::

:::osservazione{#metabolismo titolo="Usable energy"}
**Metabolism** is the set of chemical reactions of a cell. Breakdown reactions split large molecules and release energy; synthesis reactions build large molecules and require it. ATP carries energy from the former to the latter. When it loses one of its three phosphate groups it releases energy and becomes **ADP** (adenosine diphosphate); breakdown reactions supply the energy to attach it again.

**Cellular respiration** is the process by which the cell obtains ATP from glucose using oxygen, and for this reason it is called aerobic. It begins in the cytosol with **glycolysis**, which splits glucose into two smaller molecules and produces little ATP. It continues in the mitochondria, where most of the ATP is formed: the two molecules are broken down to carbon dioxide and the electrons removed from glucose, which is thereby oxidised, pass to oxygen, the final acceptor, which forms water.

The cells of plants also have mitochondria, because chloroplasts produce the sugars and it is the mitochondria that obtain ATP from them. Plants therefore respire in the dark too, consuming their reserves.
:::

## DNA, RNA and gene expression

:::definizione{#dna-rna titolo="Information and complementarity"}
A **strand** of DNA is a long chain of nucleotides. Each nucleotide contains the sugar deoxyribose, a phosphate group and one of four bases, indicated by their initial: adenine (A), thymine (T), guanine (G) and cytosine (C). Hereditary information is the order of the bases along the chain.

A DNA molecule is made of two strands wound into a **double helix**. The bases of one strand pair with those of the other: A with T, G with C. Two bases that pair are called **complementary**, so the sequence of one strand determines that of the other. In the double helix, therefore, there are as many A as T and as many G as C: if A is $30\%$ of the bases, T is $30\%$ and G and C are $20\%$ each, that is, half of the remaining $40\%$. The equality holds for the whole double helix: the strand ATGCCA, on its own, has two A and one T.

The two ends of a strand are different and are called $5'$ ('five prime') and $3'$ ('three prime'). The notation $5'\to3'$ indicates the direction from the first to the second. The two strands are **antiparallel**, that is, they run in opposite directions: $5'$-ATGCCA-$3'$ is paired with $3'$-TACGGT-$5'$.

RNA contains the sugar ribose, has the base uracil (U) in place of thymine and is usually a single strand.

**Replication** is the copying of DNA, which precedes cell division. The two strands separate and each acts as a **template**, that is, as a model for the complementary strand. Each new double helix contains one old strand and one new strand, so replication is called **semiconservative**. The enzyme **DNA polymerase** adds nucleotides at the $3'$ end, so the new strand grows in the $5'\to3'$ direction.

A **gene** is a stretch of DNA with the instructions for building an RNA, which usually serves to build a protein.
:::

:::definizione{#espressione titolo="Transcription and translation"}
The **expression** of a gene is the use of its information to build a protein, in two steps.

**Transcription** is the copying of a gene into an RNA molecule. The copy that the ribosomes read in order to build the protein is **mRNA** (messenger RNA), which in eukaryotes leaves the nucleus and reaches the ribosomes in the cytoplasm. The enzyme RNA polymerase reads one strand of the gene as a template and builds the complementary RNA, with U opposite A. From the template $3'$-TACGGTACCATT-$5'$ the mRNA $5'$-AUGCCAUGGUAA-$3'$ is obtained. In eukaryotes, before translation, the RNA is processed: **splicing** removes the **introns**, the stretches not needed for the protein, and joins the others, the **exons**.

**Translation** is the building of the protein according to the sequence of the mRNA. The ribosome reads the mRNA in the $5'\to3'$ direction, three bases at a time. Each group of three bases is a **codon** and corresponds to an amino acid. The amino acids are carried by **tRNAs** (transfer RNAs), small RNA molecules with an **anticodon**, three bases complementary to the codon. The ribosome begins at the start codon AUG, which corresponds to methionine, joins the amino acids and stops at a stop codon, which does not correspond to any amino acid. The mRNA of the example contains the codons AUG, CCA, UGG, UAA: the first three correspond to methionine, proline and tryptophan, the fourth is a stop.

::grafico{assi=0 griglia=0 xmin=0 xmax=12 ymin=0 ymax=4 vettori="2.6,2,4.7,2;8,2,9.7,2" etichette="1.1,2,DNA;5.2,2,mRNA;10,2,protein;2.5,3,transcription;7.6,3,translation" titolo="The first step retains a nucleotide language; the second produces an amino acid sequence"}

In the figure transcription connects two sequences of nucleotides, DNA and mRNA; translation leads to a sequence of amino acids, the protein.

The standard **genetic code**, used by almost all organisms, assigns each codon its amino acid. With 4 bases, the codons of three bases are $4^3=64$: three are stop codons and 61 correspond to the 20 amino acids. Several codons therefore correspond to the same amino acid, and the code is called **degenerate**: GCU and GCC both correspond to alanine. Each codon, however, has only one meaning.

A **mutation** is a change in the DNA sequence. The effect on the protein depends on the point where it occurs and on how the codon changes.

- A **synonymous** substitution leaves the same amino acid, so the protein does not change: from GCU to GCC it is still alanine.
- Another substitution changes the amino acid: from GAG to GUG glutamic acid becomes valine.
- A substitution that creates a stop codon, such as from UGG to UGA, shortens the protein.
- The insertion of one base in a stretch that codes for the protein shifts the **reading frame**, that is, the division into codons, from that point onwards: with a U inserted after AUG, the mRNA of the example is read AUG, UCC, AUG, GUA.
:::

## Inheritance and crosses

:::definizione{#genetica-mendeliana titolo="Genes, alleles and segregation"}
- An **allele** is a variant of a gene. In the pea the gene for seed colour has the allele $A$ for yellow and the allele $a$ for green. The place of a gene on the chromosome is called its **locus** (plural loci).
- A **diploid** organism has two copies of each chromosome, one maternal and one paternal, so it has two alleles for each gene.
- An individual is **homozygous** if the two alleles are the same ($AA$ or $aa$) and **heterozygous** if they are different ($Aa$).
- The **genotype** is the pair of alleles. The **phenotype** is the trait that is observed.
- An allele is **dominant** if it determines the phenotype of the heterozygote; the other is **recessive**. In the pea yellow is dominant: $AA$ and $Aa$ have yellow seeds, $aa$ has green seeds. Here dominance is called complete.

**Gametes** are the reproductive cells, such as the sperm cell and the egg cell. They have only one copy of each chromosome, so only one allele per gene, because when they form the two alleles separate (law of **segregation**). A heterozygote $Aa$ therefore produces $A$ gametes and $a$ gametes, each with probability $\dfrac12$. The rule holds for **autosomal** genes, located on the chromosomes that do not determine sex.

In the table of the cross $Aa\times Aa$ the rows are the gametes of one parent, the columns those of the other, and each cell is the genotype of an offspring.

| Gametes | $A$ | $a$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $A$ | $AA$ | $Aa$ |
| $a$ | $Aa$ | $aa$ |

Each cell has probability $\dfrac12\cdot\dfrac12=\dfrac14$. The genotypes $AA$, $Aa$, $aa$ occupy one, two and one cell, so they are in the ratio $1:2:1$. The three cells with at least one $A$ give yellow seeds and the cell $aa$ green seeds, so the ratio of the phenotypes is $3:1$. Among the three yellow cells only one is $AA$, so a plant with yellow seeds is $AA$ with probability $\dfrac13$.
:::

:::attenzione
The ratio $3:1$ gives the probability for each offspring, $\dfrac34$ for the dominant phenotype, and not the composition of every group of four: four offspring of $Aa\times Aa$ all have the dominant phenotype with probability $\left(\dfrac34\right)^4=\dfrac{81}{256}\approx 0.32$.

A dominant allele is not for that reason more frequent, more advantageous or on the increase: the allele for polydactyly (extra fingers) is dominant and is rare.
:::

:::osservazione{titolo="When predictions change"}
- **Two genes on different chromosomes** are transmitted independently (independent assortment). An $AaBb$ individual produces the gametes $AB$, $Ab$, $aB$, $ab$, each with probability $\dfrac12\cdot\dfrac12=\dfrac14$.
- **Two genes close together on the same chromosome** are called linked and are transmitted together in most gametes. The four probabilities are then different from $\dfrac14$.
- In **incomplete dominance** the heterozygote has an intermediate phenotype. In the snapdragon, red flowers and white flowers give pink flowers.
- In **codominance** the heterozygote shows both alleles. A person with the alleles A and B of the blood group has group AB.
- The **environment** modifies the phenotype without changing the genes: the same hydrangea has blue or pink flowers depending on the acidity of the soil.

A **pedigree** is the diagram of a family that shows who has a trait. Two parents without the trait who have a child with it are both heterozygous, and the allele is recessive.
:::

## Cell cycle, mitosis and meiosis

:::definizione{#ciclo-cellulare titolo="Counting chromosomes and DNA"}
The **cell cycle** is the succession of phases between the birth of a cell and its division. The part that precedes division, **interphase**, has three phases: **G1** (G from *gap*, an interval), in which the cell grows; **S** (from synthesis), in which it replicates its DNA; **G2**, the second gap, in which it prepares to divide.

After the S phase each chromosome is made of two identical DNA molecules, the **sister chromatids**, joined at one point. Two joined chromatids count as a single chromosome. A human cell has 46 chromosomes before and after the S phase, and afterwards it has $46\cdot 2=92$ chromatids. The S phase therefore doubles the DNA and not the number of chromosomes.

In a diploid cell the chromosomes form pairs of **homologues**, one maternal and one paternal, with the same genes. A **haploid** cell has only one chromosome per pair. The number of chromosomes is written $2n$ in a diploid cell and $n$ in a haploid one: in humans $2n=46$ and $n=23$. After the S phase the pairs are the same, so the cell is still diploid.

**Mitosis** is the division of the nucleus into two nuclei with the same chromosomes. It serves growth and the replacement of lost cells. In prophase the chromosomes shorten and become visible; in metaphase they line up at the centre; in anaphase the sister chromatids separate, move to opposite sides and from that moment count as chromosomes; in telophase a nucleus forms around each group. **Cytokinesis** then divides the cytoplasm; if it is missing, one cell with two nuclei remains. Bacteria, which have no nucleus, divide by **binary fission**: they copy the chromosome and divide in two.
:::

:::definizione{#meiosi titolo="Two divisions and one replication"}
**Meiosis** is the division that produces four haploid cells from one diploid cell. In animals it serves to form the gametes. In **fertilisation** two gametes with $n$ chromosomes unite, and the cell obtained, the **zygote**, has $2n$ again.

Meiosis comprises two divisions, preceded by a single replication of DNA.

- In **meiosis I** the homologous chromosomes pair (prophase I) and then separate. Each cell receives one chromosome per pair, still made of two chromatids, and is haploid.
- In **meiosis II**, which follows without another replication, the sister chromatids separate, as in mitosis.

The table gives the counts for $2n=4$, in each cell once division is complete. **Ploidy** indicates whether the cell is haploid or diploid.

| Stage | Chromosomes | Chromatids | Ploidy |
| --- | ---: | ---: | --- |
| G1 | 4 | 4 | diploid |
| After S | 4 | 8 | diploid |
| After meiosis I | 2 | 4 | haploid |
| After meiosis II | 2 | 2 | haploid |

The four cells are genetically different. During pairing two non-sister chromatids, one from each homologue, exchange corresponding stretches (**crossing over**). In addition, each pair orients itself at random before separating, so with $n$ pairs the combinations are $2^n$, that is, about 8.4 million for $n=23$.

In males one meiosis produces four sperm cells. In females the cytoplasm divides into unequal parts and a single egg cell forms. In plants meiosis produces **spores**, haploid cells from which the generation that forms the gametes arises.
:::

## Tissues, organ systems and regulation

:::definizione{#tessuti titolo="From cell to organism"}
A **tissue** is a set of similar cells with the same task. Several tissues form an **organ**, such as the stomach, and several organs working together form an **organ system**, such as the digestive one.

- **Epithelial** tissue, or epithelium, lines surfaces, like the outer layer of the skin, and forms glands.
- **Connective** tissue supports and connects the others. Its cells produce the material in which they are immersed, the **extracellular matrix**, hard in bone, elastic in cartilage and liquid in blood.
- **Muscle** tissue is made of cells that contract, that is, shorten.
- **Nervous** tissue is made of **neurons**, cells that receive and transmit electrical signals, the impulses.

The skin protects the body and regulates its temperature. Skeleton, joints and muscles support and move the body: a muscle, by shortening, pulls the bone to which it is attached. The nervous system coordinates responses: a neuron passes the impulse to the next cell at a point of contact, the **synapse**, where it releases a **neurotransmitter**, a molecule that binds to proteins of that cell, the receptors. The reproductive system produces the gametes and makes sexual reproduction possible.
:::

:::definizione{#apparati titolo="Exchange, transport and excretion"}
**Digestion** is the splitting of the molecules of food into small molecules, for example of starch into glucose. **Absorption** is their passage to the blood across the epithelium of the intestine. The wall of the intestine is folded into **villi** and the membrane of its cells into **microvilli**, which increase the absorbing surface.

The **alveoli** of the lungs are small thin-walled sacs, surrounded by **capillaries**, the thinnest blood vessels. The **partial pressure** of a gas is the part of the pressure due to that gas in a mixture, and a gas diffuses from where it is higher to where it is lower. That of oxygen is higher in the alveoli than in the incoming blood, so oxygen passes into the blood, where most of it binds to the haemoglobin of red blood cells. That of carbon dioxide is higher in the blood, so the gas passes into the air.

An **artery** is a vessel that carries blood from the heart to the organs. A **vein** carries it back to the heart. In the capillaries, between the two, blood exchanges substances with the tissues. The **pulmonary** circulation goes from the heart to the lungs and back; the **systemic** circulation goes from the heart to the other organs and back. Arteries and veins are distinguished by the direction of the blood: the pulmonary arteries carry oxygen-poor blood to the lungs.

The kidneys remove from the blood the waste products of metabolism, such as urea, and regulate water and salts. In the **glomerulus**, a tuft of capillaries, part of the plasma, the liquid of the blood, is filtered into a **tubule**. The tubule reabsorbs, that is, returns to the blood, almost all the water and the useful substances, and secretes other wastes into the filtrate. Urine therefore has a different composition from the filtrate. **Excretion** is the removal of waste substances produced by cells; faeces, by contrast, are residues of undigested food.
:::

:::esempio{titolo="A control response"}
**Homeostasis** is the maintenance of internal conditions within a narrow range. One case is the **blood glucose level**, the concentration of glucose in the blood, which rises after a meal. The pancreas then releases **insulin**, a hormone, which makes glucose enter muscle cells and makes the liver store it as glycogen, so the blood glucose level falls again. A response that reduces the change that caused it is called **negative feedback**. The blood glucose level therefore does not stay fixed, but oscillates within a range. Some cells, such as neurons, take up glucose even without insulin.
:::

## Plants: organs, transport and photosynthesis

:::definizione{#piante titolo="Structures linked to function"}
- The **root** anchors the plant to the soil and absorbs water and mineral salts. **Root hairs**, thin extensions of the surface cells, increase the absorbing surface.
- The **stem** supports leaves and flowers and contains the transport vessels.
- The **leaf** is the organ of photosynthesis. It has small adjustable openings, the **stomata**, through which gases pass.
- The **flower** is the reproductive organ of **angiosperms**, the flowering plants. After fertilisation the ovule, where the egg cell is found, becomes the **seed**, which contains the embryo, the reserves and a protective coat, and the ovary that encloses it becomes the **fruit**.

Growth in length takes place in two stages. **Apical meristems**, groups of cells at the tips of roots and shoots, produce new cells by mitosis. The cells produced then elongate by absorbing water.

**Xylem** is the set of vessels that carry water and salts from the roots to the leaves. **Phloem** carries sugars from the organs that supply them, the sources, to those that consume or store them. In summer the source is the leaf, which produces them; in spring it is a root that gives up its reserves.

**Transpiration** is the loss of water vapour through the stomata. The water that evaporates draws more water from the xylem and makes it rise from the roots. With little water in the soil the stomata close, so the plant loses less water, but receives less carbon dioxide and photosynthesis slows down.
:::

:::definizione{#fotosintesi titolo="Light and carbon"}
**Photosynthesis** is the process by which plants, algae and some bacteria build sugars from carbon dioxide ($\mathrm{CO_2}$) and water ($\mathrm{H_2O}$), using the energy of light, which becomes chemical energy of the sugars. It is called oxygenic because it releases oxygen ($\mathrm{O_2}$). The overall balance, in which $\mathrm{C_6H_{12}O_6}$ is glucose, is
$$
6\,\mathrm{CO_2}+6\,\mathrm{H_2O}+\text{light}
\longrightarrow \mathrm{C_6H_{12}O_6}+6\,\mathrm{O_2}.
$$
The balance summarises two groups of reactions of the chloroplast, which contains flattened sacs, the **thylakoids**, immersed in a liquid, the **stroma**.

1. The **light reactions** take place on the thylakoid membranes. Chlorophyll absorbs light, and the energy is used to split water molecules, releasing oxygen, and to produce ATP.
2. **Carbon fixation** takes place in the stroma. With the energy of ATP, carbon dioxide is turned into sugars.

The oxygen released therefore comes from water, and the carbon of the sugars from carbon dioxide.

The rate of photosynthesis depends on three factors: light, carbon dioxide and temperature. It is set by the factor furthest from its favourable value, called the **limiting factor**: with little light an increase in carbon dioxide does not speed it up.

The plant respires all the time and carries out photosynthesis only in the light, so the gas exchange measured in the light is the difference between the two processes. Suppose that in one hour, in weak light, a leaf releases 2 milligrams of carbon dioxide by respiring and absorbs 2 by photosynthesis: the net exchange is $2-2=0$. This is the **compensation point**, at which the two processes continue and their exchanges cancel out.
:::

## Biodiversity, classification and evolution

:::definizione{#classificazione titolo="Relationships and names"}
**Classification** is the grouping of organisms according to their relationships. The broadest groups are three **domains**: Bacteria and Archaea, which are prokaryotes, and Eukarya (the eukaryotes), which includes animals, plants, fungi and single-celled organisms.

A **virus** is a particle made of a nucleic acid, DNA or RNA, enclosed in a protein coat, the **capsid**, and, in some cases, in a lipid envelope. A virus has no ribosomes and no metabolism, and multiplies only inside a host cell, using its ribosomes. It is therefore not a cell and does not belong to any domain.

A **species** is a set of organisms that interbreed and produce fertile offspring. Its scientific name has two words (**binomial nomenclature**): the first indicates the **genus**, a group of closely related species, and the second distinguishes the species. *Canis lupus*, the wolf, and *Canis latrans*, the coyote, are different species of the same genus; the human species is *Homo sapiens*.

A **phylogenetic tree** is a branching diagram that represents a hypothesis of relationship among species. Each fork is a common ancestor, and two species are the more closely related the more recent their common ancestor. The common ancestor of humans and chimpanzees is more recent than that of humans and mice, so humans are more closely related to chimpanzees. The order of the species from left to right has no meaning and does not indicate progress.

**Homologous** structures derive from the same structure of a common ancestor, even if they have different tasks: the human arm and the bat wing contain the same bones. Analogous structures have the same task and different origins, like the wing of a bird and that of an insect.
:::

:::definizione{#evoluzione titolo="Variation and reproductive success"}
**Evolution** is the change in the hereditary traits of a population, that is, the set of individuals of a species living in the same area, over the generations, that is, the change in the frequency of its alleles. The differences among individuals arise from mutations, which create new alleles, and from **recombination** (crossing over and the union of gametes), which brings alleles together in new combinations. Three processes change the frequencies.

- **Natural selection**. Individuals with a hereditary trait that, in a given environment, makes them reproduce more leave more offspring, so the allele for that trait becomes more frequent. In a population of bacteria some cells have, through a random mutation, resistance to an antibiotic. The antibiotic kills the others and the resistant ones multiply, so it has selected a mutation that already existed.
- **Genetic drift**. It is the random change in frequencies, stronger in small populations, and therefore after a collapse in the number of individuals, called a bottleneck, and when a few individuals found a new population.
- **Gene flow**. It is the passage of alleles between populations, carried by the individuals that migrate.

An **adaptation** is a hereditary trait that increases reproductive success in a given environment. White fur hides the Arctic hare on snow, not on dark ground.

**Speciation** is the formation of a new species. It takes place when two populations stop exchanging alleles, that is, are in **reproductive isolation**, because of a geographical barrier or of different breeding periods. **Extinction** is the disappearance of all the individuals of a species. If the species disappears from one region only, the extinction is local.
:::

## Ecology: populations, interactions and energy

:::definizione{#ecologia titolo="Levels of organization"}
- A **population** is the set of individuals of one species in a given area.
- A **community** is the set of the populations of all the species of the area.
- An **ecosystem** is the community together with the physical environment (water, light, temperature) and their interactions.

The **richness** of a community is the number of its species. The **abundance** of a species is the number of its individuals. Two woods with three species have the same richness, but different abundances if the trees are 34, 33 and 33 in the first and 98, 1 and 1 in the second.

The number of individuals of a population changes according to the balance
$$
\text{change}=\text{births}-\text{deaths}+\text{immigrants}-\text{emigrants}.
$$
A population without immigrants or emigrants is called closed; if it also has no births it has only deaths, so it decreases.

**Carrying capacity** is the maximum number of individuals that the environment sustains over time with its resources. Near this number growth slows down, because the resources per individual decrease and competition increases. Carrying capacity depends on the environment: a pond sustains fewer fish in a year of drought.
:::

:::definizione{#flusso-energia titolo="Production and trophic levels"}
- **Producers**, such as plants and algae, build their own organic substances, that is, carbon-based molecules, from carbon dioxide by photosynthesis. They are therefore called **autotrophs**.
- **Consumers** obtain them by eating other organisms. They are therefore called **heterotrophs**. Herbivores are primary consumers and their predators secondary consumers.
- **Decomposers**, fungi and bacteria, break down dead organisms to simple substances, which producers reuse.

A **food chain** is a sequence of organisms in which each is the food of the next. The position in the chain is the **trophic level**.

::grafico{assi=0 griglia=0 xmin=0 xmax=12 ymin=0 ymax=4 vettori="2.6,2,4.7,2;8.8,2,9.7,2" etichette="1.1,2,grass;5.1,2,grasshopper;10,2,lizard;4.5,3.3,energy and matter" titolo="Arrows follow transfer from food to consumer"}

In the figure the grass is the producer, the grasshopper the primary consumer and the lizard the secondary consumer; the arrows go from the organism eaten to the one that eats it. Several interwoven chains form a **food web**.

**Gross primary production** is the energy that producers fix by photosynthesis. **Net primary production** is gross production minus the energy that producers consume by respiring. It is the energy of the new plant **biomass** (organic matter), available to herbivores. **Secondary production** is the new biomass of consumers.

Suppose that the grass fixes $1000\,\mathrm{kJ}$ (kilojoules) and consumes 600 by respiring: net production is $1000-600=400\,\mathrm{kJ}$. If the problem specifies an efficiency of $10\%$, one tenth of the energy passes to each level: $400\cdot 0.10=40\,\mathrm{kJ}$ to the grasshoppers and $40\cdot 0.10=4\,\mathrm{kJ}$ to the lizards. In real chains the efficiency varies. Matter is recycled by decomposers, whereas energy enters as light and at each step is partly lost as heat.
:::

:::osservazione{#interazioni titolo="Effects on both partners"}
An interaction between two species is classified according to its effect on each: a benefit ($+$), harm ($-$) or no effect ($0$).

- **Competition** ($-$ and $-$). Lions and hyenas hunt the same prey.
- **Predation** ($+$ and $-$). The fox kills and eats the hare.
- **Parasitism** ($+$ and $-$). The tick feeds on the blood of the dog without killing it.
- **Mutualism** ($+$ and $+$). The bee receives nectar and transports pollen.
- **Commensalism** ($+$ and $0$). The remora is carried by the shark.

The interaction is recognised by its effects on the two species, and not by the fact that they live close together.
:::
