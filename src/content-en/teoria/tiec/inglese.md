## Present and past tenses

:::definizione{#present-simple-e-continuous titolo="Present simple and present continuous"}
The **present simple** (*I work*, *she works*) expresses habits, general truths, and fixed schedules. The verb takes *-s* in the third-person singular. Questions and negatives use *do* and *does* (*Does she work here?*, *She doesn't work here*). The **present continuous** (*I am working*) uses *am*, *is*, or *are* followed by the *-ing* form of the verb. It describes an action in progress at the moment of speaking or a temporary situation.

- *She works in a bank.* (her usual job)
- *She is working from home this week.* (a temporary situation)

The choice between the two tenses therefore depends on the kind of action, and adverbial expressions in the sentence give clues. *Usually*, *every day*, and *often* go with the present simple, while *now*, *at the moment*, and the exclamation *Look!* go with the present continuous.
:::

:::osservazione{#verbi-di-stato titolo="Stative verbs"}
Some verbs describe states rather than actions, including *know*, *like*, *love*, *want*, *believe*, *understand*, *own*, and *need*. These verbs stay in the present simple even when the state holds at the moment of speaking, so we say *I know the answer* and *I need help now*. In a quiz, an option that uses a stative verb in the *-ing* form, such as *I am knowing*, is incorrect.
:::

:::definizione{#past-simple-e-continuous titolo="Past simple and past continuous"}
The **past simple** (*worked*, *went*) describes an action completed at a specific time in the past, often indicated by *yesterday*, *last year*, *in 2019*, or *two days ago*. Regular verbs add *-ed*. Irregular verbs have their own forms (*go*, *went*; *buy*, *bought*; *leave*, *left*). Questions and negatives use *did* with the base form, as in *Did you see it?* and *I didn't see it*. The **past continuous** (*was working*, *were working*) describes an action in progress at a particular time in the past.

Together, the two tenses distinguish a background action from an event. In *I was cooking when the phone rang*, *was cooking* describes the action in progress, while *rang* identifies the event that interrupts it.
:::

## Present perfect, past perfect, and future forms

:::definizione{#present-perfect titolo="Present perfect"}
The **present perfect** uses *have* or *has* followed by the past participle (*I have finished*, *she has gone*). It connects a past event or state with the present and has three uses.

- An experience at an unspecified time, as in *I have visited London twice*.
- An action that has just finished or whose effects are apparent now, as in *I have lost my keys* (I do not have them now).
- A state that began in the past and continues now, with *for* indicating duration and *since* the starting point, as in *She has lived here for ten years* and *She has lived here since 2016*.

In the third case Italian uses the present tense (*vive qui da dieci anni*, meaning “she has lived here for ten years”), so translating word for word produces the error *She lives here since 2016*.
:::

:::attenzione
The present perfect is *not* used with an expression that refers to a finished time in the past. The sentence *I have seen him yesterday* is incorrect. The correct form is *I saw him yesterday*, because *yesterday* refers to a time that is over and therefore requires the past simple.
:::

:::definizione{#present-perfect-continuous titolo="Present perfect continuous"}
The **present perfect continuous** uses *have been* or *has been* followed by the *-ing* form of the verb (*I have been working*). It describes an action that began in the past and is still in progress or has just ended, with the emphasis on duration. For example, *I have been waiting for an hour* corresponds to the Italian *aspetto da un'ora*. Stative verbs take the present perfect simple, as in *I have known her since school*.
:::

:::definizione{#past-perfect titolo="Past perfect"}
The **past perfect** uses *had* followed by the past participle (*had left*) and corresponds to the Italian tense called the trapassato prossimo. It describes an action that took place *before* another point in the past. In *When we arrived, the film had already started*, the film started before we arrived. The past perfect therefore shows which event came first when a narrative refers to two past times. It also occurs in the third conditional and in reported speech.
:::

:::definizione{#futuro titolo="Three ways to express the future"}
English has three main ways of referring to the future. The choice depends on the type of prediction or plan.

- *will* + base form expresses decisions made at the moment of speaking, promises, and predictions based on an opinion. *I'm thirsty, I'll get some water.* *I think it will be a good year.*
- *be going to* + base form expresses intentions already formed and predictions based on current evidence. *I'm going to study economics.* *Look at those clouds, it's going to rain.*
- The present continuous expresses arrangements already made with other people for a particular date or time. *I'm meeting the bank manager on Friday.*

The present simple follows *when*, *as soon as*, *before*, and *until* when they refer to the future, as in *I'll call you when I arrive* (in Italian, *ti chiamerò quando arriverò*).
:::

## Conditionals

:::teorema{#periodo-ipotetico-zero-e-primo titolo="Zero and first conditionals"}
A conditional sentence has a clause introduced by *if* that states the condition and a main clause that states the consequence.

- The **zero conditional** uses *if* + present simple and a main clause in the present simple. It expresses general truths and laws, as in *If you heat water to 100 degrees, it boils*.
- The **first conditional** uses *if* + present simple and a main clause with *will*, an imperative, or another modal verb. It expresses a possible future condition, as in *If it rains tomorrow, we will stay at home*.

The *if*-clause stays in the present even when it refers to the future, following the same rule as clauses introduced by *when* and *as soon as*.
:::

:::attenzione
The *if*-clause of the first conditional does not take *will*. The sentence *If it will rain, we will stay at home* is incorrect. The correct form is *If it rains, we will stay at home*, even though Italian uses a future verb in *se pioverà* (“if it rains”).
:::

:::teorema{#periodo-ipotetico-secondo titolo="The second conditional"}
In the **second conditional**, the *if*-clause uses the past simple and the main clause uses *would* followed by the base form. It expresses an unreal or unlikely present or future situation. Italian uses *se* with the imperfect subjunctive and a main clause in the present conditional. For example, *If I had more money, I would buy a car* corresponds to *se avessi più soldi, comprerei un'auto*. Here the past simple marks distance from reality rather than past time. With the verb *be*, English often uses *were* for all persons, as in the expression *If I were you, I would accept the offer*, which gives advice.
:::

:::teorema{#periodo-ipotetico-terzo titolo="The third conditional"}
In the **third conditional**, the *if*-clause uses the past perfect and the main clause uses *would have* followed by the past participle. It describes a past possibility that did not materialize. Italian uses *se* with the pluperfect subjunctive and a main clause in the past conditional. The sentence *If we had left earlier, we would have caught the train* corresponds to the Italian *se fossimo partiti prima, avremmo preso il treno*. It therefore tells us that the speakers left late and missed the train. The tense in the *if*-clause identifies the conditional type. The first uses the present simple, the second the past simple, and the third the past perfect.
:::

## Modal verbs and the passive voice

:::definizione{#verbi-modali titolo="Modal verbs"}
**Modal verbs** (*can*, *could*, *may*, *might*, *must*, *should*) take the base form without *to*. They do not take *-s* in the third-person singular and form questions and negatives without *do*, as in *She can swim*, *Can she swim?*, and *She can't swim*. *Have to*, by contrast, behaves like an ordinary verb (*she has to*, *does she have to?*). The main meanings are as follows.

- *can* and *could* express ability and permission (*Can I open the window?*). The form *could* is also the past tense of *can* (*I could swim when I was five*).
- *may* and *might* express possibility (*It may rain*, *He might be late*).
- *must* expresses an obligation the speaker feels or a deduction the speaker considers certain (*You look tired, you must be exhausted*).
- *have to* expresses an externally imposed obligation (*I have to wear a uniform at work*).
- *should* gives advice (*You should see a doctor*).

The forms *should to* and *must to* are incorrect because a modal verb always takes the base form of the following verb.
:::

:::attenzione
*Mustn't* and *don't have to* have different meanings. *You mustn't park here* expresses a prohibition (*parking here is forbidden*), while *You don't have to come* means that coming is unnecessary (*you are under no obligation to come*). *Needn't* has the same meaning as *don't have to*. For example, *You needn't hurry* means *there is no need to hurry*.
:::

:::teorema{#modali-al-passato titolo="Modal verbs with past and passive forms"}
To refer to a past event, use a modal verb + *have* + past participle.

- *must have done* expresses a deduction the speaker considers certain about the past (*The streets are wet, it must have rained*).
- *can't have done* expresses the conclusion that a past event was impossible (*She can't have seen us, she was in Rome*).
- *may have done*, *might have done*, and *could have done* express a past possibility.
- *should have done* expresses criticism or regret about something that was not done (*You should have told me*, meaning *it was a mistake not to tell me*).

This structure works because *have* + past participle places the action in the past, while the modal stays in the present and conveys the speaker’s current judgment. Past obligation takes *had to*, as in *I had to work late yesterday*. In the passive, the modal is followed by *be* and the past participle, as in *The form must be signed* and *Tickets can be bought online*.
:::

:::definizione{#forma-passiva titolo="The passive voice"}
The **passive voice** uses the verb *be*, in the tense of the active sentence, followed by the past participle. When the person or thing performing the action is mentioned, *by* introduces it. The passive is used when the focus is on the action or on the person or thing affected by it.

- present simple: *Cars are made in this factory.*
- past simple: *The bridge was built in 1990.*
- present perfect: *The results have been published.*
- future: *The decision will be announced tomorrow.*
- present continuous: *The road is being repaired.*

The tense of *be* is the tense of the verb in the active sentence. For example, *They built the bridge in 1990* becomes *The bridge was built in 1990* because *built* is in the past simple.
:::

## Reported speech and relative pronouns

:::teorema{#discorso-indiretto titolo="Reported speech"}
When a speaker’s words are reported using a past-tense reporting verb (*said*, *told*, *asked*), the tenses often shift one step back because the original remarks now belong to the reporter’s past.

| Direct speech | Reported speech |
|---|---|
| present simple (*I work*) | past simple (*he worked*) |
| present continuous (*I am working*) | past continuous (*he was working*) |
| past simple, present perfect (*I worked*, *I have worked*) | past perfect (*he had worked*) |
| *will* | *would* |
| *can*, *may* | *could*, *might* |

Pronouns and references to time and place also change when the reporter’s point of view changes. For example, *today* becomes *that day*, *tomorrow* becomes *the next day* or *the following day*, *yesterday* becomes *the day before*, and *here* becomes *there*. The verb *say* does not take the listener as its direct object (*She said that…*), while *tell* requires an object naming the listener (*She told me that…*). Consequently, *She said me* is incorrect. Indirect questions use statement word order, without *do*. For example, *"Where do you live?"* becomes *He asked me where I lived*.
:::

:::definizione{#pronomi-relativi titolo="Relative pronouns"}
**Relative pronouns** introduce clauses that give information about a noun.

- *who* refers to people (*the man who called you*).
- *which* refers to things and animals (*the car which I bought*).
- *that* refers to people and things in clauses that identify the person or thing being discussed.
- *whose* expresses possession for people and things (*the student whose bag was stolen*, meaning *the student whose bag someone stole*).
- *where* refers to places (*the town where I was born*).
- *whom* refers to a person who is the object of a verb or preposition in formal usage (*the person to whom I wrote*).

An object relative pronoun can be omitted from a clause that identifies the noun, as in *the book (that) you lent me*. Clauses set off by commas add nonessential information and do not take *that*, as in *My brother, who lives in Turin, is a doctor*.
:::

## Comparatives, quantifiers, and prepositions

:::teorema{#comparativi-e-superlativi titolo="Comparatives and superlatives"}
One-syllable adjectives and two-syllable adjectives ending in *-y* form the comparative with *-er* and the superlative with *the* … *-est*. Examples are *cheap*, *cheaper*, *the cheapest* and *easy*, *easier*, *the easiest*. Most other two-syllable adjectives and longer adjectives take *more* and *the most*, as in *expensive*, *more expensive*, *the most expensive*. The word *than* introduces the second term of a comparison, as in *This phone is cheaper than that one*. The two comparative constructions are alternatives, so *more cheaper* is incorrect.

Common irregular forms include *good*, *better*, *the best*; *bad*, *worse*, *the worst*; and *far*, *further*, *the furthest*. Equality is expressed with *as* … *as*, as in *She is as tall as her brother*. A superlative is often followed by the present perfect with *ever*, as in *the best film I have ever seen*.
:::

:::teorema{#quantificatori titolo="Quantifiers"}
The choice of quantifier depends on the type of noun. **Count nouns** have plural forms (*books*, *eggs*). **Noncount nouns** have no plural in these uses (*money*, *milk*, *information*, *advice*).

| | Count nouns | Noncount nouns |
|---|---|---|
| A large number or amount | *many* | *much* |
| A small number or amount | *few*, *a few* | *little*, *a little* |

*Some* is used in affirmative sentences and in offers or requests (*Would you like some tea?*), while *any* is used in negatives and questions (*Is there any milk?*). *A lot of* can be used with either type of noun. *Few* and *little* without an article carry a negative sense (*not many or much, not enough*), while *a few* and *a little* carry a positive sense (*some, a small amount*). The sentence *I have a few friends here* means that the speaker does have some friends here. *Information* and *advice* are noncount nouns even though their Italian equivalents *informazioni* and *consigli* are plural, so *informations* and *an advice* are incorrect.
:::

:::definizione{#preposizioni titolo="Prepositions of time and place"}
**Prepositions of time** range from those used for broad periods to those used for precise moments.

- *in* is used for months, years, seasons, and parts of the day (*in May*, *in 2007*, *in the morning*).
- *on* is used for days and dates (*on Monday*, *on 12 May*, *on Christmas Day*).
- *at* is used for clock times and certain specific times (*at 9 o'clock*, *at night*, *at the weekend*).

Use *for* to give a duration (*for two hours*), *since* to give a starting point (*since Monday*), and *during* to name the period in which something happens (*during the meeting*). The preposition *by* indicates a deadline (*by Friday*, meaning *no later than Friday*).

The main **prepositions of place** are *in* for being inside an area (*in the room*, *in Milan*), *on* for a surface or level (*on the table*, *on the second floor*), and *at* for a point or a place where an activity takes place (*at the station*, *at home*, *at work*). Movement toward a place takes *to* (*go to school*), and movement from outside to inside takes *into* (*walk into the room*). With *arrive*, cities and countries take *in*, while buildings and specific locations take *at*, as in *arrive in London* and *arrive at the airport*.
:::

## Vocabulary, phrasal verbs, false friends, and synonyms

:::definizione{#phrasal-verbs titolo="Phrasal verbs"}
A **phrasal verb** is a verb followed by a particle (*up*, *off*, *on*, *out*, *in*). Together they have a new meaning, often quite different from that of the verb alone. The following are among the most common in B1–B2 texts.

| Phrasal verb | Meaning |
|---|---|
| *call off* | cancel |
| *put off* | postpone |
| *carry on* | continue |
| *find out* | discover |
| *give up* | quit, stop doing something |
| *look after* | take care of |
| *look forward to* | anticipate with pleasure |
| *fill in* | complete (a form) |
| *set up* | start, establish |
| *turn down* | refuse (an offer) |
| *take over* | acquire, assume control of |
| *turn up* | appear, arrive |

A verb following *look forward to* takes the *-ing* form. For example, *I look forward to hearing from you* is a common closing line in formal emails.
:::

:::definizione{#falsi-amici titolo="False friends"}
**False friends** are English words that resemble Italian words but have a different meaning.

| English word | Meaning in English and Italian | Similar Italian word | English translation of the Italian word |
|---|---|---|---|
| *actually* | in fact (in realtà) | attualmente | *currently* |
| *eventually* | in the end (alla fine) | eventualmente | *possibly*, *if necessary* |
| *sensible* | levelheaded, reasonable (assennato, ragionevole) | sensibile | *sensitive* |
| *library* | a place to borrow books (biblioteca) | libreria | *bookshop* |
| *comprehensive* | complete (completo) | comprensivo | *understanding* |
| *argument* | a quarrel or a reasoned case (litigio, argomentazione) | argomento | *topic* |
| *pretend* | feign (fingere) | pretendere | *expect*, *demand* |
| *attend* | go to, participate in (frequentare, partecipare) | attendere | *wait for* |
| *sympathetic* | understanding, supportive (comprensivo, solidale) | simpatico | *nice*, *friendly* |
| *convenient* | handy, practical (comodo, pratico) | conveniente | *cheap* |

A false friend is often one of the incorrect options on a vocabulary quiz because it appeals to readers who translate by sound. In *She attends a course in Milan*, the verb means “goes to,” the Italian *frequenta*.
:::

:::osservazione{#lessico-in-contesto titolo="Synonyms and vocabulary in context"}
Many questions ask which word completes a sentence or which word is closest in meaning to a given word. Many English words have several meanings, but only one fits a particular context. The following pairs are often confused.

- *raise* (lift or increase something) and *rise* (go up or increase on its own), as in *The company raised its prices* and *Prices rose last year*.
- *borrow* (take something on loan) and *lend* (give something on loan), as in *Can I borrow your pen?* and *Can you lend me your pen?*
- *remember* (keep something in mind) and *remind* (prompt someone to remember something), as in *Remind me to call her*.
- *make* and *do*, as in *make a mistake*, *make a decision*, *do homework*, and *do business*.

Two words are synonyms in a given context if replacing one with the other keeps the sentence correct and preserves its meaning. In *The meeting was postponed*, *postponed* can be replaced with *put off*, whereas *cancelled* would mean that the meeting had been *called off*.
:::

## Notices, messages, and short passages

:::definizione{#avvisi-e-messaggi titolo="Notices, signs, and messages"}
Some questions present a short text, such as a sign, notice, text message, email, or note, and ask what it means or which statement is true. These texts use standard expressions.

| Expression | Meaning |
|---|---|
| *Out of order* | broken or temporarily unavailable |
| *Staff only* | reserved for employees |
| *No entry* | entry is prohibited |
| *Sold out* | none left to buy |
| *Handle with care* | be careful when handling the item |
| *Refunds only with receipt* | you must show the receipt to get your money back |
| *We apologise for any inconvenience* | we are sorry for the trouble caused |

Formal emails use *Dear Sir or Madam*, *I am writing to…*, *Please find attached…* (*I have enclosed…*), *I look forward to hearing from you*, and closings such as *Yours faithfully* and *Kind regards*.
:::

:::esempio{titolo="Reading a notice"}
The sign *Customers are reminded that bags must be left at the entrance* says that customers have to leave their bags at the entrance. *Are reminded* is the passive of *remind* (*prompt someone to remember*), and *must be left* is a passive modal construction that expresses an obligation. An option such as *Customers can leave their bags at the entrance if they want* turns that obligation into a choice, so it is incorrect.
:::

:::osservazione{#brani-brevi titolo="Understanding a passage"}
Questions on an English passage are of the same types as those in the Italian reading comprehension test. They ask about the main idea, explicit information, inferences, or the meaning of a word in context. See the chapter on reading comprehension for details. The incorrect options also follow the same patterns.

- An option repeats words in the passage but changes a detail, such as a number, the subject, or the word *some* to *all*.
- An option is plausible but the passage does not support it.
- An option reverses a relationship, for example by swapping cause and effect or reading *reopened* as *opened*.

Linking words reveal the structure of a text. *However*, *although*, and *despite* introduce a contrast. A cause can be introduced by *because*, *since*, or *due to*. A consequence can be introduced by *therefore*, *as a result*, or *so*, while *moreover* and *in addition* add information. A specific sentence in the passage always supports the correct answer.
:::

## Quick strategies for quizzes

:::osservazione{#metodi-rapidi titolo="Quick strategies for English quizzes"}
With about one minute per question, you can find many answers by looking at a few features of the sentence.

- **Time expressions**. *Yesterday*, *ago*, and *last* point to the past simple. Expressions such as *since*, *for*, *already*, *yet*, *ever*, and *never* can point to the present perfect. When *by the time* or *when* is followed by the past simple, the past perfect can mark the earlier action. Expressions such as *now* and *Look!* point to the present continuous.
- **Tense of the *if*-clause**. The present simple, past simple, and past perfect identify the first, second, and third conditionals, respectively. The main clause accordingly takes *will*, *would*, or *would have*.
- **Eliminating grammatically incorrect options**. A modal followed by *to*, a double comparative (*more bigger*), *say* with the listener as its direct object, *will* in the *if*-clause of a first conditional, and a stative verb in the *-ing* form are all errors, so options containing them can be ruled out before considering their meaning.
- **False friends**. Check the actual meaning of an option that looks very similar to an Italian word (*actually*, *eventually*, *sensible*), because it is often a distractor.
- **Notices and passages**. Read the question first, then look for the relevant sentence in the text. An option that says *all*, *always*, or *never* where the text says *some*, *often*, or *rarely* is incorrect.

If two options remain, reread the full sentence with each one and check which follows all the rules discussed above.
:::
