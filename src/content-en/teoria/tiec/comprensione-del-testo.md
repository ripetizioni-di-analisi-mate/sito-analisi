titolo: Reading comprehension
sommario: How to read an informational or argumentative passage and answer TIEC comprehension questions about claims and arguments, explicit and implicit information, supported and unsupported inferences, meaning in context, connectives, the main idea, numerical data, and common distractors.

## Informational and argumentative texts

:::definizione{#testo-informativo titolo="Informational text"}
An **informational text** reports facts, figures, and explanations about a subject without arguing for a position. Its sentences describe what happened, how widespread a phenomenon is, or what accounts for it according to those who have studied it. An article reporting how the number of music streaming subscribers has changed over five years, with figures and the reasons analysts give for the change, is an informational text.

Recognizing the type of text helps you understand what the questions ask. Questions on an informational text mainly concern the facts reported and how they are related: what increased, why, and compared with what.
:::

:::definizione{#testo-argomentativo titolo="Argumentative text"}
An **argumentative text** takes a position and gives reasons to support it. The author wants to persuade the reader that a statement is true or a course of action is right. An article arguing that towns should keep libraries open into the evening because students who work can only visit after 6 p.m. is an argumentative text.

Many TIEC passages combine the two types by reporting data and then drawing on them to argue for a position. Questions about the author’s opinion concern only statements in which the author makes a judgment. In these passages, therefore, distinguish statements of fact from statements that express an evaluation.
:::

:::definizione{#tesi-e-argomenti titolo="Thesis, arguments, examples, and conclusion"}
An argumentative text generally contains the following elements.

- The **thesis** is the position the author defends.
- The **arguments** are the reasons given in support of the thesis.
- Examples and data are specific cases and figures that lend credibility to the arguments.
- A counterargument, when present, puts forward an opposing position that the author introduces in order to answer it.
- The conclusion restates the thesis, often as a proposal.

The thesis can appear at the beginning, in the middle, or at the end. A sentence states the thesis if the passage still makes sense when the arguments are followed by *therefore* and that sentence. Questions such as “What is the author’s thesis?” and “What purpose does the figure in the second paragraph serve?” ask you to identify these roles.
:::

:::esempio{titolo="The roles of sentences in a short argumentative text"}
Consider the passage: “Midsize companies would benefit from allowing employees to work from home one day a week. Avoiding the commute saves time and money and leaves people less tired when they start work. At a logistics company that adopted this policy, sick leave fell within a year. Some people worry that working from home weakens collaboration among colleagues, but one day a week still leaves four days in the office. A cautious policy, therefore, offers benefits without serious risks.”

| statement | role |
| --- | --- |
| companies would benefit from allowing one day of work from home | thesis |
| avoiding the commute saves time and money | argument |
| sick leave fell at the logistics company | example with supporting data |
| working from home weakens collaboration | counterargument |
| one day still leaves four days in the office | response to the counterargument |
| a cautious policy offers benefits without serious risks | conclusion |

The passage mentions the concern about collaboration, but the author does not share it. An option attributing that concern to the author is therefore wrong even if it repeats words from the text.
:::

## Explicit and implicit information

:::definizione{#informazione-esplicita titolo="Explicit information"}
Information is **explicit** when the text states it, even if an answer choice uses different words. If the passage says, “In 2025 the company’s revenue rose for the third consecutive year,” then “The company’s revenue has risen for three years in a row” is explicit. It expresses the same information in a different form.

Prompts such as “According to the passage…” and “The passage states that…” ask for explicit information. The correct answer restates a specific sentence in the passage, and you can point to that sentence.
:::

:::definizione{#informazione-implicita titolo="Implicit information"}
Information is **implicit** when the text does not state it directly but it follows from what the text says. If a passage says, “The store opens at 9 a.m., and the newspapers are delivered an hour before it opens,” then “The newspapers arrive at 8 a.m.” is implicit. It does not appear in the passage, yet anyone who accepts the two statements must also accept this conclusion.

Prompts such as “It can be inferred from the passage that…” and “The passage implies that…” ask for implicit information. The correct answer follows from one or more statements in the passage through a logical step that adds no new facts.
:::

:::attenzione{#conoscenze-del-lettore titolo="What the reader already knows is irrelevant"}
A statement that is true in the real world but absent from the passage is *not* a correct answer. If a passage describes rising rents in university towns without giving a cause, the option “Rents are rising because many apartments are rented to tourists” might be true, but the passage does not support it. In a comprehension question the text is the only source, so that option is wrong.
:::

## Supported and unsupported inferences

:::definizione{#inferenza titolo="Inference"}
An **inference** is a conclusion the reader draws from the text. An inference is **supported** when it follows from the statements in the text without requiring further information. It is **unsupported** when it requires a fact the text does not provide or asserts more than the text says.

Consider the statement “In the northern district, three of the four newsstands that were there five years ago have closed.”

- “Of the newsstands that were there five years ago, only one is still open” is a supported inference because four minus three is one.
- “There is only one newsstand in the northern district today” is unsupported because new ones may have opened in the meantime.
- “Newsstands are closing because people read the news on their phones” is unsupported because the text gives no cause for the closures.
:::

:::teorema{#controllo-dell-inferenza titolo="Testing an inference"}
An option gives a supported inference if it remains true in *every* situation consistent with the text. If there is a situation in which all the statements in the passage are true and the option is false, the inference is unsupported.

This rule works because a supported inference adds nothing to the text and therefore holds whenever the text is true. In the newsstand example, imagine that two new newsstands opened in the district. The statement in the text would still be true, but the option “There is only one newsstand today” would be false. The option is therefore unsupported.
:::

:::osservazione{#parole-spia titolo="Words that change the scope of a statement"}
Many unsupported inferences repeat a statement from the text but change one word to make a stronger claim.

- *Quantity*: the text says “some” or “many,” but the option says “all” or “most.”
- *Frequency*: the text says “often,” but the option says “always.”
- *Certainty*: the text says “could” or “is expected to,” but the option says “will.”
- *Time*: the text says “so far” or “over the last two years,” but the option says “always.”

If the passage says “Many of the store’s customers pay with their phones,” the option “Most customers pay with their phones” is unsupported because “many” can also describe a sizable minority.
:::

:::attenzione{#correlazione-e-causa titolo="Two developments in the same period"}
Two developments that increase over the same period are *not* necessarily cause and effect. If a passage says, “Over the past three years, both the number of kilometers of bike lanes in the city and sales of electric bikes have increased,” the option “The new lanes drove up sales” assigns a cause that the text does not identify and is therefore unsupported. It is supported only if the passage itself presents one fact as the cause of the other, for example by using *because* or *as a result of*.
:::

## The meaning of words and expressions in context

:::definizione{#significato-nel-contesto titolo="Meaning in context"}
Many words have more than one meaning, and the sentence selects one of them. A word’s **meaning in context** is the sense it has in the passage. For instance, the Italian word *rete* refers to a set of connected points in “The company’s sales network includes forty stores,” but it refers to the internet in “Online purchases have increased.”

Questions such as “In the passage, the word… means” ask for meaning in context. Often one of the options gives another meaning of the word that would be correct in a dictionary but is wrong in that sentence.
:::

:::teorema{#prova-della-sostituzione titolo="The substitution test"}
An option gives the meaning in context if substituting it for the word in the passage leaves the sense of the sentence and its connection to the neighboring sentences unchanged.

The test works because a synonym in context leaves the meaning intact. In “The company *contained* its transportation costs by renegotiating its contracts,” substituting *limited* leaves the meaning unchanged. Substituting *included*, another meaning of *contained*, gives a sentence that does not fit the reference to renegotiating contracts.
:::

:::osservazione{#riferimenti-nel-testo titolo="Pronouns and expressions that refer back"}
Italian pronouns such as *questo* (“this”), *ciò* (“that”), *esso* (“it”), and the object pronoun *lo* (“it” or “him”), as well as expressions such as “this phenomenon” or “that choice,” refer back to something already mentioned. Questions such as “What does… refer to?” ask you to identify it. Look in the immediately preceding sentences for a word or phrase that fits in gender, number, and meaning.

In the passage “Many downtown stores have reduced their evening hours. This decision has put people who work until 7 p.m. at a disadvantage,” the phrase “this decision” refers to the reduction in evening hours, the only decision mentioned in the preceding sentence.
:::

:::osservazione{#espressioni-figurate titolo="Figurative expressions"}
Passages on current affairs often use Italian figurative expressions such as *a macchia d’olio* (“like a spreading oil slick”), *il rovescio della medaglia* (“the other side of the coin”), and *fare i conti con* (“come to grips with”). Their meaning in context is figurative. When an Italian passage says that online shopping spread “a macchia d’olio,” it means that online shopping spread rapidly over a wider and wider area. An answer about oily substances takes the expression literally.
:::

## Connectives and logical relationships between statements

:::definizione{#connettivi titolo="Connectives and logical relationships"}
A **connective** is a word or phrase that links two sentences or two parts of a sentence and indicates their logical relationship. The table lists the relationships most frequently found in passages.

| relationship | what the part introduced by the connective does | connectives |
| --- | --- | --- |
| cause | gives the reason for something | because, since, given that, as |
| explanation | confirms or clarifies what came before | in fact, that is |
| consequence | gives something that follows from what came before | so, therefore, consequently, thus |
| contrast | runs counter to what came before | but, however, nevertheless, instead |
| concession | would lead the reader to expect the opposite of the main clause | although, though, even though |
| addition | adds information of the same kind | furthermore, also, moreover |

Questions ask what relationship a connective expresses in the passage, or which connective could replace it without changing the meaning of the sentence.
:::

:::teorema{#connettivi-equivalenti titolo="Equivalent connectives"}
Two connectives can be interchanged in a sentence if they express the same logical relationship, though the verb mood or the order of the clauses may have to change. In Italian, *benché* (“although”) and *sebbene* (“though”) take the subjunctive, while *poiché* (“since”), *anche se* (“even though”), and *tuttavia* (“however”) are used here with the indicative.

The sentences “Although the price rose, sales increased” and “The price rose. Nevertheless, sales increased” have the same meaning. In both, the first fact would suggest a decline in sales and the second says the opposite. “The price rose, so sales increased” has a different meaning because it presents the price increase as the cause of sales growth.
:::

:::attenzione{#concessione-e-causa titolo="Mistaking a concession for a cause"}
A clause introduced by *even though*, *although*, or *though* does *not* explain the main clause. In “Even though wages stayed the same, spending on travel increased,” flat wages would suggest a decline in spending. An option stating that “Travel spending increased because wages stayed the same” turns the concession into a cause and reverses the relationship between the facts.
:::

## Main idea and title

:::definizione{#idea-principale titolo="Main idea"}
The **main idea** of a passage is the statement that sums up what the entire text says. Each paragraph develops, explains, or supports it. In an argumentative text it usually coincides with the thesis. In an informational text it is the general fact that the details describe.

To find the main idea, summarize each paragraph in a sentence and look for the statement that encompasses all the summaries. “What is the main idea?”, “Which statement best summarizes the passage?”, and “Which title is most appropriate?” ask for that statement in different ways.
:::

:::teorema{#titolo-adatto titolo="Choosing the best title"}
The best title covers the *whole* passage and *only* the passage. A title that is **too broad** covers a wider subject than the passage. One that is **too narrow** covers only a single detail or paragraph.

Consider a passage describing a decline in births in a mountain town, the closure of two of its three schools, and the move of some families down to the valley.

| title | assessment |
| --- | --- |
| The Italian population | too broad |
| The school bus in one village | too narrow |
| Fewer births, fewer schools: a mountain town changes | appropriate |
| Mountain schools are the best | not stated in the passage |
:::

:::osservazione{#scopo-del-testo titolo="The author’s purpose"}
Some questions ask the purpose of a passage: to inform, persuade, criticize a decision, propose a solution, or compare two situations. Look for clues in the language. Conditional verb forms such as the Italian *dovrebbe* (“should”), judgments such as *it is a mistake*, and proposals suggest that the author wants to persuade. Data, descriptions, and explanations attributed to others (“according to experts”) suggest an informational text. A passage describing how filtered-water dispensers work and how much they are used, without passing judgment, aims to inform even if the reader concludes that the dispensers are useful.
:::

## Numerical data in a text

:::definizione{#dati-nel-testo titolo="Reading numerical data"}
Passages contain percentages, changes, and comparisons. Every percentage refers to a **total**, so “$40\%$ of customers” and “$40\%$ of sales” are different quantities. To interpret a figure, identify the quantity measured, the reference total, and the period covered.

If the passage says that “In 2025, $30\%$ of a company’s $2000$ employees worked from home at least one day a week,” the number who worked from home is
$$
\frac{30}{100} \cdot 2000 = 600 .
$$
The calculations required in the passages are short. Percentage calculations are covered in the chapter “Sets, Numbers, and Percentages.”
:::

:::osservazione{#punti-percentuali titolo="Percentage points and percent change"}
When a share rises from $20\%$ to $25\%$, the difference is $5$ **percentage points**, while the percent change is
$$
\frac{25 - 20}{20} \cdot 100 = 25\% .
$$
If the passage says the share “rose by $5$ points,” the option “It rose by $5\%$” confuses the two measures and is wrong. Both interpretations often appear among the answer choices.
:::

:::osservazione{#confronti-numerici titolo="Double, half, one-third"}
Words expressing comparisons correspond to specific operations.

- “It doubled” means it was multiplied by $2$, an increase of $100\%$.
- “It fell by one-third” means $\dfrac{2}{3}$ remains.
- “More than half” means more than $50\%$.

If the number of subscribers rises from $1200$ to $3000$, the ratio is $\dfrac{3000}{1200} = 2.5$. The number therefore more than doubled, and an option saying it tripled is wrong.
:::

:::attenzione{#percentuale-e-quantita titolo="A higher percentage and a larger quantity"}
A higher percentage does *not* necessarily mean a larger quantity. If $60\%$ of $10\,000$ residents use public transportation in one city, and $40\%$ of $30\,000$ residents do so in another, there are $6000$ users in the first city and $12\,000$ in the second. The option “More people use public transportation in the first city” compares the percentages in place of the quantities and is wrong.
:::

## Common distractors

:::definizione{#distrattori-tipici titolo="Distractors"}
A **distractor** is an incorrect option designed to look right. In reading comprehension questions, distractors often repeat words from the passage to appeal to readers who look only for matching words. There are five common types.

- *True but unstated*: a plausible statement, or one that is true in the real world, about something the passage does not mention.
- *Too broad*: a statement about a subject wider than the one the passage addresses.
- *Too narrow*: a true detail from the passage offered as the answer to a question about the whole.
- *Reversed*: a statement that reverses a relationship in the passage, such as cause and effect, increase and decrease, or the positions of the proposer and the objector.
- *Exaggerated*: a statement that strengthens a claim in the passage by using words such as “all,” “always,” or “certainly.”

Identifying the type of distractor gives you a specific reason to eliminate each wrong option.
:::

:::esempio{titolo="Five options for a short passage"}
Consider the passage: “A provincial city introduced a single monthly pass for buses and shared bikes. In the first year, twice as many people subscribed as expected, especially university students. The city now intends to extend the pass to neighboring municipalities.” The question asks which statement best summarizes the passage.

| option | type |
| --- | --- |
| A combined bus and bike pass exceeded expectations, and the city wants to extend it | correct |
| Shared bikes reduce traffic | true but unstated |
| Public transportation is important to cities | too broad |
| Many subscribers are university students | too narrow |
| There were half as many subscribers as expected | reversed |

The option about students reports a fact from the passage, but it covers only one detail of the second sentence, so it does not summarize the text.
:::

## Quick approaches to the questions

:::osservazione{#tempo-della-prova titolo="Time available"}
The TIEC has $48$ questions in $60$ minutes, divided into four areas with $12$ questions each. Reading comprehension is one of the four. The average time per question is
$$
\frac{60 \cdot 60}{48} = 75 \text{ seconds} .
$$
In comprehension questions some of this time goes toward reading the passage. When several questions follow one passage, you only need to read it once, saving time across the set. To meet the minimum requirement in this area, you need at least $6$ correct answers out of $12$.
:::

:::teorema{#leggere-prima-le-domande titolo="Reading the questions before the passage"}
Read the questions, without their answer choices, before the passage. As you read, you will already know what to look for: a figure, the meaning of a word, the author’s thesis, or the relationship expressed by a connective. This works because targeted reading helps you find the relevant sentences immediately and reduces rereading.

If a question asks what *nuanced* means in the second paragraph, pause at that sentence and those around it. If another question asks for the author’s thesis, mark statements that express judgments or use conditional forms.
:::

:::teorema{#riga-che-giustifica titolo="The line that supports the answer"}
An option is correct when you can point to the sentence in the passage that supports it and the option says neither more nor less than that sentence. If defending an option requires outside knowledge or a stronger word than the text uses, the option is a distractor.

The rule follows from the definitions of explicit information and supported inference, both of which rely only on statements in the text. If the passage says “Some retailers still accept cash so as not to exclude older customers,” the option “Retailers accept cash so as not to exclude older customers” drops the word “some” and generalizes to all retailers. No line therefore supports it.
:::

:::osservazione{#eliminazione-delle-opzioni titolo="Eliminating options"}
When the answer is not obvious, eliminate options in the following order.

1. Options that contradict the passage, often by *reversing* a relationship.
2. Options using stronger words than the passage, such as “all,” “always,” and “certainly.”
3. Options the passage does not mention, even if they are true in the real world.

Usually one or two options remain. Compare them with the line that supports the answer. In questions with a negative, such as “Which of the following statements is NOT supported by the passage?”, the procedure is reversed: three options have a supporting line, and the correct answer is the only one that does not.
:::
