## States of matter

:::definizione{#stati titolo="Solid, liquid and gas"}
Every substance is made of very small particles, that is, molecules or atoms. The same substance can be found in three states.

- In a **solid** the particles oscillate about fixed positions and do not continually exchange places. For this reason a solid has a shape of its own.
- In a **liquid** the particles stay close together and move past one another. A liquid takes the shape of its container and keeps almost the same volume.
- In a **gas** the particles are much further apart. The **sample**, that is, the portion of substance being studied, fills the whole available volume.

The **system** is the part of matter being studied, for example the contents of a glass. A **phase** is a part of the system with the same physical properties at every point, such as rigidity and density. The ice and the liquid water in a glass are made of the same molecules, but the ice is rigid and the water flows. They are therefore two phases of the same substance.
:::

:::osservazione{#coexist titolo="Coexistence and fixed pressure"}
A **pure substance** is made of a single kind of molecule, such as distilled water. Two phases are **in equilibrium** when they are present together and neither grows at the expense of the other without energy being exchanged with the outside. A pure substance has two phases in equilibrium at only one temperature, called the **transition temperature**, which depends on the applied pressure.

**Normal atmospheric pressure** is the average pressure of the air at sea level and is about $101\,\mathrm{kPa}$. The pascal ($\mathrm{Pa}$) is the unit of pressure and one kilopascal is $1\,\mathrm{kPa}=1000\,\mathrm{Pa}$. At this pressure liquid water and ice coexist near $0\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$.

If energy enters, part of the ice melts and the **liquid fraction**, that is, the part of the total mass that is liquid, increases. As long as both phases are present, the temperature stays approximately fixed at $0\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$. If the pressure changes, the transition temperature also changes, so it is found again for the new pressure.
:::

:::esempio{#frazioni titolo="Same temperature, different amounts of liquid"}
Two vessels contain $0.100\,\mathrm{kg}$ and $0.200\,\mathrm{kg}$ of ice at $0\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$, at normal atmospheric pressure. The joule ($\mathrm J$) is the unit of energy and one kilojoule is $1\,\mathrm{kJ}=1000\,\mathrm J$. Each vessel receives $16.7\,\mathrm{kJ}$ of **useful** energy, that is, $16.7\,\mathrm{kJ}$ that actually reach the ice, and the temperature stays at zero.

The symbol $L_f$ denotes the energy needed to melt one kilogram of ice. For water $L_f=334\,\mathrm{kJ/kg}$, which reads *334 kilojoules per kilogram*. The same mass therefore melts in each vessel:
$$
\frac{16.7\,\mathrm{kJ}}{334\,\mathrm{kJ/kg}}=0.050\,\mathrm{kg}.
$$
In the first vessel this mass is $\dfrac{0.050}{0.100}=0.5$, that is, half of the ice. In the second it is $\dfrac{0.050}{0.200}=0.25$, that is, a quarter.

In both vessels the thermometer reads $0\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$. The thermometer reading therefore shows that melting is taking place and does not indicate how much has already become liquid. To tell the two vessels apart, a measurement of the melted mass or of the energy transferred during melting is needed. More ice requires more energy to reach the same melted fraction, and indeed melting half of the ice takes $16.7\,\mathrm{kJ}$ in the first vessel and $33.4\,\mathrm{kJ}$ in the second. The melted fraction equals $1$, that is, all the ice is liquid, only at the end of the transition.

In the graph the horizontal axis shows the energy received $Q$ in kilojoules and the vertical axis the melted percentage. The steeper line belongs to the $0.100\,\mathrm{kg}$ sample, which reaches $100\%$ at $33.4\,\mathrm{kJ}$. The other reaches $100\%$ at $66.8\,\mathrm{kJ}$.

::grafico{linee="0,0 33.4,100;0,0 66.8,100" punti="16.7,50,pieno;16.7,25,pieno" segmenti="16.7,0,16.7,50" etichette="19,45,50%;19,18,25%" xmin=0 xmax=72 ymin=0 ymax=112 nomi="Q (kJ),Melted (%)" titolo="Equal energy inputs melt equal masses. The melted percentage is higher for the 0.100 kg sample."}
:::

## Transitions between the phases

:::definizione{#transizioni titolo="Names of the transitions"}
Each transition between two states has a name. **Vapour** is a substance in the gas state, such as water vapour.

- **Melting** changes a solid into a liquid and **freezing** is the reverse transition, from liquid to solid.
- **Vaporisation** changes a liquid into a gas and **condensation** changes a gas into a liquid.
- **Sublimation** is the direct change from solid to gas, without passing through the liquid. **Deposition** is the reverse process, from gas to solid.

**Dry ice**, that is, solid carbon dioxide, becomes a gas without forming a liquid, so it sublimes. **Frost** is made of ice crystals that the water vapour in the air deposits directly on a very cold surface.
:::

:::osservazione{titolo="Heat sign for the sample"}
**Heat** $Q$ is the energy that the sample receives or gives up by exchanging it with the **surroundings**, that is, with everything around it. The sign of $Q$ is fixed before substituting values. Here $Q$ is positive if the energy enters the sample and negative if it leaves. The table applies to transitions at the equilibrium temperature and pressure, with $L>0$, where $L$ is the energy per kilogram required by the transition.

| Initial and final phases | Transition | Sign of $Q$ |
| --- | --- | ---: |
| Solid → liquid | Melting | $+$ |
| Liquid → solid | Freezing | $-$ |
| Liquid → gas | Vaporisation | $+$ |
| Gas → liquid | Condensation | $-$ |
| Solid → gas | Sublimation | $+$ |
| Gas → solid | Deposition | $-$ |

If the surroundings receiving the energy are chosen as the system, the sign is the opposite of the sample's sign. The **magnitude** of a number is its value without sign and is written between two vertical bars, for example $|-33.4|=33.4$. For a transition and its reverse, involving the same mass under the same conditions, the two heat transfers have the same magnitude and opposite signs. The durations of the two transitions, on the other hand, change if the power changes, that is, the energy that enters or leaves every second.
:::

:::esempio{#ciclo titolo="Melting and freezing at different powers"}
A $0.10\,\mathrm{kg}$ sample of water starts as ice at zero. **Power** $P$ is measured in watts, with $1\,\mathrm W=1\,\mathrm{J/s}$ (one joule per second). **Useful power** is the part that actually reaches the sample.

- Melting. With $L_f=334\,\mathrm{kJ/kg}$ the sample absorbs $0.10\cdot334\,\mathrm{kJ}=33.4\,\mathrm{kJ}=33\,400\,\mathrm J$. At a useful power of $334\,\mathrm W$ melting takes $\dfrac{33\,400\,\mathrm J}{334\,\mathrm W}=100\,\mathrm s$.
- Freezing. Immediately afterwards a refrigerator, that is, a device that removes energy like a fridge, extracts $167\,\mathrm W$ from the sample. To freeze, the sample gives up $33\,400\,\mathrm J$ again, which at this power takes $\dfrac{33\,400\,\mathrm J}{167\,\mathrm W}=200\,\mathrm s$.

At every instant the graph shows the total energy exchanged by the sample since the start, with inputs counted as positive and outputs as negative. The total rises from $0$ to $33.4\,\mathrm{kJ}$ in the first $100\,\mathrm s$ and returns to $0$ in the following $200\,\mathrm s$. At the end the algebraic total, that is, the sum with signs, is zero. In the two intervals the vertical coordinate, that is, the value on the vertical axis, changes by $33.4\,\mathrm{kJ}$ in magnitude, so the two energies transferred have the same magnitude.

::grafico{linee="0,0 100,33.4 300,0" punti="100,33.4,pieno;300,0,pieno" xmin=0 xmax=320 ymin=0 ymax=40 nomi="t (s),Q (kJ)" titolo="Over 100 s, 33.4 kJ enters. Over the following 200 s, the same energy leaves the sample."}
:::

## Latent heat

:::teorema{#latente titolo="Energy needed for a phase change"}
The **specific latent heat** $L$ of a transition is the energy needed to convert one kilogram of substance at the transition temperature. It is measured in joules per kilogram ($\mathrm{J/kg}$). It is called *latent*, that is, hidden, because during the transition the thermometer reading does not change.

Let $m_{\mathrm{tr}}$ denote the mass that changes phase. The small letters written below, $\mathrm{tr}$, are called a **subscript** and stand for "transformed". One kilogram requires $L$ joules in magnitude, so two kilograms require $2L$. At fixed transition pressure and temperature, heat is therefore directly proportional to the mass converted, that is, it doubles if the mass doubles:
$$
|Q|=m_{\mathrm{tr}}L.
$$

In the sample balance, melting and vaporisation have $Q>0$, that is, $Q=m_{\mathrm{tr}}L$. Freezing and condensation have $Q<0$, that is, $Q=-m_{\mathrm{tr}}L$. The value of $L$ is always a positive quantity and the sign depends only on the direction of the transition. The same applies to sublimation and deposition, each with its own latent heat.

At normal atmospheric pressure, the following values are used for water. The subscript $f$ denotes melting (fusion) and the subscript $v$ vaporisation.
$$
L_f=334\,000\,\mathrm{J/kg}=334\,\mathrm{kJ/kg},
\qquad
L_v=2.26\cdot10^6\,\mathrm{J/kg}=2260\,\mathrm{kJ/kg}.
$$
Freezing uses the same $L_f$ and condensation the same $L_v$, because they are the reverse transitions.
:::

:::esempio{titolo="Using the mass actually converted"}
$20.04\,\mathrm{kJ}=20\,040\,\mathrm J$ is supplied to $0.10\,\mathrm{kg}$ of ice at its melting point. The mass melted is
$$
m_{\mathrm{tr}}=\frac{20\,040\,\mathrm J}{334\,000\,\mathrm{J/kg}}=0.060\,\mathrm{kg}.
$$
There is $0.10-0.060=0.040\,\mathrm{kg}$ of ice left. Melting all the mass would take $0.10\cdot334\,000\,\mathrm J=33\,400\,\mathrm J$, that is, $33.4\,\mathrm{kJ}$. The energy supplied is smaller, so both ice and water remain and the temperature of the system stays at the melting point.

In the graph the melted mass, in grams, grows with the energy received and is $60\,\mathrm g$ at $20.04\,\mathrm{kJ}$. At $33.4\,\mathrm{kJ}$ it reaches the $100\,\mathrm g$ of available ice and then stays constant.

::grafico{linee="0,0 33.4,100 41,100" punti="20.04,60,pieno;33.4,100,pieno" etichette="18,67,60 g;34,105,100 g" xmin=0 xmax=43 ymin=0 ymax=120 nomi="Q (kJ),m (g)" titolo="The melted mass grows until all the available ice has melted."}
:::

:::esempio{#calibrazione-latente titolo="Finding latent heat from an interval"}
A **calorimeter** is an insulated vessel in which energy exchanges are measured. This calorimeter has a display showing the total energy supplied since it was switched on. It contains $0.10\,\mathrm{kg}$ of a pure solid substance, held at constant pressure. Melting starts when the display shows $4.5\,\mathrm{kJ}$ and ends when it shows $29.5\,\mathrm{kJ}$. The first $4.5\,\mathrm{kJ}$ warm the solid up to the melting temperature. The energy for melting is therefore the difference between the two readings:
$$
Q_{\mathrm{fus}}=(29.5-4.5)\,\mathrm{kJ}=25\,\mathrm{kJ},
\qquad
L_f=\frac{25\,\mathrm{kJ}}{0.10\,\mathrm{kg}}=250\,\mathrm{kJ/kg}.
$$

During melting the thermometer always shows the same temperature, so its reading gives a temperature and not the amount of energy for the transition. The final calorimeter reading alone also includes the $4.5\,\mathrm{kJ}$ of the initial heating and would lead to $\dfrac{29.5\,\mathrm{kJ}}{0.10\,\mathrm{kg}}=295\,\mathrm{kJ/kg}$, a value that is too high. The calculation holds if the readings measure the energy that reached the sample. If part of the energy warms the apparatus, this part is first removed from the readings and then the difference is taken.

The experiment is repeated with twice the mass, $0.20\,\mathrm{kg}$, at the same pressure. If the readings are correct, the difference between the reading at the end of melting and the one at the start doubles and becomes $50\,\mathrm{kJ}$. A constant error, that is, the same nonzero amount added to every reading in both trials, cancels in the difference. With $1\,\mathrm{kJ}$ extra, for example, the readings are $5.5$ and $30.5\,\mathrm{kJ}$ and the difference is still $25\,\mathrm{kJ}$. Energy lost to the surroundings during the measurement does not cancel in this way and requires a separate correction.
:::

## Heating curves

:::osservazione{#curva titolo="Sloped sections and plateaus"}
The **specific heat capacity** $c$ of a substance is the energy needed to raise the temperature of one kilogram of that substance by one degree. It is measured in $\mathrm{J/(kg\,K)}$, joules per kilogram per kelvin. A change of $1\,\mathrm K$ (one kelvin) is equal to a change of $1\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$. To warm a mass $m$ by $\Delta T$ degrees, where $\Delta T$ (read *delta T*) is the final temperature minus the initial one, the energy needed is
$$
Q=mc\,\Delta T.
$$
The product $C=mc$ is called the **heat capacity** of the body. It is measured in $\mathrm{J/K}$ and gives the energy needed to raise the temperature of the whole body by one degree. For $0.20\,\mathrm{kg}$ of ice, with $c_g=2100\,\mathrm{J/(kg\,K)}$, the heat capacity is $0.20\cdot2100=420\,\mathrm{J/K}$.

In the graph of temperature versus energy received, the **slope** of a straight section is the rise in temperature divided by the energy that produces it. In a section with a single phase, treating $m$ and $c$ as constant, dividing $Q=mc\,\Delta T$ by $mc$ gives $\Delta T=\dfrac{Q}{mc}$. The slope is therefore $\dfrac{1}{mc}$. The heat of a sloped section, which changes the temperature, is also called **sensible heat**.

During a phase change at the equilibrium temperature the temperature does not change, so the section is horizontal. For complete conversion the width of the horizontal section along the energy axis is $mL$. If the horizontal axis measures time instead of energy and the useful power is constant, the energy received is power times time and $Q=Pt$ is substituted.
:::

:::esempio{titolo="From ice at minus ten degrees to lukewarm water"}
$0.20\,\mathrm{kg}$ of ice is warmed from $-10\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$ to water at $20\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$. The values used are the specific heat capacity of ice $c_g=2100\,\mathrm{J/(kg\,K)}$, that of liquid water $c_a=4200\,\mathrm{J/(kg\,K)}$ and $L_f=334\,000\,\mathrm{J/kg}$.

- To warm the ice from $-10$ to $0\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$, that is, by $10\,\mathrm K$, takes $0.20\cdot2100\cdot10=4200$ joules.
- To melt the ice at $0\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$ takes $0.20\cdot334\,000=66\,800$ joules. At the end of melting the horizontal coordinate, that is, the value on the horizontal axis, is $4200+66\,800=71\,000$ joules.
- To warm the water from $0$ to $20\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$ takes another $0.20\cdot4200\cdot20=16\,800$ joules.

The first section is steeper than the third, because ice has a smaller specific heat capacity than water and so its slope $\dfrac{1}{mc}$ is greater.

::grafico{linee="0,-10 4.2,0 71,0 87.8,20" punti="4.2,0,pieno;71,0,pieno;87.8,20,pieno" xmin=0 xmax=93 ymin=-12 ymax=24 nomi="Q (kJ),T (°C)" titolo="The plateau extends from 4.2 to 71 kJ. The change of slope distinguishes ice from liquid water."}
:::

:::esempio{#raffreddamento titolo="Cooling and freezing a sample"}
The starting point is $0.20\,\mathrm{kg}$ of water at $20\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$, and the steps of the heating example are followed backwards, with the same values of $c_g$, $c_a$ and $L_f$.

- To bring the water to zero, $16.8\,\mathrm{kJ}$ is extracted.
- For freezing, $66.8\,\mathrm{kJ}$ is extracted. At the end of the plateau the total energy extracted is $16.8+66.8=83.6\,\mathrm{kJ}$.
- To bring the ice to $-10\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$, another $4.2\,\mathrm{kJ}$ is extracted, and the total reaches $83.6+4.2=87.8\,\mathrm{kJ}$.

The horizontal axis counts the extracted energy $E$ as a positive number. In the sample balance, on the other hand, the heat is negative over all three sections, because the energy leaves. With $40\,\mathrm{kJ}$ extracted, the point on the graph lies on the plateau, so the temperature is zero and water and ice are present together. Of these $40\,\mathrm{kJ}$, the first $16.8\,\mathrm{kJ}$ cooled the water and only $40-16.8=23.2\,\mathrm{kJ}$ concern freezing. The graph therefore shows both the temperature and the energy already spent on the phase change.

## Heating in several stages

:::teorema{#fasi titolo="Adding the energy contributions"}
Each section is calculated with the formula for its phase, that is, $Q=mc\,\Delta T$ with the specific heat capacity of the phase present, or $Q=mL$ during the transition. The starting point is ice at a temperature $T_i<0\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$ and the end point is liquid water at $T_f>0\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$, at normal atmospheric pressure. The subscripts $i$ and $f$ denote the initial and the final temperature. The total energy is the sum of three terms:
$$
Q=m c_g(0-T_i)+mL_f+mc_a(T_f-0).
$$

- The first term warms the ice up to zero. Since $T_i$ is negative, $0-T_i$ is positive. For example, with $T_i=-10\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$ one gets $0-(-10)=10$.
- The second term melts all the ice.
- The third term warms the water from zero up to $T_f$.

Within each phase the specific heat capacity is taken to be constant. The complete path requires all three terms. For the reverse path, from water at $T_f$ to ice at $T_i$, the same contributions change sign.
:::

:::esempio{#durata titolo="Two powers and a threshold"}
Melting $0.20\,\mathrm{kg}$ of ice at zero takes $0.20\cdot334\,\mathrm{kJ}=66.8\,\mathrm{kJ}$. A source first delivers $200\,\mathrm W$ of useful power for $100\,\mathrm s$, that is, $200\cdot100=20\,000\,\mathrm J=20\,\mathrm{kJ}$. This leaves $66.8-20=46.8\,\mathrm{kJ}$. The second power is $400\,\mathrm W$, so the remainder takes
$$
\frac{46\,800\,\mathrm J}{400\,\mathrm W}=117\,\mathrm s.
$$
The total time is $100+117=217\,\mathrm s$. The calculation is therefore split into two intervals, one for each power. In the graph the slope of each section is the power, so the second section is twice as steep as the first.

::grafico{linee="0,0 100,20 217,66.8" punti="100,20,pieno;217,66.8,pieno" xmin=0 xmax=235 ymin=0 ymax=72 nomi="t (s),Q (kJ)" titolo="The second slope is twice the first. At 217 s, energy reaches the threshold for complete melting."}
:::

:::esempio{#dispersioni titolo="Distinguishing electrical and useful power"}
A heater draws $275\,\mathrm W$ from the mains, which is its **electrical power**. While $0.10\,\mathrm{kg}$ of ice already at zero melts, $75\,\mathrm W$ goes to the surroundings. This energy is called **lost** energy. Both powers are taken as constant and the energy stored by the apparatus is neglected. The sample receives $275-75=200$ joules every second, that is, $200\,\mathrm W$ of useful power. Melting requires $33.4\,\mathrm{kJ}$, so it is complete after
$$
\frac{33\,400\,\mathrm J}{200\,\mathrm W}=167\,\mathrm s.
$$

Over that interval the electrical energy is $275\cdot167=45\,925\,\mathrm J\simeq45.9\,\mathrm{kJ}$. The symbol $\simeq$ reads "approximately equal to". The difference $45.9-33.4\simeq12.5\,\mathrm{kJ}$ is the energy transferred to the surroundings. The ratio of useful power to electrical power is called the **efficiency** and here it is $\dfrac{200}{275}\simeq0.73$, that is, about $73\%$.

Dividing the melting energy by the electrical power would give $\dfrac{33\,400}{275}\simeq121\,\mathrm s$, a time shorter than the true one, because the energy lost would be counted as received by the ice. If the losses changed between stages, the useful power of each interval would be calculated.

::grafico{f="0.275*x;0.2*x" legenda="\text{Electrical energy};\text{Energy to sample}" punti="167,45.925,pieno;167,33.4,pieno" segmenti="167,0,167,45.925" xmin=0 xmax=190 ymin=0 ymax=55 nomi="t (s),E (kJ)" titolo="At 167 s the useful energy curve reaches 33.4 kJ. Electrical energy use is greater."}
:::

:::esempio{#recipiente titolo="Heat absorbed by the vessel"}
A calorimeter starts at $0\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$ and holds $0.10\,\mathrm{kg}$ of ice at the same temperature. $43\,800\,\mathrm J$ is supplied. At the end only liquid water at $20\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$ is present and the vessel is also at $20\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$. Heat losses and other transfers are neglected. With $L_f=334\,000\,\mathrm{J/kg}$ and $c_a=4200\,\mathrm{J/(kg\,K)}$ the energy absorbed by the vessel can be found.

- Melting the ice requires $0.10\cdot334\,000=33\,400\,\mathrm J$.
- Warming the resulting water by $20\,\mathrm K$ requires $0.10\cdot4200\cdot20=8400\,\mathrm J$.
- The vessel is left with the energy $43\,800-33\,400-8400=2000\,\mathrm J$.

The vessel also warmed by $20\,\mathrm K$. Its heat capacity is therefore $\dfrac{2000\,\mathrm J}{20\,\mathrm K}=100\,\mathrm{J/K}$. This capacity belongs to the entire vessel, while $c_a$ applies to one kilogram of water. If there were also an unknown loss to the outside, the $2000\,\mathrm J$ would include that loss and on its own would not give the vessel's heat capacity.
:::

## Ice in water

:::osservazione{#miscela titolo="Deciding whether all the ice melts"}
An **insulated** vessel exchanges no energy with the outside. If its heat capacity is **negligible**, the energy it absorbs is so small that it does not count in the balance. Water at $T_a>0\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$ and ice at $0\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$ are put in a vessel of this kind. The water gives up energy to the ice. The subscript $a$ denotes the water and the subscript $g$ the ice.

- The maximum heat the water releases by cooling to zero is $Q_{\mathrm{disp}}=m_a c_a (T_a-0)$, where $\mathrm{disp}$ stands for "available".
- The heat needed to melt all the ice is $Q_{\mathrm{fus}}=m_gL_f$.

Comparing the two values establishes which balance to use.

- If $Q_{\mathrm{disp}}<Q_{\mathrm{fus}}$, $\dfrac{Q_{\mathrm{disp}}}{L_f}$ kilograms melt and both phases remain at $0\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$.
- If $Q_{\mathrm{disp}}=Q_{\mathrm{fus}}$, the last ice melts too and the result is water at $0\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$.
- If $Q_{\mathrm{disp}}>Q_{\mathrm{fus}}$, the energy left over, $Q_{\mathrm{disp}}-Q_{\mathrm{fus}}$, warms all the water. The mass to be warmed after melting is the initial water plus the melted ice.
:::

:::esempio{titolo="Compare thresholds before calculating"}
$0.4\,\mathrm{kg}$ of water at $20\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$ is mixed with $0.2\,\mathrm{kg}$ of ice at zero.
$$
Q_{\mathrm{disp}}=0.4\cdot4200\cdot20\,\mathrm J=33\,600\,\mathrm J,
\qquad
Q_{\mathrm{fus}}=0.2\cdot334\,000\,\mathrm J=66\,800\,\mathrm J.
$$
The first value is smaller than the second, so not all the ice melts. While ice remains the final temperature is zero. The mass that melts is
$$
\frac{33\,600\,\mathrm J}{334\,000\,\mathrm{J/kg}}\simeq0.101\,\mathrm{kg},
$$
and $0.2-0.101\simeq0.099\,\mathrm{kg}$ of ice remains.

In the graph the horizontal axis shows the initial temperature of the water $T_i$. The rising line is $Q_{\mathrm{disp}}$, which grows by $1.68\,\mathrm{kJ}$ for each degree because $0.4\cdot4200=1680\,\mathrm{J/K}$. The horizontal line is the threshold $Q_{\mathrm{fus}}=66.8\,\mathrm{kJ}$. The two lines cross at about $39.8\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$, and the point at $20\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$ lies below the threshold.

::grafico{f="1.68*x;66.8" punti="20,33.6,pieno;39.7619,66.8,pieno" xmin=0 xmax=48 ymin=0 ymax=85 nomi="T_i (°C),Q (kJ)" legenda="\mathrm{available};\mathrm{threshold}" titolo="The stated initial temperature lies before the intersection."}
:::

:::esempio{#miscela-completa titolo="Energy balance after complete melting"}
In an insulated vessel of negligible heat capacity, $0.50\,\mathrm{kg}$ of water at $30\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$ is mixed with $0.10\,\mathrm{kg}$ of ice at zero, with $c_a=4200\,\mathrm{J/(kg\,K)}$.

- By cooling to zero the water can release $0.50\cdot4200\cdot30=63\,000\,\mathrm J=63\,\mathrm{kJ}$.
- Melting all the ice takes $0.10\cdot334\,\mathrm{kJ}=33.4\,\mathrm{kJ}$.
- All the ice melts and $63-33.4=29.6\,\mathrm{kJ}$ is left over to warm the now liquid mass, which is $0.50+0.10=0.60\,\mathrm{kg}$.

$$
T_f=\frac{29.6\cdot1000}{(0.50+0.10)\cdot4200}\,{}^\circ\mathrm C
  \simeq 11.7\,{}^\circ\mathrm C.
$$

The freshly melted ice also warms up, so the division is by $0.60\,\mathrm{kg}$. Dividing by the initial water alone would give $\dfrac{29\,600}{0.50\cdot4200}\simeq14.1\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$, a final temperature that is too high.

The boundary between the two cases is reached when the water releases exactly $33.4\,\mathrm{kJ}$ by cooling to zero. Each degree of cooling of the water provides $0.50\cdot4200=2100\,\mathrm J$, so the boundary is at an initial temperature of $\dfrac{33\,400}{2100}\simeq15.9\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$. Below the boundary, water and ice coexist at zero. Above it, if the initial temperature rises, the final temperature also rises.

::grafico{linee="0,0 15.9048,0 44,23.4127" punti="15.9048,0,pieno;30,11.746,pieno" xmin=0 xmax=47 ymin=-3 ymax=26 nomi="Initial T (°C),Final T (°C)" titolo="For the stated masses the threshold is about 15.9 °C initially. Above it all the ice melts."}
:::

:::osservazione{#ghiaccio-freddo titolo="When the ice starts below zero"}
If the ice starts below zero, part of the water's energy first goes into raising the temperature of the ice to zero, and only afterwards can the ice melt. Comparing just the heat released by the water with $m_gL_f$ would therefore give a melted mass that is too large. The first check therefore concerns the warming. The heat that the water releases by cooling to zero is compared with the heat the ice needs to reach zero, and this establishes whether the water brings the ice to zero without any of the water freezing.

For example, take $0.20\,\mathrm{kg}$ of water at $4\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$ and $0.30\,\mathrm{kg}$ of ice at $-10\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$, with $c_g=2100\,\mathrm{J/(kg\,K)}$.

- By cooling to zero the water releases $0.20\cdot4200\cdot4=3360\,\mathrm J=3.36\,\mathrm{kJ}$.
- Bringing the ice from $-10$ to $0\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$ takes $0.30\cdot2100\cdot10=6300\,\mathrm J=6.30\,\mathrm{kJ}$.
- The heat still missing is $6.30-3.36=2.94\,\mathrm{kJ}$.

This heat comes from the freezing of part of the initial water, equal to $\dfrac{2940\,\mathrm J}{334\,000\,\mathrm{J/kg}}\simeq0.0088\,\mathrm{kg}$. The water available, $0.20\,\mathrm{kg}$, is more than this mass, so at equilibrium two phases are still present at the melting temperature.
:::

## Evaporation, boiling and pressure

:::definizione{#pressione titolo="Conditions for a phase change"}
The **free surface** of a liquid is the surface in contact with the air. In **evaporation**, some molecules leave the free surface of a liquid even below the boiling temperature, as happens to the water in a puddle. In **boiling**, vapour bubbles form throughout the volume of the liquid, also at the bottom and inside it.

The boiling point, that is, the temperature at which boiling starts, depends on pressure. Water boils near $100\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$ at normal atmospheric pressure. When the external pressure is lower, as high in the mountains, the boiling temperature is lower. When it is higher, as in a pressure cooker, the boiling temperature is higher.
:::

:::attenzione{titolo="Interpreting a flat section"}
A measured curve can have a nearly horizontal section even without a change of phase. This happens when a **thermostat**, that is, a device that switches the heating on and off, holds the temperature steady, or when the energy leaving to the surroundings equals the energy entering. For example, a kettle with a thermostat keeps water at $70\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$ and the curve is flat, but the water stays entirely liquid.

A flat section is due to melting or boiling if two phases are seen together at the stated pressure. **Impurities**, that is, small amounts of other substances dissolved in the sample, and a changing pressure make the section less flat than in the ideal model.
:::

:::osservazione{#saturazione titolo="Vapour pressure and the onset of boiling"}
In a closed vessel some water molecules leave the liquid surface and become vapour, while other vapour molecules return to the liquid. After some time the number of molecules leaving equals the number returning, and liquid and vapour are in equilibrium. If there is also air above the liquid, the **partial pressure** of the vapour is the part of the total pressure due to the vapour alone. At a fixed temperature, the partial pressure of the vapour at this equilibrium is the **saturation vapour pressure**. It rises with temperature.

Boiling starts when the saturation vapour pressure reaches the external pressure. To predict it, these two values are compared, neglecting the small pressure differences within the liquid and the effects associated with forming the first bubbles.

For water the saturation vapour pressure is about $70\,\mathrm{kPa}$ at $90\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$ and about $101\,\mathrm{kPa}$ at $100\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$. In a vessel at $70\,\mathrm{kPa}$, boiling thus begins near $90\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$. At normal atmospheric pressure, $101\,\mathrm{kPa}$, it begins at $100\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$. In the graph the two horizontal dashed lines indicate these two external pressures. If the pressure changes during the experiment, the boiling temperature also changes, so it is found again. The energy already supplied to the liquid does not by itself determine the boiling temperature.

:::esempio{#condensa-liquido titolo="From saturated vapour to warm liquid water"}
**Saturated vapour** is vapour in equilibrium with its liquid. At normal atmospheric pressure, $0.020\,\mathrm{kg}$ of saturated vapour at $100\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$ is cooled until it becomes water at $75\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$.

- Condensation releases $0.020\cdot2260\,\mathrm{kJ}=45.2\,\mathrm{kJ}$.
- The liquid water then cools by $100-75=25\,\mathrm K$ and releases $0.020\cdot4200\cdot25\,\mathrm J=2100\,\mathrm J=2.1\,\mathrm{kJ}$.
- In total $45.2+2.1=47.3\,\mathrm{kJ}$ is transferred to the surroundings.

The first $45.2\,\mathrm{kJ}$ leaves at constant temperature, while the mass of vapour decreases. In this constant-pressure model, the temperature falls below $100\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$ only after all the vapour has condensed. If less than the $45.2\,\mathrm{kJ}$ of complete condensation were extracted, some vapour would still remain at the end.
