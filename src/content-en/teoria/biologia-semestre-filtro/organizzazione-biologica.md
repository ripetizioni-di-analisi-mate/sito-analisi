## Cells, homeostasis and evolution

:::definizione{#teoria-cellulare titolo="Cells and the continuity of life"}
A **cell** is the smallest living unit. It is enclosed by a membrane, contains the hereditary instructions and exchanges matter and energy with its surroundings. **Cell theory** makes three statements.

- Every organism is made of one or more cells.
- The cell is the unit of structure and function of organisms.
- Every cell comes from a cell that existed before it.

A bacterium and a yeast are organisms made of a single cell, that is, they are unicellular. A person is made of tens of trillions of cells of different types, that is, a person is multicellular. In a yeast culture, for example, each cell grows and then divides in two, so all the cells in the culture descend from the initial ones.

All cells have four components in common.

- The **plasma membrane** separates the inside of the cell from the outside and controls what enters and what leaves.
- The **cytoplasm** is the content of the cell enclosed by the membrane.
- **DNA** (deoxyribonucleic acid) is the molecule that contains the hereditary instructions. The cell uses these instructions by copying stretches of them into a similar molecule, **RNA** (ribonucleic acid).
- **Ribosomes** are the structures that build proteins by following those instructions.

The set of chemical reactions that take place in a cell is called **metabolism**. Some cells lose part of these components as they mature. The mature human red blood cell (erythrocyte), for example, no longer has a nucleus or ribosomes. It is still a cell, because it derives from a precursor cell that had them.

**Homeostasis** is the ability of a cell or an organism to keep an internal quantity within a narrow range while the environment changes. Human body temperature, for example, stays close to 37 °C in both summer and winter. The same happens in the cell for the concentration of a substance. If the substance enters faster, the cell consumes or exports more of it, and the internal amount returns to its previous value. An amount that stays constant is therefore the result of two opposite and equal flows, one inward and one outward, and maintaining them requires energy. For this reason homeostasis is studied by disturbing the system: one condition is changed and the time the quantity takes to return to its initial value is measured.
:::

:::esempio{titolo="A stable measurement can depend on active flows"}
8 units of a substance enter a cell per minute and the cell consumes 8 per minute. The balance is $8-8=0$, so the internal amount stays constant. An inhibitor is added, that is, a substance that slows consumption. Consumption falls to 3 units per minute, while entry stays at 8. The balance becomes $8-3=5$, so the internal amount increases by 5 units per minute. Before the inhibitor the amount was constant because the cell consumed the substance at the same rate at which it entered.
:::

:::definizione{#albero-vita titolo="Relatedness, variation and evolution"}
A **phylogenetic tree** is a diagram that shows which groups of organisms descend from a common ancestor. It is read like a family tree. The tips of the branches are the groups being compared, for example three species A, B and C. Each point where a branch splits in two is called a **node** and represents the common ancestor of the groups on the two branches. Two groups are more closely related the more recent their common ancestor is. In the figure A and B separate at a node more recent than the one joining them to C, so A is more closely related to B than to C.

Relatedness is read only from the nodes. The order in which the tips are drawn carries no information, because two branches can swap places around their node without changing the common ancestors. The length of a branch indicates elapsed time or amount of change only if the figure includes a scale.

Comparing molecules found in all organisms, in particular the RNA that is part of ribosomes, divides living things into three large groups called **domains**: Bacteria, Archaea and Eukarya. Bacteria and archaea are **prokaryotes**, that is, cells without a nucleus. **Eukaryotes** have cells with a nucleus and include animals, plants, fungi and protists. Bacteria and archaea look alike under the microscope, but they differ in membrane composition, in the cell wall and in the enzymes that copy and read DNA. In these enzymes archaea are closer to eukaryotes than to bacteria. The three domains are branches of the same tree and none of them is more evolved than the others.

**Evolution** is the change, from one generation to the next, in the hereditary characteristics of a population. A population is the set of individuals of the same species living in the same place. Evolution requires individuals to differ from one another in characteristics passed on to their descendants. **Natural selection** acts on these differences. Individuals with a characteristic that helps survival and reproduction in that environment leave more descendants, so that characteristic becomes more frequent in later generations.

In a population of bacteria, for example, a few individuals have a variant that makes them resistant to an antibiotic. When the antibiotic is given, the sensitive bacteria die and the resistant ones multiply, so after a few generations almost the whole population is resistant. The resistant variant existed before the treatment and had arisen by a random mutation. The antibiotic only changed its frequency. Evolution therefore concerns populations and not the single individual, which keeps its hereditary characteristics throughout its life.

## Viruses and the production of new virions

:::definizione{#virus titolo="Genome, capsid and dependence on the host"}
A **virus** is an infectious agent much smaller than a cell, made of a nucleic acid, that is, DNA or RNA, enclosed in a protein coat. Outside the cell the virus is an inert particle, called a **virion**, which has up to three components.

- The **genome** is the nucleic acid carrying the instructions of the virus. It is either DNA or RNA.
- The **capsid** is the protein shell that encloses and protects the genome.
- The **envelope** is a lipid layer surrounding the capsid. Only some viruses have one; they take it from a membrane of the infected cell and insert their own proteins into it.

A virus enters a cell only if a protein on its surface binds a specific molecule of the cell membrane, called a **receptor**. For this reason each virus infects only certain cell types, and this preference is called **tropism**. HIV (human immunodeficiency virus), for example, binds the protein CD4 and therefore infects the lymphocytes that display it. Binding to the receptor is necessary but may not be enough, because after entry the virus also needs molecules inside the cell.

Viruses are **obligate intracellular parasites**, that is, they multiply only inside a cell. A virion has no ribosomes and produces no energy. To make its proteins it therefore uses the ribosomes, the amino acids and the ATP (adenosine triphosphate, the molecule that supplies energy to cellular reactions) of the infected cell, called the **host**. Some viruses carry one or more enzymes in the virion, for example a polymerase, which is an enzyme able to copy a nucleic acid. These viruses too depend on the host for everything else. Viruses have a genome that mutates and are subject to selection, so they evolve as organisms do.

The envelope is made of lipids, so substances that dissolve fats, such as alcohol and soaps, damage it. A virus with a damaged envelope loses the proteins it uses to bind the receptor and is generally no longer infectious.
:::

:::definizione{#cicli-virali titolo="From entry to genome expression"}
The cycle of a virus in an animal cell takes place in five stages.

1. **Attachment**. A protein of the virion binds the receptor on the cell membrane.
2. **Entry**. The cell takes the virion into a vesicle (endocytosis) or, in enveloped viruses, the envelope fuses with a membrane of the cell.
3. **Uncoating**. The capsid opens and the genome is free inside the cell.
4. **Synthesis**. The cell produces copies of the viral genome and viral proteins.
5. **Assembly and release**. Genomes and proteins join into new virions, which leave the cell.

The fourth stage has a constraint that is the same for all viruses. Ribosomes build proteins only by reading a **messenger RNA (mRNA)**, so every virus must obtain an mRNA from its own genome. The **Baltimore classification** divides viruses into seven classes according to the type of genome and the route from the genome to the mRNA.

| Genome | Characteristic step towards mRNA |
|---|---|
| Double-stranded DNA | Transcription of DNA |
| Single-stranded DNA | Formation of double-stranded DNA, then transcription |
| Double-stranded RNA | Transcription by RNA-dependent RNA polymerase |
| Positive-sense single-stranded RNA | The genome can function as mRNA |
| Negative-sense single-stranded RNA | Production of positive-sense RNA by viral polymerase |
| RNA with reverse transcription | DNA intermediate, then transcription |
| DNA with reverse transcription | mRNA from DNA; an RNA intermediate is used in genome replication |

For single-stranded RNA genomes a distinction is made by **polarity**. A **positive** (+) RNA has the same sequence as the mRNA, so ribosomes translate it as soon as it enters the cell. A **negative** (−) RNA has the sequence complementary to the mRNA, so ribosomes cannot read it. A positive RNA must first be copied from a negative one. This copy is made by an **RNA-dependent RNA polymerase**, that is, an enzyme that synthesises RNA using RNA as a template. Animal cells do not have an enzyme of this kind. For this reason a negative-strand RNA virus, such as influenza virus, carries the ready-made polymerase inside the virion. A positive-strand RNA virus, such as poliovirus, has it produced by the host ribosomes, which translate its genome directly.

A **bacteriophage**, or phage, is a virus that infects bacteria. After its genome enters, two cycles are possible.

- In the **lytic cycle** the bacterium immediately produces viral genomes and proteins, the new virions assemble and the cell bursts (lysis), releasing them.
- In the **lysogenic cycle** the phage genome inserts into the bacterial chromosome and stays there inactive. In this form it is called a **prophage**. At each division the bacterium copies the prophage together with its own DNA, so all daughter cells contain it and no virion is produced.

A phage capable of both cycles is called **temperate**. The classic example is phage λ of *Escherichia coli*. A stimulus that damages the bacterial DNA, for example ultraviolet light, makes the prophage leave the chromosome and starts the lytic cycle. This step is called **induction**.

A **retrovirus**, such as HIV, has an RNA genome that is copied into DNA inside the cell. The route has three steps.

1. **Reverse transcriptase**, an enzyme carried by the virion, synthesises DNA using the viral RNA as a template.
2. **Integrase**, a second viral enzyme, inserts this DNA into a chromosome of the cell. The inserted viral DNA is called a **provirus**.
3. The cell transcribes the provirus like one of its own genes. The resulting RNAs serve both as mRNA for viral proteins and as genomes for new virions.

Animal viruses leave the cell in two main ways. In lysis the cell bursts. In **budding** the virion leaves by wrapping itself in a patch of membrane, which becomes its envelope. In this case the cell survives for some time and keeps producing virions.

![Lytic cycle and lysogeny of a temperate bacteriophage](/figure/biologia-semestre-filtro/organizzazione-biologica/viral-en.svg)
:::

:::esempio{titolo="Locating a block in the cycle"}
A retrovirus infects a cell culture treated with an experimental drug. The cells are tested for viral DNA. Viral DNA is present, but it is not inserted into the chromosomes. The presence of viral DNA shows that reverse transcriptase has worked, because it is the enzyme that produces it. The next step, insertion into the chromosome, has not taken place, and the enzyme that carries it out is integrase. The drug therefore blocks integrase. The blocked step is found by looking for the last product that is still formed.
:::

## Prokaryotes and information exchange

:::definizione{#procarioti titolo="Membranes, walls and bacterial structures"}
A **prokaryotic** cell has no nucleus. Its DNA, usually a single circular chromosome, lies in a region of the cytoplasm called the **nucleoid**, which is not separated by membranes. The cytoplasm contains the ribosomes and the enzymes of metabolism. The other membrane-bound organelles are also missing, including mitochondria, which in eukaryotes produce most of the ATP. In bacteria that respire, the proteins of respiration are embedded in the plasma membrane and ATP is produced there.

Outside the plasma membrane almost all bacteria have a **cell wall**, that is, a rigid layer that gives the cell its shape and prevents it from bursting when water enters by osmosis. The wall is made of **peptidoglycan**, a mesh of sugar chains linked to one another by short chains of amino acids. Bacteria fall into two groups according to their wall.

- In **Gram-positive** bacteria the peptidoglycan layer is thick.
- In **Gram-negative** bacteria the peptidoglycan layer is thin and outside it there is a second membrane, called the **outer membrane**, which contains lipopolysaccharide (LPS).

The two names come from the **Gram stain**. The bacteria are stained with a violet dye (crystal violet with iodine), washed with alcohol and then treated with a second, red dye (safranin). The thick layer of Gram-positive bacteria retains the violet during the wash, so these cells stay purple. Gram-negative bacteria lose it, so they take up the second dye and appear pink.

Other structures are present only in some bacteria.

- The **capsule** is a layer outside the wall, usually made of sugars, which protects the cell and helps it adhere to surfaces.
- **Fimbriae** and **pili** are short protein filaments used for adhesion. One particular type of pilus connects two bacteria during DNA transfer.
- The **flagellum** is a long filament that rotates and moves the cell.

Some bacteria, the mycoplasmas, have no wall. Archaea have a wall without peptidoglycan and a membrane with lipids of a different structure, with ether bonds in place of ester bonds. Many archaea live in extreme environments, such as hot springs and very salty waters, and many others live in ordinary environments such as soil, the sea and the gut.

![Comparison of Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacterial envelopes](/figure/biologia-semestre-filtro/organizzazione-biologica/gram-en.svg)
:::

:::definizione{#trasferimento-orizzontale titolo="Three routes of gene transfer"}
A bacterium receives DNA in two ways. In **vertical transmission** it receives it from the mother cell, when this divides. In **horizontal gene transfer** it receives a stretch of DNA from a bacterium that is not its mother cell. Horizontal transfer takes place by three routes, which differ in what carries the DNA.

- **Transformation**. The bacterium takes up free DNA from the environment, released for example by dead cells. A bacterium able to do this is called competent. If a DNase, that is, an enzyme that degrades DNA, is added to the medium, the transfer does not occur.
- **Conjugation**. A donor bacterium passes DNA to a recipient through direct contact between the two cells, established by a pilus. The transferred DNA is often a **plasmid**, that is, a small circular DNA molecule separate from the chromosome. If a filter keeps the two populations apart, the transfer does not occur.
- **Transduction**. A bacteriophage, while assembling, packages a stretch of bacterial DNA by mistake and injects it into the next bacterium it infects. DNase in the medium does not prevent it, because the DNA is protected inside the capsid.

These tests distinguish the three routes in an experiment. A transfer blocked by DNase is transformation. A transfer blocked by the filter that separates the cells, and insensitive to DNase, is conjugation. A transfer that occurs even with DNase and with a filter that lets phages through is transduction.

The DNA received changes the characteristics of the bacterium only if it stays in the cell, as a plasmid or inserted into the chromosome, and if its genes are expressed. A plasmid with a penicillin resistance gene, for example, makes the bacterium resistant to penicillin and not to an antibiotic of a different type. Horizontal transfer leaves the number of cells unchanged. The number increases by **binary fission**, that is, the division of one bacterium into two daughter cells.

:::

## Eukaryotes, compartments and differentiation

:::definizione{#eucarioti-endosimbiosi titolo="Internal organisation and endosymbiosis"}
A **eukaryotic** cell is divided internally into **compartments**, that is, spaces enclosed by a membrane, also called organelles. Each compartment has its own tasks.

- The **nucleus** contains the chromosomes. Here DNA is transcribed into RNA, while proteins are built outside the nucleus, in the cytosol, which is the liquid part of the cytoplasm.
- The **endoplasmic reticulum** produces lipids and the proteins destined for membranes or for the outside of the cell. Its membrane is continuous with the envelope of the nucleus.
- The **Golgi apparatus** completes these proteins and sorts them to their destination.
- **Lysosomes** degrade molecules to be eliminated. Their interior is acidic, with a pH of about 5, while the cytosol has a pH of about 7.
- **Endosomes** receive the material the cell takes in from outside.

The advantage of compartments is that each one maintains different chemical conditions. Lysosomal enzymes, for example, work only in an acidic environment, and the lysosomal membrane keeps this environment separate from the rest of the cell. Compartments communicate with one another through vesicles and transport proteins.

For the origin of the nucleus there are hypotheses and no established reconstruction. According to the most widely held hypothesis, in the ancestor of eukaryotes the membrane folded inwards and surrounded the DNA, forming the nuclear envelope and the endoplasmic reticulum together. The continuity observed today between the two membranes agrees with this hypothesis. Exchanges between nucleus and cytosol pass through openings in the envelope called **nuclear pores**.

The **endosymbiotic theory** explains the origin of mitochondria. A bacterium able to respire entered a larger cell, an ancestor of eukaryotes, and remained living inside it. Over a very large number of generations it became an organelle. Four observations support the theory.

- Mitochondria have their own DNA, circular like that of bacteria.
- They have their own ribosomes, similar to bacterial ones.
- They have two membranes, and the inner one resembles the membrane of a bacterium.
- Their genes are related to those of a group of present-day bacteria, the alphaproteobacteria.

Over time most of the genes of the original bacterium were lost or moved to the nucleus. For this reason almost all mitochondrial proteins are now encoded by nuclear genes, built in the cytosol and then imported into the mitochondrion.

The same holds for the **plastids** of plants and algae, including chloroplasts, which derive from a cyanobacterium, that is, a bacterium able to photosynthesise. The endosymbiotic theory concerns only mitochondria and plastids. The nucleus, endoplasmic reticulum and Golgi have the origin described above, from infoldings of the membrane.

A **multicellular** organism is made of many cells that stay together, communicate with one another and divide in a coordinated way. Its cells are of different types, and the process by which a cell acquires a specialised form and functions is called **differentiation**. Almost all the cells of an organism contain the same DNA. Each cell type, however, uses a different part of the genes, so it produces different proteins. A neuron and a skin cell of the same person, for example, have the same genes. The neuron produces the proteins that transmit nerve signals and the skin cell produces keratin. How a cell chooses which genes to use is the subject of the units on gene regulation and signalling.

## Biomolecules: composition and function

:::definizione{#macromolecole titolo="Carbohydrates, lipids and proteins"}
**Biomolecules** are the molecules cells are made of. The four main classes are carbohydrates, lipids, proteins and nucleic acids. Their behaviour in the cell depends on how each part of the molecule interacts with water.

- A **polar** or charged part is attracted to water and is called **hydrophilic**. Examples are the hydroxyl group (OH) and the phosphate group.
- A **nonpolar** part is not attracted to water and is called **hydrophobic**. Examples are the long carbon and hydrogen chains of fats. In water, hydrophobic parts cluster together, as oil does.

The atoms of a molecule are joined by **covalent bonds**, which are strong. Between different molecules, or between distant parts of the same molecule, **non-covalent interactions** form, such as hydrogen bonds, which are weak taken one at a time. Many weak interactions together hold a protein in its shape and make two molecules of complementary shape stick to each other.

**Carbohydrates** are the sugars. The simplest unit is the **monosaccharide**, for example glucose. Two monosaccharides join through a **glycosidic bond**, and many units in a chain form a **polysaccharide**. Glycogen, starch and cellulose are all polysaccharides made only of glucose, yet they have different functions. Glycogen is the glucose store of animals and starch is that of plants. Cellulose forms the wall of plant cells. The difference lies in the type of bond between the units (α in glycogen and starch, β in cellulose) and in the branching of the chain. Human digestive enzymes cleave the α bond and not the β bond, so a person digests starch and not cellulose.

**Lipids** are largely hydrophobic molecules, so they are poorly soluble in water. Unlike the other classes they are not chains of a repeated unit. The three main types are the following.

- **Triglycerides** (triacylglycerols) consist of one glycerol molecule bound to three fatty acids. A fatty acid is a long chain of carbon and hydrogen ending in an acid group. Triglycerides are the energy store of adipose tissue.
- **Phospholipids** have a hydrophilic head, which contains a phosphate group, and two hydrophobic fatty acid tails. A molecule with a hydrophilic part and a hydrophobic part is called **amphipathic**. In water phospholipids arrange themselves in a **bilayer**, with the heads facing the water and the tails inside. This bilayer is the basic structure of all cell membranes.
- **Cholesterol** sits between the phospholipids of animal membranes and regulates their fluidity. From it the cell makes steroid hormones, such as cortisol and testosterone.

Proteins are made of **amino acids**. An amino acid has a central carbon atom bound to four groups: an amino group ($\mathrm{NH_2}$), a carboxyl group ($\mathrm{COOH}$), a hydrogen atom and a **side chain**, written R. The first three are the same in all amino acids. The side chain changes from one amino acid to another and determines its properties, because it is nonpolar, polar or charged. Proteins contain twenty amino acids. Two amino acids join through a **peptide bond**, which links the carboxyl group of the first to the amino group of the second. An amino acid that is part of a chain is called a residue.

A **protein** is a chain of amino acids folded into a precise shape. Its structure is described on four levels.

- **Primary structure** is the sequence of amino acids, that is, the order in which they follow one another in the chain.
- **Secondary structure** is the regular folding of short stretches of the chain. The two most common forms are the alpha helix, a spiral, and the beta sheet, made of stretches lying side by side. They are held by hydrogen bonds between atoms of the chain backbone.
- **Tertiary structure** is the three-dimensional shape of the whole chain. It is held by interactions between side chains.
- **Quaternary structure** is the association of several chains into one protein. Haemoglobin, for example, is made of four chains.

A **domain** is a part of the chain that folds on its own and often has a distinct function. The **active site** of an enzyme is the pocket where the molecule to be transformed binds. It is formed by amino acids that are far apart in the sequence and that folding brings close together. For this reason the function of a protein depends on its shape.

A protein loses its function in two different ways. In **denaturation** the chain loses its folding, for example because of heat or an extreme pH, and stays intact, because the peptide bonds do not break. Egg white turning white and solid when cooked is made of denatured proteins. In **proteolysis** an enzyme breaks the peptide bonds and the chain splits into fragments. This is what happens to food proteins during digestion. An inactive protein that still has its original length is therefore denatured and not digested.

![Sequence, secondary structures, folding and association of chains](/figure/biologia-semestre-filtro/organizzazione-biologica/proteins-en.svg)
:::

:::esempio{titolo="From sequence to function"}
The active site of an enzyme contains a negatively charged amino acid, which attracts a positively charged substrate. A mutation replaces this amino acid with an uncharged one. The mutant protein has the same number of amino acids and is produced in the same amount. The substrate, however, is no longer attracted into the active site, so the reaction slows down or stops. The amount of protein present does not show whether the enzyme works. To find out, its activity is measured, that is, how much product it forms in a given time.
:::

## Enzymes and protein modifications

:::definizione{#enzimi-modificazioni titolo="Catalysis, regulation and modifications"}
An **enzyme** is a molecule that speeds up a chemical reaction without being consumed. Almost all enzymes are proteins. Some are RNA molecules and are called **ribozymes**. The molecule the enzyme acts on is the **substrate** and the one obtained is the **product**. Lactase, for example, is the enzyme that splits lactose (substrate) into glucose and galactose (products).

A reaction, even one that releases energy, starts only if the molecules get over an initial energy threshold, called the **activation energy**. The higher the threshold, the slower the reaction. The enzyme binds the substrate in the active site, holds it in the right position and in this way lowers the activation energy. At the end it releases the product and is ready for another substrate molecule.

The enzyme changes the rate of the reaction and leaves its final outcome unchanged. The energy of the reactants and that of the products stay the same, as the figure shows, so the free energy difference $\Delta G$ between them also stays the same. Consequently the enzyme does not shift the equilibrium of the reaction. It makes the reaction reach equilibrium sooner, because it speeds up the forward and the reverse reaction to the same extent.

The activity of an enzyme depends on temperature and pH, because both act on the shape of the protein. As temperature rises the reaction speeds up to an optimal value, which for human enzymes is close to 37 °C. Above this value the enzyme denatures and activity falls rapidly. Each enzyme also has an optimal pH. Pepsin in the stomach works at a pH of about 2, while trypsin in the intestine works at a pH of about 8.

A **post-translational modification** is a chemical group added to a protein after it has been built. The cell uses it to regulate a protein that is already present without having to produce a new one. The most common is **phosphorylation**, that is, the addition of a phosphate group.

- A **kinase** is an enzyme that adds a phosphate group to a protein, taking it from ATP.
- A **phosphatase** is an enzyme that removes it.

Phosphate carries negative charges, so it changes the shape of the protein and with it the protein's activity. Depending on the protein and on the position where it binds, phosphorylation activates or inactivates the protein. Other modifications are acetylation (addition of an acetyl group), glycosylation (addition of sugars) and lipidation (addition of a lipid, which anchors the protein to a membrane).

**Ubiquitin** is a small protein that the cell attaches to other proteins as a signal. Attachment requires three enzymes in succession, called E1, E2 and E3. The E3 enzyme is the one that recognises the target protein. A chain of several ubiquitins attached to a protein usually sends it to the **proteasome**, a complex that degrades tagged proteins. A single ubiquitin, or chains of another type, have different functions, for example they direct the protein to another compartment. **Deubiquitinases** are enzymes that remove ubiquitin.

## DNA, RNA and chain direction

:::definizione{#acidi-nucleici titolo="Nucleotides and complementarity"}
The **nucleic acids** are DNA (deoxyribonucleic acid) and RNA (ribonucleic acid). They are chains of units called **nucleotides**. A nucleotide has three parts.

- A **sugar** with five carbon atoms. In DNA it is deoxyribose, in RNA it is ribose.
- A **nitrogenous base**. There are five bases: adenine (A), guanine (G), cytosine (C), thymine (T) and uracil (U). DNA contains A, G, C and T. RNA contains A, G, C and U.
- One or more **phosphate groups**.

Adenine and guanine have two rings and are called **purines**. Cytosine, thymine and uracil have a single ring and are called **pyrimidines**. The sugar bound to the base alone, without phosphate, is called a nucleoside.

The five carbon atoms of the sugar are numbered from 1′ to 5′. In the chain, the phosphate bound to the 5′ carbon of one nucleotide joins the 3′ carbon of the previous nucleotide through a **phosphodiester bond**. The chain therefore has two different ends, because a 5′ is left free at one end and a 3′ at the other. By convention a sequence is written and read from the 5′ end to the 3′ end.

DNA is a **double helix** made of two strands, according to the Watson and Crick model. The two strands have two properties.

- They are **complementary**. Opposite an A there is always a T, and opposite a G there is always a C. The A–T pair is held by two hydrogen bonds, the G–C pair by three.
- They are **antiparallel**, that is, oriented in opposite directions. Where one strand runs 5′ to 3′, the other runs 3′ to 5′.

If one strand has the sequence 5′-ATGC-3′, for example, the complementary strand is 3′-TACG-5′, which read in the conventional direction is written 5′-GCAT-3′.

Complementarity leads to a rule about amounts, called Chargaff's rule. In double-stranded DNA every A is paired with a T and every G with a C, so $A=T$ and $G=C$. If guanine is 20% of the bases in a DNA, then cytosine is 20%. Together they make 40%, so 60% is left for adenine and thymine, that is, 30% each. The rule holds only for the double-stranded molecule. In a single strand, such as an RNA, the bases are not paired and the four percentages are independent.

RNA is usually single-stranded. The cell contains several types of RNA, with different tasks.

- **mRNA** (messenger RNA) carries to the ribosomes the sequence to be translated into protein. It is the only type that is translated.
- **tRNA** (transfer RNA) carries amino acids to the ribosome and recognises the base triplets of the mRNA, called codons.
- **rRNA** (ribosomal RNA) forms ribosomes together with proteins and catalyses the formation of the peptide bond.
- **snRNAs** (small nuclear RNAs) take part in splicing, that is, the removal of stretches from newly transcribed RNA.
- **miRNAs** (microRNAs) are very short RNAs that reduce the production of protein from particular mRNAs.

An RNA strand folds back on itself where it contains complementary stretches, and forms short double-helical regions.

## Metabolism and energy coupling

:::definizione{#metabolismo titolo="Catabolism, anabolism and balances"}
**Metabolism** is the set of chemical reactions of the cell. It is divided into two parts.

- **Catabolism** breaks large molecules down into smaller ones and releases energy. The breakdown of glucose into carbon dioxide and water is an example.
- **Anabolism** builds large molecules from small ones and consumes energy. The synthesis of a protein from amino acids is an example.

The two parts are linked by **ATP**. Catabolism produces ATP and anabolism consumes it. Synthesis reactions often also require electrons, supplied by carrier molecules such as NADPH (nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate). This availability of electrons is called reducing power. In addition, the intermediate products of catabolism serve as starting material for anabolism.

The **free energy change**, $\Delta G$, indicates whether a reaction occurs spontaneously under the conditions considered.

- If $\Delta G<0$ the reaction releases energy and occurs spontaneously. It is called exergonic.
- If $\Delta G>0$ the reaction requires energy and does not occur on its own. It is called endergonic.

$\Delta G$ gives no information about rate. A reaction with a negative $\Delta G$ is very slow if its activation energy is high, and in that case it occurs in the cell only with an enzyme.

The cell makes an endergonic reaction occur by **coupling** it to an exergonic one, almost always the hydrolysis of ATP. Two reactions are coupled when they share an intermediate molecule, for example when the phosphate of ATP is transferred to the substrate. In this case the two $\Delta G$ values add up, and the overall reaction occurs if the sum is negative. If ATP and substrate are merely present in the same solution, without an enzyme that makes them react together, the energy of ATP is not transferred.

Two types of reaction recur throughout metabolism. In **condensation** two molecules join and a water molecule is released. In **hydrolysis** a water molecule breaks a bond and splits a molecule in two. Biological polymers (proteins, polysaccharides, nucleic acids) are formed by condensation and broken down by hydrolysis. Condensation has a positive $\Delta G$, so in the cell the monomers are first **activated**, that is, bound to an energy-rich group at the expense of ATP. In DNA synthesis, for example, nucleotides enter as triphosphates and lose two phosphates when they join the chain.

:::esempio{titolo="An energy balance with explicit assumptions"}
A synthesis reaction has $\Delta G_1=+12\,\mathrm{kJ/mol}$, so it does not occur on its own. An enzyme couples it, in a one-to-one ratio, to a second reaction with $\Delta G_2=-30\,\mathrm{kJ/mol}$. The overall change is the sum of the two.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta G_{\mathrm{tot}}&=\Delta G_1+\Delta G_2\\[6pt]
&=(12-30)\,\mathrm{kJ/mol}\\[6pt]
&=-18\,\mathrm{kJ/mol}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The sum is negative, so the coupled reaction occurs spontaneously. The result does not indicate the rate, which depends on the enzyme. The two values are data of the exercise. In the cell the $\Delta G$ of ATP hydrolysis depends on the concentrations of ATP, of ADP (adenosine diphosphate, that is, ATP that has lost one phosphate group) and of phosphate.
:::
