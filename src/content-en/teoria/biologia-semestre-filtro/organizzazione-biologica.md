## Cells, homeostasis and evolution

:::definizione{#teoria-cellulare titolo="Cells and the continuity of life"}
In a yeast culture under growth-supporting conditions, cells accumulate material and divide, generating additional cells. Each new cell receives material from a pre-existing cell and continues to exchange substances with the environment. **Cell theory** identifies the cell as the structural and functional unit of organisms and states that cells arise from other cells. An organism may consist of a single cell or many specialised cells.

A cell has a membrane enclosing an internal environment, hereditary information and systems that transform matter and energy. Metabolism comprises the reactions that sustain its organisation. Protein synthesis requires ribosomes; storing instructions and expressing them involve nucleic acids. Differentiated cells may lose some structures during maturation: a mature human erythrocyte, for example, has lost its nucleus and ribosomes, but develops from a cellular precursor.

**Homeostasis** maintains internal variables within ranges compatible with function. If the concentration of a substance increases, transport and metabolism can alter its entry, use or removal. The observed value may remain nearly constant while opposing flows continue. An apparently constant concentration may therefore require activity and energy consumption. Homeostasis is assessed by measuring the response to a disturbance and subsequent recovery, comparing living cells with appropriate controls.
:::

:::esempio{titolo="A stable measurement can depend on active flows"}
A cell takes up 8 units of a substance per minute and consumes 8. Its internal amount remains constant because the net balance is zero. An inhibitor reduces consumption to 3 units per minute without changing uptake; the initial accumulation becomes $8-3=5$ units per minute. Measuring only the initial amount would have concealed the metabolic activity that maintained it.
:::

:::definizione{#albero-vita titolo="Relatedness, variation and evolution"}
A **phylogenetic tree** represents a reconstruction of relationships by descent. An internal node indicates a common ancestor; two groups are more closely related if they share a more recent common ancestor. Whether a tip is on the right or left depends on the drawing. Rotating two branches around their node preserves relatedness. Branch length measures time or change only when a scale is specified.

Molecular comparisons, including those of ribosomal RNA, distinguish Bacteria, Archaea and Eukarya. Bacteria and Archaea have a prokaryotic organisation, but differ in membranes, walls and molecular systems. Modern reconstructions recognise a close evolutionary relationship between eukaryotes and archaea; the three domain names describe groups rather than three stages of progress.

Evolution in populations requires heritable variation. If a variant promotes reproduction in a given environment, natural selection can increase its frequency in later generations. Exposure to an antibiotic can select bacteria that are already resistant; the appearance of a specific variant is not an intentional response to a need. Reproduction and selection concern the continuity of populations: a sterile animal remains a living organism.

:::

## Viruses and the production of new virions

:::definizione{#virus titolo="Genome, capsid and dependence on the host"}
A **virion** is the complete viral particle that transfers the genome between cells. The genome is DNA or RNA and is protected by a protein capsid. Some viruses also have a lipid envelope acquired from cellular membranes and containing viral proteins. Binding between viral proteins and host receptors contributes to tropism, meaning the ability to infect particular cell types. The presence of a receptor alone may be insufficient if required intracellular factors are absent.

Viruses are obligate intracellular parasites. They possess hereditary instructions and can evolve, but depend on the cell's ribosomes and other resources to produce new particles. Some carry their own enzymes, such as polymerases: possessing an enzyme does not amount to autonomous cellular metabolism. The envelope contributes to entry and exit and exposes the virus to agents that disrupt lipid membranes; actual sensitivity depends on the virus and the conditions.
:::

:::definizione{#cicli-virali titolo="From entry to genome expression"}
Attachment recognises compatible receptors; entry of animal viruses can involve endocytosis or, for enveloped viruses, fusion with a host membrane. Uncoating makes the genome available for subsequent steps. To synthesise proteins, all viruses must make available RNA that ribosomes can read as mRNA. The Baltimore classification distinguishes the genome type and the route towards that mRNA.

| Genome | Characteristic step towards mRNA |
|---|---|
| Double-stranded DNA | Transcription of DNA |
| Single-stranded DNA | Formation of double-stranded DNA, then transcription |
| Double-stranded RNA | Transcription by RNA-dependent RNA polymerase |
| Positive-sense single-stranded RNA | The genome can function as mRNA |
| Negative-sense single-stranded RNA | Production of positive-sense RNA by viral polymerase |
| RNA with reverse transcription | DNA intermediate, then transcription |
| DNA with reverse transcription | mRNA from DNA; an RNA intermediate is used in genome replication |

Sense describes the sequence’s relationship to an mRNA: positive-sense RNA has the same informational polarity, whereas negative-sense RNA is complementary. In positive-sense RNA viruses without reverse transcription, the genome can function directly as mRNA. A negative-sense genome first requires production of translatable positive-sense RNA. This distinction does not change the 5′→3′ direction in which ribosomes read an mRNA. In negative-sense RNA viruses that depend on a viral RNA-dependent RNA polymerase, the virion must carry this enzyme to initiate the step; the host's ribosomes cannot carry it out.

In the **lytic cycle** of a bacteriophage, the cell produces viral components, assembles new virions and may finally undergo lysis. A temperate phage can establish lysogeny: its genome persists as a prophage, often integrated into the chromosome, and is replicated with the host. An induction signal may initiate virion production and lysis. Lysogeny therefore describes a mode of persistence rather than continuous release of new particles.

In retroviruses, reverse transcriptase produces DNA using RNA as a template. Integrase inserts the proviral DNA into the cellular genome; transcription of the provirus produces RNA that can serve as mRNA or as new genomes. Animal viruses can exit by lysis, budding or other routes. Budding allows acquisition of the envelope without implying that the cell remains unharmed in the long term.

![Lytic cycle and lysogeny of a temperate bacteriophage](/figure/biologia-semestre-filtro/organizzazione-biologica/viral-en.svg)
:::

:::esempio{titolo="Locating a block in the cycle"}
An experimental drug prevents integration of retroviral DNA, while synthesis of DNA from RNA continues. The target is consistent with integrase. Reverse transcriptase must still be active because its product has been measured. The distinction follows from the sequence of steps and the product that accumulates, rather than identifying the target solely from the reduction in final virions.
:::

## Prokaryotes and information exchange

:::definizione{#procarioti titolo="Membranes, walls and bacterial structures"}
In a prokaryotic cell, the chromosome occupies the **nucleoid**, a region without a nuclear envelope. The cytoplasm contains ribosomes and numerous enzyme systems. The plasma membrane regulates exchange and can support energy-related processes, such as the respiratory chain in bacteria that perform respiration. Absence of mitochondria therefore does not prevent ATP production by oxidative phosphorylation.

The wall of many bacteria contains peptidoglycan, a sugar network with peptide cross-links that helps withstand osmotic pressure. **Gram-positive bacteria** have a thick peptidoglycan layer; in Gram-negative bacteria it is thinner and lies between the plasma membrane and an outer membrane. The latter contains lipopolysaccharide in its outer leaflet. During Gram staining, the crystal violet–iodine complex is retained differently during decolourisation; Gram-positive bacteria typically remain violet, whereas Gram-negative bacteria take up the counterstain and appear pink or red, for example when safranin is used.

A capsule may contribute to adhesion and protection. Fimbriae and pili participate in adhesion and, in some systems, DNA transfer. Flagella enable movement in many species. These accessory structures are not present in every bacterium. There are also exceptions concerning the wall: mycoplasmas lack one. Archaea have lipids with ether bonds and walls without the peptidoglycan typical of bacteria; they can live in ordinary as well as extreme environments.

![Comparison of Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacterial envelopes](/figure/biologia-semestre-filtro/organizzazione-biologica/gram-en.svg)
:::

:::definizione{#trasferimento-orizzontale titolo="Three routes of gene transfer"}
**Horizontal gene transfer** introduces DNA into a cell from another cellular lineage. Vertical transmission instead takes place from a mother cell to its descendants during division. The three classical routes differ in their vehicle and experimental requirements.

- During transformation, a competent cell takes up free DNA. An extracellular DNase can degrade the DNA before entry and prevent transfer.
- During conjugation, transfer requires contact and a conjugative apparatus, often associated with a plasmid. A barrier preventing contact can block it.
- During transduction, a bacteriophage carries bacterial DNA. The capsid can protect that DNA from external DNase before infection.

To change a recipient's trait stably, the DNA must be maintained and, when necessary, expressed. An acquired plasmid may contain a resistance gene; transfer does not automatically confer resistance to every antibiotic. Binary fission instead increases the number of cells. Distinguishing transfer from division helps interpret experiments in which a new trait appears without an immediate increase in population size.

![DNA vehicles in transformation, conjugation and transduction](/figure/biologia-semestre-filtro/organizzazione-biologica/hgt-en.svg)
:::

## Eukaryotes, compartments and differentiation

:::definizione{#eucarioti-endosimbiosi titolo="Internal organisation and endosymbiosis"}
Eukaryotes separate many processes into membrane-bound **compartments**. The nucleus contains chromosomes and allows spatial separation of transcription from cytosolic translation. The endoplasmic reticulum, Golgi apparatus, endosomes and lysosomes participate in synthesis, processing, sorting and degradation of substances. The nuclear envelope is continuous with the endoplasmic reticulum. Compartmentalisation allows different local conditions, such as the acidic environment of lysosomes, while regulated transport maintains communication.

The evolutionary formation of the nucleus is reconstructed through models of membrane reorganisation and expansion in the eukaryotic ancestor. Continuity between the nuclear envelope and endoplasmic reticulum is consistent with a connected history of these membranes, but alone does not establish a precise historical sequence. Separating chromosomes from the cytosol also required regulated exchange through pores and coordination between transcription and translation. Details of the origin remain under investigation.

Endosymbiotic theory reconstructs the origin of mitochondria from a bacterial ancestor stably associated with the ancestor of eukaryotes. Gene phylogenies, organellar DNA and several features of translation support a bacterial origin; the double membrane is consistent with this reconstruction. The combined evidence is more informative than a single resemblance. During evolution, many genes were lost or transferred to the nucleus. Consequently, most mitochondrial proteins are now encoded by nuclear genes, synthesised in the cytosol and imported.

Plastids in plants and algae derive from an endosymbiotic history involving a cyanobacterial ancestor. The origin of the nucleus and endomembrane system involves other evolutionary processes: automatically attributing every organelle to an engulfed bacterium would extend the theory beyond the evidence.

**Multicellularity** requires adhesion, communication and coordination of proliferation. Differentiation produces cells with different characteristics through different programmes of gene expression. A neuron and an epithelial cell may share much of their genetic information while synthesising different sets of proteins. This connection between a shared genome and distinct functions prepares the study of gene regulation and cell signalling.

## Biomolecules: composition and function

:::definizione{#macromolecole titolo="Carbohydrates, lipids and proteins"}
The properties of biomolecules depend on their chemical groups and arrangement. Water favours interaction with polar or charged groups; nonpolar groups tend to associate in aqueous environments. Covalent bonds maintain the molecular backbone, while many noncovalent interactions contribute to recognition and folding.

**Carbohydrates** include monosaccharides and molecules formed by joining them through glycosidic bonds. Glycogen is a branched glucose reserve in animals; cellulose has a structural role in plant cell walls. Glucose composition alone does not determine function: bond type and branching alter properties and accessibility to enzymes.

Lipids comprise different families. In triacylglycerols, glycerol is esterified with three fatty acids, forming an energy reserve. Phospholipids are amphipathic: a polar region interacts with water and nonpolar regions associate with one another. This arrangement favours bilayer formation. Cholesterol contributes to the properties of animal membranes and is a precursor of signalling molecules. Lipids do not all constitute polymers of a single repeating unit.

A protein amino acid has a central carbon attached to an amino group, a carboxyl group, hydrogen and a side chain denoted by R. The properties of R distinguish nonpolar, polar or charged residues and contribute to interactions within a protein. A peptide bond joins the carboxyl group of one residue to the amino group of the next.

**Proteins** are chains of amino acids joined by peptide bonds. Primary structure is the sequence. Alpha helices and beta sheets are secondary structures stabilised mainly by hydrogen bonds in the peptide backbone. Tertiary structure describes the overall folding of one chain; quaternary structure concerns the association of multiple chains. A domain is a region with a recognisable structural organisation and often a recognisable function. An active site may bring together residues that are far apart in the sequence but close after folding.

Denaturation changes folding and may impair function without requiring peptide-bond cleavage. Proteolytic digestion instead hydrolyses peptide bonds and produces fragments. This distinction helps interpret a protein that loses activity while retaining its chain length.

![Sequence, secondary structures, folding and association of chains](/figure/biologia-semestre-filtro/organizzazione-biologica/proteins-en.svg)
:::

:::esempio{titolo="From sequence to function"}
An amino acid substitution moves a negative charge away from the active site. Even if the protein retains the same length, substrate recognition or catalysis may change. Assessing the effect requires measurements of structure and activity: the mere presence of the protein does not establish that it is functional.
:::

## Enzymes and protein modifications

:::definizione{#enzimi-modificazioni titolo="Catalysis, regulation and modifications"}
An **enzyme** accelerates a reaction by providing a pathway with a lower activation barrier. The active site orients substrates and stabilises conditions favourable to conversion; binding may involve conformational changes. Many enzymes are proteins, while some RNA molecules have catalytic activity and are called ribozymes. The enzyme is regenerated at the end of its catalytic cycle.

Under the same conditions, catalysis changes neither the free-energy difference between reactants and products nor the position of equilibrium. It accelerates approach to equilibrium by acting in both directions. A favourable reaction may remain slow if the barrier is high. Temperature and pH influence structure, group ionisation and rate; a temperature increase may therefore accelerate a reaction over one range and impair the enzyme at higher temperatures.

Post-translational modifications alter a protein's properties after its synthesis. Kinases transfer phosphate groups, often from ATP; phosphatases remove phosphate groups by hydrolysis. Phosphorylation may activate or inhibit, depending on the site and protein. Acetyltransferases and deacetylases regulate acetylation; glycosylation and lipidation may influence folding, localisation and interactions.

The ubiquitin system uses E1, E2 and E3 enzymes. E3 ligases contribute to target recognition and ubiquitin transfer; deubiquitinases can remove it. Some ubiquitin chains promote proteasomal degradation, while other modifications have signalling or trafficking roles. Meaning depends on the type of ubiquitination, so ubiquitin attachment does not always indicate immediate destruction.

## DNA, RNA and chain direction

:::definizione{#acidi-nucleici titolo="Nucleotides and complementarity"}
A **nucleotide** comprises a nitrogenous base, a pentose sugar and one or more phosphate groups. A nucleoside comprises a base and a sugar. The sugar is deoxyribose in DNA and ribose in RNA. Adenine and guanine are purines; cytosine, thymine and uracil are pyrimidines. DNA normally uses thymine, while RNA uses uracil.

Phosphodiester bonds link the group at the 3′ position of one sugar to the phosphate attached to the 5′ position of the next. Each chain therefore has an orientation. In the Watson and Crick model of the double helix, strands are complementary and antiparallel: if one is written from 5′ to 3′, the other runs from 3′ to 5′ in the same spatial arrangement. A–T pairs form two hydrogen bonds and G–C pairs form three; base stacking and environmental conditions also contribute to stability.

For double-stranded DNA, $A=T$ and $G=C$ when percentages are measured across the whole molecule. These equalities are not required for an isolated single strand. If G bases represent 20% of the total, C also represents 20%; 60% remains for A and T, so each represents 30%.

mRNA provides the template for protein synthesis; tRNAs carry amino acids and recognise codons; rRNAs contribute to ribosome structure and catalysis. snRNAs participate in splicing, and many miRNAs contribute to regulation of expression. An RNA molecule can form paired regions by folding back on itself. The presence of RNA therefore does not imply that the molecule is translated.

:::

## Metabolism and energy coupling

:::definizione{#metabolismo titolo="Catabolism, anabolism and balances"}
**Catabolism** comprises pathways that break down molecules and make energy or intermediates available. Anabolism builds molecules and requires energy input and reducing power. Pathways are connected: intermediates obtained from breakdown can support biosynthesis, while ATP and electron carriers connect different processes.

The change in free energy, denoted by $\Delta G$, describes the thermodynamic tendency under the conditions considered. A negative value means that the process is favourable in that direction; its value alone does not determine the rate. In genuine coupling, two reactions share steps or intermediates and their free-energy changes add together. The mere simultaneous presence of ATP and reactants does not guarantee coupling.

Hydrolysis uses water to cleave a bond; condensation forms a bond while releasing a small molecule, often water. Cellular synthesis of biopolymers frequently uses activated precursors and coupled reactions. Describing it solely as spontaneous joining of monomers with water loss would omit the necessary energy-related steps.
:::

:::esempio{titolo="An energy balance with explicit assumptions"}
Under the exercise conditions, a conversion has $\Delta G_1=+12\,\mathrm{kJ/mol}$ and a coupled reaction has $\Delta G_2=-30\,\mathrm{kJ/mol}$. If the mechanism couples them in a one-to-one ratio, then

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta G_{\mathrm{tot}}&=\Delta G_1+\Delta G_2\\[6pt]
&=(12-30)\,\mathrm{kJ/mol}\\[6pt]
&=-18\,\mathrm{kJ/mol}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The overall process is favourable under the stated conditions. Catalysis still needs to be considered to determine its rate. These values are exercise data; the actual free-energy change associated with ATP also depends on cellular concentrations.
:::
