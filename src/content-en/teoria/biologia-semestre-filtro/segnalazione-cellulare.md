## Extracellular matrix and tissue properties

:::definizione{#matrice titolo="Components, organisation and remodelling"}
A tendon resists tension, whereas cartilage withstands repeated compression. These properties also depend on the **extracellular matrix**, a network of macromolecules produced and secreted by cells. Collagens form structures that resist tension; elastin allows reversible deformation. Proteoglycans contain glycosaminoglycan chains rich in negative charges, which attract cations and water. The resulting hydrated gel contributes to resistance to compression.

Fibronectin is an adhesive glycoprotein with regions that bind integrins and matrix components. Laminin helps organise the basal lamina, together with type IV collagen, nidogen and proteoglycans. The basal lamina supports epithelia and other cells and contributes to their organisation. The composition, amount and orientation of molecules matter as much as their presence; two tissues containing collagen can have different mechanical properties.

**Remodelling** includes deposition, reorganisation and degradation. Matrix metalloproteinases, also controlled by specific inhibitors, cleave extracellular components. This enables migration during development and repair, but also changes adhesions and signal availability. Excessive matrix deposition can stiffen a tissue; excessive degradation can weaken it. The matrix is therefore part of the environment that regulates cell function.

![Collagen, proteoglycans and fibronectin have different functions in the matrix.](/figure/biologia-semestre-filtro/segnalazione-cellulare/matrice-en.svg)
:::

:::esempio{titolo="Interpreting a change in the matrix"}
In a cartilage sample, proteoglycans decrease while the collagen network initially remains intact. Loss of the charged chains reduces the ability to retain water. The tissue may therefore resist compression less effectively even without an initial loss of collagen fibres. However, this observation does not establish that every mechanical property has decreased by the same proportion, because tension and compression involve different components and arrangements.
:::

## Matrix adhesion and force transmission

:::definizione{#integrine titolo="Integrins, the cytoskeleton and mechanotransduction"}
An adherent cell must connect external structures to its cytoskeleton. **Integrins** are transmembrane receptors consisting of an alpha and a beta subunit. The extracellular portion recognises matrix ligands; the cytoplasmic tails recruit proteins that provide the internal connection. In focal adhesions, talin and vinculin help connect integrins to actin. In hemidesmosomes, alpha6beta4 integrin connects, through proteins such as plectin, to intermediate filaments and to laminin in the basal lamina.

Integrins can change conformation and ligand affinity. In inside-out signalling, intracellular proteins favour a higher-affinity state. In outside-in signalling, ligand binding and adhesion organisation modify the activity of proteins including the kinase FAK. The receptor can therefore participate in both adhesion and the regulation of migration, survival and proliferation.

**Mechanotransduction** converts a force or a change in mechanical properties into a biochemical response. Tension generated by actomyosin can deform adhesion proteins and expose binding sites. A stiff surface and a deformable one can therefore produce different responses even when they display the same ligand. The outcome depends on the cell type and the controls present; stiffness alone does not determine a universal response.

![Connection between matrix, integrin, adaptor proteins and actin.](/figure/biologia-semestre-filtro/segnalazione-cellulare/adesione-en.svg)
:::

:::esempio{titolo="Separating recognition from anchoring"}
A cell has integrins that bind fibronectin, but talin cannot connect to the adhesion complex. Occupied receptors demonstrate ligand recognition. Reduced force transmission instead indicates a defect in the intracellular connection. To distinguish these steps, surface binding and the forces exerted are measured separately; simply counting integrins would not describe both functions.
:::

## Cell recognition and junctions

:::definizione{#giunzioni titolo="Barriers, anchoring structures and intercellular channels"}
Cells in an epithelium must remain attached, separate different environments and coordinate some activities. **Cadherins** mediate many cell-cell adhesions that depend on extracellular calcium. Calcium stabilises their external region, enabling adhesive interactions. Molecules of the immunoglobulin superfamily, including certain CAMs, participate in adhesions with different properties; the calcium dependence of cadherins does not automatically extend to all adhesion molecules.

Junctions differ in the structure they connect and their function:

- Adherens junctions connect cadherins to the actin cytoskeleton through protein complexes that include catenins.
- Desmosomes connect specialised cadherins, desmogleins and desmocollins, to intermediate filaments in adjacent cells.
- Hemidesmosomes connect a cell to the basal lamina through integrins and intermediate filaments.
- Tight junctions, containing proteins such as claudins and occludin, regulate paracellular permeability and help separate apical and basolateral domains.
- Gap junctions directly connect two cytoplasms through channels formed by paired connexons, each consisting of six connexins.

Gap-junction channels selectively allow ions and small molecules to pass. Their permeability depends on the connexins and channel regulation. Transfer of a small dye between adjacent cells can therefore indicate direct communication; passage of a large protein would require another explanation.

![Different junctions connect actin, intermediate filaments or cytoplasms.](/figure/biologia-semestre-filtro/segnalazione-cellulare/giunzioni-en.svg)
:::

:::esempio{titolo="Two measurements in the same epithelium"}
A treatment increases the passage of an extracellular tracer between cells, while transfer of a cytosolic dye between adjacent cells remains unchanged. The first finding suggests a change in the paracellular barrier; the second indicates that the measured communication through gap junctions is preserved. These measurements examine different routes. Attributing the defect to tight junctions also requires controls for cell death and epithelial continuity.
:::

## Modes and logic of communication

:::definizione{#comunicazione titolo="Distance, receptors and response selectivity"}
A signal reaches a target cell through different routes. In **contact-dependent signalling**, the ligand remains associated with the surface of the signalling cell. A paracrine signal diffuses within the nearby tissue; an autocrine signal acts on the cell that produces it or on cells of the same type. In endocrine signalling, a hormone travels through the blood to targets that may be distant. In synaptic signalling, a neurotransmitter is released at a specialised junction.

The distance travelled by a signal and the duration of the response are separate properties. An endocrine response can begin rapidly through proteins already present. A local signal can instead modify gene expression and have longer-lasting consequences. Classification depends on the route by which the signal reaches its target.

A **target cell** has an appropriate receptor and machinery able to transmit the signal. Two cells with the same receptor can respond differently because they express different effectors, transcription factors or regulators. Ligand concentration, affinity, receptor number and exposure duration also affect the response. Increasing the ligand may have a small effect when receptors are already close to saturation.
:::

:::definizione{#trasduzione titolo="Amplification, specificity and termination"}
**Signal transduction** is the sequence of events connecting recognition of a ligand to a response. Some steps generate second messengers; others modify existing proteins through phosphorylation, nucleotide binding or interactions between domains. Amplification occurs when one active molecule modifies many molecules at the next step. A multilevel cascade can therefore generate a large response from a few occupied receptors.

Scaffold proteins assemble components in the same compartment and favour selective encounters. Phosphatases remove phosphate groups; phosphodiesterases degrade cyclic nucleotides; pumps and exchangers lower cytosolic calcium. These processes contribute to signal termination. Phosphorylation can activate or inhibit a protein, depending on the site involved. Predicting an effect therefore requires knowing the sign of the connection in the pathway under study.

![A cascade connects recognition, transduction and response, with mechanisms for signal termination.](/figure/biologia-semestre-filtro/segnalazione-cellulare/cascata-en.svg)
:::

:::esempio{titolo="Ordering the components of a pathway"}
In a linear pathway, A activates B and B activates C. Blocking A reduces C. If a form of B that is active independently of A restores C, the result is consistent with B being downstream of A. This conclusion requires C to remain functional and the intervention not to activate an alternative pathway. Restoration of the response is therefore informative when accompanied by controls for the specificity of the perturbations.
:::

## Intracellular receptors and ligand-gated channels

:::definizione{#recettori-intracellulari titolo="Gene regulation and the NO–cGMP pathway"}
Many steroid hormones cross the membrane and bind cytoplasmic or nuclear receptors. The receptor complex regulates transcription of target genes through specific DNA sequences and cofactors. A change requiring new protein synthesis appears on a timescale consistent with transcription and translation. The response also depends on gene accessibility and the cofactors expressed in that cell.

**Nitric oxide**, NO, crosses membranes and activates soluble guanylyl cyclase by binding its haem group. The enzyme produces cGMP from GTP. In vascular smooth muscle, activation of effectors such as PKG favours relaxation. Because NO is rapidly transformed, its action is often local. An intracellular receptor can therefore be an enzyme; an internal location does not always imply direct transcriptional regulation.
:::

:::definizione{#recettori-canale titolo="Ligand binding and electrical change"}
A **ligand-gated ion channel** connects recognition of a ligand to the probability of opening an ion-conducting pore. The nicotinic acetylcholine receptor is an example of a ligand-gated cation channel. At a negative resting potential in muscle, the net flow of positive charges tends to depolarise the membrane. The direction and magnitude of the current depend on electrochemical gradients and the initial potential.

Direct binding of a ligand to a channel requires fewer steps than a cascade that modifies a channel indirectly. In an experiment, a rapid current change may suggest an ionotropic mechanism, but timing alone does not identify it. The protein that binds the ligand and the intermediates required must also be established.
:::

## G proteins and second messengers

:::definizione{#proteine-g titolo="The GDP–GTP cycle and coupling to GPCRs"}
**G proteins** change activity according to their bound nucleotide. A GEF promotes GDP release, allowing available cytosolic GTP to bind. A GAP accelerates GTP hydrolysis and favours return to the GDP-bound state. This is nucleotide exchange followed by hydrolysis; the GEF does not directly add a phosphate to the bound GDP.

Monomeric G proteins include Ras and several families involved in trafficking or the cytoskeleton. Heterotrimeric G proteins consist of alpha, beta and gamma subunits. A ligand-activated **GPCR** promotes GDP–GTP exchange on Gα. Both Gα-GTP and Gβγ can regulate effectors. GTP hydrolysis on Gα contributes to termination and re-establishment of the inactive complex.

In the Gs pathway, Gα-GTP stimulates adenylyl cyclase, which converts ATP to cAMP. cAMP binds the regulatory subunits of PKA, increasing the activity of its catalytic subunits. Gαi can instead inhibit certain adenylyl cyclases. Gαq-GTP can activate phospholipase C beta, linking a GPCR to IP3 and DAG production. The effect of a GPCR therefore depends on the G protein and effectors to which it is coupled in the cell concerned.

![The G-protein cycle and the Gs–cAMP–PKA pathway.](/figure/biologia-semestre-filtro/segnalazione-cellulare/gpcr-en.svg)
:::

:::definizione{#secondi-messaggeri titolo="Phosphoinositides, calcium and compartments"}
**Second messengers** are intracellular molecules whose availability changes after receptor activation. Phospholipase C, activated in several pathways, cleaves the phospholipid PIP2 into IP3 and DAG. IP3 is soluble and reaches channel receptors on the endoplasmic reticulum membrane; their opening releases calcium into the cytosol. DAG remains in the lipid bilayer. DAG and calcium help activate conventional PKCs; other isoforms have different requirements.

Cytosolic calcium can bind calmodulin, which modifies the activity of target enzymes. The response also depends on the duration, frequency and location of calcium increases. Reticular pumps such as SERCA take calcium back up; pumps and exchangers in the plasma membrane help extrude it. A reversible response therefore requires both release and restoration of the gradients.

Phosphodiesterases degrade cAMP or cGMP according to their specificity. Inhibiting a phosphodiesterase can prolong a signal that has already been produced; the effect depends on synthesis of the cyclic nucleotide. The amount measured at any instant reflects the balance between production and removal.

![PIP2 generates soluble IP3 and membrane-bound DAG; IP3 promotes calcium release.](/figure/biologia-semestre-filtro/segnalazione-cellulare/plc-en.svg)
:::

## Desensitisation and phototransduction

:::definizione{#desensitizzazione titolo="Reduced responsiveness and the light signal"}
**Desensitisation** reduces the response during persistent or repeated stimulation. For many GPCRs, GRKs phosphorylate the activated receptor and promote arrestin binding. Coupling to the G protein decreases; internalisation may follow. Internalised receptors can be recycled or degraded. A reduced response can therefore occur even while the ligand remains present, and its duration depends on receptor fate and other controls.

In rod cells, rhodopsin contains the chromophore retinal. Absorption of light converts retinal from the 11-cis to the all-trans configuration, initiating the conformational change that activates the receptor. Rhodopsin activates transducin, which promotes the activity of the phosphodiesterase PDE6. The cGMP concentration decreases, and cGMP-dependent cation channels close. Because the inward current decreases while potassium continues to leave the cell, the membrane **hyperpolarises** and glutamate release decreases.

In darkness, cGMP maintains a fraction of channels open; the rod is relatively depolarised and releases glutamate. Rhodopsin inactivation, GTP hydrolysis on transducin and restoration of cGMP favour recovery. The decrease in intracellular calcium contributes to adaptation by regulating, among other processes, cGMP synthesis. The light response is understood by following every step, because increased phosphodiesterase activity produces a decrease in the messenger.

![Light lowers cGMP, closes channels and hyperpolarises the rod cell.](/figure/biologia-semestre-filtro/segnalazione-cellulare/visione-en.svg)
:::

## Receptor tyrosine kinases, growth and metabolism

:::definizione{#rtk-ras titolo="EGF, Ras and the MAPK cascade"}
Many **receptor tyrosine kinases**, or RTKs, are activated by ligand-induced organisation of the receptor complex. Phosphorylation of specific tyrosines creates recruitment sites for intracellular proteins. For the EGF receptor, adaptors such as Grb2 and the GEF SOS connect the receptor to Ras activation. Ras-GTP promotes Raf activation, followed by the kinases MEK and ERK. ERK modifies cytoplasmic and nuclear targets, contributing to responses such as proliferation and differentiation depending on context.

A Ras variant with reduced GTP hydrolysis can maintain signalling for longer than normal. A **proto-oncogene** normally contributes to growth, proliferation or survival. Alterations that increase or prolong its activity, or increase its expression, can convert it into an **oncogene**, favouring inappropriate responses. This gain-of-function model differs from loss of a tumour-suppressor brake. Persistent activation of one pathway alone does not describe every step in tumour transformation, which also depends on other controls and alterations.
:::

:::definizione{#insulina-pi3k titolo="Insulin, PIP3 and glucose transport"}
The insulin receptor is an RTK already present as a complex of associated subunits. Hormone binding changes receptor activity and promotes phosphorylation of substrates such as IRS. Recruited PI3K produces PIP3 from PIP2. PIP3 remains in the membrane and is recognised by appropriate binding domains, such as the Akt PH domain. This recruitment contributes to Akt activation, which requires further phosphorylation and is distinct from lipid binding alone. The lipid phosphatase PTEN converts PIP3 to PIP2 and limits this signalling branch.

In skeletal muscle and adipose tissue, insulin signalling promotes translocation of vesicles containing GLUT4 to the membrane. The number of exposed transporters therefore increases, as does the capacity for glucose entry. A rapid response can use proteins that have already been synthesised. Insulin also activates other branches, including Ras-MAPK; the hormone's various effects depend on integration of these pathways.

IP3 and PIP3 have different properties. IP3 results from PIP2 cleavage by phospholipase C and is soluble. PIP3 results from PIP2 phosphorylation by PI3K and is a membrane lipid. Identifying the enzyme and compartment distinguishes the two routes even though their abbreviations look similar.

:::
