## Exercise 1

List the multiples of $8$ between $30$ and $70$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
8 \cdot 4 &= 32 \\[6pt]
8 \cdot 5 &= 40 \\[6pt]
8 \cdot 6 &= 48 \\[6pt]
8 \cdot 7 &= 56 \\[6pt]
8 \cdot 8 &= 64
\end{aligned}
$$

The multiples of $8$ between $30$ and $70$ are $32, 40, 48, 56, 64$.

<div class="spiegazione">

The multiples of $8$ are obtained by multiplying $8$ by the natural numbers $0, 1, 2, 3, \dots$. The first four are $0$, $8$, $16$, and $24$, which are less than $30$, so we start at $8 \cdot 4 = 32$. Each multiple is obtained by adding $8$ to the previous one, so after $32$ come $40$, $48$, $56$, and $64$. The next multiple is $64 + 8 = 72$, which exceeds $70$, so the list stops at $64$.

## Exercise 2

Determine whether $84$ is a multiple of $6$ and whether it is a multiple of $8$.

$$
84 : 6 = 14 \quad \text{with remainder } 0, \qquad 6 \cdot 14 = 84
$$
$$
84 : 8 = 10 \quad \text{with remainder } 4, \qquad 8 \cdot 10 + 4 = 84
$$

The number $84$ is a multiple of $6$ and is not a multiple of $8$.

<div class="spiegazione">

A number is a multiple of another when division gives remainder $0$. The division $84 : 6$ gives quotient $14$ and remainder $0$ because $6 \cdot 14 = 60 + 24 = 84$. Therefore $84$ is a **multiple** of $6$, and $6$ is a divisor of $84$.

The division $84 : 8$ gives quotient $10$ because $8 \cdot 10 = 80$, and its remainder is $84 - 80 = 4$. The remainder is different from $0$, so $84$ **is not a multiple** of $8$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

List all the divisors of $36$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
36 &= 1 \cdot 36 \\[6pt]
36 &= 2 \cdot 18 \\[6pt]
36 &= 3 \cdot 12 \\[6pt]
36 &= 4 \cdot 9 \\[6pt]
36 &= 6 \cdot 6
\end{aligned}
$$

The divisors of $36$ are $1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 9, 12, 18, 36$.

<div class="spiegazione">

Look for divisors in pairs. Try dividing $36$ by $1$, $2$, $3$, … . Each time the division has remainder $0$, we find two divisors: the divisor tested and the quotient. For example, $36 : 3 = 12$, so $3$ and $12$ are both divisors.

Division by $5$ gives remainder $1$ because $5 \cdot 7 = 35$, so $5$ is not a divisor. Next we reach $6 \cdot 6 = 36$, where the two factors are equal. After $6$, the pairs would repeat in reverse order, as in $9 \cdot 4$, so the search is complete. Listing all the numbers we found in order gives **nine divisors**, from $1$ to $36$.

</div>

## Exercise 4

Find the common divisors of $18$ and $30$.

$$
\text{divisors of } 18: \quad 1, 2, 3, 6, 9, 18
$$
$$
\text{divisors of } 30: \quad 1, 2, 3, 5, 6, 10, 15, 30
$$

The common divisors of $18$ and $30$ are $1, 2, 3, 6$.

<div class="spiegazione">

The divisors of $18$ come from the factor pairs $1 \cdot 18$, $2 \cdot 9$, and $3 \cdot 6$. The divisors of $30$ come from the factor pairs $1 \cdot 30$, $2 \cdot 15$, $3 \cdot 10$, and $5 \cdot 6$.

A common divisor must appear in both lists. Neither $9$ nor $18$ divides $30$, whereas $5$, $10$, $15$, and $30$ do not divide $18$, so the **common divisors** are $1$, $2$, $3$, and $6$. The largest is $6$, which is the greatest common divisor of the two numbers.

</div>
