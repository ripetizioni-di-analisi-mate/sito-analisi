## Exercise 1

Bacterium A has thick peptidoglycan and one cell membrane; bacterium B has thin peptidoglycan between two membranes, with lipopolysaccharide in the outer membrane. Classify the two envelopes and predict the typical result of a correctly performed Gram stain.

![Envelope structures to compare](/figure/biologia-semestre-filtro/organizzazione-biologica/gram-en.svg)

A matches the **Gram-positive** model; B matches the **Gram-negative** model. After decolourisation, A retains the crystal violet–iodine complex and remains violet. B loses the initial dye and, with safranin as counterstain, appears pink or red.

Classification uses both peptidoglycan thickness and presence of the outer membrane.

<div class="spiegazione">

Final colour depends on how the envelope responds to the treatment steps. B’s outer membrane is distinct from the plasma membrane, with thin peptidoglycan between them. In A, the thick layer contributes to retaining the complex during decolourisation. The prediction concerns the typical result with suitable cultures and technique: sample condition or technical errors can alter a real test. The diagram shows layer organisation rather than dimensions to scale. In both cases, the nucleoid still lacks a nuclear envelope.

</div>

## Exercise 2

In system A, purified DNA transfers a trait to competent bacteria, and DNase added before entry eliminates transfer. In system B, the trait passes through purified phage particles and resists DNase while capsids remain intact. Identify the two mechanisms and the control explaining the different sensitivity.

System A indicates **transformation**, because the acquired material is free DNA. System B indicates **transduction**, because the vehicle is a bacteriophage.

To test protection, compare intact and permeabilised capsids, each with and without DNase. Measure DNA integrity directly after stopping DNase activity. If DNA remains intact with intact capsids, with or without DNase, and with permeabilised capsids without DNase, but is degraded only with permeabilised capsids plus DNase, the result supports protection by exclusion of the enzyme.

<div class="spiegazione">

The two systems can produce the same heritable trait through different routes. The distinction uses the DNA vehicle and enzyme accessibility, together with competent cells or phage particles. DNase degrades DNA it can reach; by itself, it does not demonstrate absence of a route when the substrate is protected. Loss of transfer after capsid opening alone is insufficient: damaging the vector may already prevent infection even without DNase. A direct DNA assay separates that effect from degradation of the genetic material. Permeabilised capsids without DNase control for DNA damage caused by treatment alone; free DNA exposed to DNase checks that the enzyme is active.

</div>
