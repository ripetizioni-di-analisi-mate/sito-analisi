## Exercise 1

Molecule A contains glycerol linked to three fatty acids. Molecule B has a phosphate-containing polar head and two nonpolar tails. Identify which model suits lipid storage and which suits bilayer formation in water. Explain the orientation of B’s regions.

A corresponds to a **triacylglycerol**, suited to energy storage. B matches the amphipathic model of a **membrane phospholipid**. In a bilayer, polar heads face the aqueous compartments and tails associate in the interior.

The comparison uses regions with different affinities for water as well as the chemical classification of components.

<div class="spiegazione">

A lipid does not have the same function as every other lipid merely because it belongs to the same broad class. A triacylglycerol has a large nonpolar component and is suited to accumulation in stores. The phospholipid described instead combines polar and nonpolar regions; their arrangement maintains favourable contacts with water and other tails. Completely reversing the orientation would expose many nonpolar regions to water and bury polar heads. Bilayer structure therefore follows from molecular properties and noncovalent interactions; its formation does not require joining all lipids into a covalent polymer.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Two polysaccharides consist of glucose. P is branched and is degraded by an enzyme that recognises its bonds; Q has a different structural organisation and the same enzyme does not degrade it. Explain why a shared monomer is insufficient to predict equal digestibility, and relate the case to glycogen and cellulose.

Glucose composition is shared, but **bond type and chain organisation** can differ. An enzyme recognises a specific geometry and chemical environment; the bond in Q may therefore be unsuitable as a substrate.

Glycogen is a branched reserve, whereas cellulose forms structures in plant cell walls. The distinction also concerns bond configuration, not just the presence of branches.

<div class="spiegazione">

Predicting biological function requires information about how units are connected. The same components can form molecules with different mechanical properties, accessibility and interactions. An enzyme active site recognises substrate features and stabilises an appropriate reaction pathway. Failure to degrade Q does not necessarily mean that Q lacks glucose or covalent bonds. In this comparison, controlling composition is precisely what allows the difference to be attributed to organisation. Concentration, reaction conditions and enzyme integrity must also be comparable in a real experiment.

</div>
