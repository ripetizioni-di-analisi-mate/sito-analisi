## Exercise 1

A virus has a negative-sense RNA genome. After entry, an inhibitor specifically blocks its RNA-dependent RNA polymerase. Host translation machinery remains intact. Predict the effect on initial viral mRNA production and explain why ribosomes cannot compensate for the block.

![Block in mRNA production](/figure/biologia-semestre-filtro/organizzazione-biologica/worked-rna-en.svg)

The required route is:

$$\mathrm{RNA}(-)\longrightarrow\mathrm{mRNA}\longrightarrow\text{proteins}.$$

The block affects the first step. Initial viral mRNA production **decreases or stops according to the effectiveness of inhibition**. Intact ribosomes cannot directly translate the negative-sense genome as though it were mRNA.

<div class="spiegazione">

Sense describes the sequence’s relationship to one readable as a messenger. Before protein synthesis, a positive-sense strand must be produced using RNA as a template. This requires viral polymerase, which the virion must make available at the beginning of the cycle. Ribosomes catalyse translation and do not synthesise complementary RNA strands. A healthy cellular apparatus is therefore insufficient to compensate for the blocked viral step. The reasoning separates host dependence from availability of virus-specific enzymes; a positive-sense RNA virus would have a different starting point for initial translation.

</div>

## Exercise 2

In a retrovirus experiment, the control contains viral RNA, viral DNA and integrated proviral DNA. The treated sample contains viral RNA and DNA but no integrated provirus. Assuming adequate measurement sensitivity, locate the block and distinguish the conclusion about the steps from the conclusion about the molecular target.

The relevant sequence is:

$$
\begin{aligned}\mathrm{RNA}&\xrightarrow{\text{reverse transcription}}\text{viral DNA}\\[6pt]\text{viral DNA}&\xrightarrow{\text{integration}}\text{provirus}.\end{aligned}
$$

Measured DNA shows that reverse transcription has not completely stopped. The block is consistent with **a defect between viral DNA availability and integration**. Integrase inhibition is one possibility; the findings do not exclude defects in transport or access to the nuclear target.

<div class="spiegazione">

To locate a block, products of successive steps are compared. Presence of an upstream product and absence of a downstream product narrow the affected interval. This logic is more informative than reduced final virion output alone, which could result from many different steps. However, an interval in the cycle can contain several molecular events: observing unintegrated DNA does not automatically prove that the treatment binds integrase itself. An enzyme-activity assay or DNA-localisation measurement would help distinguish alternatives. The exercise therefore requires a conclusion proportionate to the evidence.

</div>
