## Exercise 1

A double-stranded DNA fragment contains 100 base pairs. The complete molecule contains 60 guanines. Calculate the numbers of cytosines, adenines and thymines and their percentages of all bases.

The total number of bases is $2\cdot100=200$. By complementarity, $C=G=60$. The remainder is

$$
A+T=200-60-60=80.
$$

Since $A=T$, this gives $A=T=40$. Percentages are

$$
\begin{aligned}
G=C&=\frac{60}{200}\cdot100\%=30\%,\\[6pt]
A=T&=\frac{40}{200}\cdot100\%=20\%.
\end{aligned}
$$

The check gives $60+60+40+40=200$ bases and $30+30+20+20=100\%$.

<div class="spiegazione">

The first step distinguishes base pairs from individual bases. A double helix with 100 pairs contains 200 bases, so 200 is the percentage denominator. Equality between complementary bases applies to the complete double-stranded molecule specified in the problem. Subtracting G and C gives the remaining total for A and T; their equality allows it to be divided into two parts. If the data concerned only one strand, this procedure would not automatically apply. The two final checks verify both the overall count and percentage normalisation.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A DNA strand is 5′-ATGCC-3′. Write the complement in the same spatial arrangement and then from 5′ to 3′. Count the hydrogen bonds in the five canonical pairs, ignoring other interactions.

In the same spatial arrangement:

- given strand: 5′-ATGCC-3′;
- complement: 3′-TACGG-5′.

Reading the complement from 5′ to 3′ gives **5′-GGCAT-3′**. There are two A–T pairs and three G–C pairs, so

$$2\cdot2+3\cdot3=4+9=13$$

hydrogen bonds connect the paired bases.

<div class="spiegazione">

Pairing is performed by replacing each base with its complement while initially preserving its position relative to the given strand. The ends must be opposite because the double helix is antiparallel. Only then is the reading order reversed to write the new strand using the 5′-to-3′ convention. Bond counting uses two for A–T and three for G–C; it is not multiplied by two again because each bond already joins the two bases in a pair. The value 13 describes these bonds, not the full stabilisation energy, to which base stacking also contributes.

</div>
