## Exercise 1

Mitogens are removed from one culture before the restriction point and from another just after it. DNA is intact and other nutrients are sufficient in both. Why may the second enter S while the first arrests?

Before the restriction point, the proliferative programme still depends on mitogenic signals. After this transition, the network supporting S-phase entry can maintain commitment for the cycle already initiated. The different response is therefore compatible with a **different regulatory state**, despite the same final environment.

![Mitogenic signals, Rb phosphorylation and S-phase gene activation](/figure/biologia-semestre-filtro/ciclo-cellulare/rb-en.svg)

<div class="spiegazione">

The comparison depends on the timing of withdrawal, not just medium composition at measurement. The cell retains consequences of earlier signalling: in the Rb–E2F model, transcriptional restraint has been reduced and the replication programme initiated. This does not remove DNA-damage checkpoints or demonstrate indefinite growth-factor independence in later cycles.

</div>

## Exercise 2

An Rb variant still binds E2F but does not respond to the phosphorylations that normally reduce this binding. Cyclin–CDK complexes are active after mitogenic stimulation. What prediction follows for E2F target genes in this model?

E2F-dependent genes may remain relatively repressed because upstream activity fails to remove the mutant brake effectively. Active CDKs alone do not establish that E2F is released. A useful comparison measures E2F-target expression and Rb–E2F binding in the variant and control.

<div class="spiegazione">

Follow the causal sequence to its interruption: the signal reaches the kinase, but the target does not respond normally. This is the opposite of losing Rb inhibitory function, which would weaken the brake. Specifying the variant’s behaviour prevents automatically assigning the same effect to every mutation in one gene.

</div>
