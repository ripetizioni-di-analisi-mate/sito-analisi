## Exercise 1

After DNA damage, cells A increase p53 and p21 and reduce S-phase entry. Cells B increase p53 but not p21; other conditions are equivalent. What does the comparison suggest, and what outcome remains unproven?

The comparison suggests a defect linking the p53 response to p21 accumulation in B. This specific brake on CDK complexes is expected to be weaker. It does not establish that B repaired its DNA or necessarily became cancerous; residual damage and additional cellular properties must be measured.

<div class="spiegazione">

An increase in p53 indicates a response, not proof that every downstream effect occurred. Failure to accumulate p21 can reflect regulation, stability or gene alterations, so the observation does not identify a particular mutation by itself. Arrest must also be distinguished from successful repair: pausing proliferation provides time but does not automatically restore DNA.

</div>

## Exercise 2

One cell acquires a constitutively active growth-signalling receptor; another loses the function of both RB1 copies. Classify the two mechanisms and explain why they cannot both be described as simply “more protein”.

A constitutively active receptor represents a possible **oncogenic gain of function**, signalling without normal control. RB1 loss represents **loss of tumour-suppressor function**, removing a brake. In the first case activity can change without increased abundance; in the second, a protein may still be present but functionally inactive.

<div class="spiegazione">

Classification follows the effect on the circuit, not merely the amount detected by an antibody. A persistently active signal and a lost brake can both promote proliferation through different steps. Even loss of both RB1 copies does not summarise every property involved in tumour progression. The case illustrates a cellular mechanism, not a diagnosis from two isolated observations.

</div>
