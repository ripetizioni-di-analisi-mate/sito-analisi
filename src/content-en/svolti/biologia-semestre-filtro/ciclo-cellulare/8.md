## Exercise 1

A human cell is described as a “newly ovulated secondary oocyte”. State its normal meiotic stage, ploidy and DNA content. Why does calling it haploid not mean that meiosis has finished?

The secondary oocyte is normally arrested in **metaphase II**, with $n=23$ duplicated chromosomes and $2C$ DNA. Homologues separated in meiosis I, so it is haploid; sister chromatids must still separate to reach $n,1C$. Normal completion follows activation associated with fertilisation.

<div class="spiegazione">

Haploid means one homologue of each type, not one DNA copy when sisters remain joined. The first division has reduced ploidy; the second has begun but is incomplete. This arrest must not be confused with the primary oocyte’s earlier, prolonged prophase-I arrest.

</div>

## Exercise 2

In a model without cell loss, 12 primary spermatocytes complete both meiotic divisions. How many spermatids result? Does subsequent sperm formation require another division? Compare functional gamete numbers with 12 primary oocytes completing oogenesis.

Each primary spermatocyte produces four spermatids: $12\cdot4=48$. Spermiogenesis remodels their shape and components without a third meiotic division. In the ideal model, 12 primary oocytes yield **12 large functional gametes**, plus polar bodies, rather than 48 equivalent eggs.

<div class="spiegazione">

The factor of four comes from two divisions after one DNA replication. Oogenesis partitions cytoplasm asymmetrically, concentrating resources in one cell, so nuclear products and functional gametes are not equivalent counts. Absence of loss is specified: real yields cannot be inferred merely by multiplying the initial cell number by four.

</div>
