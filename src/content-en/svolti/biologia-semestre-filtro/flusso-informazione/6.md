## Exercise 1

Transcription of a gene is stopped completely. Its mRNA falls from 160 to 40 units in 30 minutes. Assuming decay with a constant half-life, determine the half-life and the amount predicted 45 minutes after the stop.

The final-to-initial amount ratio is $\dfrac{40}{160}=\dfrac{1}{4}=\left(\dfrac{1}{2}\right)^2$. Two half-lives have elapsed.

$$t_{1/2}=\frac{30\,\mathrm{min}}{2}=15\,\mathrm{min}.$$

At 45 minutes, three half-lives have elapsed:

$$M(45)=160\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^3=20\,\mathrm{u}.$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The change from 160 to 40 includes two halvings, 160 → 80 and 80 → 40. Because it takes 30 minutes, each halving takes 15 minutes under the model’s conditions. The next interval, from 30 to 45 minutes, produces another halving from 40 to 20. A linear loss of four units per minute is not used: absolute loss rate decreases as fewer molecules remain. A graphical or numerical check must therefore follow an exponential curve, consistent with a constant fraction removed per interval.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Two cellular conditions produce the same amount of nascent RNA per unit time. Messenger half-life is 10 minutes in the control and 20 minutes in the treatment. Both are at steady state with first-order removal. Predict the treated-to-control mRNA ratio using $M=\dfrac{s}{k}$ and $k=\dfrac{\ln 2}{t_{1/2}}$, where $s$ is synthesis and $k$ the removal constant.

With equal synthesis, the ratio of amounts is the inverse of the ratio of removal constants:

$$\frac{M_{\mathrm{T}}}{M_{\mathrm{C}}}=\frac{k_{\mathrm{C}}}{k_{\mathrm{T}}}=\frac{20}{10}=2.$$

The treatment’s steady-state mRNA amount is therefore **twice as large**, even without increased transcription.

<div class="spiegazione">

At steady state, the number of molecules present remains constant because production and removal balance. A doubled half-life corresponds to a halved degradation constant. With the same molecular input, twice the amount must then be present for total removal to match synthesis again. The conclusion uses both steady state and first-order kinetics. Immediately after treatment, before the new balance is reached, it would be incorrect to assume the amount has already doubled.

</div>
