## Exercise 1

After complete synthesis inhibition, a protein falls from 240 to 60 units in 6 hours. Assuming a constant half-life, determine the half-life and the amount after 9 hours.

The decrease corresponds to two halvings, $240\to120\to60$.

$$t_{1/2}=\frac{6\,\mathrm{h}}{2}=3\,\mathrm{h}.$$

After 9 hours, three half-lives have elapsed:

$$P(9)=240\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^3=30\,\mathrm{u}.$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Half-life is derived from the number of halvings needed to explain the initial-to-final amount ratio. The same method applies to RNA and protein if the decay model is the same, but their molecular removal mechanisms differ. Here the observation concerns protein, so it cannot determine its mRNA’s half-life. At 9 hours, the numerical check is one more halving from 60 to 30 relative to the 6-hour measurement. New synthesis is explicitly excluded and does not enter the balance.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A protein increases after proteasome inhibition. Its gene’s nascent RNA, mRNA abundance and incorporation of new amino acids into the protein remain unchanged. State the supported explanation and an additional experiment that would directly test it.

Measured production remains unchanged, while a degradation apparatus is perturbed. The increase is therefore consistent with **reduced removal and greater protein stability**.

A previously labelled protein population can be followed over time, comparing signal disappearance with and without inhibitor. Slower decay under treatment directly supports increased stability.

<div class="spiegazione">

Protein amount alone does not separate synthesis from degradation, but the question supplies measurements controlling the main production steps. The proposed experiment follows molecules already present, so signal loss reports their fate rather than production of new chains. Comparable labelling and detection conditions and control of treatment effects on viability are needed. Interpretation remains specific to the target protein: general proteasome inhibition can also have indirect consequences that should not automatically be assigned to the studied gene.

</div>
