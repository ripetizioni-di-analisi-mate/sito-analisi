## Exercise 1

A normal human somatic cell moves from G1 to G2. Count chromosomes and nuclear DNA molecules in both phases. Separately, consider meiosis with one nondisjunction event in one of the two meiosis II cells, after normal meiosis I: how many of the four products are aneuploid for the affected chromosome?

G1 → 46 chromosomes, 46 nuclear DNA molecules.

G2 → 46 duplicated chromosomes, 92 nuclear DNA molecules.

An error in one meiosis II cell → two normal products, one with an extra copy and one lacking a copy. Two of four products are aneuploid.

<div class="spiegazione">

Replication adds one sister chromatid to each chromosome, doubling nuclear DNA molecules. Before sister separation, chromosome number remains unchanged. This prevents calling a diploid cell tetraploid merely because it completed S phase.

In the described meiosis, homologs separated normally during the first division. One of the two cells completes the second division normally and produces two correct gametes. In the other, two sisters move together: one product receives two copies of the chromosome and the other none. Relative to the normal haploid gamete, this gives one product with $n+1$ chromosomes and one with $n-1$, besides the two normal products. The count concerns one event and only the specified chromosome.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Chromosome banding shows order A–B–C–D–E, while the rearranged homolog has A–D–C–B–E. A separate analysis finds a single-base substitution in a gene within region C. Classify the two events and explain why karyotyping alone does not describe both at the same resolution.

Changed band order → inversion of B–C–D.

One-base change → a point sequence variant.

A sufficiently large inversion can be recognizable by banding; the substitution requires sequence-level analysis.

<div class="spiegazione">

Comparing the orders, every region remains present once. The middle tract reverses orientation, so the rearrangement is an inversion rather than a deletion. Centromere position is unspecified, so the available data do not distinguish paracentric from pericentric inversion.

The substitution within C acts at another scale: it can leave the size, number and arrangement of visible bands unchanged. A karyotype normal at that level would therefore not exclude the point variant. The two tests address different questions about the same genomic region. Furthermore, preserving material amount in the inversion does not guarantee absence of effects, because a breakpoint can disrupt a gene or change its regulatory context.

</div>
