## Exercise 1

A mother $X^AX^a$ and father $X^AY$ are considered for an X-linked recessive trait. In the model, heterozygous females are unaffected and penetrance in $X^aY$ males is complete. Calculate the probability of an affected son in a pregnancy of unknown sex and the probability of being affected given that the child is male. Assume equally likely sexes.

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(\text{affected son})&=\frac12\cdot\frac12=\frac14,\\[6pt]
P(\text{affected}\mid\text{male})&=\frac12.
\end{aligned}
$$

Daughters always receive $X^A$ from the father; half are $X^AX^A$ and half $X^AX^a$.

<div class="spiegazione">

A son receives Y from the father and one of the mother’s X chromosomes. The mother transmits $X^a$ with probability one half. If sex is already known, this is the only remaining choice relevant to the affected genotype, so the probability is one half.

If sex is unknown, the paternal gamete must also carry Y, an event with probability one half. Multiplying the two factors gives one quarter. The answers do not conflict because they concern different reference sets. Daughters always possess paternal X with A and are unaffected in the stated model. The specification about heterozygotes makes the teaching simplification explicit rather than extending it to every biological situation involving X-linked expression.

</div>

## Exercise 2

An X-linked dominant trait is fully penetrant. The father is $X^DY$ and the mother $X^DX^d$; all genotypes considered have equal viability. Calculate the expected affected fractions among sons and daughters and the overall trait probability with equally likely sexes.

Daughters → $X^DX^D$ or $X^DX^d$: all affected.

Sons → $X^DY$ or $X^dY$: half affected.

$$
P(\text{trait})=\frac12\cdot1+\frac12\cdot\frac12=\frac34.
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Separate the sexes because the paternal contribution changes. Every daughter receives $X^D$ from the father: whichever X she receives from the mother, she carries at least one D and shows the trait with complete penetrance. Every son instead receives paternal Y, so his result depends only on maternal X, D or d with equal probability.

The overall probability is the sum of two disjoint cases: an affected daughter or an affected son. The first contributes one half and the second one quarter, totaling three quarters. Equal viability prevents selective loss of a genotype from changing frequencies among births. Dominance determines heterozygote phenotype, while the transmission model determines which chromosomes reach the children.

</div>
