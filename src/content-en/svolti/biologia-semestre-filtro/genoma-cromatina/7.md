## Exercise 1

In a diploid cell, one chromosome of one of the two sets contains two genes, G1 and G2, at distinct loci with similar sequences. The other homologue carries variants of both. Distinguish the G1–G2 relationship from that between the two G1 versions.

G1 and G2 are distinct genes, consistent with a family arising through duplication. The two G1 versions at the same locus on the homologues are alleles.

<div class="spiegazione">

Position comes first, because sequence similarity alone is insufficient. G1 and G2 coexist at different sites within the same set; they cannot be alternative alleles of one locus. Duplication can explain relatedness and must be tested with evolutionary comparisons. The two G1 versions instead occupy corresponding sites on the homologues and are variants of that locus. The same individual can therefore possess a multigene family and two alleles for each autosomal gene. Confusing these dimensions leads to counting every allelic variant as another family gene.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Three regions respectively produce a protein, a functional rRNA and an RNA from a copy that has lost its original protein product. Classify them without automatically assigning no function to the third.

The first is a protein-coding gene; the second is a non-protein-coding RNA gene; the third may be a transcribed pseudogene with possible functions requiring investigation.

<div class="spiegazione">

Classification considers product and origin. In the second case the RNA itself functions in the ribosome, so translation is not needed for the gene to be functional. In the third case the copy has lost the ability to produce the original protein, consistent with a pseudogene. Detectable RNA nevertheless demonstrates transcription and leaves open possible additional roles. Establishing those roles would require experiments on the product and the consequences of its removal. Non-coding refers to protein production and is not a general judgement of biological usefulness.

</div>
