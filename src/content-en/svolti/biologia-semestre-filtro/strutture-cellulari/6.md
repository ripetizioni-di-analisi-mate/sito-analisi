## Exercise 1

A secreted protein is labelled during a short pulse. During the chase it appears first in the reticulum, then in the Golgi and finally in the medium. Selective COPII blockade retains it in the reticulum despite an intact entry signal. Reconstruct the steps and distinguish this defect from SRP loss.

The sequence is synthesis and reticulum entry, maturation, COPII-dependent exit, Golgi passage and secretion. The stated blockade affects reticulum exit. Loss of SRP function in the classical pathway would instead impair initial targeting of the ribosome-nascent-chain complex.

![Secretory pathway and topology](/figure/biologia-semestre-filtro/strutture-cellulari/secretoria-en.svg)

<div class="spiegazione">

The short pulse labels a molecular population that can be followed over time. The chase reveals progression rather than comparing molecules synthesized at different times. Cargo presence in the reticulum under COPII blockade shows that entry occurred; automatically assigning the defect to the first signal sequence would be inconsistent. Folding control remains an additional requirement for export. Interpretation therefore combines temporal order with selective perturbation, distinguishing observed localization from the cause of the block.

Recovery of transport after block removal would strengthen the interpretation of a reversibly impeded step. Reappearance in the Golgi and then the medium should be tracked in the same labelled population without confusion with newly synthesized molecules.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A transmembrane protein has a glycosylated domain in the reticulum lumen and a cytosolic tail. Track their orientation in a vesicle and at the plasma membrane. Identify the domain that can bind an impermeant extracellular probe after exocytosis.

The glycosylated domain remains inside the vesicle lumen; the tail stays on the cytosolic side. Fusion opens the lumen to the outside, so the glycosylated domain becomes extracellular. An external probe can reach that domain if it has an appropriate binding site.

<div class="spiegazione">

Vesicular traffic preserves topology: cargo does not cross the bilayer again at every step. The cytosolic membrane surface continues to face the cytosol during budding and fusion. Continuity between vesicle lumen and the outside explains carbohydrate exposure in the glycocalyx. The impermeant-probe test requires an intact membrane, because permeabilization would also expose the internal tail. External sugars therefore result from orientation established in the secretory pathway and maintained throughout trafficking.

Verification can compare antibodies against the two domains before and after permeabilization. Before treatment the outer domain should be accessible; afterwards both may become accessible. This control distinguishes protein orientation from simple absence of an epitope.

</div>
