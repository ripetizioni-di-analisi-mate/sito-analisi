## Exercise 1

Given $u = (2, 1, 1)$ and $v = (1, -1, 2)$ in $\mathbb{R}^3$, compute $\langle u, v \rangle$, the norms, the angle between $u$ and $v$ and the distance $d(u, v)$.

$$
\langle u, v \rangle = 2 \cdot 1 + 1 \cdot (-1) + 1 \cdot 2 = 3
$$
$$
\|u\| = \sqrt{4 + 1 + 1} = \sqrt{6},
\qquad
\|v\| = \sqrt{1 + 1 + 4} = \sqrt{6}
$$
$$
\cos\theta = \frac{\langle u, v \rangle}{\|u\|\, \|v\|} = \frac{3}{6} = \frac{1}{2}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\theta = \frac{\pi}{3}
$$
$$
d(u, v) = \|u - v\| = \|(1, 2, -1)\| = \sqrt{1 + 4 + 1} = \sqrt{6}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The standard scalar product is the sum of the products of the components in the same position, and the norm is the square root of the scalar product of a vector with itself. Since $u$ and $v$ are nonzero, the angle is the unique $\theta \in [0, \pi]$ with cosine equal to $\dfrac{\langle u, v \rangle}{\|u\|\, \|v\|}$; the quotient equals $\dfrac{1}{2}$, therefore $\theta = \dfrac{\pi}{3}$. The distance is the norm of the difference. The three sides of the triangle with vertices $0$, $u$, $v$ all measure $\sqrt{6}$, so the triangle is **equilateral**, in agreement with the angle of $\dfrac{\pi}{3}$ at the vertex $0$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Determine $k \in \mathbb{R}$ so that $u = (k, 1, 2, -1)$ and $v = (2, k, -1, 1)$ are orthogonal in $\mathbb{R}^4$. For the value found, verify the Pythagorean theorem for $u$ and $v$.

$$
\langle u, v \rangle = 2k + k - 2 - 1 = 3k - 3 = 0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad k = 1
$$
$$
u = (1, 1, 2, -1),
\qquad
v = (2, 1, -1, 1),
\qquad
u + v = (3, 2, 1, 0)
$$
$$
\|u\|^2 = 1 + 1 + 4 + 1 = 7,
\qquad
\|v\|^2 = 4 + 1 + 1 + 1 = 7,
\qquad
\|u + v\|^2 = 9 + 4 + 1 + 0 = 14
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Two vectors are orthogonal if and only if their scalar product is zero. The scalar product of $u$ and $v$ is a polynomial of degree one in $k$, which vanishes only for $k = 1$, therefore the vectors are **orthogonal** for $k = 1$ and for no other value. For $k = 1$ the **Pythagorean theorem** states that $\|u + v\|^2 = \|u\|^2 + \|v\|^2$, and the computation confirms it, since $14 = 7 + 7$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Determine the unit vectors of $\mathbb{R}^3$ orthogonal both to $a = (1, 1, 0)$ and to $b = (1, 0, -1)$.

$$
\begin{cases}
\langle x, a \rangle = x_1 + x_2 = 0 \\[4pt]
\langle x, b \rangle = x_1 - x_3 = 0
\end{cases}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\begin{cases}
x_2 = -x_1 \\[4pt]
x_3 = x_1
\end{cases}
$$
$$
x = t\,(1, -1, 1), \quad t \in \mathbb{R},
\qquad
\|(1, -1, 1)\| = \sqrt{3}
$$
$$
x = \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\,(1, -1, 1)
\qquad \text{or} \qquad
x = -\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\,(1, -1, 1)
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

A vector $x = (x_1, x_2, x_3)$ is orthogonal to $a$ and to $b$ if it makes both scalar products vanish, therefore the two conditions form a **homogeneous system** of two equations in three unknowns. The two equations are independent, so the solutions depend on one parameter and form the line spanned by $(1, -1, 1)$. The unit vectors of this line are the two vectors obtained by dividing $(1, -1, 1)$ by its norm $\sqrt{3}$ and taking both orientations: $\pm \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\,(1, -1, 1)$.

</div>

## Exercise 4

Determine $k \in \mathbb{R}$ so that the angle between $u = (1, 0, 1)$ and $v = (1, k, 0)$ is $\dfrac{\pi}{3}$.

$$
\langle u, v \rangle = 1,
\qquad
\|u\| = \sqrt{2},
\qquad
\|v\| = \sqrt{1 + k^2}
$$
$$
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}\, \sqrt{1 + k^2}} = \cos\frac{\pi}{3} = \frac{1}{2}
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\sqrt{2\,(1 + k^2)} = 2
$$
$$
2\,(1 + k^2) = 4
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
k^2 = 1
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
k = \pm 1
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The vector $v$ is nonzero for every $k$, because its first component is $1$, therefore the angle is defined for every value of the parameter. Since the cosine function is injective on $[0, \pi]$, the angle equals $\dfrac{\pi}{3}$ if and only if the cosine of the angle equals $\dfrac{1}{2}$. In the equation thus obtained both sides are positive, so squaring produces an equivalent equation, which reduces to $k^2 = 1$. The problem therefore has **two solutions**, $k = 1$ and $k = -1$.

</div>
