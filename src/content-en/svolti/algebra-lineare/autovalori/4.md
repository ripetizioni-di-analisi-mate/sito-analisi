## Exercise 1

Determine whether the matrix
$$
A = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 0 & 2 \\ -1 & 2 & -2 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}
$$
is diagonalizable and write a diagonal matrix similar to $A$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
p_A(\lambda) &= (1 - \lambda) \begin{vmatrix} 3 - \lambda & 0 \\ -1 & 2 - \lambda \end{vmatrix} \\[6pt]
&= (1 - \lambda)(3 - \lambda)(2 - \lambda)
\end{aligned}
$$
$$
\lambda_1 = 1, \quad \lambda_2 = 2, \quad \lambda_3 = 3
\qquad \text{distinct}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
D = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 3 \end{pmatrix}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The third row of $A - \lambda I_3$ contains only the entry $1 - \lambda$, therefore the **Laplace expansion** along that row reduces the computation to a determinant of order $2$, which is triangular. The eigenvalues $1$, $2$, $3$ are real and pairwise distinct, so by the theorem on **distinct real eigenvalues** the matrix is diagonalizable, without computing any eigenspace. The diagonal matrix similar to $A$ has the eigenvalues on its diagonal; their order depends on the order of the columns of the diagonalizing matrix, which is not required here.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Determine whether the matrix
$$
A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -2 \\ 1 & 3 \end{pmatrix}
$$
is diagonalizable over $\mathbb{R}$.

$$
p_A(\lambda) = \lambda^2 - 4\lambda + 5,
\qquad
\Delta = 16 - 20 = -4 < 0
$$
$$
p_A \text{ has no real roots}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
A \text{ is not diagonalizable over } \mathbb{R}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The trace and the determinant are $4$ and $3 + 2 = 5$, therefore the characteristic polynomial is $\lambda^2 - 4\lambda + 5$. The discriminant is negative, so the polynomial has no real roots and the first condition of the **diagonalizability criterion** is *not* satisfied: the matrix **is not diagonalizable** over $\mathbb{R}$. The complex roots are $2 \pm i$; the matrix has no real eigenvalues, therefore no line through the origin of $\mathbb{R}^2$ is mapped into itself.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Determine whether the matrix
$$
A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}
$$
is diagonalizable.

$$
\begin{aligned}
p_A(\lambda) &= (2 - \lambda) \begin{vmatrix} 1 - \lambda & 0 \\ 1 & 1 - \lambda \end{vmatrix} \\[6pt]
&= (2 - \lambda)(1 - \lambda)^2
\end{aligned}
$$
$$
m_a(1) = 2, \qquad m_a(2) = 1
$$
$$
A - I_3 = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}
\xrightarrow{R_2 \to R_2 + R_1}
\begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix},
\qquad \operatorname{rk}(A - I_3) = 2
$$
$$
m_g(1) = 3 - 2 = 1 < 2 = m_a(1)
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
A \text{ is not diagonalizable}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The expansion along the second row gives the factor $2 - \lambda$ and a triangular determinant, therefore the eigenvalue $1$ is double and the eigenvalue $2$ is simple. The roots are all real, so the first condition of the criterion is satisfied, and it remains to compare the multiplicities of the double eigenvalue. In the matrix $A - I_3$ the second row is the opposite of the first, while the first and the third are independent, therefore the rank is $2$ and $m_g(1) = 1$. Since $m_g(1) < m_a(1)$, the second condition of the criterion is not satisfied and the matrix **is not diagonalizable**. Indeed, the eigenspaces $V_1$ and $V_2$ have dimension $1$ each, therefore they contain at most two independent eigenvectors, while a basis of $\mathbb{R}^3$ requires three.

</div>

## Exercise 4

Determine which of the matrices
$$
B = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 3 \end{pmatrix},
\qquad
C = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 3 \end{pmatrix}
$$
are diagonalizable, and deduce whether $B$ and $C$ are similar.

$$
p_B(\lambda) = p_C(\lambda) = (2 - \lambda)^2 (3 - \lambda),
\qquad m_a(2) = 2, \quad m_a(3) = 1
$$
$$
B - 2I_3 = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix},
\qquad \operatorname{rk} = 2,
\qquad m_g(2) = 1 < 2
$$
$$
C - 2I_3 = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix},
\qquad \operatorname{rk} = 1,
\qquad m_g(2) = 2
$$
$$
B \text{ not diagonalizable},
\qquad
C \text{ similar to } \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 3 \end{pmatrix}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The two matrices are upper triangular with the same diagonal, therefore they have the same characteristic polynomial: the eigenvalue $2$ is double and the eigenvalue $3$ is simple. The difference lies in the position of the off-diagonal entry $1$. In $B - 2I_3$ the first and the third rows are independent, so the rank is $2$, $m_g(2) = 1$ and $B$ **is not diagonalizable**. In $C - 2I_3$ the first and the third rows coincide, so the rank is $1$, $m_g(2) = 2 = m_a(2)$ and $C$ **is diagonalizable**, similar to $D = \operatorname{diag}(2, 2, 3)$. The matrices $B$ and $C$ **are not similar**, because if they were, $B$ would be similar to $C$ and therefore to $D$, that is, diagonalizable, contrary to what has just been proved. Having the same characteristic polynomial does *not*, by itself, guarantee similarity.

</div>
