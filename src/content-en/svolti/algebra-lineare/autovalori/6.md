## Exercise 1

Compute $A^n$ for every integer $n \geq 1$, where
$$
A = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & -1 \\ 2 & 0 \end{pmatrix} .
$$

$$
p_A(\lambda) = \lambda^2 - 3\lambda + 2 = (\lambda - 1)(\lambda - 2)
$$
$$
A - I_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ 2 & -1 \end{pmatrix}
\ \Longrightarrow\ V_1 = \langle (1, 2) \rangle,
\qquad
A - 2I_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 2 & -2 \end{pmatrix}
\ \Longrightarrow\ V_2 = \langle (1, 1) \rangle
$$
$$
P = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 2 & 1 \end{pmatrix},
\qquad
D = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix},
\qquad
P^{-1} = \frac{1}{-1} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ -2 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} -1 & 1 \\ 2 & -1 \end{pmatrix}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
A^n &= P D^n P^{-1} \\[6pt]
&= \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2^n \\ 2 & 2^n \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} -1 & 1 \\ 2 & -1 \end{pmatrix} \\[6pt]
&= \begin{pmatrix} 2^{n+1} - 1 & 1 - 2^n \\ 2^{n+1} - 2 & 2 - 2^n \end{pmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$
$$
n = 1 : \quad \begin{pmatrix} 4 - 1 & 1 - 2 \\ 4 - 2 & 2 - 2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & -1 \\ 2 & 0 \end{pmatrix} = A
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The eigenvalues $1$ and $2$ are distinct, therefore the matrix is diagonalizable and by the theorem on **powers of a diagonalizable matrix** one has $A^n = P D^n P^{-1}$. The matrix $D^n$ is diagonal with entries $1^n = 1$ and $2^n$, so the product $P D^n$ is obtained by multiplying the first column of $P$ by $1$ and the second by $2^n$. The inverse of $P$ is computed with the formula for matrices of order $2$, since $\det P = 1 - 2 = -1$. The row-by-column product with $P^{-1}$ gives the final formula; for example the entry in position $(1, 1)$ is $1 \cdot (-1) + 2^n \cdot 2 = 2^{n+1} - 1$. The check with $n = 1$ gives back $A$, therefore the formula is **consistent**.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Compute $A^n$ for every integer $n \geq 1$, where
$$
A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{pmatrix} .
$$

$$
p_A(\lambda) = \lambda^2 - 2\lambda - 3 = (\lambda - 3)(\lambda + 1)
$$
$$
V_3 = \langle (1, 1) \rangle,
\qquad
V_{-1} = \langle (1, -1) \rangle
$$
$$
P = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix},
\qquad
D = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix},
\qquad
P^{-1} = \frac{1}{2} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
A^n &= \frac{1}{2} \begin{pmatrix} 3^n & (-1)^n \\ 3^n & -(-1)^n \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix} \\[6pt]
&= \frac{1}{2} \begin{pmatrix} 3^n + (-1)^n & 3^n - (-1)^n \\ 3^n - (-1)^n & 3^n + (-1)^n \end{pmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The matrix is **symmetric**, therefore it is diagonalizable; in any case the eigenvalues $3$ and $-1$ are distinct. The eigenspace $V_3$ is obtained from the matrix $A - 3I_2$, which gives the equation $x = y$, and the eigenspace $V_{-1}$ from the matrix $A + I_2$, which gives $x + y = 0$. Since $\det P = -2$, the inverse is $P^{-1} = -\dfrac{1}{2} \begin{pmatrix} -1 & -1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, that is, the matrix written above. The matrix $D^n$ contains $(-1)^n$, which equals $1$ for even $n$ and $-1$ for odd $n$, therefore the formula holds in both cases without distinguishing them. For $n = 1$ one obtains $\dfrac{1}{2} \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 4 \\ 4 & 2 \end{pmatrix} = A$; for $n = 2$ one obtains $\dfrac{1}{2} \begin{pmatrix} 10 & 8 \\ 8 & 10 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 5 & 4 \\ 4 & 5 \end{pmatrix}$, which coincides with the product $A \cdot A$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Compute $A^{2026}$ and $A^{2027}$, where
$$
A = \begin{pmatrix} 5 & -6 \\ 4 & -5 \end{pmatrix} .
$$

$$
\operatorname{tr} A = 0, \qquad \det A = -25 + 24 = -1,
\qquad
p_A(\lambda) = \lambda^2 - 1 = (\lambda - 1)(\lambda + 1)
$$
$$
A - I_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & -6 \\ 4 & -6 \end{pmatrix}
\ \Longrightarrow\ V_1 = \langle (3, 2) \rangle,
\qquad
A + I_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 6 & -6 \\ 4 & -4 \end{pmatrix}
\ \Longrightarrow\ V_{-1} = \langle (1, 1) \rangle
$$
$$
P = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 2 & 1 \end{pmatrix},
\qquad
D = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix},
\qquad
D^{2026} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = I_2,
\qquad
D^{2027} = D
$$
$$
A^{2026} = P\, I_2\, P^{-1} = I_2,
\qquad
A^{2027} = P D P^{-1} = A
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The eigenvalues are $1$ and $-1$, distinct, therefore the matrix is diagonalizable with $A = PDP^{-1}$. The powers of $D$ have on the diagonal $1^n = 1$ and $(-1)^n$, which equals $1$ for an even exponent and $-1$ for an odd exponent. So $D^{2026} = I_2$, and from $A^{2026} = P D^{2026} P^{-1}$ it follows that $A^{2026} = P P^{-1} = I_2$; similarly $D^{2027} = D$ and $A^{2027} = A$. The result is confirmed by observing that $A^2 = I_2$, as shown by the direct computation $A^2 = \begin{pmatrix} 25 - 24 & -30 + 30 \\ 20 - 20 & -24 + 25 \end{pmatrix}$. It was not necessary to compute $P^{-1}$, since in the two final products it appears only in the form $P P^{-1}$ or $P D P^{-1} = A$.

</div>

## Exercise 4

Compute $A^n$ for every integer $n \geq 1$, where
$$
A = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & -2 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 & 0 \end{pmatrix} .
$$

$$
p_A(\lambda) = -\lambda \begin{vmatrix} 3 - \lambda & -2 \\ 0 & 1 - \lambda \end{vmatrix} = -\lambda\,(3 - \lambda)(1 - \lambda)
$$
$$
A = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & -2 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 & 0 \end{pmatrix}
\ \Longrightarrow\
\begin{cases}
3x - 2y = 0 \\[4pt]
y = 0
\end{cases}
\ \Longrightarrow\ V_0 = \langle (0, 0, 1) \rangle
$$
$$
A - I_3 = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -2 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 & -1 \end{pmatrix}
\ \Longrightarrow\
\begin{cases}
x = y \\[4pt]
z = 2y
\end{cases}
\ \Longrightarrow\ V_1 = \langle (1, 1, 2) \rangle
$$
$$
A - 3I_3 = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -2 & 0 \\ 0 & -2 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 & -3 \end{pmatrix}
\ \Longrightarrow\
\begin{cases}
y = 0 \\[4pt]
z = 0
\end{cases}
\ \Longrightarrow\ V_3 = \langle (1, 0, 0) \rangle
$$
$$
P = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 2 & 0 \end{pmatrix},
\qquad
D = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 3 \end{pmatrix},
\qquad
P^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -2 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & -1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
A^n &= P D^n P^{-1} \\[6pt]
&= \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 & 3^n \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -2 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & -1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \\[6pt]
&= \begin{pmatrix} 3^n & 1 - 3^n & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 & 0 \end{pmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The third column of $A - \lambda I_3$ has the single entry $-\lambda$, therefore the **Laplace expansion** along that column immediately gives the factored polynomial, with the distinct eigenvalues $0$, $1$, $3$. The eigenvalue $0$ indicates that $A$ is not invertible, and its eigenspace is the kernel of $A$: the equations $y = 0$ and $3x - 2y = 0$ give $x = y = 0$ with $z$ free. For $\lambda = 1$ the first row gives $x = y$ and the third gives $z = 2y$; for $\lambda = 3$ the rows give $y = 0$ and then $z = 0$. The inverse of $P$, with $\det P = -1$, is computed with the Gauss-Jordan method or with cofactors, and it is checked with the product $P P^{-1} = I_3$. Since $n \geq 1$, one has $0^n = 0$, and in the product $P D^n$ the first column of $P$ becomes zero, the second stays unchanged and the third is multiplied by $3^n$. For $n = 1$ the final formula gives back $A$.

</div>
