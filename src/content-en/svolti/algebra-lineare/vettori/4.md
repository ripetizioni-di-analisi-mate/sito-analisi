## Exercise 1

Compute $\vec{u} \times \vec{v}$ for $\vec{u} = (1, 2, -1)$ and $\vec{v} = (3, 0, 1)$ and verify that the result is orthogonal to both vectors.

$$
\vec{u} \times \vec{v} =
\begin{vmatrix}
\vec{i} & \vec{j} & \vec{k} \\
1 & 2 & -1 \\
3 & 0 & 1
\end{vmatrix}
= \begin{vmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{vmatrix} \vec{i}
- \begin{vmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 3 & 1 \end{vmatrix} \vec{j}
+ \begin{vmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 0 \end{vmatrix} \vec{k}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
\vec{u} \times \vec{v}
&= (2 - 0)\,\vec{i} - (1 + 3)\,\vec{j} + (0 - 6)\,\vec{k} \\[6pt]
&= (2, -4, -6)
\end{aligned}
$$
$$
(2, -4, -6) \cdot (1, 2, -1) = 2 - 8 + 6 = 0,
\qquad
(2, -4, -6) \cdot (3, 0, 1) = 6 + 0 - 6 = 0
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The **cross product** is computed by expanding the symbolic determinant along the first row: each component is the $2 \times 2$ determinant obtained by deleting the row of unit vectors and the column of the corresponding unit vector. The second component must be taken with the minus sign, therefore $-\,(1 \cdot 1 - (-1) \cdot 3) = -4$. The result is $(2, -4, -6)$, and the two zero dot products confirm that it is **orthogonal** to both $\vec{u}$ and $\vec{v}$, as the geometric meaning of the cross product predicts.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Find the unit vectors orthogonal to both $\vec{u} = (1, 2, -2)$ and $\vec{v} = (1, 1, 0)$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\vec{u} \times \vec{v}
&= \big( 2 \cdot 0 - (-2) \cdot 1,\ \ (-2) \cdot 1 - 1 \cdot 0,\ \ 1 \cdot 1 - 2 \cdot 1 \big) \\[6pt]
&= (2, -2, -1)
\end{aligned}
$$
$$
\|\vec{u} \times \vec{v}\| = \sqrt{4 + 4 + 1} = 3
$$
$$
\pm\, \frac{\vec{u} \times \vec{v}}{\|\vec{u} \times \vec{v}\|} = \pm \left( \frac{2}{3},\ -\frac{2}{3},\ -\frac{1}{3} \right)
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The vectors $\vec{u}$ and $\vec{v}$ are not parallel, so the vectors orthogonal to both form a line through the origin, the line of the cross product $\vec{u} \times \vec{v}$. The vectors of magnitude $1$ on this line are two, with opposite orientations, and they are obtained by dividing $\vec{u} \times \vec{v}$ by its magnitude and changing the sign. Since $\|\vec{u} \times \vec{v}\| = 3$, the required **unit vectors** are $\left( \dfrac{2}{3}, -\dfrac{2}{3}, -\dfrac{1}{3} \right)$ and the opposite vector $\left( -\dfrac{2}{3}, \dfrac{2}{3}, \dfrac{1}{3} \right)$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Knowing that $\vec{u} \times \vec{v} = (1, -2, 2)$, compute $(2\vec{u} - \vec{v}) \times (\vec{u} + 3\vec{v})$ and its magnitude.

$$
\begin{aligned}
(2\vec{u} - \vec{v}) \times (\vec{u} + 3\vec{v})
&= 2\,\vec{u} \times \vec{u} + 6\,\vec{u} \times \vec{v} - \vec{v} \times \vec{u} - 3\,\vec{v} \times \vec{v} \\[6pt]
&= \vec{0} + 6\,\vec{u} \times \vec{v} + \vec{u} \times \vec{v} - \vec{0} \\[6pt]
&= 7\,\vec{u} \times \vec{v} \\[6pt]
&= (7, -14, 14)
\end{aligned}
$$
$$
\|(7, -14, 14)\| = 7\,\|(1, -2, 2)\| = 7 \cdot \sqrt{1 + 4 + 4} = 21
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The components of $\vec{u}$ and $\vec{v}$ are not known, so the computation is carried out with the **algebraic properties** of the cross product. By linearity the product expands like a product of binomials, keeping the order of the factors in each term. By anticommutativity $\vec{u} \times \vec{u} = \vec{v} \times \vec{v} = \vec{0}$ and $-\,\vec{v} \times \vec{u} = \vec{u} \times \vec{v}$. Therefore the result is $7\,\vec{u} \times \vec{v} = (7, -14, 14)$, and its magnitude is $7$ times the magnitude of $(1, -2, 2)$, that is, $21$.

</div>

## Exercise 4

Find the vector $\vec{w}$ orthogonal to both $\vec{u} = (1, 1, 0)$ and $\vec{v} = (0, 1, 1)$ such that $\vec{w} \cdot (1, 2, 3) = 4$.

$$
\vec{u} \times \vec{v} = \big( 1 \cdot 1 - 0 \cdot 1,\ \ 0 \cdot 0 - 1 \cdot 1,\ \ 1 \cdot 1 - 1 \cdot 0 \big) = (1, -1, 1)
$$
$$
\vec{w} = t\,(1, -1, 1), \qquad t \in \mathbb{R}
$$
$$
\vec{w} \cdot (1, 2, 3) = t\,(1 - 2 + 3) = 2t = 4
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad t = 2
$$
$$
\vec{w} = (2, -2, 2)
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Since $\vec{u}$ and $\vec{v}$ are not parallel, the vectors orthogonal to both are *exactly* the multiples of $\vec{u} \times \vec{v} = (1, -1, 1)$, that is, the vectors $t\,(1, -1, 1)$ with $t \in \mathbb{R}$. The condition on the dot product with $(1, 2, 3)$ becomes a first-degree equation in $t$, which has the unique solution $t = 2$. The required vector is therefore $(2, -2, 2)$; as a check, $(2, -2, 2) \cdot (1, 1, 0) = 0$ and $(2, -2, 2) \cdot (0, 1, 1) = 0$.

</div>
