## Exercise 1

Compute the angle between the planes $\pi_1 : 2x - y + z = 1$ and $\pi_2 : x + y + 2z = 0$.

$$
\vec{n}_1 = (2, -1, 1),
\qquad
\vec{n}_2 = (1, 1, 2)
$$
$$
\vec{n}_1 \cdot \vec{n}_2 = 2 - 1 + 2 = 3,
\qquad
\|\vec{n}_1\| = \|\vec{n}_2\| = \sqrt{6}
$$
$$
\cos\theta = \frac{|3|}{\sqrt{6} \cdot \sqrt{6}} = \frac{3}{6} = \frac{1}{2}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\theta = \frac{\pi}{3}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The **angle between two planes** is the acute or right angle formed by their normal vectors, which are read off the coefficients of the equations. The normal vectors are not proportional, so the planes are intersecting. The dot product equals $3$ and the two norms equal $\sqrt{4 + 1 + 1} = \sqrt{6}$ and $\sqrt{1 + 1 + 4} = \sqrt{6}$. The cosine of the angle is therefore $\dfrac{1}{2}$, and the only angle in $\left[0, \dfrac{\pi}{2}\right]$ with this cosine is $\theta = \dfrac{\pi}{3}$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Compute the angle between the line
$$
r :
\begin{cases}
x + y - z = 0 \\[4pt]
x - y = 2
\end{cases}
$$
and the plane $\pi : 2x - y + z = 5$.

$$
\vec{v} = (1, 1, -1) \times (1, -1, 0) =
\begin{vmatrix}
\vec{i} & \vec{j} & \vec{k} \\
1 & 1 & -1 \\
1 & -1 & 0
\end{vmatrix}
= (-1, -1, -2)
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\vec{v} = (1, 1, 2)
$$
$$
\vec{n} = (2, -1, 1),
\qquad
\vec{v} \cdot \vec{n} = 2 - 1 + 2 = 3,
\qquad
\|\vec{v}\| = \|\vec{n}\| = \sqrt{6}
$$
$$
\sin\theta = \frac{|3|}{\sqrt{6} \cdot \sqrt{6}} = \frac{1}{2}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\theta = \frac{\pi}{6}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The line is given in Cartesian form, so its direction vector is the cross product of the two normal vectors; the result $(-1, -1, -2)$ is replaced by its opposite $(1, 1, 2)$, which determines the same direction. For the **angle between a line and a plane** the dot product of $\vec{v}$ and $\vec{n}$ gives the *sine* of the angle, because it measures the angle between the line and the normal, which is the complement of $\theta$. The dot product equals $3$ and the norms both equal $\sqrt{6}$, hence $\sin\theta = \dfrac{1}{2}$ and the line forms the angle $\theta = \dfrac{\pi}{6}$ with the plane. Using the cosine instead of the sine would give $\dfrac{\pi}{3}$, which is the angle between the line and the normal.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Compute the angle between the lines
$$
r :
\begin{cases}
x = 1 + t \\[4pt]
y = 2t \\[4pt]
z = -1 + 2t
\end{cases}
\qquad
s :
\begin{cases}
x - y = 1 \\[4pt]
z = 3
\end{cases}
$$

$$
\vec{v} = (1, 2, 2),
\qquad
\vec{w} = (1, -1, 0) \times (0, 0, 1) =
\begin{vmatrix}
\vec{i} & \vec{j} & \vec{k} \\
1 & -1 & 0 \\
0 & 0 & 1
\end{vmatrix}
= (-1, -1, 0)
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\vec{w} = (1, 1, 0)
$$
$$
\vec{v} \cdot \vec{w} = 1 + 2 + 0 = 3,
\qquad
\|\vec{v}\| = 3,
\qquad
\|\vec{w}\| = \sqrt{2}
$$
$$
\cos\theta = \frac{3}{3\sqrt{2}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\theta = \frac{\pi}{4}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The direction vector of $r$ is read off the coefficients of $t$, while that of $s$ is obtained as the cross product of the normal vectors of the two planes defining it. For the **angle between two lines** we use the absolute value of the dot product divided by the product of the norms. The dot product equals $3$, the norm of $\vec{v}$ is $\sqrt{1 + 4 + 4} = 3$ and that of $\vec{w}$ is $\sqrt{2}$, hence $\cos\theta = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}$ and the lines form the angle $\theta = \dfrac{\pi}{4}$. The formula holds whatever the relative position of the lines, so computing the angle does not require knowing whether $r$ and $s$ are intersecting or skew.

</div>

## Exercise 4

Given the planes $\pi_1 : x + ky + z = 1$ and $\pi_2 : kx + y - z = 0$, with $k \in \mathbb{R}$, find the values of $k$ for which the planes are perpendicular and those for which they form an angle of $\dfrac{\pi}{3}$.

$$
\vec{n}_1 = (1, k, 1),
\qquad
\vec{n}_2 = (k, 1, -1),
\qquad
\vec{n}_1 \cdot \vec{n}_2 = k + k - 1 = 2k - 1
$$
$$
\pi_1 \perp \pi_2
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
2k - 1 = 0
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
k = \frac{1}{2}
$$
$$
\cos\theta = \frac{|2k - 1|}{\sqrt{k^2 + 2} \cdot \sqrt{k^2 + 2}} = \frac{|2k - 1|}{k^2 + 2}
$$
$$
\frac{|2k - 1|}{k^2 + 2} = \frac{1}{2}
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
2\,|2k - 1| = k^2 + 2
$$
$$
\begin{cases}
2k - 1 \geq 0 \\[4pt]
4k - 2 = k^2 + 2
\end{cases}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
k^2 - 4k + 4 = 0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
k = 2
$$
$$
\begin{cases}
2k - 1 < 0 \\[4pt]
-4k + 2 = k^2 + 2
\end{cases}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
k^2 + 4k = 0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
k = 0 \ \text{ or } \ k = -4
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Two planes are perpendicular if and only if the normal vectors are orthogonal, so we impose $\vec{n}_1 \cdot \vec{n}_2 = 0$, and the planes turn out to be **perpendicular** for $k = \dfrac{1}{2}$. For the angle $\dfrac{\pi}{3}$ we impose that the cosine equal $\dfrac{1}{2}$; the two norms coincide and their product is $k^2 + 2$. The equation with the absolute value is solved by distinguishing the sign of $2k - 1$. If $2k - 1 \geq 0$ we obtain $(k - 2)^2 = 0$, that is, $k = 2$, which satisfies the condition $2k - 1 \geq 0$. If $2k - 1 < 0$ we obtain $k(k + 4) = 0$, and both solutions $k = 0$ and $k = -4$ satisfy the condition $2k - 1 < 0$. The planes therefore form an angle of $\dfrac{\pi}{3}$ **for three values** of the parameter, $k \in \{ -4, 0, 2 \}$. For $k = -1$ the normal vectors $(1, -1, 1)$ and $(-1, 1, -1)$ are opposite and the planes are parallel, in agreement with the formula, which gives $\cos\theta = 1$.

</div>
