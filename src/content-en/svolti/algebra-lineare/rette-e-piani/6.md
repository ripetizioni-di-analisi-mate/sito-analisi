## Exercise 1

Find the orthogonal projection $H$ of the point $P = (4, -1, 3)$ onto the plane $\pi : x - y + 2z + 1 = 0$ and the symmetric $P'$ of $P$ with respect to $\pi$.

$$
\vec{n} = (1, -1, 2),
\qquad
P + t\,\vec{n} = (4 + t,\ -1 - t,\ 3 + 2t)
$$
$$
(4 + t) - (-1 - t) + 2(3 + 2t) + 1 = 0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
12 + 6t = 0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
t = -2
$$
$$
H = (4 - 2,\ -1 + 2,\ 3 - 4) = (2, 1, -1)
$$
$$
P' = 2H - P = (4 - 4,\ 2 + 1,\ -2 - 3) = (0, 3, -5)
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The **orthogonal projection** of $P$ onto $\pi$ is the point where the plane meets the line through $P$ perpendicular to $\pi$, which has the normal vector $\vec{n}$ as direction vector. Substituting the generic point of this line into the equation of the plane gives a first-degree equation in $t$, with solution $t = -2$, and hence $H = (2, 1, -1)$; as a check, $2 - 1 - 2 + 1 = 0$. The **symmetric** $P'$ is the point for which $H$ is the midpoint of the segment $PP'$, therefore $P' = 2H - P = (0, 3, -5)$. Equivalently, $P'$ is obtained by continuing past $H$ by the same amount, that is, with twice the value of the parameter, $t = -4$, which again gives $(0, 3, -5)$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Find the orthogonal projection $H$ of the point $P = (4, 2, 0)$ onto the line $r : x = 2t$, $y = 1 - t$, $z = 1 + t$, the symmetric $P'$ of $P$ with respect to $r$, and the distance from $P$ to $r$.

$$
\vec{v} = (2, -1, 1),
\qquad
\sigma : 2(x - 4) - (y - 2) + z = 0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\sigma : 2x - y + z - 6 = 0
$$
$$
2(2t) - (1 - t) + (1 + t) - 6 = 0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
6t - 6 = 0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
t = 1
$$
$$
H = (2, 0, 2),
\qquad
P' = 2H - P = (4 - 4,\ 0 - 2,\ 4 - 0) = (0, -2, 4)
$$
$$
d(P, r) = \|\overrightarrow{HP}\| = \|(2, 2, -2)\| = \sqrt{12} = 2\sqrt{3}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The **orthogonal projection of a point onto a line** is obtained by intersecting the line with the plane $\sigma$ that passes through $P$ and is perpendicular to $r$, that is, with the plane through $P$ that has the direction vector $\vec{v}$ as normal vector. Substituting the parametric equations of $r$ into the equation of $\sigma$ gives $t = 1$, hence $H = (2, 0, 2)$. As a check, $\overrightarrow{HP} = (2, 2, -2)$ is orthogonal to $\vec{v}$, since $4 - 2 - 2 = 0$. The symmetric with respect to the line is computed with the same formula as the symmetric with respect to a plane, $P' = 2H - P$. The distance from $P$ to $r$ is the length of $\overrightarrow{HP}$, that is, $2\sqrt{3}$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Find the equations of the orthogonal projection of the line $r : x = 1 + t$, $y = t$, $z = 2 + 2t$ onto the plane $\pi : x + y + z = 0$.

$$
r :
\begin{cases}
x - y - 1 = 0 \\[4pt]
2y - z + 2 = 0
\end{cases}
$$
$$
\lambda\,(x - y - 1) + \mu\,(2y - z + 2) = 0,
\qquad
\vec{n}_{\lambda, \mu} = (\lambda,\ -\lambda + 2\mu,\ -\mu)
$$
$$
\vec{n}_{\lambda, \mu} \cdot (1, 1, 1) = \lambda - \lambda + 2\mu - \mu = \mu = 0
$$
$$
\lambda = 1, \ \mu = 0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
x - y - 1 = 0
$$
$$
r' :
\begin{cases}
x - y - 1 = 0 \\[4pt]
x + y + z = 0
\end{cases}
\qquad
r' :
\begin{cases}
x = 1 + t \\[4pt]
y = t \\[4pt]
z = -1 - 2t
\end{cases}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The direction vector $(1, 1, 2)$ of $r$ is not parallel to the normal vector $(1, 1, 1)$ of $\pi$, so $r$ is not perpendicular to the plane and its **orthogonal projection** is a line. This line is the intersection of $\pi$ with the plane that contains $r$ and is perpendicular to $\pi$. To find that plane we write the Cartesian equations of $r$, eliminating the parameter with $t = y$, and use the **pencil of planes** with axis $r$. Perpendicularity to $\pi$ requires the normal vector of the pencil to be orthogonal to $(1, 1, 1)$, a condition that gives $\mu = 0$; the required plane is therefore $x - y - 1 = 0$, the first of the two planes defining $r$. The projection $r'$ is the intersection of this plane with $\pi$. For the parametric equations we set $y = t$, which gives $x = 1 + t$ and $z = -x - y = -1 - 2t$. As a check, the point where $r$ meets $\pi$, obtained for $t = -\dfrac{3}{4}$, is $\left( \dfrac{1}{4}, -\dfrac{3}{4}, \dfrac{1}{2} \right)$, and it belongs to $r'$ since $\dfrac{1}{4} + \dfrac{3}{4} - 1 = 0$.

</div>
