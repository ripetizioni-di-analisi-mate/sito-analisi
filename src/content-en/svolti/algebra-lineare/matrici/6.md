## Exercise 1

Solve the matrix equation $AX = B$, where
$$
A =
\begin{pmatrix}
3 & 2 \\
1 & 1
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
B =
\begin{pmatrix}
1 & 0 & 2 \\
1 & 1 & -1
\end{pmatrix} .
$$

$$
\left(\begin{array}{cc|cc}
3 & 2 & 1 & 0 \\
1 & 1 & 0 & 1
\end{array}\right)
\xrightarrow{R_1 \leftrightarrow R_2}
\left(\begin{array}{cc|cc}
1 & 1 & 0 & 1 \\
3 & 2 & 1 & 0
\end{array}\right)
\xrightarrow{R_2 \to R_2 - 3R_1}
\left(\begin{array}{cc|cc}
1 & 1 & 0 & 1 \\
0 & -1 & 1 & -3
\end{array}\right)
$$
$$
\xrightarrow{R_2 \to -R_2}
\left(\begin{array}{cc|cc}
1 & 1 & 0 & 1 \\
0 & 1 & -1 & 3
\end{array}\right)
\xrightarrow{R_1 \to R_1 - R_2}
\left(\begin{array}{cc|cc}
1 & 0 & 1 & -2 \\
0 & 1 & -1 & 3
\end{array}\right)
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
X = A^{-1} B
&=
\begin{pmatrix}
1 & -2 \\
-1 & 3
\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}
1 & 0 & 2 \\
1 & 1 & -1
\end{pmatrix} \\[6pt]
&=
\begin{pmatrix}
1 - 2 & 0 - 2 & 2 + 2 \\
-1 + 3 & 0 + 3 & -2 - 3
\end{pmatrix}
=
\begin{pmatrix}
-1 & -2 & 4 \\
2 & 3 & -5
\end{pmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The product $AX$ is defined and is $2 \times 3$ like $B$ only if $X$ is $2 \times 3$. The reduction of $(A \mid I_2)$ brings the left block to $I_2$, therefore by the **Gauss-Jordan theorem** $A$ is invertible, with $A^{-1}$ equal to the right block. Multiplying both sides of $AX = B$ on the *left* by $A^{-1}$ gives $A^{-1} A X = A^{-1} B$, that is, $X = A^{-1} B$, and the equation has **a unique solution**. The result is checked with the first column: $A$ times the vector $(-1, 2)$ gives $(-3 + 4, \ -1 + 2) = (1, 1)$, which is the first column of $B$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Given the matrices
$$
A =
\begin{pmatrix}
1 & 2 \\
1 & 3
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
B =
\begin{pmatrix}
2 & 5 \\
0 & 1
\end{pmatrix},
$$
solve the equation $XA = B$ and compare its solution with that of $AX = B$.

$$
\left(\begin{array}{cc|cc}
1 & 2 & 1 & 0 \\
1 & 3 & 0 & 1
\end{array}\right)
\xrightarrow{R_2 \to R_2 - R_1}
\left(\begin{array}{cc|cc}
1 & 2 & 1 & 0 \\
0 & 1 & -1 & 1
\end{array}\right)
\xrightarrow{R_1 \to R_1 - 2R_2}
\left(\begin{array}{cc|cc}
1 & 0 & 3 & -2 \\
0 & 1 & -1 & 1
\end{array}\right)
$$
$$
XA = B
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
X = B A^{-1}
=
\begin{pmatrix}
2 & 5 \\
0 & 1
\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}
3 & -2 \\
-1 & 1
\end{pmatrix}
=
\begin{pmatrix}
6 - 5 & -4 + 5 \\
0 - 1 & 0 + 1
\end{pmatrix}
=
\begin{pmatrix}
1 & 1 \\
-1 & 1
\end{pmatrix}
$$
$$
AX = B
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
X = A^{-1} B
=
\begin{pmatrix}
3 & -2 \\
-1 & 1
\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}
2 & 5 \\
0 & 1
\end{pmatrix}
=
\begin{pmatrix}
6 & 13 \\
-2 & -4
\end{pmatrix}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The matrix $A$ is invertible, since the reduction of $(A \mid I_2)$ arrives at $(I_2 \mid A^{-1})$. In the equation $XA = B$ the unknown is multiplied by $A$ on the *right*, so both sides are multiplied on the right by $A^{-1}$: from $X A A^{-1} = B A^{-1}$ it follows that $X = B A^{-1}$. In the equation $AX = B$ one instead multiplies on the left and obtains $X = A^{-1} B$. The two solutions are **different**, because the matrix product is not commutative. The check of the first solution is the product $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{pmatrix} A$, which has first row $(1 + 1, \ 2 + 3) = (2, 5)$ and second row $(-1 + 1, \ -2 + 3) = (0, 1)$, that is, $B$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Given the matrix
$$
A =
\begin{pmatrix}
1 & 2 \\
2 & 4
\end{pmatrix},
$$
solve the equation $AX = B$ in the two cases
$$
B =
\begin{pmatrix}
3 & 1 \\
6 & 2
\end{pmatrix}
\qquad \text{and} \qquad
B' =
\begin{pmatrix}
3 & 1 \\
5 & 2
\end{pmatrix} .
$$

$$
X =
\begin{pmatrix}
a & b \\
c & d
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
AX =
\begin{pmatrix}
a + 2c & b + 2d \\
2a + 4c & 2b + 4d
\end{pmatrix}
$$
$$
AX = B
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\begin{cases}
a + 2c = 3 \\[4pt]
b + 2d = 1 \\[4pt]
2a + 4c = 6 \\[4pt]
2b + 4d = 2
\end{cases}
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\begin{cases}
a = 3 - 2c \\[4pt]
b = 1 - 2d
\end{cases}
$$
$$
X =
\begin{pmatrix}
3 - 2s & 1 - 2t \\
s & t
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad s, t \in \mathbb{R}
$$
$$
AX = B'
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\begin{cases}
a + 2c = 3 \\[4pt]
2a + 4c = 5
\end{cases}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
2a + 4c = 2\,(a + 2c) = 6 \neq 5
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The second row of $A$ is twice the first, so $A$ has a zero row in row echelon form and **is not invertible**; the method $X = A^{-1} B$ does not apply, and one writes $X$ with four unknown entries. By the definition of the product, the second row of $AX$ is *always* twice the first. In the first case the second row of $B$ is also twice the first, therefore the last two equations of the system are the first two multiplied by $2$, and two independent conditions on four unknowns remain. Setting $c = s$ and $d = t$, the equation has **infinitely many solutions**, depending on the two parameters $s$ and $t$. In the second case the second row of $B'$ is *not* twice the first, and the system contains the two equations $a + 2c = 3$ and $2a + 4c = 5$. The first gives $2a + 4c = 6$, which contradicts the second, so the two equations are incompatible. Therefore the equation $AX = B'$ **has no solutions**.

</div>
