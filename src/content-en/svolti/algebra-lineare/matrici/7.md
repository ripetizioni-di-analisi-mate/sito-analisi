## Exercise 1

Find all matrices $X \in M_2(\mathbb{R})$ that commute with
$$
A =
\begin{pmatrix}
1 & 1 \\
0 & 1
\end{pmatrix} .
$$

$$
X =
\begin{pmatrix}
a & b \\
c & d
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
AX =
\begin{pmatrix}
a + c & b + d \\
c & d
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
XA =
\begin{pmatrix}
a & a + b \\
c & c + d
\end{pmatrix}
$$
$$
AX = XA
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\begin{cases}
a + c = a \\[4pt]
b + d = a + b \\[4pt]
d = c + d
\end{cases}
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\begin{cases}
c = 0 \\[4pt]
d = a
\end{cases}
$$
$$
X =
\begin{pmatrix}
a & b \\
0 & a
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad a, b \in \mathbb{R}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

One writes $X$ with four unknown entries and computes the two products. The equality $AX = XA$ is equivalent to the equality of the four corresponding entries; the entries in position $(2, 1)$ always coincide, so three equations remain. The first and the third both give $c = 0$, the second gives $d = a$. Therefore the matrices that commute with $A$ are **the upper triangular matrices with the two diagonal entries equal**, which depend on two parameters. The result contains, with $b = 0$, the multiples of the identity and, with $a = b = 1$, the matrix $A$ itself, in agreement with the fact that every matrix commutes with $I_2$ and with itself.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Find all matrices $X \in M_2(\mathbb{R})$ that commute with
$$
A =
\begin{pmatrix}
1 & 1 \\
2 & 0
\end{pmatrix},
$$
and show that they are all of the form $\alpha I_2 + \beta A$.

$$
AX =
\begin{pmatrix}
a + c & b + d \\
2a & 2b
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
XA =
\begin{pmatrix}
a + 2b & a \\
c + 2d & c
\end{pmatrix}
$$
$$
AX = XA
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\begin{cases}
a + c = a + 2b \\[4pt]
b + d = a \\[4pt]
2a = c + 2d \\[4pt]
2b = c
\end{cases}
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\begin{cases}
c = 2b \\[4pt]
a = b + d
\end{cases}
$$
$$
X =
\begin{pmatrix}
b + d & b \\
2b & d
\end{pmatrix}
=
d
\begin{pmatrix}
1 & 0 \\
0 & 1
\end{pmatrix}
+ b
\begin{pmatrix}
1 & 1 \\
2 & 0
\end{pmatrix}
= d\, I_2 + b A,
\qquad b, d \in \mathbb{R}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

With a generic $X$, the equality $AX = XA$ gives four linear equations in the unknowns $a$, $b$, $c$, $d$. The first and the fourth both give $c = 2b$, the second gives $a = b + d$. The third is a consequence of the others, since substituting $a = b + d$ and $c = 2b$ it becomes $2b + 2d = 2b + 2d$. The parameters $b$ and $d$ remain free, and the matrix $X$ splits into the sum of $d\, I_2$ and $b A$. Therefore the matrices that commute with $A$ are **exactly the linear combinations** $\alpha I_2 + \beta A$, with $\alpha = d$ and $\beta = b$. Every matrix of this form commutes with $A$ also by the distributive property, since $A(\alpha I_2 + \beta A) = \alpha A + \beta A^2 = (\alpha I_2 + \beta A) A$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Find all matrices $X \in M_3(\mathbb{R})$ that commute with
$$
A =
\begin{pmatrix}
1 & 0 & 0 \\
0 & 1 & 0 \\
0 & 0 & 2
\end{pmatrix} .
$$

$$
X = (x_{ij}),
\qquad
(AX)_{ij} = a_{ii}\, x_{ij},
\qquad
(XA)_{ij} = x_{ij}\, a_{jj}
$$
$$
AX = XA
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
(a_{ii} - a_{jj})\, x_{ij} = 0
\quad \text{for all } i, j
$$
$$
a_{11} - a_{33} = a_{22} - a_{33} = -1 \neq 0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
x_{13} = x_{23} = x_{31} = x_{32} = 0
$$
$$
X =
\begin{pmatrix}
x_{11} & x_{12} & 0 \\
x_{21} & x_{22} & 0 \\
0 & 0 & x_{33}
\end{pmatrix}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Multiplying $X$ on the left by a diagonal matrix multiplies row $i$ of $X$ by $a_{ii}$, while multiplying it on the right multiplies column $j$ by $a_{jj}$; therefore the entry in position $(i, j)$ of $AX$ is $a_{ii}\, x_{ij}$ and that of $XA$ is $x_{ij}\, a_{jj}$. The equality of the two products is therefore equivalent to the nine equations $(a_{ii} - a_{jj})\, x_{ij} = 0$. When $a_{ii} = a_{jj}$ the equation is satisfied for every value of $x_{ij}$; this happens for $i, j \in \{1, 2\}$ and for $i = j = 3$. When $a_{ii} \neq a_{jj}$, that is, when *exactly one* of the two indices equals $3$, the equation forces $x_{ij} = 0$. The matrices that commute with $A$ are therefore **the block matrices** with an arbitrary $2 \times 2$ block in the upper left and an arbitrary entry in the lower right. The result shows that a matrix commuting with a diagonal matrix is *not* necessarily diagonal, when the diagonal has repeated entries.

</div>
