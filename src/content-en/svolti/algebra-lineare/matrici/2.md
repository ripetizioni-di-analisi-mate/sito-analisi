## Exercise 1

Given the matrices
$$
A =
\begin{pmatrix}
1 & -1 & 2 \\
0 & 3 & 1
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
B =
\begin{pmatrix}
2 & 1 \\
1 & 0 \\
-1 & 4
\end{pmatrix},
$$
compute, if defined, the products $AB$ and $BA$.

$$
(2 \times 3) \cdot (3 \times 2) = 2 \times 2,
\qquad
(3 \times 2) \cdot (2 \times 3) = 3 \times 3
$$
$$
AB =
\begin{pmatrix}
2 - 1 - 2 & 1 + 0 + 8 \\
0 + 3 - 1 & 0 + 0 + 4
\end{pmatrix}
=
\begin{pmatrix}
-1 & 9 \\
2 & 4
\end{pmatrix}
$$
$$
BA =
\begin{pmatrix}
2 + 0 & -2 + 3 & 4 + 1 \\
1 + 0 & -1 + 0 & 2 + 0 \\
-1 + 0 & 1 + 12 & -2 + 4
\end{pmatrix}
=
\begin{pmatrix}
2 & 1 & 5 \\
1 & -1 & 2 \\
-1 & 13 & 2
\end{pmatrix}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The product $AB$ is defined because the number of columns of $A$, that is $3$, equals the number of rows of $B$, and the result has as many rows as $A$ and as many columns as $B$. The product $BA$ is defined for the same reason, since $B$ has $2$ columns and $A$ has $2$ rows, and the result is $3 \times 3$. Each entry is obtained by multiplying a row of the first factor by a column of the second: for example the entry in position $(1, 2)$ of $AB$ is the product of the first row $(1, -1, 2)$ of $A$ by the second column $(1, 0, 4)$ of $B$, that is, $1 \cdot 1 + (-1) \cdot 0 + 2 \cdot 4 = 9$. The two matrices $AB$ and $BA$ have different dimensions, therefore they **are different**.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Given the matrices
$$
A =
\begin{pmatrix}
1 & 2 \\
-1 & 0
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
B =
\begin{pmatrix}
0 & 1 \\
3 & -2
\end{pmatrix},
$$
determine whether $A$ and $B$ commute and compare $(A + B)^2$ with $A^2 + 2AB + B^2$.

$$
AB =
\begin{pmatrix}
0 + 6 & 1 - 4 \\
0 + 0 & -1 + 0
\end{pmatrix}
=
\begin{pmatrix}
6 & -3 \\
0 & -1
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
BA =
\begin{pmatrix}
0 - 1 & 0 + 0 \\
3 + 2 & 6 + 0
\end{pmatrix}
=
\begin{pmatrix}
-1 & 0 \\
5 & 6
\end{pmatrix}
$$
$$
A + B =
\begin{pmatrix}
1 & 3 \\
2 & -2
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
(A + B)^2 =
\begin{pmatrix}
1 + 6 & 3 - 6 \\
2 - 4 & 6 + 4
\end{pmatrix}
=
\begin{pmatrix}
7 & -3 \\
-2 & 10
\end{pmatrix}
$$
$$
A^2 =
\begin{pmatrix}
-1 & 2 \\
-1 & -2
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
B^2 =
\begin{pmatrix}
3 & -2 \\
-6 & 7
\end{pmatrix}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
A^2 + 2AB + B^2
&=
\begin{pmatrix}
-1 + 12 + 3 & 2 - 6 - 2 \\
-1 + 0 - 6 & -2 - 2 + 7
\end{pmatrix} \\[6pt]
&=
\begin{pmatrix}
14 & -6 \\
-7 & 3
\end{pmatrix}
\neq (A + B)^2
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The products $AB$ and $BA$ are both $2 \times 2$ but have different entries, for example $(AB)_{11} = 6$ and $(BA)_{11} = -1$, therefore $A$ and $B$ **do not commute**. By the distributive property $(A + B)^2 = A^2 + AB + BA + B^2$, and this expression coincides with $A^2 + 2AB + B^2$ *if and only if* $BA = AB$. Since the two matrices do not commute, the formula for the square of a binomial valid for numbers gives a result different from $(A + B)^2$, as the computations confirm. The value of $(A + B)^2$ is checked with the correct formula: the sum $A^2 + AB + BA + B^2$ has first entry $-1 + 6 - 1 + 3 = 7$ and, proceeding in the same way, gives back the matrix $\begin{pmatrix} 7 & -3 \\ -2 & 10 \end{pmatrix}$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Given the symmetric matrix and the vector
$$
A =
\begin{pmatrix}
2 & 1 & 0 \\
1 & -1 & 3 \\
0 & 3 & 1
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
x =
\begin{pmatrix}
1 \\
-2 \\
1
\end{pmatrix},
$$
compute $Ax$ as a linear combination of the columns of $A$ and the number $x^T A x$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
Ax
&= 1
\begin{pmatrix}
2 \\
1 \\
0
\end{pmatrix}
- 2
\begin{pmatrix}
1 \\
-1 \\
3
\end{pmatrix}
+ 1
\begin{pmatrix}
0 \\
3 \\
1
\end{pmatrix} \\[6pt]
&=
\begin{pmatrix}
2 - 2 + 0 \\
1 + 2 + 3 \\
0 - 6 + 1
\end{pmatrix}
=
\begin{pmatrix}
0 \\
6 \\
-5
\end{pmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$
$$
x^T A x =
\begin{pmatrix}
1 & -2 & 1
\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}
0 \\
6 \\
-5
\end{pmatrix}
= 0 - 12 - 5 = -17
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The product of $A$, which is $3 \times 3$, by the column vector $x$, which is $3 \times 1$, is a $3 \times 1$ column vector. By the reading of the **column-by-column product**, $Ax$ is the linear combination of the columns of $A$ with coefficients the components $1$, $-2$, $1$ of $x$; adding the three columns multiplied in this way gives $(0, 6, -5)$. By the **associative property** $x^T A x = x^T (Ax)$, so it suffices to multiply the row vector $x^T$, which is $1 \times 3$, by the column vector $Ax$ just found. The result is a $1 \times 1$ matrix, that is, the number $-17$. Expressions of this type are quadratic forms, which appear in the study of conics and quadrics.

</div>
