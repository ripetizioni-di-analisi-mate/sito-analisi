## Exercise 1

Determine whether the function $f : \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}^3$
$$
f(x, y) = (x + 2y,\ 3x - y,\ x)
$$
is linear.

$$
u = (x_1, y_1), \qquad v = (x_2, y_2), \qquad \lambda \in \mathbb{R}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
f(u + v) &= \big( (x_1 + x_2) + 2(y_1 + y_2),\ 3(x_1 + x_2) - (y_1 + y_2),\ x_1 + x_2 \big) \\[6pt]
&= (x_1 + 2y_1,\ 3x_1 - y_1,\ x_1) + (x_2 + 2y_2,\ 3x_2 - y_2,\ x_2) \\[6pt]
&= f(u) + f(v)
\end{aligned}
$$
$$
f(\lambda u) = (\lambda x_1 + 2\lambda y_1,\ 3\lambda x_1 - \lambda y_1,\ \lambda x_1) = \lambda\, f(u)
$$
$$
f(x, y) =
\begin{pmatrix}
1 & 2 \\
3 & -1 \\
1 & 0
\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}
x \\
y
\end{pmatrix}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

To prove linearity the two conditions with *generic* vectors and scalars are needed, so we write $u$ and $v$ with literal components. In the sum $f(u + v)$ each component is a polynomial of degree one with no constant term, therefore it splits into the part with index $1$ and the part with index $2$, and we obtain $f(u) + f(v)$. In the same way $\lambda$ factors out of each component of $f(\lambda u)$. The two conditions are satisfied, so $f$ **is linear**. The same conclusion can be read from the expression $f(x, y) = A \begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \end{pmatrix}$, since every function of the form $x \mapsto Ax$ is linear; the rows of $A$ contain the coefficients of the three components.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Determine whether the functions
$$
g(x, y) = (x - y,\ xy),
\qquad
h(x, y, z) = (x + z,\ y - 1)
$$
are linear.

$$
h(0, 0, 0) = (0, -1) \neq (0, 0)
$$
$$
g(0, 0) = (0, 0),
\qquad
g(2, 2) = (0, 4),
\qquad
2\, g(1, 1) = 2\,(0, 1) = (0, 2)
$$
$$
g\big( 2 \cdot (1, 1) \big) \neq 2\, g(1, 1)
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

A linear map sends the zero vector to the zero vector. Since $h(0, 0, 0) = (0, -1)$, the function $h$ **is not linear**; the cause is the constant term $-1$ in the second component.

For $g$ the check on the zero vector is passed, since $g(0, 0) = (0, 0)$; this check is *necessary* and not sufficient, therefore we look for a counterexample to one of the two conditions. The product $xy$ is of degree two, so doubling the vector multiplies the second component by $4$. Indeed $g(2, 2) = (0, 4)$, while $2\, g(1, 1) = (0, 2)$, therefore $g$ **is not linear**.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Let $T : \mathbb{R}_2[x] \to \mathbb{R}^2$ be defined by
$$
T(p) = \big( p(1),\ p'(0) \big) .
$$
Prove that $T$ is linear, write the matrix with respect to the basis $(1, x, x^2)$ of $\mathbb{R}_2[x]$ and the standard basis of $\mathbb{R}^2$, and compute $T(1 + 2x - x^2)$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
T(p + q) &= \big( p(1) + q(1),\ p'(0) + q'(0) \big) = T(p) + T(q) \\[6pt]
T(\lambda p) &= \big( \lambda p(1),\ \lambda p'(0) \big) = \lambda\, T(p)
\end{aligned}
$$
$$
T(1) = (1, 0), \qquad T(x) = (1, 1), \qquad T(x^2) = (1, 0)
$$
$$
M(T) =
\begin{pmatrix}
1 & 1 & 1 \\
0 & 1 & 0
\end{pmatrix}
$$
$$
\begin{pmatrix}
1 & 1 & 1 \\
0 & 1 & 0
\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}
1 \\
2 \\
-1
\end{pmatrix}
=
\begin{pmatrix}
2 \\
2
\end{pmatrix}
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
T(1 + 2x - x^2) = (2, 2)
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The value at a point of the sum of two polynomials is the sum of the values, and the derivative of the sum is the sum of the derivatives; moreover a multiplicative constant can be taken out of both the value and the derivative. Hence the two components of $T$ both satisfy the conditions, and $T$ **is linear**.

The columns of the matrix are the images of the basis polynomials. For $p = 1$ we have $p(1) = 1$ and $p' = 0$; for $p = x$ we have $p(1) = 1$ and $p' = 1$; for $p = x^2$ we have $p(1) = 1$ and $p'(0) = 2 \cdot 0 = 0$. The polynomial $1 + 2x - x^2$ has coordinates $(1, 2, -1)$ with respect to the basis, and the product of the matrix by these coordinates gives $(2, 2)$. The result is confirmed directly, since $p(1) = 1 + 2 - 1 = 2$ and $p'(x) = 2 - 2x$ equals $2$ at $x = 0$.

</div>

## Exercise 4

Determine for which values of the parameter $k \in \mathbb{R}$ the function
$$
f_k(x, y) = \big( x + k^2 - 1,\ (k - 1)\,xy + y \big)
$$
is linear.

$$
f_k(0, 0) = (k^2 - 1,\ 0) = (0, 0)
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
k = 1 \ \text{ or } \ k = -1
$$
$$
k = 1 : \quad f_1(x, y) = (x,\ y)
$$
$$
k = -1 : \quad f_{-1}(x, y) = (x,\ -2xy + y),
\qquad
f_{-1}(2, 2) = (2, -6),
\qquad
2\, f_{-1}(1, 1) = (2, -2)
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The condition $f_k(0, 0) = (0, 0)$ is necessary for linearity, so it is imposed first. The first component of $f_k(0, 0)$ is $k^2 - 1$, which vanishes only for $k = \pm 1$. For all other values of $k$ the function is not linear.

The two values found remain to be examined. For $k = 1$ the term $(k - 1)\,xy$ vanishes and $f_1$ is the identity of $\mathbb{R}^2$, which is linear. For $k = -1$ the degree-two term $-2xy$ remains, and the counterexample with $(1, 1)$ and $\lambda = 2$ shows that $f_{-1}(2, 2) = (2, -6)$ is different from $2\, f_{-1}(1, 1) = (2, -2)$. Therefore $f_k$ is linear **if and only if** $k = 1$.

</div>
