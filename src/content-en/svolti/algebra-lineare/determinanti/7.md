## Exercise 1

Solve the matrix equation $AX = B$, with
$$
A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 5 & 3 \end{pmatrix},
\qquad
B = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 2 & 1 \end{pmatrix} .
$$

$$
\det A = 6 - 5 = 1,
\qquad
A^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & -1 \\ -5 & 2 \end{pmatrix}
$$
$$
AX = B
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
X = A^{-1} B
= \begin{pmatrix} 3 & -1 \\ -5 & 2 \end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}
= \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}
$$
$$
AX = \begin{pmatrix} 2 - 1 & -2 + 2 \\ 5 - 3 & -5 + 6 \end{pmatrix}
= \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 2 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = B
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Since $\det A = 1 \neq 0$, by the **invertibility criterion** the matrix $A$ is invertible, and its inverse is written with the formula of order $2$. Multiplying both sides of $AX = B$ *on the left* by $A^{-1}$ gives $A^{-1} A X = A^{-1} B$, that is, $X = A^{-1} B$. The solution is **unique**, because every solution must coincide with $A^{-1} B$. The product $AX$ returns $B$ and confirms the result.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Solve the matrix equation $XA = B$, with
$$
A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix},
\qquad
B = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 2 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix} .
$$

$$
\det A = (1 + 1 + 0) - (0 + 1 + 0) = 1
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
C_{11} &= 0, & C_{12} &= 1, & C_{13} &= -1, \\[4pt]
C_{21} &= -1, & C_{22} &= 1, & C_{23} &= 0, \\[4pt]
C_{31} &= 1, & C_{32} &= -1, & C_{33} &= 1
\end{aligned}
\qquad
A^{-1} = (\operatorname{cof} A)^T = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 & -1 \\ -1 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}
$$
$$
X = B A^{-1}
= \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 2 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 & -1 \\ -1 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}
= \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The unknown $X$ is to the left of $A$, so both sides are multiplied *on the right* by $A^{-1}$, and from $XAA^{-1} = BA^{-1}$ it follows that $X = BA^{-1}$. The matrix product is not commutative, therefore writing $A^{-1} B$ would be wrong, and in this case it would not even be defined, given that $A^{-1}$ has order $3$ and $B$ has two rows. The determinant, computed with Sarrus' rule, equals $1$, and the cofactors, transposed, give $A^{-1}$ directly. The matrix $X$ has two rows and three columns, like $B$. The check $XA = B$ on the first row gives $(0, 1, 1)\,A = (1, 2, 2)$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Let
$$
A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 & 2 \\ 1 & 2 & 5 \end{pmatrix},
\qquad
b = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} .
$$
Find $X \in M_{3,1}(\mathbb{R})$ such that $AX = X + b$.

$$
AX = X + b
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
AX - I_3 X = b
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
(A - I_3)\, X = b
$$
$$
C = A - I_3 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 2 & 4 \end{pmatrix},
\qquad
\det C = (4 + 2 + 0) - (1 + 4 + 0) = 1
$$
$$
C^{-1} = (\operatorname{cof} C)^T = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -2 & 1 \\ 2 & 3 & -2 \\ -1 & -1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}
$$
$$
X = C^{-1} b = \begin{pmatrix} 0 + 0 + 1 \\ 2 + 0 - 2 \\ -1 + 0 + 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The terms with $X$ are moved to the left-hand side, writing $X = I_3 X$ so as to factor on the right, and one obtains $AX - I_3 X = (A - I_3) X$. Factoring as $(A - 1) X$ makes no sense, because $1$ is a number and $A$ a matrix. The matrix $C = A - I_3$ is obtained by subtracting $1$ from the diagonal entries of $A$; its determinant is $1$, so $C$ is **invertible** and the equation has the unique solution $X = C^{-1} b$. The cofactors of $C$ are $C_{11} = 0$, $C_{12} = 2$, $C_{13} = -1$, $C_{21} = -2$, $C_{22} = 3$, $C_{23} = -1$, $C_{31} = 1$, $C_{32} = -2$, $C_{33} = 1$, and their transposed matrix is $C^{-1}$. The check confirms the result, since $AX$ is the first column of $A$, that is, $(2, 0, 1)$, and it coincides with $X + b = (1, 0, 0) + (1, 0, 1)$.

</div>

## Exercise 4

Let
$$
A_k = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & k & 1 \\ 2 & 1 & k \\ 1 & 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad k \in \mathbb{R} .
$$
Determine for which $k$ the matrix is invertible and, for those values, compute the entries in positions $(1, 3)$ and $(3, 1)$ of $A_k^{-1}$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\det A_k &= (1 + k^2 + 2) - (1 + k + 2k) \\[6pt]
&= k^2 - 3k + 2 \\[6pt]
&= (k - 1)(k - 2)
\end{aligned}
$$
$$
A_k \text{ invertible} \iff k \in \mathbb{R} \setminus \{1, 2\}
$$
$$
C_{31} = (-1)^{3+1} \begin{vmatrix} k & 1 \\ 1 & k \end{vmatrix} = k^2 - 1,
\qquad
C_{13} = (-1)^{1+3} \begin{vmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{vmatrix} = 1
$$
$$
(A_k^{-1})_{13} = \frac{C_{31}}{\det A_k} = \frac{(k - 1)(k + 1)}{(k - 1)(k - 2)} = \frac{k + 1}{k - 2},
\qquad
(A_k^{-1})_{31} = \frac{C_{13}}{\det A_k} = \frac{1}{(k - 1)(k - 2)}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

With Sarrus' rule the diagonals to the right give $1 \cdot 1 \cdot 1 + k \cdot k \cdot 1 + 1 \cdot 2 \cdot 1 = k^2 + 3$, those to the left give $1 \cdot 1 \cdot 1 + 1 \cdot k \cdot 1 + k \cdot 2 \cdot 1 = 3k + 1$; the difference is the trinomial $k^2 - 3k + 2$, with roots $1$ and $2$. By the **invertibility criterion** the matrix is invertible for every $k$ different from $1$ and $2$. For a single entry of the inverse, $(A^{-1})_{ij} = \dfrac{C_{ji}}{\det A}$, with the **indices swapped**, so the entry in position $(1, 3)$ requires $C_{31}$, which is obtained by deleting the third row and the first column. The cancellation of $k - 1$ is legitimate, because $k \neq 1$ by hypothesis.

</div>
