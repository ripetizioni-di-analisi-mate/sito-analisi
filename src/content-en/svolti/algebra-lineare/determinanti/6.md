## Exercise 1

Let $A, B \in M_3(\mathbb{R})$ with $\det A = 2$ and $\det B = -3$. Compute
$$
\det (A B^T), \qquad \det (3A), \qquad \det (-A), \qquad \det (A^{-1} B^2), \qquad \det (A^3 B^{-1}) .
$$

$$
\det (A B^T) = \det A \cdot \det B^T = \det A \cdot \det B = 2 \cdot (-3) = -6
$$
$$
\det (3A) = 3^3 \det A = 27 \cdot 2 = 54,
\qquad
\det (-A) = (-1)^3 \det A = -2
$$
$$
\det (A^{-1} B^2) = \frac{1}{\det A} \cdot (\det B)^2 = \frac{1}{2} \cdot 9 = \frac{9}{2}
$$
$$
\det (A^3 B^{-1}) = (\det A)^3 \cdot \frac{1}{\det B} = 8 \cdot \left( -\frac{1}{3} \right) = -\frac{8}{3}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

**Binet's theorem** turns the determinant of any product into the product of the determinants of the factors, and gives $\det (A^k) = (\det A)^k$. The other three rules used are $\det B^T = \det B$, $\det (B^{-1}) = \dfrac{1}{\det B}$ and $\det (kA) = k^n \det A$ with $n = 3$, since multiplying the matrix by $k$ multiplies each of the three rows by $k$. For this reason $\det (3A)$ equals $27 \cdot 2$, and $\det (-A)$ equals $-2$, given that $(-1)^3 = -1$. The inverses $A^{-1}$ and $B^{-1}$ exist, since $\det A$ and $\det B$ are nonzero, and all the results are obtained without knowing the entries of the matrices.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Let
$$
A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 3 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix},
\qquad
B = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 4 & 2 & 0 \\ 5 & 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix} .
$$
Compute $\det (AB)$, $\det (A^2 B^T)$ and $\det (B^{-1} A)$ without carrying out matrix products.

$$
\det A = 2 \cdot 3 \cdot 1 = 6,
\qquad
\det B = 1 \cdot 2 \cdot (-1) = -2
$$
$$
\det (AB) = 6 \cdot (-2) = -12
$$
$$
\det (A^2 B^T) = (\det A)^2 \cdot \det B = 36 \cdot (-2) = -72
$$
$$
\det (B^{-1} A) = \frac{\det A}{\det B} = \frac{6}{-2} = -3
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The matrix $A$ is upper triangular and $B$ is lower triangular, so by the **determinant of a triangular matrix** the two determinants are the products of the diagonal entries, $6$ and $-2$. Since $\det B \neq 0$, the matrix $B$ is invertible and $B^{-1}$ makes sense. By **Binet's theorem**, together with $\det B^T = \det B$ and $\det (B^{-1}) = \dfrac{1}{\det B}$, the three required determinants are obtained with products and quotients of numbers. The product $AB$ is not triangular, and computing it and then expanding the determinant would require many more steps.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Let $A \in M_n(\mathbb{R})$ be **skew-symmetric**, that is, with $A^T = -A$, and let $n$ be odd. Prove that $\det A = 0$ and verify it on
$$
A = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 & 2 \\ -1 & 0 & 3 \\ -2 & -3 & 0 \end{pmatrix} .
$$

$$
\det A = \det A^T = \det (-A) = (-1)^n \det A = -\det A
$$
$$
2 \det A = 0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\det A = 0
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
\begin{vmatrix} 0 & 1 & 2 \\ -1 & 0 & 3 \\ -2 & -3 & 0 \end{vmatrix}
&= \bigl(0 + 1 \cdot 3 \cdot (-2) + 2 \cdot (-1) \cdot (-3)\bigr) - (0 + 0 + 0) \\[6pt]
&= -6 + 6 = 0
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

By the **properties of the determinant** the transpose has the same determinant, and by hypothesis $A^T = -A$. The matrix $-A$ is obtained by multiplying each of the $n$ rows by $-1$, so $\det (-A) = (-1)^n \det A$. Since $n$ is odd, $(-1)^n = -1$, and one obtains $\det A = -\det A$, that is, $\det A = 0$. For the given matrix Sarrus' rule confirms the result, since the three diagonals to the left contain a zero of the main diagonal, while the diagonals to the right give $-6$ and $+6$. For $n$ even the conclusion is false, as shown by the skew-symmetric matrix $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, which has determinant $1$.

</div>

## Exercise 4

Let $A \in M_3(\mathbb{R})$ be invertible with $A^2 = 2A$. Compute $\det A$.

$$
\det (A^2) = \det (2A)
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
(\det A)^2 = 2^3 \det A = 8 \det A
$$
$$
\det A\, (\det A - 8) = 0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\det A = 0 \ \text{ or } \ \det A = 8
$$
$$
A \text{ invertible} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \det A \neq 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \det A = 8
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Take the determinant of both sides of the equality $A^2 = 2A$. The left-hand side, by **Binet's theorem**, has determinant $(\det A)^2$; the right-hand side, since the matrix has order $3$, has determinant $2^3 \det A = 8 \det A$. The quadratic equation in the unknown $\det A$ has the solutions $0$ and $8$. By the invertibility criterion an invertible matrix has nonzero determinant, so $\det A = 8$. The same result is obtained by multiplying $A^2 = 2A$ on the left by $A^{-1}$, which gives $A = 2I_3$, and indeed $\det (2I_3) = 8$.

</div>
