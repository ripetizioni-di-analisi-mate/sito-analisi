## Exercise 1

A rhombus has diagonals of lengths $8$ and $6$. Find its area, then verify it by finding a side length and the corresponding height.

$$
\begin{aligned}
    A&=\frac{8\cdot6}{2}=24,\\
    l&=\sqrt{4^2+3^2}=5,\qquad h=\frac{24}{5},\qquad lh=24.
    \end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The diagonals bisect each other at right angles, so the four triangles have legs of lengths $4$ and $3$ and area $6$ each. A side of the rhombus is a hypotenuse of one of these triangles. Once that length is known, find the altitude of this triangle to the side of the rhombus: its area is $6$, so that altitude is $12/5$. The centre lies halfway between opposite sides, making the rhombus height twice that distance.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A rhombus has area $60\,\mathrm{cm}^2$ and longer diagonal $12\,\mathrm{cm}$. Find its shorter diagonal.

$$
d=\frac{2A}{D}=\frac{120}{12}=10\,\mathrm{cm}.
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The product of the diagonals must be twice the area, or $120\,\mathrm{cm}^2$. Dividing by the longer diagonal gives the other length, $10$. This is positive and smaller than the given $12$. Check using the four right triangles with legs of $6$ and $5$ centimetres. Each has area $15\,\mathrm{cm}^2$, so four restore $60$. Halving the area instead of doubling it would account for the factor $1/2$ twice.

</div>

## Exercise 3

In a convex quadrilateral with perpendicular diagonals, $AO=4$, $OC=3$, $BO=5$, and $OD=1$. Find the area and decide whether the diagonals have to bisect each other.

$$
\begin{aligned}
    AC&=4+3=7,\qquad BD=5+1=6,\\
    A&=\frac{7\cdot5}{2}+\frac{7\cdot1}{2}=21.
    \end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Diagonal $AC$ is the common base of two triangles, one above it and one below. Perpendicular segment $BD$ gives their altitudes of $5$ and $1$. Adding the contributions multiplies $7$ by half their sum, giving $21$. Here $O$ bisects neither diagonal. For this formula it suffices that the quadrilateral is convex and the diagonals are perpendicular, so that the two altitudes add to the whole diagonal $BD$.

</div>
