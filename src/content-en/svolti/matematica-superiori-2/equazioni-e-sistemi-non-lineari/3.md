## Exercise 1

Solve $\sqrt{x+4}=5$ and explain why squaring adds no solutions.

$$
\begin{aligned}
         x+4&\ge0\\[6pt]
         \sqrt{x+4}=5&\iff x+4=25\\[6pt]
         x&=21.
         \end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The domain requires $x\ge-4$. On that domain the left side is nonnegative, and $5$ already has the correct sign. Equating the squares is therefore reversible: the only nonnegative number with square $25$ is $5$. The linear equation gives the allowed value $21$. Substituting gives $\sqrt{21+4}=\sqrt{25}=5$. If the right side were $-5$, squaring would give the same candidate, but the original equation would have no solutions because its sign would conflict with the principal square root.

</div>

## Exercise 2

For which real $k$ does $\sqrt{x+5}=k$ have a solution? Find it.

$$
\begin{aligned}
         k<0&\implies S=\varnothing,\\[6pt]
         k\ge0&\implies x+5=k^2,\\[6pt]
         S&=\{k^2-5\}\quad(k\ge0).
         \end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The radical is defined for $x\ge-5$ and gives only nonnegative values. When $k<0$, equality is impossible before any calculation. For $k\ge0$, the candidate $k^2-5$ always lies in the domain because $k^2\ge0$. Substituting gives $\sqrt{k^2}=|k|=k$. The boundary value $k=0$ is allowed and corresponds to $x=-5$, the first point of the domain. This distinguishes a zero radicand from division by zero.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Solve $\sqrt{x+9}=\sqrt x+2$ and check the result in both radicals.

::grafico{f="sqrt(x+9);sqrt(x)+2" legenda="y=\sqrt{x+9};y=\sqrt x+2" xmin=-0.4 xmax=6.1 ymin=-0.4 ymax=5.15 punti="1.5625,3.25,pieno,P" titolo="The common point has horizontal coordinate 25/16"}

$$
\begin{aligned}
         x+9&=x+4\sqrt x+4\\[6pt]
         \sqrt x&=\frac54\\[6pt]
         x&=\frac{25}{16}.
         \end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The common domain is $x\ge0$. The right side is at least two, so both original sides are nonnegative. Squaring gives the cross term $4\sqrt x$, which remains after cancelling $x$. The new relation has $\dfrac54>0$ on the right, making the second squaring reversible too. At the value found, the radicals are $\dfrac{13}{4}$ and $\dfrac54$. Indeed $\dfrac54+2=\dfrac{13}{4}$, the common vertical coordinate at the intersection.

</div>
