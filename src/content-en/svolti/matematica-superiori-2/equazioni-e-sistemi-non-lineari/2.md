## Exercise 1

Solve $x^3-16x=0$. Which case disappears if you divide by $x$ first?

$$
\begin{aligned}
         x^3-16x&=x(x^2-16)=x(x-4)(x+4)\\[6pt]
         S&=\{-4,0,4\}.
         \end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Factor out $x$, then factor the difference of squares $x^2-16$. Each linear factor identifies a value: $0$ from the common factor, and $4$ and $-4$ from the other two. Division by $x$ would be valid only when $x\ne0$. It would find the two opposite roots, and the excluded case would need a separate check. Indeed, at $x=0$ both original terms vanish. The full solution set combines all three cases.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Solve $(x-1)(x+2)^2=0$ and compare how the graph behaves at the two zeroes.

::grafico{f="(x-1)*(x+2)^2" xmin=-3.25 xmax=1.9 ymin=-6.4 ymax=8 punti="-2,0,pieno;1,0,pieno" titolo="The curve touches the axis at the double zero and crosses at the simple zero"}

$$
\begin{aligned}
         (x-1)(x+2)^2=0&\iff x\in\{-2,1\}\\[6pt]
         (x+2)^2&>0\qquad (x\ne-2).
         \end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The two factors vanish at $1$ and $-2$. The second is squared, so $-2$ has multiplicity two. Near $-2$, the factor $x-1$ remains negative while the square is positive on both sides. The graph touches the axis and stays below it. Near $1$, the square stays positive and $x-1$ changes sign, so the curve crosses the axis. The multiplicity is even at $-2$, where the sign stays the same, and odd at $1$, where it reverses.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Solve $x^3-x^2-6x=0$. Check the factorisation before using the zero-product property.

$$
\begin{aligned}
         x^3-x^2-6x&=x(x^2-x-6)\\[6pt]
         &=x(x-3)(x+2)\\[6pt]
         S&=\{-2,0,3\}.
         \end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

After factoring out the common term, the quadratic needs two factors whose constants have product $-6$ and sum $-1$. The values $-3$ and $2$ meet both conditions. Expanding $(x-3)(x+2)$ indeed recovers $x^2-x-6$. Now one factor vanishes at each of the three stated values. The two nonzero roots are not opposites. The term in $x^2$ comes from the linear coefficient in the quadratic after factoring out the common term.

</div>
