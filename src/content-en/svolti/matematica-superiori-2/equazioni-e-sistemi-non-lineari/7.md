## Exercise 1

Solve $\begin{cases}x+y=9\\x^2+y^2=41\end{cases}$ by finding the product first.

$$
\begin{aligned}
         81&=41+2xy\quad\implies xy=20,\\[6pt]
         t^2-9t+20&=(t-4)(t-5),\\[6pt]
         S&=\{(4;5),(5;4)\}.
         \end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The square of the sum contains the two squares and twice the product. Since the original conditions give $81$ and $41$, the cross term must equal $40$. Thus $xy=20$, giving the auxiliary equation. The check in the original system is $4+5=9$ and $4^2+5^2=16+25=41$. Swapping the coordinates leaves both relations unchanged. Choosing the same root twice would change the required sum.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Find the intersections of $y=x^2$ and $y=3x-2$. Check how the coordinates pair up.

::grafico{f="x^2;3*x-2" legenda="y=x^2;y=3x-2" xmin=-1.05 xmax=3.15 ymin=-1.5 ymax=8.1 punti="1,1,pieno;2,4,pieno" segmenti="1,0,1,1;2,0,2,4" titolo="Each vertical coordinate is read at its own horizontal coordinate"}

$$
\begin{aligned}
         x^2&=3x-2\\[6pt]
         (x-1)(x-2)&=0\\[6pt]
         S&=\{(1;1),(2;4)\}.
         \end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

At an intersection the two values of $y$ agree, so solve $x^2-3x+2=0$. The roots are $1$ and $2$. For the first, the vertical coordinate is $1^2=1$ and the line gives $3\cdot1-2=1$. For the second, it is $2^2=4$ and the line also gives $3\cdot2-2=4$. Recalculate the vertical coordinate for each horizontal one. Taking every possible pairing of the resulting numbers would introduce points outside the system.

</div>

## Exercise 3

For which $q$ does $\begin{cases}x+y=6\\x^2+y^2=q\end{cases}$ have just one real pair? Classify the other values too.

$$
\begin{aligned}
         (x-y)^2&=2q-36,\\[6pt]
         q=18&\implies x=y=3.
         \end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The formula follows by subtracting the square of the sum from twice the sum of squares. If $q<18$, the squared difference would be negative, so there are no real pairs. At $q=18$, the difference is zero and sum $6$ fixes pair $(3;3)$. If $q>18$, the differences are $\pm\sqrt{2q-36}$, and each determines a distinct pair together with the sum. At $q=20$, for example, the pairs are $(2;4)$ and $(4;2)$.

</div>
