## Exercise 1

An equilateral triangle has side length $10$ cm. Find its altitude and area.

$$
\begin{aligned}
h&=\sqrt{10^2-5^2}=5\sqrt3\,\mathrm{cm},\\[6pt]
\mathcal A&=\dfrac{10\cdot 5\sqrt3}{2}=25\sqrt3\,\mathrm{cm}^2 .
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The altitude meets the midpoint of the base because the equilateral triangle is isosceles with respect to each side. This gives two right triangles with hypotenuse $10$ cm and a leg of $5$ cm along the base. The other leg is the altitude of the whole triangle. Find the area using the full base and that height, or add the areas of the halves. The length $5\sqrt3$ cm must be shorter than the hypotenuse, as comparison of their squares confirms.

</div>

## Exercise 2

The altitude of an equilateral triangle measures $6\sqrt3$ cm. Find the side length and perimeter.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\dfrac{\sqrt3}{2}l&=6\sqrt3,\\[6pt]
l&=\dfrac{2\cdot6\sqrt3}{\sqrt3}=12\,\mathrm{cm},\\[6pt]
p_{\mathrm{tri}}&=3l=36\,\mathrm{cm}.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

In the right triangle formed by halving the equilateral triangle, the altitude is a leg and the side is the hypotenuse. To recover the side, invert the linear relation between those lengths by multiplying the altitude by the reciprocal of $\dfrac{\sqrt3}{2}$. The radical in the given length cancels. To check, a side of $12$ cm has half-base $6$ cm, and the Pythagorean theorem gives $\sqrt{12^2-6^2}=6\sqrt3$ cm.

Choosing another side as base gives the same calculation, since an equilateral triangle's three altitudes have equal lengths.

</div>

## Exercise 3

In a right triangle the leg opposite $30^\circ$ measures $5$ cm. Find the hypotenuse, the other leg, and the area.

$$
\begin{aligned}
c&=2\cdot5=10\,\mathrm{cm},\\[6pt]
b&=\sqrt{10^2-5^2}=5\sqrt3\,\mathrm{cm},\\[6pt]
\mathcal A&=\dfrac{25\sqrt3}{2}\,\mathrm{cm}^2 .
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The known leg is half the hypotenuse because the triangle has the same angles as an equilateral triangle cut along its altitude. Double the given leg to obtain the hypotenuse. The other leg comes from the Pythagorean theorem and is opposite the acute angle of $60^\circ$. Half the product of the legs gives the area. Doubling that result also gives the area of the equilateral triangle of side length $10$ cm.

The angle condition identifies which leg to double. The leg opposite $60^\circ$ is the longer one.

</div>
