## Exercise 1

For which value of $k$ does the system $\begin{cases}kx+y=1\\4x+y=2\end{cases}$ have no solution?

$$
D=k-4
$$

$$
D=0\iff k=4
$$

$$
1=2\quad\text{is impossible}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

When the determinant is nonzero, the system has a unique solution. When it is zero, the equations have the same left side and different constant terms. The system therefore has no solution for that value.

Subtracting the equations gives $(k-4)x=-1$. When $k=4$, the left side is always zero and the right side remains $-1$. For any other value, dividing by $k-4$ gives $x=-\dfrac1{k-4}$. Either equation then determines $y$.

The result is $4$.

For $k$ different from four, the lines have distinct slopes, so this conflict does not occur. In the singular case the first equation is $4x+y=1$ and the second requires two on that same left side.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Classify $\begin{cases}kx+y=2\\x+ky=2\end{cases}$ for every real $k$. Give the possible pairs in the special cases.

$$
\begin{aligned}
D&=k^2-1,\qquad (k-1)(x-y)=0,\\[6pt]
k=1&\quad\Longrightarrow\quad x+y=2,\\[6pt]
k=-1&\quad\Longrightarrow\quad 0=4,\\[6pt]
k\notin\{-1,1\}&\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
x=y=\frac2{k+1}.
\end{aligned}
$$

::grafico{f="2-3*x;(2-x)/3" xmin=-1 xmax=3 ymin=-2 ymax=4 punti="0.5,0.5,pieno,P" titolo="For k = 3 the two lines meet at a pair with equal coordinates"}

<div class="spiegazione">

The determinant vanishes at two inputs. When $k=1$, both rows coincide and every pair $(t;2-t)$ solves the system. When $k=-1$, the left sides are opposites but the constants are equal and positive, so adding requires $0=4$. In the other cases subtraction gives $x=y$, and substitution allows division by $k+1$. The diagram checks $k=3$, which gives $(0.5;0.5)$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Classify the system $\begin{cases}kx+2y=8\\3x+y=4\end{cases}$ as $k\in\mathbb R$ varies.

$$
(k-6)x=0
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
k\ne6 &\implies x=0,\quad y=4\\[6pt]
k=6 &\implies (x;y)=(t;4-3t),\quad t\in\mathbb R.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Subtracting twice the second equation from the first eliminates $y$ and the constant terms. When $k\ne6$, the factor $k-6$ is nonzero, and the only possibility is $x=0$. When $k=6$, subtraction gives $0=0$. In that case, only the condition $3x+y=4$ remains. Any real value $t$ may be assigned to $x$, because the corresponding value $y=4-3t$ satisfies both original equations.

In the ordinary case $(0;4)$ lies on both lines regardless of $k$. At $k=6$ the same pair remains a solution and more appear. The parameter changes the number of solutions without removing that common point.

</div>
