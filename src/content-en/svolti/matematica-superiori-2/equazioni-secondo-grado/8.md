## Exercise 1

Find how many distinct real roots $x^2-8x+k=0$ has as $k$ varies. Find the root in the double case.

::grafico{f="(x-4)^2+2;(x-4)^2;(x-4)^2-1" legenda="k=18;k=16;k=15" xmin=-0.4 xmax=7.8 ymin=-1.8 ymax=8 punti="3,0,pieno;4,0,pieno;5,0,pieno" titolo="At the threshold k = 16 the vertex lies on the axis"}

$$
\begin{aligned}
         x^2-8x+k&=(x-4)^2+k-16,\\[6pt]
         \Delta&=64-4k,\\[6pt]
         \Delta=0&\iff k=16,\qquad x=4.
         \end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Since the leading coefficient is always one, the degree stays fixed. The parabola has axis $x=4$ and minimum $k-16$. For $k<16$, the minimum is negative and there are two intersections with the axis. At $k=16$, the vertex lies on it and gives the double root $4$. For $k>16$, the minimum is positive, so there are no real roots. The expression $64-4k$ gives the same three cases through the discriminant's sign.

</div>

## Exercise 2

For which values of $k$ does $x^2+10x+k=0$ have two distinct negative roots?

$$
\begin{aligned}
         \Delta>0&\iff 100-4k>0\iff k<25,\\[6pt]
         x_1+x_2&=-10,\qquad x_1x_2=k,\\[6pt]
         k&\in(0,25).
         \end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Two distinct real roots require a strictly positive discriminant. For both values to be negative their product must also be positive, so $k>0$. In that interval the roots have the same sign, and their sum $-10$ rules out a positive sign. The two conditions are thus sufficient as well as necessary. At $k=0$, one root is zero, which is not negative. At $k=25$, the roots coincide at $-5$ and are no longer distinct.

</div>

## Exercise 3

For which $k$ does $(k-1)x^2+4x+1=0$ have exactly one distinct real solution? Find it in each case.

$$
\begin{aligned}
         k=1&\implies 4x+1=0\implies x=-\frac14,\\[6pt]
         k\ne1&\implies \Delta=16-4(k-1)=20-4k,\\[6pt]
         \Delta=0&\iff k=5,\quad x=-\frac12.
         \end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

First distinguish the parameter that makes the coefficient of $x^2$ zero. At $k=1$, the equation is linear and its solution is $-\dfrac14$. Away from that case, one distinct solution requires discriminant zero, giving $k=5$. The equation is then $4x^2+4x+1=(2x+1)^2=0$, and its only root is $-\dfrac12$, a double root. The two values of $k$ give the same number of distinct solutions, but their multiplicities differ because only the second case leaves a square.

</div>
