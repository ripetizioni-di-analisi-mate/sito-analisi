## Exercise 1

Calculate the descriptive variance of $1$, $4$, and $7$. Check it using the mean of the squares.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\bar x&=\frac{1+4+7}{3}=4,\\[5pt]
\sigma^2&=\frac{(-3)^2+0^2+3^2}{3}=\frac{18}{3}=6,\\[5pt]
\frac{1^2+4^2+7^2}{3}-4^2&=22-16=6.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The deviations $-3$ and $3$ cancel in the signed sum, but each contributes nine units after squaring. For **descriptive variance**, average all three squared deviations, including the zero. Dividing by the number of nonzero deviations would give too much weight to the extreme values. The alternative check separates the contribution of the mean by using the squared original values.

</div>

## Exercise 2

The values $2$, $4$, and $7$ occur $1$, $2$, and $1$ times. Calculate their descriptive variance and check the sum of the deviations.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\bar x&=\frac{2+2\cdot4+7}{4}=\frac{17}{4},\\[5pt]
\sigma^2&=\frac{81+2+121}{16\cdot4}
=\frac{51}{16}.
\end{aligned}
$$

| Value | Count | Deviation |
|---|---:|---:|
| $2$ | $1$ | $-\dfrac{9}{4}$ |
| $4$ | $2$ | $-\dfrac{1}{4}$ |
| $7$ | $1$ | $\dfrac{11}{4}$ |

<div class="spiegazione">

The mean uses four terms because $4$ occurs twice. Express each deviation in quarters. The signed deviations with their counts add to $\dfrac{-9-2+11}{4}=0$. For the variance weight the squares, so the square of $-\dfrac{1}{4}$ occurs twice. The final denominator is four, the number of measurements. As a check, using the squared original observations gives $\dfrac{85}{4}-\left(\dfrac{17}{4}\right)^2=\dfrac{51}{16}$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Series $A$ contains $-4$, six zeros, and $4$. Series $B$ contains four $-3$s and four $3$s. Compare their means, ranges, and descriptive variances.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\bar x_A&=\bar x_B=0,\\[5pt]
R_A&=8,\qquad R_B=6,\\[5pt]
\sigma_A^2&=\frac{32}{8}=4,\qquad \sigma_B^2=\frac{72}{8}=9.
\end{aligned}
$$

::grafico{xmin=-5.1 xmax=5.4 ymin=-0.35 ymax=12.5 nomi="x," punti="-4,5.4;0,5.4;0,6.4;0,7.4;0,8.4;0,9.4;0,10.4;4,5.4;-3,0.7;-3,1.7;-3,2.7;-3,3.7;3,0.7;3,1.7;3,2.7;3,3.7" etichette="-4.67,8.1,A;-4.67,2.0,B" titolo="The observations at the centre keep A’s variance lower even with wider extremes"}

<div class="spiegazione">

The symmetric pairs cancel in both means. The extremes are farther away in $A$, so $A$ has the greater range. But six observations in $A$ equal the mean and contribute zero to the variance. All eight observations in $B$ are three units from the centre. That gives $B$ the **larger variance**. The range depends on the extremes, while variance also accounts for how often each deviation occurs.

</div>
