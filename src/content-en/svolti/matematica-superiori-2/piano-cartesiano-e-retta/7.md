## Exercise 1

Find the intersection of $y=x+3$ and $y=-x+9$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
x+3&=-x+9,\\
2x&=6,\\
x&=3,\qquad y=3+3=6.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

A common point has the same $y$-coordinate in both equations, so set the two expressions for $y$ equal. The resulting equation has the unique solution $x=3$. The first line gives $y=6$ and the second gives $-3+9=6$. The intersection is therefore $(3;6)$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Find the intersection of the lines $x=-2$ and $3x+2y=4$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
3(-2)+2y&=4,\\
2y&=10,\\
y&=5,\qquad P=(-2;5).
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The first equation fixes the $x$-coordinate directly. Substituting $-2$ into the second gives a linear equation in $y$ alone. The point $( -2;5)$ satisfies both equations because its $x$-coordinate is $-2$ and $-6+10=4$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Determine for which values of $k$ the lines $y=kx+2$ and $y=3x-4$ have a common point. Find its $x$-coordinate when it exists.

$$
\begin{aligned}
kx+2&=3x-4,\\
(k-3)x&=-6,\\
x&=\frac{6}{3-k}\qquad (k\ne 3).
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

When $k=3$, the lines have the same slope and intercepts $2$ and $-4$. Equality of the $y$-coordinates would require $0=-6$, so the lines are distinct and parallel. When $k\ne3$, the coefficient of $x$ is nonzero. There is a unique solution, and substituting it into either equation gives the $y$-coordinate.

</div>
