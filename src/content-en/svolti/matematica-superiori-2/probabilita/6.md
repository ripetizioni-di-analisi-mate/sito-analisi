## Exercise 1

A bag contains three red, two green, and one blue ball. Draw twice uniformly with replacement. Find the probability that the balls have the same color.

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(RR)&=\left(\frac36\right)^2=\frac9{36},\\[6pt]
P(GG)&=\left(\frac26\right)^2=\frac4{36},\\[6pt]
P(BB)&=\left(\frac16\right)^2=\frac1{36},\\[6pt]
P(RR\cup GG\cup BB)&=\frac{9+4+1}{36}=\frac7{18}.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Use $R$, $G$, and $B$ for red, green, and blue. Replacement restores the same proportions at each step. Each single-color path uses two edges of equal weight. The diagram shows only the paths that contribute to the event. Their probabilities can be added because their first colors differ. Labeling the balls gives the same counts, $3\cdot3$, $2\cdot2$, and $1\cdot1$, out of thirty-six ordered pairs.

## Exercise 2

For a fair die, let $A$ mean an even score, $B$ a score of at least three, and $C$ a score of at most three. Check independence for the pairs $A,B$ and $A,C$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(A)&=\frac12,\quad P(B)=\frac46,\quad P(C)=\frac12,\\[6pt]
A\cap B&=\{4,6\},\quad P(A\cap B)=\frac26=P(A)P(B),\\[6pt]
A\cap C&=\{2\},\quad P(A\cap C)=\frac16\ne\frac14=P(A)P(C).
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Both event pairs concern a single roll. The criterion always compares the intersection with the product. Two of the four values in $B$ are even, the same one-half share as on the whole die. Only one of the three values in $C$ is even. Knowing that $B$ occurred therefore leaves the proportion of even outcomes unchanged, while knowing that $C$ occurred reduces it.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A tile may carry badge $D$. Draw twice uniformly with replacement from the same bag. The probability of seeing two badges is $\dfrac{49}{100}$. Find the probability of one badge on a single draw. If the bag has $20$ tiles, how many carry the badge?

$$
\begin{aligned}
p^2&=\frac{49}{100},\qquad 0\le p\le1,\\[6pt]
p&=\frac7{10},\qquad 20p=14,\\[6pt]
\left(\frac{14}{20}\right)^2&=\frac{49}{100}.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The same probability $p$ appears in both factors because replacement restores the composition before the first draw. The algebraic equation also has a negative root, which lies outside the interval of possible probabilities. The number of badge tiles is the fraction $p$ of twenty. The final check starts from that whole-number count, models the two draws, and recovers the given probability.

</div>
