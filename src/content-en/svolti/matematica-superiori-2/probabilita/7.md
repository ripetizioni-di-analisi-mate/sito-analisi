## Exercise 1

A box contains four yellow tiles and two black tiles. Draw twice uniformly without replacement. Find the probability of two different colours and check it using the complement.

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(YK)+P(KY)&=\frac46\cdot\frac25+\frac26\cdot\frac45=\frac8{15},\\[6pt]
P(YY)+P(KK)&=\frac46\cdot\frac35+\frac26\cdot\frac15=\frac7{15},\\[6pt]
1-\frac7{15}&=\frac8{15}.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The letters $Y$ and $K$ identify yellow and black in the diagram. After the first draw, five tiles remain. If the first was yellow, both black tiles are still present. If it was black, all four yellow ones remain. The two orders give the same product but must be added because they are different outcomes. The complement uses the other two paths, which have different weights.

## Exercise 2

A bag contains six red balls and four blue ones. Draw twice uniformly without replacement. Find the probability that the second ball is red before the first colour is known, and then given that the first was blue.

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(R_2\mid R_1)&=\frac59,\qquad P(R_2\mid B_1)=\frac69=\frac23,\\[6pt]
P(R_2)&=\frac6{10}\cdot\frac59+\frac4{10}\cdot\frac69
=\frac{54}{90}=\frac35.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Before the first colour is known, both paths ending in red are included. A red first draw leaves five reds, while a blue first draw leaves six. The contributions are added because the first colour distinguishes the paths. Knowing that the first was blue keeps only the second branch. The proportion of reds in that updated bag is used, which is greater than the initial proportion.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Draw three balls uniformly without replacement from three reds and two blues. What is the probability of getting exactly two reds?

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(RRB)&=\frac35\cdot\frac24\cdot\frac23=\frac15,\\[6pt]
P(RBR)&=\frac35\cdot\frac24\cdot\frac23=\frac15,\\[6pt]
P(BRR)&=\frac25\cdot\frac34\cdot\frac23=\frac15,\\[6pt]
P(RRB\cup RBR\cup BRR)&=\frac35.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

There is exactly one blue ball in the draw, so its position picks one of the three paths. Each product has the two red counts, $3$ and $2$, and the blue count $2$, even though their order changes. The denominators are always $5$, $4$, $3$. The paths therefore have equal probabilities, which are added because no complete draw can belong to two different orders.

</div>
