## Exercise 1

Solve the Dirichlet problem in the unit disk with boundary value $u(1,\theta)=\cos\theta-\sin(2\theta)$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
u(r,\theta)&=r\cos\theta-r^2\sin(2\theta), \\
&=r\cos\theta-2(r\cos\theta)(r\sin\theta), \\
u(x,y)&=x-2xy, \\
u_{xx}(x,y)&=0, \\
u_{yy}(x,y)&=0, \\
\Delta u(x,y)&=0, \\
u(1,\theta)&=\cos\theta-\sin(2\theta).
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The boundary data contain two frequencies. The regular harmonic extension for the first mode has radial factor $r$, and for the second it has factor $r^2$. These follow from the radial equation, which admits powers $r^n$ and $r^{-n}$ for mode $n$. The latter diverges at the center, so continuity selects the positive power. At $r=1$ both powers equal one and the boundary data are preserved.

The double-angle formula gives $\sin(2\theta)=2\sin\theta\cos\theta$. In Cartesian coordinates the radial product can thus be written as $2xy$. Its polynomial form shows that the function is smooth even at the origin and both pure second derivatives vanish. It is therefore harmonic on the disk and continuous on its closure. Applying the **maximum principle** to the difference of two such solutions with equal boundary values gives a difference whose maximum and minimum are both zero. Thus the solution is unique.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Solve $-u''=f$ on $(0,1)$, $u(0)=u(1)=0$, where $f=1$ on $(0,\dfrac12)$ and $f=0$ on $(\dfrac12,1)$. Seek a solution with $u$ and $u'$ continuous at the interface.

$$
\begin{aligned}
u_-(x)&=-\frac{x^2}{2}+Ax,\qquad u_+(x)=B(1-x), \\
u_-\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)&=-\frac{1}{8}+\frac{A}{2},\qquad
u_+\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)=\frac{B}{2}, \\
u_-'\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)&=-\frac{1}{2}+A,\qquad
u_+'\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)=-B, \\
A-B&=\frac{1}{4},\qquad A+B=\frac{1}{2}, \\
2A&=\frac{3}{4}, \\
A&=\frac{3}{8},\qquad B=\frac{1}{8}, \\
u(x)&=\begin{cases}
-\dfrac{x^2}{2}+\dfrac{3x}{8},&0\le x\le\dfrac{1}{2},\\[4pt]
\dfrac{1-x}{8},&\dfrac{1}{2}<x\le1.
\end{cases}
\end{aligned}
$$

::grafico{curve="t,-t^2/2+3*t/8,0,0.5;t,(1-t)/8,0.5,1" xmin=0 xmax=1 ymin=0 ymax=0.09 nomi="x,u" punti="0.5,0.0625,pieno;0.375,0.0703125,pieno" titolo="The left branch is parabolic and reaches its maximum at $x=\dfrac38$. The right branch is linear and joins it with the same slope."}

<div class="spiegazione">

The source is one on the left interval, so the second derivative is $-1$ and the branch is a parabola. On the right the source vanishes, so the second derivative is zero and the branch is affine. The expressions used in the calculation already attain the values at zero and one. Two coefficients remain, determined by continuity of both the profile and its slope at the interface.

Matching the values gives $A-B=\dfrac{1}{4}$. Matching the slopes gives $A+B=\dfrac{1}{2}$. Thus the common value is $\dfrac{1}{16}$ and the common slope is $-\dfrac{1}{8}$. The function is continuous, piecewise smooth, and zero at the endpoints, so it belongs to $H_0^1(0,1)$. On integrating by parts on each segment, the interface terms have opposite signs and equal magnitudes by continuity of $u'$. The function therefore satisfies the weak formulation with exactly the stated piecewise source.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Let $u(x)=\dfrac12-|x-\dfrac12|$ on $(0,1)$. Find the weak derivative and identify the source $-u''$ in the distributional sense.

$$
\begin{aligned}
u(x)&=\begin{cases}x,&0<x<\dfrac{1}{2},\\1-x,&\dfrac{1}{2}\le x<1,\end{cases} \\
u'&=\mathbf 1_{(0,\frac12)}-\mathbf 1_{(\frac12,1)}\quad\text{almost everywhere}, \\
\langle -u'',\varphi\rangle&=\int_0^{\frac{1}{2}}\varphi'(x)\,dx-\int_{\frac{1}{2}}^1\varphi'(x)\,dx, \\
&=\varphi\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)-\varphi(0)
  -\varphi(1)+\varphi\left(\frac{1}{2}\right), \\
&=2\varphi\left(\frac{1}{2}\right), \\
-u''&=2\delta_{\frac{1}{2}}.
\end{aligned}
$$

::grafico{f="0.5-abs(x-0.5)" xmin=0 xmax=1 ymin=0 ymax=0.62 nomi="x,u" punti="0,0,pieno;0.5,0.5,pieno;1,0,pieno" titolo="At the vertex the slope changes from $1$ to $-1$. The second derivative captures this jump in slope."}

<div class="spiegazione">

To the left of the corner the absolute value is $\dfrac{1}{2}-x$ and to the right it is $x-\dfrac{1}{2}$. The two branches of $u$ are therefore affine with opposite slopes. Integrating by parts on the two intervals shows that the interior contributions in the first derivative cancel because $u$ is continuous at the corner. This leaves the piecewise slope function, which belongs to $L^2(0,1)$. Since $u$ also belongs to $L^2$ and vanishes at the endpoints, it belongs to $H_0^1(0,1)$.

For the second derivative take $\varphi\in C_c^\infty(0,1)$. The minus sign in $-u''$ gives the integral of $u'\varphi'$. The test function vanishes at the segment endpoints, so integration leaves only evaluations at the interface. Both appear with positive sign because the slopes have opposite signs. The functional is twice evaluation at $\dfrac{1}{2}$, that is, a Dirac mass of weight two at the corner.

</div>

## Exercise 4

For $-u''=2$ on $(0,1)$ with zero endpoint values, compare the energy $J$ of the solution with that of the function $v(x)=2x(1-x)$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
u(x)&=x(1-x),\qquad v(x)=2u(x), \\
\int_0^1 u(x)\,dx&=\left[\frac{x^2}{2}-\frac{x^3}{3}\right]_0^1, \\
&=\frac{1}{6}, \\
\int_0^1|u'(x)|^2\,dx&=\int_0^1(1-4x+4x^2)\,dx, \\
&=\left[x-2x^2+\frac{4x^3}{3}\right]_0^1, \\
&=\frac{1}{3}, \\
J(u)&=\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{3}-2\cdot\frac{1}{6}, \\
&=-\frac{1}{6}, \\
J(v)&=\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{4}{3}-2\cdot\frac{2}{6}, \\
&=0, \\
J(v)-J(u)&=\frac{1}{6}.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The polynomial $u$ has second derivative $-2$ and vanishes at both endpoints, so it solves the Poisson problem. Both profiles belong to $H_0^1(0,1)$ since they are smooth with zero trace. With source two, the functional equals half the integral of the squared derivative minus twice the integral of the profile. Passing from $u$ to $2u$ quadruples the first integral and doubles the second. This difference in scaling explains why the two contributions cancel for $v$.

The comparison also follows from the weak equation. For $w=v-u$ we have $\int_0^1u'w'=2\int_0^1 w$ since $w$ has zero trace and is an admissible test function. On expanding $J(u+w)$, the linear terms in $w$ cancel by that equality, leaving half of $\int_0^1|w'|^2$. Here $w=u$, giving $\dfrac{1}{6}$, in agreement with direct calculation.

</div>
