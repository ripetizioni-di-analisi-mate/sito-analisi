## Exercise 1

Solve the heat problem $u_t=u_{xx}$ on $(0,\pi)$, with $u(t,0)=0$, $u(t,\pi)=1$, and $u(0,x)=\dfrac x\pi+2\sin x-\sin(2x)$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
p(x)&=\frac{x}{\pi},\qquad p''=0, \\
v(t,x)&=u(t,x)-p(x), \\
v_t&=v_{xx},\qquad v(t,0)=v(t,\pi)=0, \\
v(0,x)&=2\sin x-\sin(2x), \\
T_1'+T_1&=0,\qquad T_1(0)=2, \\
T_2'+4T_2&=0,\qquad T_2(0)=-1, \\
T_1(t)&=2e^{-t},\qquad T_2(t)=-e^{-4t}, \\
u(t,x)&=\frac{x}{\pi}+2e^{-t}\sin x-e^{-4t}\sin(2x).
\end{aligned}
$$

::grafico{f="x/pi+2*sin(x)-sin(2*x);x/pi+2*exp(-1)*sin(x)-exp(-4)*sin(2*x);x/pi" xmin=0 xmax=3.14159265 ymin=0 ymax=3.45 nomi="x,u" legenda="u(0,x);u(1,x);\dfrac{x}{\pi}" titolo="The endpoint values remain fixed at $0$ and $1$. The interior profile converges to the steady-state line $\dfrac{x}{\pi}$."}

<div class="spiegazione">

The affine function $p$ takes the prescribed boundary values. It is independent of time and has zero second derivative, so it also solves the heat equation. Subtracting it gives a homogeneous boundary value problem with the same operator. The remaining initial profile has only two modes and vanishes at the endpoints, so it is compatible with the conditions on $v$.

The second derivatives of the sines are $-\sin x$ and $-4\sin(2x)$, respectively. Since the sines are linearly independent, the two time coefficients satisfy separate equations with initial values $2$ and $-1$. Each solution multiplies its initial coefficient by a positive exponential, so the second coefficient remains negative. Their sum is smooth, reproduces the initial data, and satisfies the boundary conditions at all times. Uniqueness follows from the energy estimate for the difference with zero data. As $t$ increases, the exponentials tend to zero uniformly when multiplied by their bounded spatial profiles.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Solve $u_t=u_{xx}+3\sin(2x)$ on $(0,\pi)$ with zero endpoint values and $u(0,x)=0$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
u(t,x)&=T(t)\sin(2x), \\
T'(t)\sin(2x)&=-4T(t)\sin(2x)+3\sin(2x), \\
T'(t)+4T(t)&=3,\qquad T(0)=0, \\
\frac{d}{dt}\bigl(e^{4t}T(t)\bigr)&=3e^{4t}, \\
e^{4t}T(t)&=\int_0^t3e^{4s}\,ds, \\
&=\frac{3}{4}(e^{4t}-1), \\
T(t)&=\frac{3}{4}(1-e^{-4t}), \\
u(t,x)&=\frac{3}{4}(1-e^{-4t})\sin(2x).
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The source is proportional to the second spatial mode. This mode vanishes at the endpoints and its second derivative retains its shape, so multiplication by a time coefficient satisfies the boundary conditions and allows the sine coefficients to be equated. The zero initial condition gives $T(0)=0$. The second derivative introduces the coefficient $-4$, while the source contributes the constant three.

The integrating factor converts the left-hand side of the scalar equation into the derivative of a product. On integrating from zero, the zero initial value removes the integration constant, leaving the integral of an exponential. The result is smooth and satisfies the initial and boundary data. Substituting the coefficient found, the sum of $-4T$ and the scalar source three equals $3e^{-4t}$, which also equals $T'$. The coefficient tends to $\dfrac{3}{4}$ as the exponential decays, so the solution approaches the steady profile that balances the source.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Solve $u_{tt}=4u_{xx}$ on $(0,\pi)$ with fixed endpoints, $u(0,x)=\sin x$, and $u_t(0,x)=3\sin(2x)$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
u(t,x)&=T_1(t)\sin x+T_2(t)\sin(2x), \\
T_1''+4T_1&=0,\qquad T_1(0)=1,\quad T_1'(0)=0, \\
T_2''+16T_2&=0,\qquad T_2(0)=0,\quad T_2'(0)=3, \\
T_1(t)&=A_1\cos(2t)+B_1\sin(2t), \\
T_2(t)&=A_2\cos(4t)+B_2\sin(4t), \\
A_1&=1,\qquad 2B_1=0, \\
A_2&=0,\qquad 4B_2=3, \\
u(t,x)&=\cos(2t)\sin x+\frac{3}{4}\sin(4t)\sin(2x).
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Both initial functions vanish at the endpoints, so their modes satisfy the fixed-end conditions. The first sine has eigenvalue one and the second eigenvalue four for the operator $-\partial_{xx}$. Multiplication by the coefficient four in the equation gives the squared angular frequencies, four and sixteen. Linearity and independence of the two sines permit the scalar equations to be imposed separately.

The initial displacement determines the cosine coefficients because the time-dependent sines vanish at zero. The initial velocity determines the sine coefficients because the cosines have derivative zero at zero. Differentiating the second sine introduces a factor four, so its coefficient is $\dfrac{3}{4}$. The sum of the two modes is smooth, satisfies the boundary conditions, and reproduces both initial functions. The energy identity applied to the difference with zero initial data proves uniqueness.

</div>
