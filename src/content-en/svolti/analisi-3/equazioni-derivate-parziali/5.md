## Exercise 1

Solve $-u''=1$ on $(0,1)$ with $u(0)=u(1)=0$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
u''(x)&=-1, \\
u'(x)&=-x+C, \\
u(x)&=-\frac{x^2}{2}+Cx+D, \\
u(0)&=D=0, \\
u(1)&=-\frac{1}{2}+C=0, \\
C&=\frac{1}{2}, \\
u(x)&=-\frac{x^2}{2}+\frac{x}{2}, \\
&=\frac{1}{2}x(1-x).
\end{aligned}
$$

::grafico{f="0.5*x*(1-x);x*(1-x)" xmin=0 xmax=1 ymin=0 ymax=0.31 nomi="x,u" punti="0,0,pieno;1,0,pieno;0.5,0.125,pieno;0.5,0.25,pieno" legenda="\dfrac12x(1-x);x(1-x)" titolo="Both Poisson problems have zero boundary values at the endpoints. Doubling the source doubles the solution and its maximum."}

<div class="spiegazione">

The equation prescribes the curvature of $u$ as a negative constant. Integrating once gives the slope up to a constant $C$ representing $u'(0)$. The second integration introduces $D$ representing $u(0)$. The zero left boundary condition immediately gives $D=0$. At the right endpoint the linear term must offset the quadratic term at $x=1$.

The factored expression shows both zeros and positivity in the interior. The solution is a polynomial, hence belongs to $C^2([0,1])$ and satisfies the equation classically. The difference between two classical solutions would have zero second derivative and would therefore be affine. With zero values at both endpoints it must vanish identically. The graph also shows the maximum at $x=dfrac{1}{2}$, where the slope vanishes.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Solve $-u''=2$ on $(0,1)$ with $u(0)=u(1)=0$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
u''(x)&=-2, \\
u'(x)&=-2x+C, \\
u(x)&=-x^2+Cx+D, \\
u(0)&=D=0, \\
u(1)&=-1+C=0, \\
C&=1, \\
u(x)&=-x^2+x, \\
&=x(1-x).
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Isolating the second derivative leaves a constant right-hand side. The first integration gives an affine slope with coefficient $-2$, and the next gives a parabola with quadratic coefficient $-1$. The endpoint data determine the constants in order. The value at zero fixes the constant term, and the value at one then fixes the linear coefficient.

The product $x(1-x)$ shows that $u$ is positive inside the interval and zero at both ends. Its second derivative is $-2$ throughout, verifying the equation. The difference between any two solutions would be affine with zero endpoint values, hence identically zero. The slope $1-2x$ vanishes at $x=dfrac{1}{2}$ and changes from positive to negative. Thus the maximum is $dfrac{1}{4}$, twice the value for a unit source, as linearity predicts.

</div>
