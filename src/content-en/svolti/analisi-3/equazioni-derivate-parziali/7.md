## Exercise 1

On the real line, solve $u_{tt}=u_{xx}$ with $u(0,x)=x^2$ and $u_t(0,x)=0$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
u(t,x)&=\frac{(x-t)^2+(x+t)^2}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{x-t}^{x+t}0\,ds, \\
&=\frac{(x-t)^2+(x+t)^2}{2}, \\
&=\frac{x^2-2xt+t^2+x^2+2xt+t^2}{2}, \\
&=\frac{2x^2+2t^2}{2}, \\
&=x^2+t^2.
\end{aligned}
$$

::grafico{f="x^2;x^2+1;x^2+4" xmin=-2 xmax=2 ymin=0 ymax=8.5 nomi="x,u" legenda="u(0,x);u(1,x);u(2,x)" titolo="With wave speed $1$ and quadratic initial data, the profiles at times $0$, $1$, and $2$ are parabolas with the same curvature, shifted upward by $t^2$."}

<div class="spiegazione">

The coefficient of $u_{xx}$ is one, so the positive speed in d'Alembert's formula is one. The initial displacement is $C^2$ and the initial velocity is $C^1$ on the entire real line. Thus the formula applies to the Cauchy problem without boundary conditions. At time zero the two characteristic endpoints are $x-t$ and $x+t$. The integral of the initial velocity vanishes, leaving the average of the displacements at those points.

Expanding the squares produces opposite cross terms, which cancel. The two terms in $x^2$ and the two terms in $t^2$ add before division by two. At time zero we recover $x^2$ and the time derivative $2t$ vanishes. The second derivatives in $t$ and $x$ are both two, verifying the equation. At each fixed time the profile keeps its initial curvature and its vertex has vertical coordinate $t^2$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

On the real line, solve $u_{tt}=4u_{xx}$ with $u(0,x)=x^2$ and $u_t(0,x)=0$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
c&=\sqrt{4}=2, \\
u(t,x)&=\frac{(x-2t)^2+(x+2t)^2}{2}+\frac{1}{4}\int_{x-2t}^{x+2t}0\,ds, \\
&=\frac{(x-2t)^2+(x+2t)^2}{2}, \\
&=\frac{x^2-4xt+4t^2+x^2+4xt+4t^2}{2}, \\
&=\frac{2x^2+8t^2}{2}, \\
&=x^2+4t^2.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

In the wave equation the spatial coefficient is the square of the speed. The positive square root of four is two, so over time $t$ the two characteristic feet are at distance $2t$ from $x$. The initial displacement is a polynomial and the initial velocity vanishes identically. Both have the regularity required for d'Alembert's formula. The integral term vanishes even though the integration interval has length $4t$.

Averaging the squares retains only even powers of the shift $2t$. The two products of $x$ with the shift have opposite signs and cancel, while the squared shifts add. The resulting profile has $u(0,x)=x^2$ and $u_t(0,x)=0$ because the added term is quadratic in $t$. Moreover, $u_{tt}=8$ and $u_{xx}=2$, so $u_{tt}=4u_{xx}$ holds. The spatial curvature stays at its initial value while the vertex height grows as $4t^2$.

</div>
