## Exercise 1

Find the harmonic conjugate $v$ of $u(x,y)=x^2-y^2$ on $\mathbb R^2$, subject to $v(0,0)=0$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
u_x&=2x, \qquad u_y=-2y\\[6pt]
u_{xx}+u_{yy}&=2-2\\[6pt]
&=0\\[6pt]
v_y&=u_x\\[6pt]
&=2x\\[6pt]
v(x,y)&=2xy+C(x)\\[6pt]
v_x&=2y+C'(x)\\[6pt]
v_x=-u_y&\Longrightarrow C'(x)=0\\[6pt]
v(0,0)&=C(0)=0\\[6pt]
v(x,y)&=2xy
\end{aligned}
$$

::grafico{uguale=1 nomi="x,y" xmin=-2.6 xmax=2.6 ymin=-2.6 ymax=2.6 f="x;-x" segmenti="-2.3,0,2.3,0;0,-2.3,0,2.3" legenda="y=x;y=-x" titolo="The diagonals form the zero level set of $u$, and the axes form the zero level set of $v$."}

<div class="spiegazione">

The second derivatives of $u$ are continuous and its Laplacian vanishes, so $u$ is harmonic. The plane is simply connected, so the harmonic conjugate theorem gives a global conjugate. To find it, use $v_y=u_x$ and integrate with respect to $y$, holding $x$ fixed. Two antiderivatives with respect to $y$ may differ by a quantity that varies between vertical lines, giving the term $C(x)$.

The second Cauchy–Riemann equation equates $v_x=2y+C'(x)$ with $-u_y=2y$. Equality for every $(x,y)$ implies $C'(x)=0$ on $\mathbb R$, so $C$ is constant. The condition at the origin sets this constant to zero. The resulting function has continuous partial derivatives and satisfies both equations. Hence $u+iv$ is holomorphic, as can also be checked by expanding $(x+iy)^2$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Find the harmonic conjugate $v$ of $u(x,y)=2(x^2-y^2)$ on $\mathbb R^2$, subject to $v(0,0)=0$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
u_x&=4x, \qquad u_y=-4y\\[6pt]
\Delta u&=4-4\\[6pt]
&=0\\[6pt]
v_y&=4x\\[6pt]
v(x,y)&=4xy+C(x)\\[6pt]
v_x&=4y+C'(x)\\[6pt]
-u_y&=4y\\[6pt]
C'(x)&=0\\[6pt]
C(0)&=v(0,0)=0\\[6pt]
v(x,y)&=4xy
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The factor $2$ in the real part scales both second derivatives by the same amount, so their contributions to the Laplacian still cancel. Since $u$ is harmonic on the plane and the domain is simply connected, the topological hypothesis for a global conjugate also holds. The equation $v_y=4x$ requires that along each vertical line $v$ be affine in $y$ with slope $4x$. Before applying the second equation, the term $C(x)$ gives the value of the conjugate on the real axis.

The identity $v_x=-u_y$ shows that the derivative of $C$ vanishes, so it is constant along the entire axis. The normalization $v(0,0)=0$ determines the constant. We can check the resulting function directly using $v_x=4y$ and $v_y=4x$. It is the imaginary part of $2z^2$, whose real part is precisely the given function.

</div>
