## Exercise 1

Determine where $f(z)=|z|^2$ is complex differentiable and compute its derivative there.

$$
\begin{aligned}
u&=x^2+y^2, \qquad v=0\\[6pt]
u_x&=2x, \qquad v_y=0\\[6pt]
u_y&=2y, \qquad v_x=0\\[6pt]
u_x=v_y,\quad u_y=-v_x&\Longleftrightarrow x=y=0\\[6pt]
\frac{f(h)-f(0)}{h}&=\frac{h\overline h}{h} \qquad(h\ne0)\\[6pt]
&=\overline h\\[6pt]
\left|\frac{f(h)-f(0)}h\right|&=|h|\longrightarrow0\\[6pt]
f'(0)&=0
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The function is real-valued, so the imaginary part and all its derivatives vanish identically. If the complex derivative exists, the Cauchy–Riemann equations require both partial derivatives of the real part to vanish. The first gives $x=0$ and the second $y=0$, leaving only the origin to examine.

In the difference quotient at zero, use the identity $|h|^2=h\overline h$. For $h\ne0$, division by $h$ gives the conjugate of $h$. Its modulus is $|h|$, so the quotient tends to zero along all paths. The sufficient Cauchy–Riemann criterion also applies directly because both components are polynomials and hence real differentiable. The origin is an isolated point of complex differentiability because every neighborhood contains points where at least one necessary condition fails.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Determine where $f(z)=|z|^4$ is complex differentiable and compute its derivative there.

$$
\begin{aligned}
u&=(x^2+y^2)^2, \qquad v=0\\[6pt]
u_x&=4x(x^2+y^2), \qquad v_y=0\\[6pt]
u_y&=4y(x^2+y^2), \qquad v_x=0\\[6pt]
u_x=u_y=0&\Longleftrightarrow x=y=0\\[6pt]
\frac{f(h)-f(0)}h&=\frac{(h\overline h)^2}h \qquad(h\ne0)\\[6pt]
&=h\overline h^2\\[6pt]
|h\overline h^2|&=|h|^3\longrightarrow0\\[6pt]
f'(0)&=0
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Apply the chain rule to differentiate the real part. At every nonzero point the factor $x^2+y^2$ is positive. Thus for $u_x$ and $u_y$ to vanish together, both coordinates would have to be zero. Since the derivatives of the imaginary part are zero, the necessary Cauchy–Riemann conditions can hold only at the origin.

To check that point directly, use $f(0)=0$. Write $|h|^4=(h\overline h)^2$ and cancel a factor $h$ in the difference quotient, which is valid for every nonzero increment. The modulus of the quotient is $|h|^3$ and tends to zero uniformly in the argument of $h$. Thus the complex derivative at the origin exists and equals zero. The components are real differentiable throughout the plane, so the sufficient criterion gives the same conclusion at this point.

</div>
