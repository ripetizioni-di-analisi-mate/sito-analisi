## Exercise 1

Solve the equation $e^z=-e$ in $\mathbb C$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
z&=x+iy\\[6pt]
e^{x+iy}&=e^x(\cos y+i\sin y)\\[6pt]
|e^{x+iy}|&=e^x\\[6pt]
e^x&=|-e|=e\\[6pt]
x&=1\\[6pt]
\cos y+i\sin y&=-1\\[6pt]
y&=(2m+1)\pi, \qquad m\in\mathbb Z\\[6pt]
z&=1+i(2m+1)\pi, \qquad m\in\mathbb Z
\end{aligned}
$$

::grafico{uguale=1 nomi="Re,Im" xmin=-1.6 xmax=1.6 ymin=-1.6 ymax=1.6 curve="cos(t),sin(t),0,6.283185307179586" punti="-1,0,pieno,−1" vettori="0,0,-1,0" titolo="The trigonometric factor equals $-1$ for angles congruent to $\pi$."}

<div class="spiegazione">

Write the unknown in terms of its real part $x$ and imaginary part $y$. In the exponential formula the trigonometric factor has modulus one, while $e^x$ is positive real. Since the right-hand side has modulus $e$, equality of moduli requires $e^x=e$. The real exponential is strictly increasing, so $x=1$ is the only possible real part.

After division by $e$, we must determine which points of the unit circle correspond to $-1$. The sine must be zero and the cosine must equal $-1$. The first condition gives the integer multiples of $\pi$, and the second selects the odd multiples. For each integer $m$ these values give the trigonometric factor $-1$, so they satisfy the original equation. Periodicity in the imaginary part adds $2\pi i$ to a solution, while the modulus fixes the real part.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Solve the equation $e^z=-e^2$ in $\mathbb C$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
|e^{x+iy}|&=|-e^2|\\[6pt]
e^x&=e^2\\[6pt]
x&=\log(e^2)\\[6pt]
&=2\\[6pt]
\frac{e^{x+iy}}{e^2}&=\cos y+i\sin y\\[6pt]
\cos y+i\sin y&=-1\\[6pt]
y&=\pi+2m\pi, \qquad m\in\mathbb Z\\[6pt]
z&=2+i(2m+1)\pi, \qquad m\in\mathbb Z
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The exponential of $x+iy$ has modulus $e^x$, so the given modulus $e^2$ determines $x$ through the real logarithm. Both moduli are strictly positive, which justifies taking logarithms. Dividing by the modulus reduces the equation to equality of two points on the unit circle. The normalized right-hand side is $-1$, so the argument must be congruent to $\pi$ modulo $2\pi$.

The two trigonometric conditions select the entire congruence class, including negative angles such as $-\pi$. For example, $2-i\pi$ and $2+i\pi$ give the same value because their imaginary parts differ by a period. For every integer $m$, the cosine of $(2m+1)\pi$ equals $-1$ and its sine is zero. The modulus remains $e^2$, so every substitution gives $-e^2$. This confirms that the family found is exactly the solution set.

</div>
