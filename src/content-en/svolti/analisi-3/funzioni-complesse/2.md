## Exercise 1

For which real values of the parameters $a,b$ is $f(x+iy)=x^2-ay^2+ibxy$ holomorphic on $\mathbb C$?

$$
\begin{aligned}
u&=x^2-ay^2, \qquad v=bxy\\[6pt]
u_x&=2x, \qquad v_y=bx\\[6pt]
u_y&=-2ay, \qquad -v_x=-by\\[6pt]
u_x=v_y&\Longleftrightarrow(2-b)x=0\\[6pt]
u_y=-v_x&\Longleftrightarrow(b-2a)y=0\\[6pt]
b&=2\\[6pt]
a&=\frac b2\\[6pt]
&=1\\[6pt]
f(x+iy)&=x^2-y^2+2ixy\\[6pt]
&=(x+iy)^2
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Separate the real part $u$ and the imaginary part $v$ of $f$. Both are polynomials in two variables and hence have continuous partial derivatives throughout the plane. The **Cauchy–Riemann criterion** therefore reduces holomorphicity to two identities that must hold at every point. The first identity contains the factor $x$. Evaluating it at a point with $x=1$ gives $b=2$. Evaluating the second identity at a point with $y=1$ gives $2a=b$, so $a=1$.

Choosing nonzero coordinates determines the coefficients, since on the axes one of the two identities may hold without imposing any condition. Substituting the values obtained makes both equations identities for every pair $(x,y)$. Sufficiency now follows from the regularity already established. We also have $f(z)=z^2$, whose polynomial form confirms holomorphicity throughout the plane.

</div>

## Exercise 2

For which real values of the parameters $a,b$ is $f(x+iy)=2x^2-ay^2+ibxy$ holomorphic on $\mathbb C$?

$$
\begin{aligned}
u&=2x^2-ay^2, \qquad v=bxy\\[6pt]
u_x&=4x, \qquad v_y=bx\\[6pt]
u_y&=-2ay, \qquad -v_x=-by\\[6pt]
u_x-v_y&=(4-b)x\\[6pt]
u_y+v_x&=(b-2a)y\\[6pt]
4-b=0&\Longrightarrow b=4\\[6pt]
b-2a=0&\Longrightarrow a=2\\[6pt]
f(x+iy)&=2x^2-2y^2+4ixy\\[6pt]
&=2(x+iy)^2
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

For holomorphicity throughout the plane, both differences in the calculation must vanish. Since $u$ and $v$ are of class $C^1$ everywhere, this condition is also sufficient. The function $x\mapsto(4-b)x$ vanishes identically only if its coefficient is zero, giving $b=4$. Similarly, the coefficient of $y$ in the second expression must vanish, so $2a=b=4$.

Differentiating $2x^2$ with respect to $x$ gives a factor $4$, while differentiating $-ay^2$ with respect to $y$ gives the factor $-2a$. This explains why $b$ appears with the same value in the two conditions and why both must be checked. With these parameters the real part is $2(x^2-y^2)$ and the imaginary part is $4xy$, giving $2z^2$. The verification holds throughout $\mathbb C$ as required, including on the axes.

</div>
