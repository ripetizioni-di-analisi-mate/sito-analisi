## Exercise 1

Determine whether $\displaystyle\lim_{z\to0}\dfrac{(\operatorname{Re}z)^2}{|z|^2}$ exists.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\gamma_1(t)&=t, \qquad \gamma_2(t)=it \qquad(t>0)\\[6pt]
\frac{(\operatorname{Re}\gamma_1(t))^2}{|\gamma_1(t)|^2}
&=\frac{t^2}{t^2}\\[6pt]
&=1\\[6pt]
\frac{(\operatorname{Re}\gamma_2(t))^2}{|\gamma_2(t)|^2}
&=\frac{0}{t^2}\\[6pt]
&=0\\[6pt]
\lim_{z\to0}\frac{(\operatorname{Re}z)^2}{|z|^2}
&\quad\text{does not exist}
\end{aligned}
$$

::grafico{uguale=1 nomi="Re,Im" xmin=-1.4 xmax=3 ymin=-1.4 ymax=3 vettori="2.6,0,0.5,0;0,2.5,0,0.5" etichette="1.6,-0.5,value 1;0.75,1.8,value 0" titolo="The quotient is constant along each ray, with a different value on each."}

<div class="spiegazione">

The quotient is defined on the punctured plane, so both chosen rays lie in the domain for $t>0$. If the complex limit exists, it must be the same along every path in the domain approaching the origin. On the positive real axis the real part is $t$ and the modulus is $t$, so their squares agree and the quotient always equals one. On the positive imaginary axis the real part is zero and the modulus is again $t$, so the quotient always equals zero.

The two values remain distinct as $t$ tends to zero. Since a limit would have to equal both, it does not exist. In polar coordinates the formula $\dfrac{(r\cos\theta)^2}{r^2}=\cos^2\theta$ for $r>0$ shows the same behavior. The radius cancels, so dependence on the angle persists as the point approaches the origin. The two rays in the figure correspond to $\theta=0$ and $\theta=\dfrac{\pi}{2}$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Show that $g(z)=\dfrac{\overline z^2}{z}$ for $z\ne0$ has a continuous extension to zero. Is the extension complex differentiable there?

$$
\begin{aligned}
|g(z)|&=\frac{|\overline z|^2}{|z|}\\[6pt]
&=|z|\longrightarrow0\\[6pt]
g(0)&=0\\[6pt]
\frac{g(h)-g(0)}h&=\frac{\overline h^2}{h^2}\\[6pt]
&=e^{-4i\theta}\qquad(h=re^{i\theta},\ r>0)\\[6pt]
\theta=0&\Longrightarrow e^{-4i\theta}=1\\[6pt]
\theta=\frac\pi4&\Longrightarrow e^{-4i\theta}=-1\\[6pt]
g'(0)&\quad\text{does not exist}
\end{aligned}
$$

::grafico{uguale=1 nomi="Re,Im" xmin=-1.2 xmax=3 ymin=-1.2 ymax=3 vettori="2.5,0,0.5,0;1.75,1.75,0.35,0.35" etichette="1.75,-0.48,quotient 1;1.65,2.1,quotient −1" titolo="The modulus of the function tends to zero along both paths. The difference quotients have opposite values."}

<div class="spiegazione">

The conjugate has the same modulus as $z$, so dividing by $|z|$ leaves the factor $|z|$. The bound is an equality at every point of the domain, implying that the limit of $g$ at zero is zero. Assigning this value at the origin gives the unique continuous extension.

To test differentiability, divide once more by the increment $h$. The conjugate of $re^{i\theta}$ is $re^{-i\theta}$, so division cancels $r^2$ and subtracts the angular exponents. The difference quotient depends only on the argument and is therefore constant along each fixed ray. It equals $1$ on the positive real axis, but equals $-1$ on the ray at angle $\dfrac{\pi}{4}$. Both paths approach the same point of the extended domain, so these different values rule out a derivative. On both positive coordinate axes the quotient would equal $1$ because substituting $\dfrac{\pi}{2}$ into the exponent gives a full period. The second ray was chosen to distinguish the values.

</div>

## Exercise 3

For $z=x+iy\ne0$, set $f(z)=\dfrac{xy^2}{x^2+y^2}$ and define $f(0)=0$. Check continuity and determine whether $f$ is complex differentiable at zero.

$$
\begin{aligned}
|f(z)|&=|x|\frac{y^2}{x^2+y^2}\\[6pt]
&\le|x|\\[6pt]
&\le|z|\longrightarrow0\\[6pt]
\frac{f(t)-f(0)}t&=0\qquad(t\ne0)\\[6pt]
f\bigl(t(1+i)\bigr)&=\frac{t^3}{2t^2}\\[6pt]
&=\frac t2\\[6pt]
\frac{f(t(1+i))-f(0)}{t(1+i)}&=\frac1{2(1+i)}\\[6pt]
&=\frac{1-i}{4}\\[6pt]
f'(0)&\quad\text{does not exist}
\end{aligned}
$$

::grafico{uguale=1 nomi="Re,Im" xmin=-1.3 xmax=3.2 ymin=-1.3 ymax=3.2 vettori="2.6,0,0.5,0;1.85,1.85,0.35,0.35" etichette="1.6,-0.5,quotient 0;2,2.58,quotient (1−i)/4" titolo="The real ray and the diagonal give different difference quotients."}

<div class="spiegazione">

The fraction $\dfrac{y^2}{x^2+y^2}$ lies between zero and one at every point other than the origin. Since $|x|\le |z|$ also holds, the bound applies along every approach. Hence the limit at zero agrees with the assigned value, and $f$ is continuous there. Elsewhere it is a quotient with nonzero denominator and is therefore continuous.

Along the real axis the numerator of $f$ vanishes because $y=0$, so the difference quotient is zero. Along the diagonal both coordinates equal $t$, giving $f(t+it)=\dfrac t2$ and a nonzero constant after division by the increment. These two values rule out complex differentiability at the origin. The function also vanishes on the imaginary axis, so the partial derivatives of its real part at the origin exist and are zero. This counterexample shows that the sufficient criterion requires both the Cauchy–Riemann equations and real differentiability.

</div>
