## Exercise 1

Compute $\operatorname{Log}\!\left(e\,e^{\frac{3\pi i}{4}}\right)$ using the principal argument in $(-\pi,\pi)$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
z&=e\,e^{\frac{3\pi i}{4}}\\[6pt]
|z|&=e\left|e^{\frac{3\pi i}{4}}\right|\\[6pt]
&=e\\[6pt]
-\pi&<\frac{3\pi}{4}<\pi\\[6pt]
\operatorname{Arg}z&=\frac{3\pi}{4}\\[6pt]
\operatorname{Log}z&=\log|z|+i\operatorname{Arg}z\\[6pt]
&=\log e+\frac{3\pi i}{4}\\[6pt]
&=1+\frac{3\pi i}{4}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The given number is in polar form with positive radius $e$. Since the exponential of a purely imaginary number has modulus one, the radius is exactly $|z|$. The angle $\dfrac{3\pi}{4}$ lies in the open interval for the principal argument and determines a point in the second quadrant. Therefore $z$ lies in the domain of $\operatorname{Log}$ because it is nonzero and lies off the negative real axis.

The real part of the principal logarithm is the natural logarithm of the radius, and its imaginary part is the argument just found. Substitution gives $1$ and $\dfrac{3\pi}{4}$, respectively. By Euler's formula, exponentiating the result gives $z$. Of all values obtained from $1$ by using congruent angles, only $\dfrac{3\pi}{4}$ lies in the prescribed interval. The principal branch therefore selects a unique value.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Compute $\operatorname{Log}\!\left(e^2e^{\frac{3\pi i}{4}}\right)$ using the principal argument in $(-\pi,\pi)$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
|e^2e^{\frac{3\pi i}{4}}|&=e^2\left|e^{\frac{3\pi i}{4}}\right|\\[6pt]
&=e^2\\[6pt]
\arg z&=\frac{3\pi}{4}+2m\pi, \qquad m\in\mathbb Z\\[6pt]
-\pi<\frac{3\pi}{4}+2m\pi<\pi&\Longleftrightarrow m=0\\[6pt]
\operatorname{Log}z&=\log(e^2)+i\operatorname{Arg}z\\[6pt]
&=2+\frac{3\pi i}{4}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The real factor $e^2$ is positive, so it scales the distance of $z$ from the origin without rotating the point. All possible arguments are therefore obtained by adding integer multiples of $2\pi$ to $\dfrac{3\pi}{4}$. A positive multiple takes the angle above $\pi$, while a negative one takes it below $-\pi$. Only the given angle remains, strictly inside the interval for this branch.

The point lies on the ray at angle $\dfrac{3\pi}{4}$ and therefore avoids the principal branch cut. The real part of the logarithm is calculated using the inverse of the real exponential on $(0,+\infty)$, giving $\log(e^2)=2$. The imaginary part remains $\dfrac{3\pi}{4}$. Increasing the radius from $e$ to $e^2$ adds one to the real part of the logarithm while leaving its imaginary part unchanged. This follows from the separate roles of the modulus and argument in polar form.

</div>
