## Exercise 1

For $f(z)=z^2$ at $z_0=1-i$, find the length scale factor, rotation, and area scale factor of the differential.

$$
\begin{aligned}
f'(z)&=2z\\[6pt]
a:=f'(1-i)&=2-2i\\[6pt]
Df(z_0)[h]&=ah\\[6pt]
Df(z_0)[1]&=2-2i\\[6pt]
Df(z_0)[i]&=(2-2i)i=2+2i\\[6pt]
|a|&=\sqrt{2^2+(-2)^2}\\[6pt]
&=2\sqrt{2}\\[6pt]
\operatorname{Arg}a&=-\frac\pi4\\[6pt]
\det Df(z_0)&=|a|^2\\[6pt]
&=8
\end{aligned}
$$

::grafico{uguale=1 nomi="Re,Im" xmin=-1.4 xmax=3.2 ymin=-3 ymax=3 vettori="0,0,2,-2;0,0,2,2" etichette="2.25,-2.35,Df(1);2.2,2.37,Df(i)" titolo="The two image vectors remain perpendicular and have the same length."}

<div class="spiegazione">

The function is holomorphic throughout the plane, so its real differential acts by complex multiplication by $a=f'(z_0)$. At the given point $a$ is nonzero, so the differential is invertible and represents a scaling and a rotation. The length scale factor is $|a|$, found by taking the square root of the sum of the squared components. The multiplier lies in the fourth quadrant and its components have equal absolute values. Hence its argument is $-\dfrac\pi4$, corresponding to a clockwise rotation.

Applying the differential to the coordinate vectors $1$ and $i$ gives $2-2i$ and $2+2i$. The two image vectors have equal length and are perpendicular because the dot product of their real coordinate vectors is $4-4=0$. Therefore the image of the unit square has area equal to the square of the length scale factor, namely $8$. The positive determinant confirms that orientation is preserved.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Describe the image of the half-plane $\operatorname{Re}z>0$ under $w=\dfrac{z-1}{z+1}$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
|z+1|^2-|z-1|^2&=(x+1)^2-(x-1)^2\\[6pt]
&=4x\\[6pt]
x>0&\Longrightarrow |w|<1\\[6pt]
w(z+1)&=z-1\\[6pt]
z(1-w)&=1+w\\[6pt]
z&=\frac{1+w}{1-w}\\[6pt]
&=\frac{(1+w)(1-\overline w)}{|1-w|^2}\\[6pt]
\operatorname{Re}z&=\frac{1-|w|^2}{|1-w|^2}>0\\[6pt]
f\bigl(\{\operatorname{Re}z>0\}\bigr)&=\{|w|<1\}
\end{aligned}
$$

::grafico{uguale=1 nomi="Re,Im" xmin=-2.2 xmax=3 ymin=-2 ymax=2.5 punti="-1,0,vuoto,−1;1,0,pieno,1;1.2,1.7,pieno,P;0,-1.25,pieno,Q" segmenti="-1,0,1.2,1.7;1,0,1.2,1.7;-1,0,0,-1.25;1,0,0,-1.25" titolo="The imaginary axis consists of the points equidistant from $1$ and $-1$. The point $P$ is closer to $1$."}

<div class="spiegazione">

The pole of the transformation is $-1$, so the denominator stays nonzero on the right half-plane. The modulus of $w$ is the ratio of the distances from $z$ to $1$ and $-1$. To compare them, subtract their squares. The terms in $y^2$ cancel, so the difference has the sign of $x$. For every $x>0$ the distance to $1$ is smaller than the distance to $-1$, giving $|w|<1$.

To show that the entire disk is attained, take any point satisfying $|w|<1$. The point differs from $1$, so we can solve the equation for $z$ by dividing by $1-w$. Multiplying numerator and denominator by the conjugate of $1-w$ gives a strictly positive real part. The resulting preimage belongs to the half-plane and maps to $w$ by construction. The two inclusions show that the image is exactly the open unit disk.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Find the harmonic conjugate of $u=e^x\cos y$ satisfying $v(0,0)=0$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
u_x&=e^x\cos y, \qquad u_y=-e^x\sin y\\[6pt]
\Delta u&=e^x\cos y-e^x\cos y\\[6pt]
&=0\\[6pt]
v_y&=u_x\\[6pt]
v(x,y)&=e^x\sin y+C(x)\\[6pt]
v_x&=e^x\sin y+C'(x)\\[6pt]
v_x=-u_y&\Longrightarrow C'(x)=0\\[6pt]
v(0,0)&=C(0)=0\\[6pt]
v(x,y)&=e^x\sin y\\[6pt]
u+iv&=e^{x+iy}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The function $u$ is of class $C^2$, and its Laplacian vanishes because the second derivative with respect to $x$ is $u$ and the one with respect to $y$ is $-u$. The plane is simply connected, so the harmonic conjugate theorem applies globally. Integrating $v_y=u_x$ with respect to $y$ gives $e^x\sin y$ with a term $C(x)$, which stays constant when only $y$ varies.

The derivative of the resulting function with respect to $x$ must be $-u_y$. Since the terms $e^x\sin y$ agree, equality requires $C'(x)=0$. The condition at the origin sets the constant to zero because $e^0\sin0=0$. The two components satisfy both Cauchy–Riemann equations and are of class $C^1$, so $u+iv$ is holomorphic on the plane. The complex exponential formula gives this same pair of components and identifies the function as $e^z$.

</div>

## Exercise 4

Show that $u(z)=\log|z|$ has no harmonic conjugate on the punctured plane.

$$
\begin{aligned}
u(x,y)&=\frac12\log(x^2+y^2)\\[6pt]
u_x&=\frac{x}{x^2+y^2},\qquad u_y=\frac{y}{x^2+y^2}\\[6pt]
dv&=-\frac{y}{x^2+y^2}\,dx+\frac{x}{x^2+y^2}\,dy\\[6pt]
\gamma(t)&=(\cos t,\sin t), \qquad 0\le t\le2\pi\\[6pt]
\frac{d}{dt}(v\circ\gamma)(t)&=\sin^2 t+\cos^2 t\\[6pt]
&=1\\[6pt]
v(\gamma(2\pi))-v(\gamma(0))&=\int_0^{2\pi}1\,dt\\[6pt]
&=2\pi\\[6pt]
\gamma(2\pi)=\gamma(0)&\Longrightarrow 0=2\pi\quad\text{(contradiction)}
\end{aligned}
$$

::grafico{uguale=1 nomi="Re,Im" xmin=-1.65 xmax=1.7 ymin=-1.65 ymax=1.65 curve="cos(t),sin(t),0,6.283185307179586" punti="1,0,pieno,start and finish;0,0,vuoto,origin excluded" vettori="1,0,1,0.36" titolo="The counterclockwise circle is a closed path around the origin, which lies outside the domain."}

<div class="spiegazione">

On the punctured plane $u$ is smooth and harmonic. The domain contains closed curves winding around the origin, so the simple connectivity hypothesis of the global theorem fails. To decide whether a conjugate nevertheless exists, suppose it is defined on the entire domain and use the Cauchy–Riemann equations to find its differential.

On the unit circle the denominator $x^2+y^2$ equals one. With the counterclockwise parametrization the two products in the chain rule become $\sin^2 t$ and $\cos^2 t$, so the derivative of $v\circ\gamma$ would equal one everywhere along the curve. Integration over one turn gives a change of $2\pi$. But the path ends where it began, so a single-valued function must have net change zero. This contradiction rules out a global conjugate. On any disk avoiding the origin the local existence theorem applies because the disk is simply connected.

</div>
