## Exercise 1

Compute $\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_0^1(1+x+\cdots+x^n)\,dx$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
s_n(x)&=\sum_{k=0}^{n}x^k \\
s_{n+1}(x)-s_n(x)&=x^{n+1}\ge0 && (0\le x<1) \\
s_n(x)&=\frac{1-x^{n+1}}{1-x} && (0\le x<1) \\
\lim_{n\to\infty}s_n(x)&=\frac1{1-x} && (0\le x<1) \\
\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_0^1 s_n(x)\,dx
&=\int_0^1\frac{dx}{1-x} \\
&=\lim_{b\uparrow1}[-\log(1-x)]_0^b \\
&=\lim_{b\uparrow1}[-\log(1-b)] \\
&=+\infty.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The partial sums $s_n$ are polynomials and therefore measurable. Every summand is nonnegative on $[0,1]$. Passing from $s_n$ to $s_{n+1}$ adds $x^{n+1}$, so the sums form an increasing sequence. The geometric sum formula gives the limit $\dfrac1{1-x}$ for $x<1$, since $x^{n+1}$ tends to zero. At $x=1$ the sum equals $n+1$, but the value at this one point does not affect the integral.

The hypotheses of the **monotone convergence theorem** hold. The limit of the integrals therefore equals the integral of the limit, even if infinite. The antiderivative on $[0,b]$ with $b<1$ is $-\log(1-x)$. As $b$ tends to one, $1-b$ tends to zero from the right and its logarithm tends to minus infinity. By another application of monotone convergence, the Lebesgue integral of the positive function equals the limit of these truncated integrals.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Compute $\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_0^1\dfrac{\sin(nx)}{1+n^2x^2}\,dx$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
f_n(x)&=\frac{\sin(nx)}{1+n^2x^2} \\
|f_n(x)|&\le\frac1{1+n^2x^2} \\
&\le1 && (0\le x\le1) \\
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac1{1+n^2x^2}&=0 && (x>0) \\
f_n(0)&=0 \\
\int_0^1 1\,dx&=1 \\
\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_0^1 f_n(x)\,dx
&=\int_0^1\lim_{n\to\infty} f_n(x)\,dx \\
&=\int_0^1 0\,dx \\
&=0.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The denominator is positive throughout the interval, so each $f_n$ is continuous and measurable. At fixed $x>0$, the term $n^2x^2$ tends to infinity. Since the absolute value of sine is at most one, the absolute value of the quotient is at most the reciprocal of the denominator. This reciprocal tends to zero, giving the pointwise limit by comparison. At zero the numerator vanishes for all indices, so the limit is zero there as well.

The first bound depends on $n$ and identifies the limit. The second, using the constant one, gives a common majorant. This function is integrable because the domain has length one. The **dominated convergence theorem** therefore applies even though the integrands change sign, since the majorant bounds their absolute values. The integral of the limiting function is zero.

</div>

## Exercise 3

For $f_n=\mathbf1_{(n,n+1)}$ on $\mathbb R$, compare $\liminf_n\int f_n$ with $\int\liminf_n f_n$ and determine whether there is a common integrable majorant.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_{\mathbb R}\mathbf1_{(n,n+1)}\,dm
&=(n+1)-n \\
&=1 \\
n>x&\ \Longrightarrow\ f_n(x)=0 \\
\liminf_{n\to\infty}f_n(x)&=0 \\
\int_{\mathbb R}\liminf_n f_n\,dm&=0 \\
\liminf_n\int_{\mathbb R}f_n\,dm&=1 \\
g\ge f_n\ \ \forall n
&\ \Longrightarrow\ \int_{\mathbb R}g\,dm
   \ge \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}m((n,n+1)) \\
&=+\infty.
\end{aligned}
$$

::grafico{linee="1,1 2,1;3,1 4,1;5,1 6,1" punti="1,1,vuoto;2,1,vuoto;3,1,vuoto;4,1,vuoto;5,1,vuoto;6,1,vuoto" etichette="1.5,1.17,n = 1;3.5,1.17,n = 3;5.5,1.17,n = 5" xmin=0 xmax=6.8 ymin=-0.2 ymax=1.55 titolo="Three terms of the sequence have the same area and occupy intervals that move to the right."}

<div class="spiegazione">

Each $f_n$ is the indicator of a measurable interval of length one and is therefore measurable and nonnegative. Its integral is one independently of $n$. Fix a real point $x$. For all indices exceeding $x$ the rectangle lies to its right, so the function vanishes at that point. Hence the pointwise limit inferior is zero. **Fatou's lemma** applies and its inequality is strict.

Suppose $g$ is integrable and dominates each $f_n$ almost everywhere. The countable union of the exceptional null sets is null. Outside that union $g$ is at least one on all the intervals $(n,n+1)$ and at least zero elsewhere. Since the intervals are disjoint, their union has measure equal to the sum of infinitely many ones. The integral of $g$ would then be infinite, contradicting integrability.

</div>
