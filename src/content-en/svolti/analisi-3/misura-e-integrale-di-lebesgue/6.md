## Exercise 1

Compute $\int_0^\infty\int_0^\infty e^{-x-2y}\,dy\,dx$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_0^\infty e^{-x-2y}\,dy
&=e^{-x}\lim_{R\to\infty}\left[-\frac{e^{-2y}}{2}\right]_0^R \\
&=e^{-x}\lim_{R\to\infty}\frac{1-e^{-2R}}{2} \\
&=\frac{e^{-x}}{2} \\
\int_0^\infty\int_0^\infty e^{-x-2y}\,dy\,dx
&=\frac12\int_0^\infty e^{-x}\,dx \\
&=\frac12\lim_{R\to\infty}(1-e^{-R}) \\
&=\frac12.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The integrand is continuous and positive on the quadrant, so it is measurable and nonnegative. Each half-line is a countable union of intervals of finite measure, so both measure spaces are sigma-finite. **Tonelli's theorem** then identifies the integral on the quadrant with an iterated integral. Its hypotheses hold before we know whether the integral is finite.

In the inner integral $x$ remains fixed. The factor $e^{-x}$ therefore comes outside the integral in $y$, leaving the antiderivative of $e^{-2y}$ to be evaluated. Its limit at infinity is zero and its value at zero is $-\dfrac12$. Thus the inner integral is proportional to $e^{-x}$. Integrating next in $x$, the antiderivative $-e^{-x}$ gives an integral of one on the half-line. The product of the two contributions is one half. In the graph, sections at fixed $y$ show the same exponential factor in $x$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Compute $\int_0^\infty\int_0^\infty e^{-2x-3y}\,dy\,dx$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_0^\infty e^{-2x-3y}\,dx
&=e^{-3y}\lim_{R\to\infty}\left[-\frac{e^{-2x}}{2}\right]_0^R \\
&=e^{-3y}\lim_{R\to\infty}\frac{1-e^{-2R}}{2} \\
&=\frac{e^{-3y}}{2} \\
\int_0^\infty\int_0^\infty e^{-2x-3y}\,dy\,dx
&=\int_0^\infty\left(\int_0^\infty e^{-2x-3y}\,dx\right)dy \\
&=\frac12\lim_{R\to\infty}\left[-\frac{e^{-3y}}{3}\right]_0^R \\
&=\frac12\cdot\frac13 \\
&=\frac16.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Both half-lines have sigma-finite measure, and the product of the exponentials is measurable and positive. By **Tonelli's theorem**, the given integral equals the iterated integral with respect to $x$ first. Nonnegativity justifies interchanging the order before computing the integral of the absolute value.

For fixed $y$, write the exponential as the product of $e^{-2x}$ and $e^{-3y}$. The latter factor is independent of the inner variable, so it multiplies the difference of the endpoint values of the antiderivative. This difference equals $\dfrac12$, since the antiderivative vanishes at infinity and equals $-\dfrac12$ at zero. After the inner integration, the factor depending on $y$ has exponent $-3y$. Evaluating the antiderivative at its endpoints gives $\dfrac13$. Multiplying the two values gives the integral on the quadrant. The integrand equals its absolute value and the result is finite, so it also belongs to $L^1$.

</div>
