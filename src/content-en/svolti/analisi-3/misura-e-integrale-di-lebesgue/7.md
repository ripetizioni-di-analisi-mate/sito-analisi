## Exercise 1

Compute $\int_0^\infty x e^{-x}\,dx$ by differentiating the formula $\int_0^\infty e^{-ax}dx=\dfrac1a$ with respect to $a$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
F(a)&=\int_0^\infty e^{-ax}\,dx=\frac1a && (a>0) \\
\partial_a e^{-ax}&=-xe^{-ax} \\
|\partial_a e^{-ax}|&\le xe^{-\frac{x}{2}} && \left(\frac12\le a\le\frac32\right) \\
\int_0^\infty xe^{-\frac{x}{2}}\,dx
&=\left[-(2x+4)e^{-\frac{x}{2}}\right]_0^\infty \\
&=4 \\
-\int_0^\infty xe^{-ax}\,dx&=F'(a) \\
&=-\frac1{a^2} \\
\int_0^\infty xe^{-x}\,dx&=1.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The function $F$ is defined for $a>0$ since the exponential is integrable on the half-line for each such parameter. For fixed $x$, the integrand is differentiable in $a$. For fixed $a$, it is continuous and therefore measurable in $x$. To justify differentiation at $a=1$, it suffices to bound the derivative on an interval containing one in its interior.

For $a$ between $\dfrac12$ and $\dfrac32$, the exponent $-ax$ is at most $-\dfrac{x}{2}$ since $x>0$. Hence $x e^{-\frac{x}{2}}$ bounds the absolute derivative and is integrable by the stated antiderivative. The theorem on **differentiation under the integral sign** applies. Differentiating the integrand introduces a minus sign, and differentiating $\dfrac1a$ introduces another. The signs cancel before evaluation at $a=1$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Compute $\int_0^\infty x e^{-2x}\,dx$ by differentiating the formula $\int_0^\infty e^{-ax}dx=\dfrac1a$ with respect to $a$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
F(a)&=\int_0^\infty e^{-ax}\,dx=\frac1a &&(a>0) \\
\partial_a e^{-ax}&=-xe^{-ax} \\
|\partial_a e^{-ax}|&\le xe^{-x} && (1\le a\le3) \\
\int_0^\infty xe^{-x}\,dx
&=[-(x+1)e^{-x}]_0^\infty \\
&=1 \\
-\int_0^\infty xe^{-2x}\,dx&=F'(2) \\
&=-\frac1{2^2} \\
\int_0^\infty xe^{-2x}\,dx&=\frac14.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The derivative is required at parameter $2$. The interval $[1,3]$ lies in the domain $a>0$ and contains $2$ in its interior. Throughout this interval, $e^{-ax}\le e^{-x}$ for every $x>0$, so the same function $xe^{-x}$ bounds the absolute value of the derivative with respect to $a$. The graph compares the majorant with the absolute derivatives for $a=2$ and $a=3$.

The majorant is integrable, as follows from its antiderivative tending to zero at infinity. For every positive parameter the integrand is integrable in $x$, and for every $x>0$ it is differentiable in $a$. Thus the hypotheses for **differentiation under the integral sign** hold. Evaluating the derivative of $a\mapsto\dfrac1a$ at $2$ gives minus the required integral, since the derivative of the integrand is $-xe^{-ax}$.

</div>
