## Exercise 1

Compute $\int_D y\,dx\,dy$ over the triangle $D=\{(x,y):0<y<x<2\}$ by reversing the order of integration.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_D y\,dx\,dy
&=\int_0^2\left(\int_y^2 y\,dx\right)dy \\
&=\int_0^2(2y-y^2)\,dy \\
&=\left[y^2-\frac{y^3}{3}\right]_0^2 \\
&=4-\frac83 \\
&=\frac43.
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_D y\,dx\,dy
&=\int_0^2\left(\int_0^x y\,dy\right)dx \\
&=\int_0^2\frac{x^2}{2}\,dx \\
&=\left[\frac{x^3}{6}\right]_0^2 \\
&=\frac86 \\
&=\frac43.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The triangle $D$ is open, hence measurable. Also $y>0$ on $D$, so $y\mathbf1_D$ is measurable and nonnegative on the product of two real lines with Lebesgue measure, both sigma-finite. **Tonelli's theorem** permits either order of integration.

In the first order, fix $y$ between zero and two. The horizontal section begins on the diagonal $x=y$ and ends on the side $x=2$. The integrand is constant on this section, so the inner integral is $y$ times its length $2-y$. In the other order, fix $x$ between zero and two. The vertical section begins on the side $y=0$ and ends on the diagonal $y=x$. Now integrate $y$, whose antiderivative evaluated at the endpoints gives $x^2$ divided by two. The two outer integrals give the same value. The omitted sides have planar measure zero.

</div>

## Exercise 2

On $\mathbb N^2$, with $\mathbb N=\{1,2,\ldots\}$, determine whether $a_{ij}=2^{-i-j}(-1)^j$ is absolutely summable and find its sum.

$$
\begin{aligned}
|a_{ij}|&=2^{-i}2^{-j} \\
\sum_{i,j\ge1}|a_{ij}|
&=\left(\sum_{i\ge1}2^{-i}\right)\left(\sum_{j\ge1}2^{-j}\right) \\
&=\left(\frac{\frac12}{1-\frac12}\right)^2 \\
&=1.
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\sum_{i,j\ge1}a_{ij}
&=\left(\sum_{i\ge1}2^{-i}\right)
  \left(\sum_{j\ge1}\left(-\frac12\right)^j\right) \\
&=1\cdot\frac{-\frac12}{1+\frac12} \\
&=-\frac13.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The positive integers with counting measure are sigma-finite because they are a countable union of singletons of measure one. Every function on $\mathbb N^2$ is measurable for the power-set sigma-algebras. The absolute values of $a_{ij}$ are nonnegative and factor, so **Tonelli's theorem** allows summation in $j$ and then in $i$. For fixed $i$ the factor $2^{-i}$ is constant. Both geometric series of absolute values start at the first positive index and sum to one.

The sum of absolute values is finite, so the absolute integrability hypothesis of **Fubini's theorem** holds. For the signed terms the sum in $i$ is still one, whereas in $j$ the alternating factor gives a geometric series with ratio $-\dfrac12$. Its first term is $-\dfrac12$ since the index starts at one. Dividing by one minus the ratio gives $-\dfrac13$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Differentiate $H(t)=\int_0^1e^{-tx}\,dx$ at $t=0$ without using the explicit formula for $H$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
F(t,x)&=e^{-tx} \\
\partial_tF(t,x)&=-xe^{-tx} \\
|\partial_tF(t,x)|&\le xe^x && (|t|\le1) \\
\int_0^1 xe^x\,dx&=[(x-1)e^x]_0^1 \\
&=1 \\
H'(0)&=\int_0^1\partial_tF(0,x)\,dx \\
&=-\int_0^1x\,dx \\
&=-\left[\frac{x^2}{2}\right]_0^1 \\
&=-\frac12.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

For every real $t$, the function $x\mapsto e^{-tx}$ is continuous on $[0,1]$, so it is measurable and integrable. For every $x$ its derivative with respect to $t$ exists and equals $-xe^{-tx}$. On the parameter interval $[-1,1]$, we have $-tx\le x$ for $0\le x\le1$. Monotonicity of the exponential therefore gives $xe^x$ as a bound on the absolute derivative, independently of $t$.

The majorant is integrable, as is seen by evaluating its antiderivative $(x-1)e^x$ at both endpoints. The theorem on **differentiation under the integral sign** applies at $t=0$, an interior point of the chosen interval. At this parameter value the exponential factor in the derivative is one, so the integral reduces to that of $-x$. Its antiderivative is $-x^2$ divided by two. Evaluation gives a negative derivative, consistent with the fact that $e^{-tx}$ decreases as $t$ increases for $x>0$.

</div>

## Exercise 4

For $t>-1$, compute $K'(t)$ if $K(t)=\int_0^1\log(1+tx)\,dx$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
F(t,x)&=\log(1+tx) \\
\partial_tF(t,x)&=\frac{x}{1+tx} \\
1+tx&\ge\min(1,1+a)>0 && (a\le t\le b,\ 0\le x\le1) \\
|\partial_tF(t,x)|&\le\frac1{\min(1,1+a)} \\
K'(t)&=\int_0^1\frac{x}{1+tx}\,dx .
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{x}{1+tx}&=\frac1t\left(1-\frac1{1+tx}\right) && (t\ne0) \\
K'(t)&=\left[\frac{x}{t}-\frac{\log(1+tx)}{t^2}\right]_0^1 \\
&=\frac{1}{t}-\frac{\log(1+t)}{t^2} \\
&=\frac{t-\log(1+t)}{t^2}.
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
K'(0)&=\int_0^1x\,dx \\
&=\left[\frac{x^2}{2}\right]_0^1 \\
&=\frac12.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

For each $t>-1$ and $x\in[0,1]$ the argument of the logarithm is positive, so the integrand is continuous in $x$ and differentiable in $t$. Fix the parameter and choose a compact interval $[a,b]$ contained in $(-1,\infty)$ that has the parameter in its interior. For $a\ge0$, we have $1+tx\ge1$. For $a<0$ we have $tx\ge ax\ge a$, so the denominator is at least $1+a$. In both cases the derivative is bounded by a constant integrable on $[0,1]$.

The theorem on **differentiation under the integral sign** applies. When $t\ne0$, write the numerator as $\dfrac1t((1+tx)-1)$, then integrate the constant and the reciprocal of the affine function separately. Both contributions evaluated at zero vanish. For $t=0$ the decomposition would divide by zero, so use the derivative of the integrand directly. In that case it equals $x$.

</div>
