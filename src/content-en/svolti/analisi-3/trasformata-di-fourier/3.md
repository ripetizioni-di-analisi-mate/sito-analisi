## Exercise 1

For $f(x)=e^{-|x-1|}$, find $\widehat f(\xi)$.

Reduce the problem to the two-sided exponential $g(t)=e^{-|t|}$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\widehat f(\xi)&=\int_{\mathbb R}e^{-|x-1|}e^{-i\xi x}\,dx\\
&=\int_{\mathbb R}e^{-|t|}e^{-i\xi(t+1)}\,dt,\qquad t=x-1,\\
&=e^{-i\xi}\int_{\mathbb R}e^{-|t|}e^{-i\xi t}\,dt\\
&=e^{-i\xi}\widehat g(\xi)\\
&=\frac{2e^{-i\xi}}{1+\xi^2}.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Translation preserves the integral of the modulus, so $f$ and $g$ both belong to $L^1(\mathbb R)$. We may therefore substitute $t=x-1$ in the absolutely convergent integral. The domain remains the whole line and the differential is unchanged. Only $t$ remains inside the absolute value, while $x=t+1$ in the kernel gives the factor $e^{-i\xi}$, independent of $t$. The remaining integral is the transform of the exponential centered at the origin and equals $\dfrac2{1+\xi^2}$. The factor taken outside has modulus one because $\xi$ is real, so translation preserves the modulus of the transform. The sign of the phase change follows from the convention $e^{-i\xi x}$. At $\xi=0$ the phase factor is one, so the transform still has value two, equal to the integral of the shifted function.

</div>

## Exercise 2

For $f(x)=e^{-|x-2|}$, find $\widehat f(\xi)$.

Apply the translation formula to the even function $g(x)=e^{-|x|}$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
f(x)&=g(x-2),\\
\widehat f(\xi)&=e^{-2i\xi}\widehat g(\xi)\\
&=\frac{2}{1+\xi^2}\bigl(\cos(2\xi)-i\sin(2\xi)\bigr)\\
&=\frac{2e^{-2i\xi}}{1+\xi^2},\\[4pt]
|\widehat f(\xi)|&=\frac{2}{1+\xi^2}.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The given function is centered at $x=2$ and is therefore $g$ shifted two units to the right. The two-sided exponential belongs to $L^1$ by its decay on both half-lines, so the translation formula applies. The new phase factor is $-2\xi$ because the kernel has a negative sign in the exponent. Euler's formula gives the real and imaginary parts separately. The real part is proportional to $\cos(2\xi)$ and is even. The imaginary part is proportional to $-\sin(2\xi)$ and is odd, as expected for the transform of a real function. For example, at $\xi=\dfrac\pi4$ the phase factor is $-i$ and the transform is purely imaginary. The modulus remains that of $\widehat g$ because the squares of the sine and cosine of the same angle sum to one.

</div>
