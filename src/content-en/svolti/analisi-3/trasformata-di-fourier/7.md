## Exercise 1

For a Schwartz function $f$, express $\widehat{f'}(\xi)$ in terms of $\widehat f$.

Integrate by parts on $[-R,R]$ and let $R$ tend to infinity.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_{-R}^R f'(x)e^{-i\xi x}\,dx
&=\left[f(x)e^{-i\xi x}\right]_{-R}^R
-\int_{-R}^R f(x)(-i\xi)e^{-i\xi x}\,dx\\
&=f(R)e^{-i\xi R}-f(-R)e^{i\xi R}
+i\xi\int_{-R}^R f(x)e^{-i\xi x}\,dx,\\[4pt]
\widehat{f'}(\xi)&=0+i\xi\int_{\mathbb R} f(x)e^{-i\xi x}\,dx\\
&=i\xi\widehat f(\xi).
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

A Schwartz function and all its derivatives decay faster than any inverse power. In particular $f$ and $f'$ are integrable, so both integrals on $[-R,R]$ approach the integrals over the line. The boundary term is a difference of values of $f$ multiplied by exponentials of unit modulus. Since $f(R)$ and $f(-R)$ tend to zero, this term vanishes in the limit. In the integration by parts, differentiating the kernel with respect to $x$ gives the coefficient $-i\xi$. The minus sign in the formula turns it into $+i\xi$, independent of the integration variable. Passing to the limit therefore gives $i\xi$ times the transform of $f$. In particular the transform of $f'$ is zero at zero frequency. This equals the integral of $f'$ over the line, the difference of the two zero limits of $f$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

For a Schwartz function $f$, express $\widehat{f''}(\xi)$ in terms of $\widehat f$.

Apply the first-derivative formula to $f'$ and then to $f$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\widehat{f''}(\xi)&=\widehat{(f')'}(\xi)\\
&=i\xi\widehat{f'}(\xi)\\
&=i\xi\bigl(i\xi\widehat f(\xi)\bigr)\\
&=i^2\xi^2\widehat f(\xi)\\
&=-\xi^2\widehat f(\xi).
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The Schwartz class is closed under differentiation, so $f'$ and $f''$ are integrable as well. Since $f'$ tends to zero at both ends, the boundary term for the transform of $(f')'$ vanishes. The first application gives the factor $i\xi$ multiplying the transform of $f'$. To compute the latter, use the original hypothesis on $f$, which is itself integrable and tends to zero at both ends. The second factor is again $i\xi$, so their product is $-\xi^2$. The imaginary multiplier for the first derivative thus gives a negative real multiplier for the second. For example, if $\widehat f$ is real and even, so is $\widehat{f''}$. At zero frequency the multiplier vanishes, in agreement with the fact that the integral of $f''$ is the difference between the two limits of $f'$.

</div>
