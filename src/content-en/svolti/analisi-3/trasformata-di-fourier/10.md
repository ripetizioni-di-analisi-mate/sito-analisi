## Exercise 1

For $f=\mathbf1_{[-1,2]}$, find $f'$ as a distribution and compute its transform.

Evaluate the distributional derivative on a test function $\varphi\in\mathcal S(\mathbb R)$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\langle f',\varphi\rangle
&=-\langle f,\varphi'\rangle\\
&=-\int_{-1}^2\varphi'(x)\,dx\\
&=-\bigl(\varphi(2)-\varphi(-1)\bigr)\\
&=\langle\delta_{-1}-\delta_2,\varphi\rangle,\\[4pt]
f'&=\delta_{-1}-\delta_2,\\
\widehat{f'}(\xi)
&=\widehat{\delta_{-1}}(\xi)-\widehat{\delta_2}(\xi)\\
&=e^{i\xi}-e^{-2i\xi}.
\end{aligned}
$$

::grafico{linee="-1.8,0 -1,0;-1,1 2,1;2,0 2.8,0" vettori="-1,0,-1,1.45,+δ;2,0,2,-0.6,−δ" punti="-1,1,pieno;2,1,pieno" xmin=-1.85 xmax=2.85 ymin=-0.9 ymax=1.75 titolo="The jump is positive on entering the interval and negative on leaving it."}

<div class="spiegazione">

The integrable indicator defines a tempered distribution by integration against test functions. By definition the distributional derivative acts on a test function by applying the original distribution to its derivative with a minus sign. On the support the integrand is $\varphi'$, so the fundamental theorem of calculus gives the endpoint values. These are precisely the actions of the delta distributions at the two endpoints. The positive sign on the left corresponds to the jump from zero to one, and the negative sign on the right to the jump from one to zero. A delta at $a$ has transform $e^{-ia\xi}$. Substitution of $a=-1$ and $a=2$ gives the two stated terms. At zero frequency both exponentials equal one, so the derivative's transform vanishes, in agreement with the zero algebraic sum of the jumps.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Find the transform of the second distributional derivative of the delta at $a=1$.

For $\varphi\in\mathcal S(\mathbb R)$, apply the definition of the distributional derivative twice.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\langle\delta_1'',\varphi\rangle
&=-\langle\delta_1',\varphi'\rangle\\
&=\langle\delta_1,\varphi''\rangle\\
&=\varphi''(1),\\[4pt]
\widehat{\delta_1''}(\xi)
&=(i\xi)^2\widehat{\delta_1}(\xi)\\
&=(i\xi)^2e^{-i\xi}\\
&=-\xi^2e^{-i\xi}.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Evaluation at one is a continuous functional on the Schwartz class and defines the tempered distribution $\delta_1$. The derivative of a Schwartz test function is again Schwartz, so we may apply the distributional derivative definition twice. Each differentiation contributes a minus sign, giving positive sign in the final action on $\varphi''$. In frequency the differentiation formula remains valid for tempered distributions and gives a factor $i\xi$ each time. The product is $-\xi^2$ because $i^2=-1$. The original delta is shifted by one, introducing the phase $e^{-i\xi}$ in its transform. The quadratic factor multiplies this phase without changing the argument in its exponent. At zero frequency the result vanishes because the quadratic factor has a double zero. The transform has modulus $\xi^2$ because the phase has modulus one.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Solve $u_t=\dfrac12 u_{xx}$ on the line with initial data $u(0,x)=2e^{-2(x-1)^2}$ and compute the mass at every time.

Take the transform in $x$ and set $A(t)=1+4t$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\widehat u(0,\xi)&=2e^{-i\xi}\sqrt{\frac\pi2}e^{-\frac{\xi^2}8}\\
&=\sqrt{2\pi}\,e^{-i\xi}e^{-\frac{\xi^2}8},\\
\partial_t\widehat u(t,\xi)&=-\frac{\xi^2}2\widehat u(t,\xi),\\
\widehat u(t,\xi)&=e^{-\frac{t\xi^2}2}\widehat u(0,\xi)\\
&=\sqrt{2\pi}\,e^{-i\xi}e^{-\frac{A(t)\xi^2}8}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Identify the exponent in the transform of a Gaussian centered at $1$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
u(t,x)&=\frac{2}{\sqrt{A(t)}}e^{-\frac{2(x-1)^2}{A(t)}},\\
\int_{\mathbb R}u(t,x)\,dx&=\widehat u(t,0)\\
&=\sqrt{2\pi}.
\end{aligned}
$$

::grafico{f="2*exp(-2*(x-1)^2);sqrt(2)*exp(-(x-1)^2);2/sqrt(5)*exp(-2*(x-1)^2/5)" legenda="t=0;t=\frac14;t=1" xmin=-2.6 xmax=4.6 ymin=-0.1 ymax=2.22 titolo="Diffusion preserves the center $x=1$ and the total mass while lowering the peak."}

<div class="spiegazione">

The initial profile is a Gaussian shifted by one unit and multiplied by two. Its transform follows from the Gaussian formula and the phase factor $e^{-i\xi}$. For sufficiently regular, rapidly decaying solutions we can transform the equation in space, with the second spatial derivative giving the multiplier $-\xi^2$. For each frequency we obtain an ordinary differential equation in $t$. The evolution factor $e^{-\frac{t\xi^2}{2}}$ combines with the initial Gaussian factor, giving denominator $A(t)$ for the spatial Gaussian. For $t\ge0$ this is positive, so the recovered profile is Schwartz and inversion applies. The phase corresponds to the same center $x=1$. To retain the coefficient $\sqrt{2\pi}$ in frequency we divide the initial height by $\sqrt{A(t)}$. The mass is the transform at zero frequency, where the phase and evolution factors are both one. Thus diffusion preserves the mass.

</div>

## Exercise 4

Solve $u_t=u_{xx}$ on the line with initial data $u(0,x)=xe^{-x^2}$ by differentiating in space.

Let $v$ be the solution with initial profile $e^{-x^2}$ and set $B(t)=1+4t$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\widehat v(t,\xi)
&=\sqrt\pi\, e^{-\frac{\xi^2}4}e^{-t\xi^2}\\
&=\sqrt\pi\,e^{-\frac{B(t)\xi^2}4},\\
v(t,x)&=\frac1{\sqrt{B(t)}}e^{-\frac{x^2}{B(t)}},\\
v_x(t,x)&=-\frac{2x}{B(t)^{\frac32}}e^{-\frac{x^2}{B(t)}},\\
u(t,x)&=-\frac12 v_x(t,x)\\
&=\frac{x}{B(t)^{\frac32}}e^{-\frac{x^2}{B(t)}} .
\end{aligned}
$$

::grafico{f="x*exp(-x^2);x*exp(-x^2/2)/(2*sqrt(2));x*exp(-x^2/5)/(5*sqrt(5))" legenda="t=0;t=\frac14;t=1" xmin=-4.2 xmax=4.2 ymin=-0.55 ymax=0.55 titolo="The positive and negative parts remain symmetric during diffusion, and their integrals have opposite signs and the same magnitude."}

<div class="spiegazione">

The required initial profile is $-\dfrac12$ times the derivative of $e^{-x^2}$, so start with the solution $v$ for Gaussian initial data. Its transform is the initial transform multiplied by $e^{-t\xi^2}$, the solution factor for the frequency equation with unit diffusivity. Inversion applies because both the Gaussians and their transforms are integrable. The recovered Gaussian has parameter $\dfrac1{B(t)}$ and prefactor $\dfrac1{\sqrt{B(t)}}$. Since $v$ is smooth for $t\ge0$, differentiating with respect to $x$ commutes with the derivatives in the heat equation. Hence $-\dfrac12v_x$ satisfies the same equation and equals the required initial data at $t=0$. The coefficient $B(t)^{-\frac32}$ is the prefactor times the reciprocal of $B(t)$ arising from differentiation of the exponent. The function stays odd in $x$ and absolutely integrable, so its two half-line integrals cancel and its mass is zero at every time.

</div>
