## Exercise 1

Let $g=\mathbf1_{[-1,1]}$. Find the transform of $f(x)=e^{2ix}g(2(x-1))$, specifying its value at $\xi=2$.

Set $p(x)=g(2x)$ and $q(x)=p(x-1)$, so that $f(x)=e^{2ix}q(x)$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\widehat p(\xi)&=\frac12\widehat g\left(\frac\xi2\right),\\
\widehat q(\xi)&=\frac12e^{-i\xi}\widehat g\left(\frac\xi2\right),\\
\widehat f(\xi)&=\widehat q(\xi-2)\\
&=\frac12e^{-i(\xi-2)}\widehat g\left(\frac{\xi-2}2\right)\\
&=e^{-i(\xi-2)}\frac{\sin\left(\frac{\xi-2}2\right)}{\frac{\xi-2}2},
\qquad \xi\ne2,\\[4pt]
\widehat f(2)&=\frac12\widehat g(0)=1 .
\end{aligned}
$$

::grafico{f="abs(2*sin(x)/x);abs(sin((x-2)/2)/((x-2)/2))" punti="0,2,pieno;2,1,pieno" nomi="ξ,modulo" xmin=-9 xmax=10 ymin=-0.1 ymax=2.25 titolo="The original transform has its maximum magnitude at zero. After scaling and modulation, the maximum moves to $2$ and has value $1$."}

<div class="spiegazione">

The indicator belongs to $L^1$, so the scaling, translation, and modulation formulas remain applicable after each spatial operation. Start with $p$ because $g(2(x-1))$ is $p$ shifted by one. Scaling reduces the width from two to one, giving the factor $\dfrac12$. Translation introduces $e^{-i\xi}$. Modulation replaces $\xi$ by $\xi-2$ throughout $\widehat q$, including in the phase. The indicator formula gives the ratio of sine to its argument, with limiting value one when that argument is zero. The same value follows by integrating $f(x)e^{-2ix}$ because the exponentials cancel, leaving an indicator of width one. The modulus in the graph retains this height at $\xi=2$ because the phase factor has modulus one.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Find the transform of $f(x)=xe^{-x^2}$ and determine whether it is real, purely imaginary, or has both a nonzero real and a nonzero imaginary part.

The transform of $g(x)=e^{-x^2}$ is known and $f=xg$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\widehat g(\xi)&=\sqrt\pi\,e^{-\frac{\xi^2}4},\\
\widehat g'(\xi)
&=-\frac{\xi\sqrt\pi}{2}e^{-\frac{\xi^2}4},\\
\widehat f(\xi)&=i\widehat g'(\xi)\\
&=-\frac{i\xi\sqrt\pi}{2}e^{-\frac{\xi^2}4},\\[4pt]
\operatorname{Re}\widehat f(\xi)&=0.
\end{aligned}
$$

::grafico{f="-sqrt(pi)*x*exp(-x^2/4)/2" nomi="ξ,Im F" xmin=-5 xmax=5 ymin=-0.95 ymax=0.95 titolo="For $f(x)=xe^{-x^2}$, only the imaginary part is shown. Its sign is opposite to that of $\xi$."}

<div class="spiegazione">

The product $xg$ is integrable because Gaussian decay dominates linear growth. The derivative of the kernel with respect to $\xi$ has modulus $|x|$, so $|xg(x)|$ is an integrable bound for the differentiated integrand. Differentiating under the integral gives $\widehat g'=-i\widehat{(xg)}$. Multiplication by $i$ gives the transform of $f$. The derivative of the quadratic exponent is $-\dfrac\xi2$, while $\sqrt\pi$ remains constant. For real $\xi$ all factors except $i$ are real, so the transform is purely imaginary. The linear factor $\xi$ is odd and the Gaussian is even, making the transform odd. The vanishing of the real part also follows from the original function because $xe^{-x^2}$ times the cosine is odd and integrates to zero.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Find the transform of $f(x)=\operatorname{sgn}(x)e^{-2|x|}$ and check the result at $\xi=0$.

Split the integral into half-lines and use $x=-t$ in the negative half-line contribution.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\widehat f(\xi)
&=\int_0^\infty e^{-2x}e^{-i\xi x}\,dx
-\int_{-\infty}^0 e^{2x}e^{-i\xi x}\,dx\\
&=\int_0^\infty e^{-(2+i\xi)x}\,dx
-\int_0^\infty e^{-(2-i\xi)t}\,dt\\
&=\frac1{2+i\xi}-\frac1{2-i\xi}\\
&=\frac{(2-i\xi)-(2+i\xi)}{(2+i\xi)(2-i\xi)}\\
&=-\frac{2i\xi}{4+\xi^2},\\[4pt]
\widehat f(0)&=0 .
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The function is integrable since its modulus is $e^{-2|x|}$ almost everywhere. On the negative half-line the sign of $f$ is minus one, giving a minus sign before the corresponding integral. With $x=-t$ the interval becomes the positive half-line and the kernel becomes $e^{i\xi t}$. Both exponents have real part minus two, so the antiderivatives vanish at infinity. Subtracting the reciprocals gives the real denominator $4+\xi^2$. The constant terms in the numerator cancel and the imaginary terms have the same sign, leaving $-2i\xi$. At zero the numerator vanishes and the denominator is four. The direct integral is also zero since the two half-lines give equal areas of opposite signs. At nonzero frequencies the transform is purely imaginary, and its imaginary part has sign opposite to that of the frequency.

</div>
