## Exercise 1

Let $f=\mathbf1_{[-1,1]}$ and $g=\mathbf1_{[0,1]}$. Compute $(f*g)(x)$ on the entire real line.

Let $I_x$ be the intersection of the two intervals restricting $y$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
-1\le x-y\le1
&\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad x-1\le y\le x+1,\\
I_x&=[x-1,x+1]\cap[0,1],\\
(f*g)(x)&=\int_{I_x}1\,dy\\
&=\max\bigl(0,\min(x+1,1)-\max(x-1,0)\bigr).
\end{aligned}
$$

Substitute the endpoints separately in each region where the intersection takes a different form.

$$
(f*g)(x)=
\begin{cases}
0 & x\le -1,\\
x+1 & -1<x<0,\\
1 & 0\le x\le1,\\
2-x & 1<x<2,\\
0 & x\ge2 .
\end{cases}
$$

::grafico{linee="-1.5,0 -1,0 0,1 1,1 2,0 2.5,0" poligoni="-1,0 0,1 1,1 2,0" punti="-1,0,pieno;0,1,pieno;1,1,pieno;2,0,pieno" xmin=-1.6 xmax=2.6 ymin=-0.15 ymax=1.25 titolo="The horizontal coordinate of the convolution trapezoid is $x$, the parameter that shifts the interval $[x-1,x+1]$."}

<div class="spiegazione">

Both factors are indicators of bounded intervals, so the integrand equals one exactly where both restrictions hold. The factor $g$ gives the fixed interval $[0,1]$, and $f$ gives the moving interval $[x-1,x+1]$. When $x\le-1$, the moving right endpoint is at most zero, giving intersection length zero. For $-1<x<0$ the intersection runs from zero to $x+1$. For $0\le x\le1$ the moving interval contains all of $[0,1]$ and the length is one. For $1<x<2$ the intersection runs from $x-1$ to one and has length $2-x$. From $x=2$ onward the moving left endpoint is at least one, and the length returns to zero. A single point of contact has measure zero. The graph is therefore a trapezoid with two sloping sides and a horizontal segment corresponding to complete overlap.

</div>

## Exercise 2

For $h(x)=\max(1-|x|,0)$, derive $\widehat h$ without integrating directly over the two linear pieces.

Recognize the triangle as the convolution of $g=\mathbf1_{[-\dfrac12,\dfrac12]}$ with itself.

$$
\begin{aligned}
(g*g)(x)&=\left|\left[x-\frac12,x+\frac12\right]\cap
\left[-\frac12,\frac12\right]\right|\\
&=\max(1-|x|,0)\\
&=h(x),\\[4pt]
\widehat h(\xi)&=\widehat g(\xi)^2\\
&=\left(\frac{2\sin\left(\frac\xi2\right)}{\xi}\right)^2,
\qquad \xi\ne0,\\
\widehat h(0)&=\widehat g(0)^2=1 .
\end{aligned}
$$

::grafico{f="(2*sin(x/2)/x)^2" punti="0,1,pieno;6.283185,0,pieno;-6.283185,0,pieno" nomi="ξ,F" xmin=-15 xmax=15 ymin=-0.1 ymax=1.16 titolo="The transform of the unit triangular function is the square of the transform of the indicator of an interval of length one."}

<div class="spiegazione">

Both intervals have length one, and their centers are $|x|$ apart. When the distance is less than one, the overlap length is one minus that distance. At distances of one or more the overlap has measure zero. This is exactly $h$. Since the indicator $g$ has bounded support, it belongs to $L^1$ and the **convolution theorem** applies. The transform of $h$ is the square of the transform of $g$. The symmetric interval indicator gives the sine of $\dfrac{\xi}{2}$ because its half-width is one half. At zero frequency the transform of $g$ is its area, one, and its square is also one. This equals the area of the original triangle of base two and height one. The squared transform is nonnegative and has the same nonzero zeros as $\widehat g$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Use Plancherel's theorem to evaluate $\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb R}\dfrac{\sin^2 \xi}{\xi^2}\,d\xi$.

Choose the indicator $f=\mathbf1_{[-1,1]}$, whose transform contains the required quotient.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\|f\|_2^2&=\int_{-1}^1 1\,dx=2,\\
\widehat f(\xi)&=\frac{2\sin\xi}{\xi},\qquad \xi\ne0,\\
\|\widehat f\|_2^2&=2\pi\|f\|_2^2=4\pi,\\
4\int_{\mathbb R}\frac{\sin^2\xi}{\xi^2}\,d\xi&=4\pi,\\
\int_{\mathbb R}\frac{\sin^2\xi}{\xi^2}\,d\xi&=\pi.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The indicator is bounded with bounded support, so it belongs to both $L^1$ and $L^2$. The first condition allows us to compute its transform by integration. The second allows us to apply **Plancherel's theorem**, and the two transform definitions agree on their common domain. The squared norm of $f$ is two because the indicator equals its own square. With this chapter's convention the squared norm in frequency is $2\pi$ times that in space, so it equals $4\pi$. The squared modulus of the transform contains the coefficient four, which we divide out at the end. The integrand tends to one at zero because the ratio of sine to its argument tends to one. Its value at that single point does not affect the integral. At infinity the squared sine is bounded by one, so comparison with the inverse square of the frequency also proves convergence.

</div>
