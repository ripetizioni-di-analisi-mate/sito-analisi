## Exercise 1

If $f\in L^2(\mathbb R)$ and $\|f\|_2^2=1$, use the Plancherel extension with this chapter's convention to find $\|\widehat f\|_2^2$.

Let $\widehat f$ denote the extension to $L^2$ of the transform originally defined for integrable functions.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\|f\|_2^2&=1,\\
\frac1{2\pi}\|\widehat f\|_2^2&=\|f\|_2^2\\
&=1,\\
\|\widehat f\|_2^2&=2\pi .
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The hypothesis $f\in L^2(\mathbb R)$ permits use of the extended form of **Plancherel's theorem**. The transform is the limit in $L^2$ of transforms of approximations that are both integrable and square integrable. For each approximation the defining integral converges absolutely. The norm identity passes to the limit because the norm is continuous under convergence in $L^2$. With kernel $e^{-i\xi x}$ and no coefficient in front of the forward integral, the squared norm in frequency is $2\pi$ times the original squared norm. The given quantity is already the squared norm, so it enters the right-hand side directly. Multiplying both sides by $2\pi$ gives the required quantity. For the norm itself, taking the square root would give $\sqrt{2\pi}$. This distinction accounts for the factor $2\pi$ in the squared identity.

</div>

## Exercise 2

If $f\in L^2(\mathbb R)$ and $\|f\|_2^2=2$, use the Plancherel extension with this chapter's convention to find $\|\widehat f\|_2^2$.

Compute the squared norm and the norm separately.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\|f\|_2&=\sqrt2,\\
\|\widehat f\|_2&=\sqrt{2\pi}\,\|f\|_2\\
&=\sqrt{2\pi}\sqrt2\\
&=2\sqrt\pi,\\[4pt]
\|\widehat f\|_2^2&=(2\sqrt\pi)^2\\
&=4\pi.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The condition $f\in L^2$ is the hypothesis for the Plancherel extension. The resulting transform belongs to $L^2$ and both norms are finite. Since norms are nonnegative, take the positive square root on both sides of the squared identity. The factor for the norms themselves is $\sqrt{2\pi}$. Here $\|f\|_2^2=2$, so the norm of $f$ is $\sqrt2$. Multiplying the two square roots gives $2\sqrt\pi$, and squaring yields the required value $4\pi$. In integral form this means that $|\widehat f(\xi)|^2$ integrates to $4\pi$ over the line, whatever the pointwise shape of the function. For example, the indicator of an interval of length two satisfies the same hypothesis. Any translation of it has the same norm because its transform changes only by a phase factor of unit modulus.

</div>
