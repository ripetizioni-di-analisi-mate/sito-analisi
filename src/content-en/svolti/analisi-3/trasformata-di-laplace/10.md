## Exercise 1

Solve $y'+2y=H(t-3)$ with $y(0)=2$ and check how the pieces join at $t=3$.

For $\operatorname{Re}s>0$, transform the step and separate the response to the initial condition.

$$
\begin{aligned}
sY(s)-2+2Y(s)&=\frac{e^{-3s}}s,\\
Y(s)&=\frac2{s+2}+\frac{e^{-3s}}{s(s+2)}\\
&=\frac2{s+2}+\frac{e^{-3s}}2
\left(\frac1s-\frac1{s+2}\right),\\[4pt]
y(t)&=2e^{-2t}+\frac12H(t-3)\bigl(1-e^{-2(t-3)}\bigr),\\
y(3^-)&=2e^{-6},\\
y(3^+)&=2e^{-6}+\frac12(1-e^0)\\
&=2e^{-6}.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The solution is locally absolutely continuous because it joins regular solutions on the two intervals with the same value at $t=3$. On each interval it is a sum of constants and exponentials, so both the solution and its almost-everywhere derivative are of exponential order. The derivative transform formula applies and the boundary term is $-2$. The step transform is $e^{-3s}$ times the transform of the constant because switch-on is delayed by three units. After decomposition the delay factor multiplies only the forced response. Its inverse contains the step and the shifted variable $t-3$. The factor multiplying the step tends to zero from the right at switch-on, so the solution is continuous. The equation shows that the right-hand derivative exceeds the left-hand derivative by one because $2y$ has equal one-sided limits and the forcing has a unit jump.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Solve $y''+y=\sin t$ with $y(0)=y'(0)=0$ and explain the origin of the growing term.

Denote the transform of the solution by $Y$ and that of $\cos t$ by $C$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
(s^2+1)Y(s)&=\frac1{s^2+1},\\
Y(s)&=\frac1{(s^2+1)^2},\\[4pt]
-C'(s)&=-\frac d{ds}\frac{s}{s^2+1}\\
&=-\frac{(s^2+1)-2s^2}{(s^2+1)^2}\\
&=\frac{s^2-1}{(s^2+1)^2},\\[4pt]
Y(s)&=\frac12\left(\frac1{s^2+1}
-\frac{s^2-1}{(s^2+1)^2}\right),\\
y(t)&=\frac{\sin t-t\cos t}{2}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Applying the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality to the two terms gives the envelopes $\pm\dfrac12\sqrt{1+t^2}$."}

<div class="spiegazione">

The initial data are zero, so the transform of the second derivative is $s^2Y$. The solution of the linear equation with sinusoidal forcing and its derivatives are of exponential order, so the equation can be transformed sufficiently far to the right. The angular frequency of the forcing matches that of the oscillator. Both factors have denominator $s^2+1$, giving double poles at $i$ and $-i$ in $Y$. Differentiating $C$ squares the denominator. Since $t\cos t$ is of exponential order and its transform is differentiable for $\operatorname{Re}s>0$, the identity $\mathcal L[t\cos t]=-C'$ gives the growing term. To check, $y'=\dfrac{t\sin t}{2}$ and $y''=\dfrac{\sin t+t\cos t}{2}$. Their sum $y''+y$ equals $\sin t$, and both initial conditions are zero. The term proportional to $t$ accounts for the increasing amplitude in the graph.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Find a continuous solution of exponential order to $y(t)=1+\int_0^t(t-u)y(u)\,du$ for $t\ge0$.

The function $\iota(t)=t$ and the desired solution have transforms in a common half-plane.

$$
\begin{aligned}
y(t)&=1+(\iota*y)(t),\\
Y(s)&=\frac1s+\frac1{s^2}Y(s),\\
\left(1-\frac1{s^2}\right)Y(s)&=\frac1s,\\
Y(s)&=\frac{s}{s^2-1}\\
&=\frac12\left(\frac1{s-1}+\frac1{s+1}\right),\\[4pt]
y(t)&=\frac12(e^t+e^{-t})\\
&=\cosh t,\qquad t\ge0.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The integrand contains the product of $y(u)$ and the kernel $t-u$, giving the causal convolution of $y$ with the identity function on the half-line. The function $t\mapsto t$ is continuous and of exponential order. The stipulated class for $y$ has the same properties, so the convolution theorem applies on a half-plane where both integrals converge absolutely. Solving for $Y$ gives a denominator factoring as $(s-1)(s+1)$. Half the sum of the two partial fractions recovers the numerator $s$. The inverse transform is half the sum of the two exponentials for $\operatorname{Re}s>1$. The resulting function is continuous and of exponential order. Integration by parts gives $\int_0^t(t-u)\cosh u\,du=\cosh t-1$, verifying it directly. Transform uniqueness gives uniqueness in the chosen class of continuous functions.

</div>

## Exercise 4

Let $F(s)=\dfrac{s+2}{s(s+1)^2}$. Find the initial and final values of the causal response and check them by inversion.

Use $sF(s)$ to find the two limits and then compute the response explicitly.

$$
\begin{aligned}
sF(s)&=\frac{s+2}{(s+1)^2},\\
\lim_{s\to+\infty}sF(s)&=0,\\
\lim_{s\to0^+}sF(s)&=2,\\[4pt]
F(s)&=\frac{2(s+1)^2-2s(s+1)-s}{s(s+1)^2}\\
&=\frac2s-\frac2{s+1}-\frac1{(s+1)^2},\\
f(t)&=2-2e^{-t}-te^{-t}\\
&=2-(2+t)e^{-t},\\
f(0^+)&=2-2=0,\\
\lim_{t\to+\infty}f(t)&=2.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

For positive real $s$, compare degrees to find the limit of the quotient at $+\infty$. The numerator has degree one and the denominator degree two, giving limit zero. At zero the denominator is nonzero, so direct substitution gives the limit. The only pole of $sF(s)$ is $-1$ in the open left half-plane, meeting the final value theorem's hypothesis for a rational transform. Partial fractions separate a constant, an exponential, and an exponential times $t$. The table applies on the common half-plane $\operatorname{Re}s>0$. The explicit function is right-continuous at zero and of exponential order, so the initial value theorem applies as well. At zero the exponential contribution cancels the constant term. At infinity its product with the polynomial tends to zero.

</div>
