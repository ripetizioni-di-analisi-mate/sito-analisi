## Exercise 1

Find the causal inverse Laplace transform of $\dfrac1{(s+1)(s+2)}$.

Determine the partial fraction coefficients.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac1{(s+1)(s+2)}&=\frac A{s+1}+\frac B{s+2},\\
1&=A(s+2)+B(s+1),\\
s=-1&\ \Longrightarrow\ A=1,\\
s=-2&\ \Longrightarrow\ B=-1,\\[4pt]
f(t)&=\mathcal L^{-1}\left[\frac1{s+1}-\frac1{s+2}\right](t)\\
&=e^{-t}-e^{-2t},\qquad t\ge0.
\end{aligned}
$$

::grafico{f="exp(-x)-exp(-2*x)" xmin=0 xmax=5 ymin=0 ymax=0.31 nomi="t,y" punti="0.693147,0.25,pieno" titolo="The response $e^{-t}-e^{-2t}$ vanishes at the origin and reaches its maximum of $\dfrac14$ at $t=\log 2$, as can be checked by setting the derivative to zero."}

<div class="spiegazione">

The denominator is a product of distinct linear factors, so each partial fraction has a constant numerator. Multiply the rational identity by the common denominator to obtain a polynomial identity. Setting $s=-1$ removes the term containing $B$, and setting $s=-2$ removes the one containing $A$. Use the resulting coefficients in the exponential transform table on the common half-plane $\operatorname{Re}s>-1$. Both exponentials are continuous and of exponential order, so uniqueness determines the causal response for every $t\ge0$. At zero the two contributions have equal magnitudes and opposite signs. For $t>0$ the second decays faster, so the response becomes positive and eventually tends to zero. The graph shows these different rates.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Find the causal inverse Laplace transform of $\dfrac1{(s+2)(s+3)}$.

The factorized expression is also the product of the transforms of two causal exponentials.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac1{(s+2)(s+3)}&=\mathcal L[e^{-2t}](s)\,\mathcal L[e^{-3t}](s),\\
f(t)&=\int_0^t e^{-2(t-u)}e^{-3u}\,du\\
&=e^{-2t}\int_0^t e^{-u}\,du\\
&=e^{-2t}\left[-e^{-u}\right]_0^t\\
&=e^{-2t}(1-e^{-t})\\
&=e^{-2t}-e^{-3t},\qquad t\ge0.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The first rational factor is the transform of $e^{-2t}$ and the second that of $e^{-3t}$. Both functions are continuous on the half-line and of exponential order. Both transforms converge absolutely for $\operatorname{Re}s>-2$, so the causal convolution theorem applies. The factor $e^{-2t}$ in the exponential product is independent of the integration variable and comes outside. The part depending on $u$ is $e^{-u}$ since the two decay rates differ by one. Evaluating at the endpoints leaves $1-e^{-t}$. The convolution vanishes at $t=0$ because the integration interval has length zero. For $t>0$ the integrand is positive, in agreement with the sign of the explicit expression. The two exponentials correspond to the poles $-2$ and $-3$, as the partial fractions would also show.

</div>
