## Exercise 1

Find the causal inverse transform of $F(s)=\dfrac{2s+3}{(s+1)^3}$.

Express the numerator in terms of the linear factor $s+1$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
2s+3&=2(s+1)+1,\\
F(s)&=\frac{2(s+1)+1}{(s+1)^3}\\
&=\frac2{(s+1)^2}+\frac1{(s+1)^3},\\[4pt]
f(t)&=2te^{-t}+\frac{t^2}{2!}e^{-t}\\
&=\left(2t+\frac{t^2}{2}\right)e^{-t},\qquad t\ge0.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

There is a triple pole at $-1$ because the numerator equals $1$ there. To separate the denominator powers, write the numerator as a linear polynomial in $s+1$. The term $2(s+1)$ lowers the denominator power by one, while the constant term retains the third power. The power transform formula holds for a parameter with positive real part. Here the parameter is $s+1$, so the table gives both inverse transforms on $\operatorname{Re}s>-1$. The squared denominator gives $te^{-t}$ and the cubed denominator gives $\dfrac{t^2}{2!}e^{-t}$. The factorial appears because the transform of $t^2e^{-t}$ has numerator $2!$. The sum is continuous on the half-line and of exponential order, so uniqueness identifies the causal response at every point. Both terms vanish at zero and decay at infinity.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Find the causal inverse Laplace transform of $F(s)=\dfrac{s}{(s+1)^2+4}$.

Separate the cosine and sine entries from the transform table.

$$
\begin{aligned}
s&=(s+1)-1,\\
F(s)&=\frac{(s+1)-1}{(s+1)^2+2^2}\\
&=\frac{s+1}{(s+1)^2+2^2}
-\frac12\,\frac{2}{(s+1)^2+2^2},\\[4pt]
f(t)&=e^{-t}\cos(2t)-\frac12e^{-t}\sin(2t)\\
&=e^{-t}\left(\cos(2t)-\frac12\sin(2t)\right),\qquad t\ge0.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The roots of the denominator are $-1\pm2i$. Their real part $-1$ determines the common exponential factor, while the magnitude of the imaginary part determines the angular frequency $2$. In the cosine entry the numerator is the shifted parameter $s+1$. Separating this term leaves the constant numerator $-1$. The sine table entry for angular frequency $2$ has numerator $2$, so its transform must be multiplied by $-\dfrac12$. The common half-plane is $\operatorname{Re}s>-1$ because both trigonometric functions are bounded and share the weight $e^{-t}$. By linearity the difference of the inverse transforms gives the response. Its initial value comes from the cosine, which is one at zero, while the sine vanishes there. The response tends to zero because the oscillating factor stays bounded and the exponential decays.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Use convolution to invert $\dfrac1{s(s+2)^2}$ and find the limit of the response as $t\to+\infty$.

Identify the transforms of $1$ and $te^{-2t}$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
F(s)&=\mathcal L[1](s)\,\mathcal L[te^{-2t}](s),\\
f(t)&=\int_0^t u e^{-2u}\,du\\
&=\left[-\frac{u e^{-2u}}2\right]_0^t
+\frac12\int_0^t e^{-2u}\,du\\
&=-\frac{te^{-2t}}2+\frac{1-e^{-2t}}4\\
&=\frac{1-(1+2t)e^{-2t}}4,\qquad t\ge0,\\[4pt]
\lim_{t\to+\infty}f(t)&=\frac14.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The constant function and $te^{-2t}$ are continuous and of exponential order. Both transforms converge absolutely in the common half-plane $\operatorname{Re}s>0$, so their product is the transform of their convolution. Since the first factor is constant, the convolution reduces to the accumulated integral of the second function. In integration by parts the derivative of $u$ is one and the exponential antiderivative is $-\dfrac12e^{-2u}$. The lower boundary term vanishes because it contains $u$. The remaining integral contributes $\dfrac{1-e^{-2t}}4$. At infinity the linear factor times the exponential tends to zero, leaving $\dfrac14$. The graph shows the integral increasing because its integrand is nonnegative. The final value theorem gives the same limit because $sF(s)$ has only the double pole $-2$ in the left half-plane.

</div>
