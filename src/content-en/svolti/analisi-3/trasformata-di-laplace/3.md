## Exercise 1

Compute $\mathcal L[e^{-t}\sin(t)](s)$ for $\operatorname{Re}s>-1$.

Set $z=s+1$ with $\operatorname{Re}z>0$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal L[e^{-t}\sin t](s)
&=\int_0^\infty e^{-zt}\sin t\,dt\\
&=\frac1{2i}\int_0^\infty\left(e^{-(z-i)t}-e^{-(z+i)t}\right)\,dt\\
&=\frac1{2i}\left(\frac1{z-i}-\frac1{z+i}\right)\\
&=\frac1{2i}\cdot\frac{(z+i)-(z-i)}{(z-i)(z+i)}\\
&=\frac1{z^2+1}\\
&=\frac1{(s+1)^2+1}.
\end{aligned}
$$

::grafico{f="exp(-x)*sin(x);exp(-x);-exp(-x)" xmin=0 xmax=7 ymin=-1.1 ymax=1.1 nomi="t,y" legenda="e^{-t}\sin t;e^{-t};-e^{-t}" titolo="The oscillation $e^{-t}\sin t$ lies between the envelopes $\pm e^{-t}$. This damping makes the integral converge even for some values of $s$ with negative real part."}

<div class="spiegazione">

To use the exponential transform formula, write the sine as two complex exponentials subtracted and divided by $2i$. Since $\operatorname{Re}(s+1)>0$, both $z-i$ and $z+i$ also have positive real part. The two integrals converge absolutely, so linearity allows separate evaluation. The denominators $z-i$ and $z+i$ multiply to $z^2+1$, and the difference of the numerators is $2i$. Simplification leaves one in the numerator. Substituting $z=s+1$ places the poles at $-1+i$ and $-1-i$. The graph shows the upper bound $e^{-t}$ for the modulus of the signal. Multiplication by $|e^{-st}|$ recovers the bound $e^{-(1+\operatorname{Re}s)t}$, integrable in the given half-plane.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Compute $\mathcal L[e^{-t}\sin(2t)](s)$ for $\operatorname{Re}s>-1$.

Set $z=s+1$ with $\operatorname{Re}z>0$ and integrate both trigonometric functions by parts.

$$
\begin{aligned}
I&=\int_0^\infty e^{-zt}\sin(2t)\,dt\\
&=\frac2z\int_0^\infty e^{-zt}\cos(2t)\,dt
=\frac2z J,\\
J&=\left[-\frac{e^{-zt}\cos(2t)}z\right]_0^\infty
-\frac2z I\\
&=\frac1z-\frac2z I,\\
I&=\frac2{z^2}-\frac4{z^2}I,\\
(z^2+4)I&=2,\\
\mathcal L[e^{-t}\sin(2t)](s)&=\frac2{(s+1)^2+4}.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Sine and cosine are bounded, so the weight with $\operatorname{Re}z>0$ gives absolute convergence of both integrals. Integrate by parts on finite intervals. The upper endpoint terms vanish in the limit because of $e^{-zt}$. For $I$ the lower term is zero because $\sin(0)=0$. For $J$ it contributes $\dfrac1z$ because $\cos(0)=1$. The derivatives $\dfrac d{dt}\sin(2t)=2\cos(2t)$ and $\dfrac d{dt}\cos(2t)=-2\sin(2t)$ account for the opposite signs in the two identities. Substituting $J$ in the first equation gives a linear equation for $I$. The factor $z^2+4$ is nonzero in the given half-plane because its roots have zero real part. Thus we may divide and return to the variable $s$.

</div>
