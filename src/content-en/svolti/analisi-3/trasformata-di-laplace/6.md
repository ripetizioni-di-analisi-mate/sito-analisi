## Exercise 1

Use the Laplace transform to solve $y'+y=1$ with $y(0)=0$ for $t\ge0$.

Write $Y(s)$ for the transform of $y$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
sY(s)-y(0)+Y(s)&=\frac1s,\\
(s+1)Y(s)&=\frac1s,\\
Y(s)&=\frac1{s(s+1)}\\
&=\frac{(s+1)-s}{s(s+1)}\\
&=\frac1s-\frac1{s+1},\\[6pt]
y(t)&=1-e^{-t},\qquad t\ge0.
\end{aligned}
$$

::grafico{f="1-exp(-x);0.5*(1-exp(-2*x))" xmin=0 xmax=5 ymin=0 ymax=1.14 nomi="t,y" asintoti="y=1;y=0.5" legenda="1-e^{-t};\dfrac12(1-e^{-2t})" titolo="With the same forcing term $1$, the term $2y$ preserves the initial value and slope and halves the equilibrium value."}

<div class="spiegazione">

The derivative formula requires $y$ to be locally absolutely continuous and $y,y'$ to be of exponential order. The solution of the constant-coefficient linear equation with constant forcing has these properties, so the equation can be transformed sufficiently far to the right. The initial condition $y(0)=0$ eliminates the boundary term. The forcing has transform $\dfrac1s$ for $\operatorname{Re}s>0$. To separate the inverse transforms, write the numerator one as the difference of $s+1$ and $s$. The transform table gives a constant and an exponential. Their sum is zero at $t=0$ and has derivative $e^{-t}$, so substitution into the equation gives $e^{-t}+(1-e^{-t})=1$. The exponential decays, leaving equilibrium value $1$. The graph also shows the unit initial slope imposed by the forcing.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Use the Laplace transform to solve $y'+2y=1$ with $y(0)=0$ for $t\ge0$.

Write the polynomial identity for the partial fraction decomposition of $Y(s)$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
sY(s)-y(0)+2Y(s)&=\frac1s,\\
Y(s)&=\frac1{s(s+2)}\\
&=\frac A{s}+\frac B{s+2},\\
1&=A(s+2)+Bs,\\
2A&=1,\\
A+B&=0,\\
Y(s)&=\frac1{2s}-\frac1{2(s+2)},\\[4pt]
y(t)&=\frac12\bigl(1-e^{-2t}\bigr),\qquad t\ge0.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The initial term vanishes, so the transformed left-hand side contains only $Y$ multiplied by $s+2$. The transform is valid for the regular solution of exponential order of this constant-coefficient linear equation. To invert the quotient, compare the constant term and the coefficient of $s$ in the polynomial identity. The constant term gives $A=\dfrac12$. The linear coefficient is zero, so $B=-A$. The table applies for $\operatorname{Re}s>0$, where both the constant and exponential transforms converge. The solution vanishes at $t=0$ and has derivative $e^{-2t}$. In the equation the contribution $2y$ is $1-e^{-2t}$, so adding the derivative gives $1$. The limit is $\dfrac12$, as expected from the equilibrium equation $2y=1$.

</div>
