## Exercise 1

Compute $\mathcal L[t](s)$ and give a half-plane of convergence.

For $s=\sigma+i\omega$ with $\sigma>0$, first evaluate the integral up to $R$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
I_R(s)&=\int_0^R te^{-st}\,dt\\
&=\left[-\frac{te^{-st}}s\right]_0^R+\frac1s\int_0^R e^{-st}\,dt\\
&=-\frac{Re^{-sR}}s+\frac1s\left[-\frac{e^{-st}}s\right]_0^R\\
&=-\frac{Re^{-sR}}s+\frac{1-e^{-sR}}{s^2},\\[6pt]
\mathcal L[t](s)&=\lim_{R\to+\infty} I_R(s)\\
&=\frac1{s^2},\qquad \operatorname{Re}s>0.
\end{aligned}
$$

::grafico{f="x*exp(-x)" area="x*exp(-x);0;0;6" xmin=0 xmax=6 ymin=0 ymax=0.45 nomi="t,y" titolo="For $s=1$, the integrand is $te^{-t}$. The shaded area shows the contribution up to $t=6$, while the full integral equals $1$."}

<div class="spiegazione">

The function $t\mapsto te^{-st}$ is continuous on every bounded interval, so integration by parts applies on $[0,R]$. Differentiate the factor $t$, whose derivative is constant, and integrate the exponential weight. The lower boundary term vanishes because it contains $t$ evaluated at zero. The upper term has modulus $\dfrac{Re^{-\sigma R}}{|s|}$, which tends to zero for $\sigma>0$. The term $e^{-sR}$ also tends to zero, so the limit follows from the expression for $I_R$. The same condition gives absolute convergence because the integrand has modulus $te^{-\sigma t}$. For $\sigma=0$ the modulus equals $t$, and for $\sigma<0$ it is at least $t$ on the half-line. This gives the exact half-plane of absolute convergence.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Compute $\mathcal L[t^{2}](s)$ and give a half-plane of convergence.

Set $\sigma=\operatorname{Re}s>0$ and use the integral of $te^{-st}$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
J_R(s)&=\int_0^R t^2e^{-st}\,dt\\
&=\left[-\frac{t^2e^{-st}}s\right]_0^R+\frac2s\int_0^R te^{-st}\,dt\\
&=-\frac{R^2e^{-sR}}s+\frac2s I_R(s),\\[6pt]
\mathcal L[t^2](s)&=\lim_{R\to+\infty}J_R(s)\\
&=\frac2s\cdot\frac1{s^2}\\
&=\frac2{s^3},\qquad \operatorname{Re}s>0.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The derivative of $t^2$ is $2t$, so after integration by parts the remaining integral is a multiple of the one in the previous exercise. The coefficient $2$ comes from differentiating the power and $\dfrac1s$ from the exponential antiderivative. The product vanishes at the lower endpoint. At the upper endpoint $R^2e^{-sR}$ has modulus $R^2e^{-\sigma R}$ and tends to zero because $\sigma>0$. Absolute convergence also follows by comparing $t^2e^{-\sigma t}$ with an exponential of rate $\dfrac{\sigma}{2}$, since $t^2e^{-\frac{\sigma t}{2}}$ is bounded. For $\sigma=0$ the modulus of the integrand equals $t^2$, and for $\sigma<0$ it exceeds $t^2$ when $t>0$. The absolute integral therefore diverges in both cases. Raising the degree from one to two multiplies the transform by $\dfrac2s$ and leaves the boundary of convergence unchanged.

</div>
