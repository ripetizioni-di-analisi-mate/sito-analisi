## Exercise 1

Find the Laurent expansions of $\dfrac1{z(z-1)}$ about zero for $0<|z|<1$ and $|z|>1$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac1{z(z-1)}
&=-\frac1z\cdot\frac1{1-z}\\[6pt]
&=-\frac1z\sum_{n=0}^\infty z^n\\[6pt]
&=-\sum_{n=0}^\infty z^{n-1}
\qquad (0<|z|<1)
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac1{z(z-1)}
&=\frac1{z^2}\cdot\frac1{1-z^{-1}}\\[6pt]
&=\frac1{z^2}\sum_{n=0}^\infty z^{-n}\\[6pt]
&=\sum_{n=0}^\infty z^{-n-2}
\qquad (|z|>1)
\end{aligned}
$$

::grafico{uguale=1 nomi="Re,Im" xmin=-2.6 xmax=2.6 ymin=-2.5 ymax=2.5 curve="cos(t),sin(t),0,6.283185307179586;0.5*cos(t),0.5*sin(t),0,6.283185307179586;2*cos(t),2*sin(t),0,6.283185307179586" punti="0,0,vuoto,0;1,0,vuoto,1" titolo="The unit circle separates the two expansions because it passes through the second pole."}

<div class="spiegazione">

The poles are $0$ and $1$, so the unit circle separates the two open regions of expansion. In the inner annulus write $z-1=-(1-z)$. The ratio $z$ has modulus less than one, so the reciprocal of $1-z$ has a geometric series expansion. Multiplication by $-z^{-1}$ reverses the signs of the coefficients and lowers each exponent by one.

In the outer annulus, factor $z$ out of $z-1$. The geometric ratio is now $z^{-1}$, whose modulus is less than one for $|z|>1$. The factor $z^{-2}$ gives a first term of degree $-2$. The outer annulus has no points arbitrarily close to the center, so its zero coefficient of $z^{-1}$ is not the local residue at zero. The local residue is the coefficient $-1$ in the inner expansion.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Find the expansion of $\dfrac1{(z-1)(z-3)}$ for $1<|z|<3$ and the coefficient of $z^{-1}$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac1{(z-1)(z-3)}&=\frac A{z-1}+\frac B{z-3}\\[6pt]
1&=A(z-3)+B(z-1)\\[6pt]
1&=-2A\qquad(z=1)\\[6pt]
1&=2B\qquad(z=3)\\[6pt]
A&=-\frac12,\qquad B=\frac12
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac1{z-1}
&=\frac1z\cdot\frac1{1-z^{-1}}\\[6pt]
&=\sum_{n=0}^\infty z^{-n-1}\\[6pt]
\frac1{z-3}
&=-\frac13\cdot\frac1{1-\frac z3}\\[6pt]
&=-\frac13\sum_{n=0}^\infty\left(\frac z3\right)^n
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac1{(z-1)(z-3)}
&=-\frac12\sum_{n=0}^\infty z^{-n-1}
-\frac16\sum_{n=0}^\infty\left(\frac z3\right)^n\\[6pt]
c_{-1}&=-\frac12
\end{aligned}
$$

::grafico{uguale=1 nomi="Re,Im" xmin=-3.6 xmax=3.8 ymin=-3.5 ymax=3.5 curve="cos(t),sin(t),0,6.283185307179586;3*cos(t),3*sin(t),0,6.283185307179586;2*cos(t),2*sin(t),0,6.283185307179586" punti="1,0,vuoto,1;3,0,vuoto,3" titolo="The circle of radius two lies in the middle annulus and encloses only the pole $1$."}

<div class="spiegazione">

The rational function admits a partial fraction decomposition because the roots $1$ and $3$ of its two linear factors are distinct. After multiplying by the common denominator, evaluate the polynomial identity at the two roots. Each substitution removes one term and determines the coefficient of the other.

In the given annulus, both $|z^{-1}|<1$ and $\left|\dfrac z3\right|<1$ hold. The first condition gives an expansion in negative powers for the fraction with a pole at $1$. The second gives an expansion in nonnegative powers for the fraction with a pole at $3$. Thus only the first fraction contributes to $z^{-1}$, through its index-zero term. The function is holomorphic at the origin, so the coefficient $-\dfrac12$ belongs to the intermediate annulus, where every circle centered at zero encloses the pole $1$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Classify the singularity of $\dfrac{\sin z-z}{z^4}$ at zero and compute its residue.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\sin z&=z-\frac{z^3}{3!}+\frac{z^5}{5!}+O(z^7)\\[6pt]
\sin z-z&=-\frac{z^3}{6}+\frac{z^5}{120}+O(z^7)\\[6pt]
\frac{\sin z-z}{z^4}&=-\frac1{6z}+\frac z{120}+O(z^3)
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\lim_{z\to0} z\frac{\sin z-z}{z^4}
&=\lim_{z\to0}\left(-\frac16+\frac{z^2}{120}+O(z^4)\right)\\[6pt]
&=-\frac16\ne0\\[6pt]
\operatorname{ord}_{\mathrm{pole}}(0)&=1\\[6pt]
\operatorname{Res}\left(\frac{\sin z-z}{z^4},0\right)&=-\frac16
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Sine is entire and the denominator vanishes only at zero, so the singularity is isolated. Subtracting $z$ cancels the linear sine term. The first remaining coefficient, of degree three, is $-\dfrac16$. Thus the numerator has a zero of order three. After division by $z^4$ the lowest exponent is $-1$.

Multiplying the function by $z$ gives an extension to zero with value $-\dfrac16$. Since this value is nonzero, the function has a **simple pole**. The value is also the residue because multiplying the Laurent series by $z$ turns the term with power $z^{-1}$ into the constant term. All subsequent powers have degree at least one, so no other term contributes to the residue.

</div>
