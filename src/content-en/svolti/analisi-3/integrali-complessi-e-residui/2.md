## Exercise 1

Evaluate $\int_{|z|=1}\dfrac{e^z}{z^3}\,dz$ with counterclockwise orientation.

$$
\begin{aligned}
f(z)&=e^z\\[6pt]
f'(z)&=e^z\\[6pt]
f''(z)&=e^z\\[6pt]
f''(0)&=e^0\\[6pt]
&=1
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_{|z|=1}\frac{e^z}{z^3}\,dz
&=\frac{2\pi i}{2!}f''(0)\\[6pt]
&=\frac{2\pi i}{2}\cdot1\\[6pt]
&=\pi i
\end{aligned}
$$

::grafico{uguale=1 nomi="Re,Im" xmin=-1.5 xmax=1.5 ymin=-1.5 ymax=1.5 curve="cos(t),sin(t),0,6.283185307179586" vettori="0.20,0.98,-0.20,0.98" punti="0,0,vuoto,0" titolo="The evaluation point lies inside the counterclockwise circle."}

<div class="spiegazione">

The numerator $f(z)=e^z$ is entire and hence holomorphic on an open set containing the closed unit disk. The evaluation point $0$ lies inside the circle. Since the circle is traversed counterclockwise, the positive orientation hypothesis of **Cauchy's integral formula for derivatives** also holds.

The exponent in the denominator is three, of the form $n+1$ with $n=2$. Thus the integral depends on the second derivative of the numerator at zero. We divide the derivative by $2!$ because the quadratic Taylor coefficient is $\dfrac{f''(0)}{2!}$. Dividing the series by $z^3$ makes this the coefficient of $z^{-1}$. The integral of $z^{-1}$ around the positively oriented circle is $2\pi i$, so this coefficient gives the same result.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Evaluate $\int_{|z|=1}\dfrac{e^{2z}}{z^3}\,dz$ with counterclockwise orientation.

$$
\begin{aligned}
f(z)&=e^{2z}\\[6pt]
f'(z)&=2e^{2z}\\[6pt]
f''(z)&=4e^{2z}\\[6pt]
f''(0)&=4e^0\\[6pt]
&=4
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_{|z|=1}\frac{e^{2z}}{z^3}\,dz
&=\frac{2\pi i}{2!}f''(0)\\[6pt]
&=\pi i\cdot4\\[6pt]
&=4\pi i
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The function $f(z)=e^{2z}$ is entire. The unit circle is simple, positively oriented, and encloses $0$. Thus the hypotheses of **Cauchy's integral formula for derivatives** hold. The exponent three in the denominator gives derivative order two.

Each differentiation introduces the factor $2$ by the chain rule, so the coefficient in the second derivative is $4$. Evaluate the derivative at zero before applying the factor $\dfrac{2\pi i}{2!}$. Dividing by $2!$ converts the second derivative to the quadratic Taylor coefficient. Indeed, the quadratic term of $e^{2z}$ is $2z^2$, which becomes $\dfrac{2}{z}$ after division. Its integral around the positively oriented unit circle contributes $4\pi i$.

</div>
