## Exercise 1

How many zeros does $p(z)=z^{3}+z+1$ have in the disk $|z|<2$, counted with multiplicity?

$$
\begin{aligned}
g(z)&=z^3,\qquad h(z)=z+1\\[6pt]
p(z)&=g(z)+h(z)\\[6pt]
|g(z)|&=2^3=8\qquad (|z|=2)\\[6pt]
|h(z)|&\le |z|+1\\[6pt]
&=3<8\\[6pt]
N_{|z|<2}(p)&=N_{|z|<2}(g)\\[6pt]
&=3
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Compare the given polynomial with the monomial $g(z)=z^3$. Both are entire, so they are holomorphic on a neighborhood of the closed disk of radius two. The modulus of $g$ is constant and equal to eight on the circle. By the triangle inequality the perturbation $h(z)=z+1$ has modulus at most three at every boundary point.

The inequality is strict on the entire contour, so **Rouché's theorem** applies. The polynomial $p$ and the monomial $g$ have the same number of zeros in the disk, counted with multiplicity. The monomial has only the origin as a zero, of order three. Moreover, $|p(z)|\ge|g(z)|-|h(z)|\ge5$ on the boundary, so there are no zeros on the circle. The count therefore applies to the open disk.

</div>

## Exercise 2

How many zeros does $p(z)=z^{4}+z+1$ have in the disk $|z|<2$, counted with multiplicity?

$$
\begin{aligned}
g(z)&=z^4\\[6pt]
p(z)-g(z)&=z+1\\[6pt]
\max_{|z|=2}|p(z)-g(z)|&=3\\[6pt]
\min_{|z|=2}|g(z)|&=16\\[6pt]
|p(z)|&\ge16-3\\[6pt]
&=13\qquad (|z|=2)\\[6pt]
N_{|z|<2}(p)&=N_{|z|<2}(g)\\[6pt]
&=4
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The term $z+1$ has maximum modulus three on the circle of radius two. The triangle inequality gives the upper bound, attained at $z=2$. The monomial $g(z)=z^4$ has modulus sixteen throughout the boundary. This also gives a positive lower bound for the modulus of $p$, so the circle contains no zeros.

Both polynomials are holomorphic on the entire plane. The strict inequality $|p-g|<|g|$ holds on the contour, so the hypotheses of **Rouché's theorem** are satisfied. All the zeros of $g$ occur at zero, of multiplicity four. Hence $p$ has the same number of zeros counted with multiplicity. Since $p$ has degree four, this accounts for all its complex zeros by the fundamental theorem of algebra.

</div>
