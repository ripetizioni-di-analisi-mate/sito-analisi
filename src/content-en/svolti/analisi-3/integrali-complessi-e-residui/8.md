## Exercise 1

Evaluate $\displaystyle\int_\gamma \dfrac{z^2+1}{z+1}\,dz$, where $\gamma$ is the positively oriented circle $|z+1|=\dfrac12$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
f(z)&=z^2+1\\[6pt]
z+1&=z-(-1)\\[6pt]
|(-1)+1|&=0<\frac12\\[6pt]
f(-1)&=(-1)^2+1\\[6pt]
&=2
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_\gamma \frac{z^2+1}{z+1}\,dz
&=2\pi i\, f(-1)\\[6pt]
&=2\pi i\cdot2\\[6pt]
&=4\pi i
\end{aligned}
$$

::grafico{uguale=1 nomi="Re,Im" xmin=-2 xmax=0.85 ymin=-1 ymax=1 curve="-1+0.5*cos(t),0.5*sin(t),0,6.283185307179586" punti="-1,0,vuoto,−1" titolo="The denominator vanishes at the center of the circle, even though the circle does not enclose the origin."}

<div class="spiegazione">

The circle has center $-1$ and radius $\dfrac12$. The denominator factor is $z-(-1)$, so the evaluation point is the center and lies inside the contour. The numerator $f$ is polynomial and hence holomorphic throughout the plane. The circle is simple and positively oriented. Thus the hypotheses of **Cauchy's integral formula** hold.

The denominator contains the first power of $z-(-1)$, so the derivative order in the formula is zero and we evaluate $f(-1)$ directly. The origin lies outside the circle since it is one unit from the center, but the denominator vanishes only at $-1$. Moving the contour while preserving positive orientation and keeping $-1$ inside would give the same value because the numerator is entire.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Evaluate $\displaystyle\int_\gamma \dfrac{\cos z}{(z-\pi)^3}\,dz$ on the clockwise circle $|z-\pi|=1$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
f(z)&=\cos z\\[6pt]
f'(z)&=-\sin z\\[6pt]
f''(z)&=-\cos z\\[6pt]
f''(\pi)&=-\cos\pi\\[6pt]
&=1
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_{\gamma_+}\frac{\cos z}{(z-\pi)^3}\,dz
&=\frac{2\pi i}{2!}f''(\pi)\\[6pt]
&=\pi i\\[6pt]
\int_\gamma\frac{\cos z}{(z-\pi)^3}\,dz
&=-\int_{\gamma_+}\frac{\cos z}{(z-\pi)^3}\,dz\\[6pt]
&=-\pi i
\end{aligned}
$$

::grafico{uguale=1 nomi="Re,Im" xmin=1.4 xmax=4.8 ymin=-1.5 ymax=1.5 curve="pi+cos(t),sin(t),0,6.283185307179586" vettori="2.92,0.97,3.36,0.97" punti="3.141592653589793,0,vuoto,π" titolo="The arrow on the upper segment points to the right, so the contour is clockwise."}

<div class="spiegazione">

Cosine is entire, and $\pi$ is the center of the disk of radius one. Thus **Cauchy's integral formula for derivatives** applies to the counterclockwise circle $\gamma_+$. The denominator has exponent three, so we need the second derivative of the numerator. The first differentiation gives $-\sin z$ and the second $-\cos z$. At $\pi$ the value is one because $\cos\pi=-1$.

The given circle consists of the same points traversed clockwise. Reversing the parametrization interchanges the integration limits and changes the sign of the integral. Thus the orientation enters after evaluating the derivative and the factor $\dfrac{2\pi i}{2!}$. In particular, the positive sign of $f''(\pi)$ is the same for both orientations.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Evaluate the integral of $\dfrac{e^z}{z(z-2)}$ around the positively oriented circles $|z|=1$ and $|z|=3$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
r_0&=\lim_{z\to0}z\frac{e^z}{z(z-2)}\\[6pt]
&=\left.\frac{e^z}{z-2}\right|_{z=0}\\[6pt]
&=-\frac12\\[6pt]
r_2&=\lim_{z\to2}(z-2)\frac{e^z}{z(z-2)}\\[6pt]
&=\left.\frac{e^z}{z}\right|_{z=2}\\[6pt]
&=\frac{e^2}{2}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_{|z|=1}\frac{e^z}{z(z-2)}\,dz
&=2\pi i\,r_0\\[6pt]
&=-\pi i\\[6pt]
\int_{|z|=3}\frac{e^z}{z(z-2)}\,dz
&=2\pi i(r_0+r_2)\\[6pt]
&=2\pi i\left(-\frac12+\frac{e^2}{2}\right)\\[6pt]
&=\pi i(e^2-1)
\end{aligned}
$$

::grafico{uguale=1 nomi="Re,Im" xmin=-3.6 xmax=3.6 ymin=-3.6 ymax=3.6 curve="cos(t),sin(t),0,6.283185307179586;3*cos(t),3*sin(t),0,6.283185307179586" punti="0,0,vuoto,0;2,0,vuoto,2" titolo="Increasing the radius from one to three adds the contribution of the pole $2$."}

<div class="spiegazione">

The exponential numerator is entire and vanishes neither at zero nor at two. The denominator has two distinct simple zeros, so the function has precisely the simple poles $0$ and $2$. Denote their residues by $r_0$ and $r_2$, respectively. For each, cancel the factor vanishing at the pole and evaluate the resulting extension.

The circle of radius one encloses only the origin. The circle of radius three encloses both poles. Neither passes through a pole and both are positively oriented, so the **residue theorem** applies in each case. The integral along the outer contour minus that along the inner contour is $2\pi i\,r_2$. This contribution comes from the pole $2$ between the two circles.

</div>
