## Exercise 1

Evaluate $\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb R}\dfrac{dx}{(x^2+4)^2}$ using the upper semicircle.

$$
\begin{aligned}
f(z)&=\frac1{(z^2+4)^2}\\[6pt]
(z-2i)^2 f(z)&=\frac1{(z+2i)^2}\\[6pt]
\frac{d}{dz}\frac1{(z+2i)^2}&=-\frac2{(z+2i)^3}\\[6pt]
\operatorname{Res}(f,2i)&=-\frac2{(4i)^3}\\[6pt]
&=\frac1{32i}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\left|\int_{\Gamma_R} f(z)\,dz\right|
&\le\frac{\pi R}{(R^2-4)^2}
\underset{R\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}0\\[6pt]
\int_{-R}^R f(x)\,dx
&=2\pi i\frac1{32i}-\int_{\Gamma_R} f(z)\,dz\\[6pt]
\int_{\mathbb R} \frac{dx}{(x^2+4)^2}
&=2\pi i\frac1{32i}\\[6pt]
&=\frac{\pi}{16}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The real segment from $-R$ to $R$ and the upper semicircle $\Gamma_R$ form a positively oriented contour. For $R>2$ the only enclosed singularity is $2i$. The factor $z^2+4$ has a simple zero at $2i$, so its square produces a double pole. The product $(z-2i)^2f(z)$ is holomorphic near the pole and nonzero at $2i$. Thus the residue is the first derivative of this product evaluated at the pole.

On the semicircle we have $|z^2+4|\ge R^2-4$, and the arc length is $\pi R$. The resulting bound is of order $R^{-3}$, so the arc integral tends to zero. On the real line the integrand is continuous and decays like $x^{-4}$. The real integral therefore converges absolutely, and the **residue theorem** gives its value from the upper pole alone.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Evaluate $\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb R}\dfrac{\cos(2x)}{x^2+9}\,dx$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
f(z)&=\frac{e^{2iz}}{z^2+9}\\[6pt]
|e^{2i(x+iy)}|&=|e^{2ix}e^{-2y}|\\[6pt]
&=e^{-2y}\le1\qquad (y\ge0)\\[6pt]
\operatorname{Res}(f,3i)&=\lim_{z\to3i}\frac{e^{2iz}}{z+3i}\\[6pt]
&=\frac{e^{-6}}{6i}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\left|\int_{\Gamma_R} f(z)\,dz\right|
&\le\frac{\pi R}{R^2-9}
\underset{R\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}0\\[6pt]
\int_{\mathbb R} f(x)\,dx
&=2\pi i\frac{e^{-6}}{6i}\\[6pt]
&=\frac{\pi e^{-6}}3\\[6pt]
\int_{\mathbb R}\frac{\cos(2x)}{x^2+9}\,dx
&=\operatorname{Re}\int_{\mathbb R} f(x)\,dx\\[6pt]
&=\frac{\pi e^{-6}}3
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

On the real axis the cosine is the real part of $e^{2iz}$ for $z=x$. For $z=x+iy$ the exponential has modulus $e^{-2y}$, bounded by one in the upper half-plane. For $R>3$ the upper arc $\Gamma_R$ and the real segment form a positively oriented contour enclosing only $3i$. The denominator has a simple zero there and the exponential equals $e^{-6}$, so the residue is the limit after multiplication by $z-3i$.

The arc has length $\pi R$ and the denominator has modulus at least $R^2-9$, so its contribution tends to zero. On the real line the bound $|f(x)|=\dfrac1{x^2+9}$ is integrable. Thus we may take the real part of the complex integral. Its imaginary part vanishes because it is the integral of an odd, absolutely integrable function.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Count the zeros of $p(z)=z^5-4z+1$ in the annulus $1<|z|<2$, including multiplicities.

$$
\begin{aligned}
|z^5+1|&\le 1+1\\[6pt]
&=2<4=|-4z|\qquad(|z|=1)\\[6pt]
N_{|z|<1}(p)&=N_{|z|<1}(-4z)\\[6pt]
&=1
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
|-4z+1|&\le 4\cdot2+1\\[6pt]
&=9<32=|z^5|\qquad(|z|=2)\\[6pt]
N_{|z|<2}(p)&=N_{|z|<2}(z^5)\\[6pt]
&=5\\[6pt]
N_{1<|z|<2}(p)&=5-1\\[6pt]
&=4
\end{aligned}
$$

::grafico{uguale=1 nomi="Re,Im" xmin=-2.65 xmax=2.65 ymin=-2.65 ymax=2.65 curve="cos(t),sin(t),0,6.283185307179586;2*cos(t),2*sin(t),0,6.283185307179586" etichette="0.30,0.32,1 zero;0,1.50,4 zeros" titolo="The dominant monomial changes from the inner circle to the outer circle."}

<div class="spiegazione">

First count the zeros in each disk. The polynomial and the comparison functions are entire, so the holomorphicity hypothesis holds on neighborhoods of the closed disks. On the inner circle the linear term has modulus four and the remainder has modulus at most two. Thus **Rouché's theorem** applies with the linear term, which has a single zero of multiplicity one.

On the outer circle the term $z^5$ has modulus thirty-two and the remainder has modulus at most nine. Here the theorem gives the polynomial the same number of zeros as $z^5$, namely five. The inequalities are strict, so neither boundary contains a zero. Subtracting the count in the inner disk from the one in the outer disk gives the count in the open annulus.

</div>

## Exercise 4

Evaluate $\displaystyle\int_\gamma \dfrac{f'(z)}{f(z)}\,dz$, where $f(z)=\dfrac{(z-1)^2(z+3)}{z^2(z-2)}$ and $\gamma$ is the positively oriented circle $|z|=\dfrac32$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{f'(z)}{f(z)}
&=\frac2{z-1}+\frac1{z+3}-\frac2z-\frac1{z-2}\\[6pt]
N_{\mathrm{int}}&=2 \quad(z=1)\\[6pt]
P_{\mathrm{int}}&=2 \quad(z=0)
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_\gamma \frac{f'(z)}{f(z)}\,dz
&=2\pi i\,(N_{\mathrm{int}}-P_{\mathrm{int}})\\[6pt]
&=2\pi i\,(2-2)\\[6pt]
&=0
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The rational function $f$ is meromorphic, and its numerator and denominator have no common factors. The zero $1$ has multiplicity two and $-3$ has multiplicity one. The pole $0$ has order two and $2$ has order one. Only $1$ and $0$ have modulus less than $\dfrac32$, so the other two points lie outside.

No zero or pole lies on the contour and its orientation is positive, so the **argument principle** applies. The term $\dfrac2{z-1}$ in the logarithmic derivative shows directly that the zero contributes residue two. The term $-\dfrac2z$ shows that the pole contributes residue minus two. Their sum is zero because the two interior points have the same order. The exterior singularities do not affect the calculation on this circle.

</div>
