## Exercise 1

Find the squared distance from $x$ to the subspace of constants in $L^2(0,1)$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
c &= \frac{\int_0^1x\,dx}{\int_0^1 1\,dx} \\
&= \frac12. \\[5pt]
d(x,M)^2 &= \int_0^1\left(x-\frac12\right)^2\,dx \\
&= \int_0^1\left(x^2-x+\frac14\right)\,dx \\
&= \left[\frac{x^3}{3}-\frac{x^2}{2}+\frac{x}{4}\right]_0^1 \\
&= \frac13-\frac12+\frac14 \\
&= \frac{4-6+3}{12} \\
&= \frac1{12}.
\end{aligned}
$$

::grafico{f="(x-0.5)^2;(x^2-1/3)^2" legenda="(x-\frac12)^2;(x^2-\frac13)^2" xmin=0 xmax=1.04 ymin=0 ymax=0.52 titolo="The curves show the squared residuals for the two projections. The corresponding areas are $\dfrac1{12}$ and $\dfrac4{45}$."}

<div class="spiegazione">

The subspace $M$ of constants is one-dimensional and hence closed. The orthogonal projection therefore attains the distance. Its constant value $c$ is the inner product of $x$ with $1$ divided by the squared norm of $1$. The integral of the squared generator is one, so $c=\dfrac12$.

Integrating the square of the residual gives the squared distance. The cross term is $-x$ since twice the coefficient subtracted is one. The constant term is $\dfrac14$. Evaluating the antiderivative gives three fractions, which sum to $\dfrac1{12}$ when written over the common denominator twelve. The positive sign agrees with the graph, where the integrand is positive except at the zero of the residual.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Find the squared distance from $x^{2}$ to the subspace of constants in $L^2(0,1)$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
c &= \int_0^1 x^2\,dx \\
&= \frac13. \\[4pt]
\|x^2\|_2^2 &= \left[\frac{x^5}{5}\right]_0^1 \\
&= \frac15. \\[4pt]
\|P_M(x^2)\|_2^2 &= \int_0^1 \left(\frac13\right)^2\,dx \\
&= \frac19. \\[4pt]
d(x^2,M)^2 &= \|x^2\|_2^2-\|P_M(x^2)\|_2^2 \\
&= \frac15-\frac19 \\
&= \frac{9-5}{45} \\
&= \frac4{45}.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The projection onto the constants exists because that subspace is finite-dimensional and therefore closed. The constant is $\dfrac13$, found by taking the inner product with the unit generator $1$. The residual is orthogonal to the subspace and therefore also to the projection. The decomposition into these two components thus satisfies the hypotheses of the **Pythagorean identity**.

The squared norm of the original function is the integral of $x^4$, equal to $\dfrac15$. The squared norm of the projection is $\dfrac19$, since we integrate a constant over an interval of length one. Subtracting isolates the contribution from the residual. This difference is the squared distance because the projection minimizes the residual norm.

</div>
