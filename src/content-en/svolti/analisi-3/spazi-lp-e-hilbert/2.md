## Exercise 1

For which $p\in[1,\infty)$ does $f(x)=x^{-\dfrac1{3}}$ belong to $L^p(0,1)$?

$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_\varepsilon^1 |f(x)|^p\,dx &= \int_\varepsilon^1 x^{-\frac{p}{3}}\,dx \\[3pt]
&=
\begin{cases}
\frac{1-\varepsilon^{1-\frac{p}{3}}}{1-\frac{p}{3}}, & p\ne 3,\\[5pt]
-\log\varepsilon, & p=3.
\end{cases} \\[6pt]
\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\int_\varepsilon^1 |f(x)|^p\,dx
&=
\begin{cases}
\frac{3}{3-p}, & 1\le p<3,\\[5pt]
+\infty, & p\ge 3.
\end{cases} \\[6pt]
f\in L^p(0,1) &\iff 1\le p<3.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The function is continuous on every interval $[\varepsilon,1]$ with $\varepsilon>0$, so an antiderivative gives its integral there. Its only singularity is at zero. Since the integrand is nonnegative and the intervals increase as $\varepsilon$ decreases, **monotone convergence** identifies the limit of the truncated integrals with the integral on $(0,1)$.

If $p<3$, the exponent $1-\dfrac p3$ is positive, so the term evaluated at $\varepsilon$ tends to zero. For $p>3$ that exponent is negative. The numerator then tends to minus infinity and the denominator is negative, so the integral diverges to positive infinity. When $p=3$, the integrand is $x^{-1}$ and the divergence is logarithmic. Thus the threshold three is excluded.

</div>

## Exercise 2

For which $p\in[1,\infty)$ does $f(x)=x^{-\dfrac1{4}}$ belong to $L^p(0,1)$?

$$
\begin{aligned}
|f(x)|^p &= x^{-\frac{p}{4}}. \\[4pt]
\int_\varepsilon^1 x^{-\frac{p}{4}}\,dx
&=
\begin{cases}
\frac{1-\varepsilon^{1-\frac p4}}{1-\frac p4}, & p\ne4,\\[5pt]
-\log\varepsilon, & p=4.
\end{cases} \\[6pt]
\int_0^1 |f(x)|^3\,dx &= \left[4x^{\frac14}\right]_0^1 \\
&=4. \\[5pt]
\int_0^1 |f(x)|^p\,dx <\infty
&\iff 1-\frac p4>0. \\[5pt]
f\in L^p(0,1) &\iff 1\le p<4.
\end{aligned}
$$

::grafico{f="x^(-3/4);1/x" legenda="|f|^3;|f|^4" xmin=0.045 xmax=1.05 ymin=0 ymax=8.7 titolo="For $f(x)=x^{-\dfrac14}$, the exponent $p=3$ is still admissible. At $p=4$ the integral diverges logarithmically."}

<div class="spiegazione">

The exponent to compare with $-1$ is that of $|f|^p$, namely $-\dfrac p4$. Nonnegativity of the integrand justifies taking the limit of the integral over $[\varepsilon,1]$. For $p<4$ the antiderivative has positive exponent and a finite limit at zero. For $p>4$ its exponent is negative, so its absolute value diverges. At $p=4$ the logarithm tends to minus infinity at zero and the truncated integral grows without bound.

The graph compares two exponents for which both curves grow near zero. Growth alone does not decide integrability. Evaluating the antiderivatives distinguishes $p=3$, which gives finite area, from the critical value $p=4$.

</div>
