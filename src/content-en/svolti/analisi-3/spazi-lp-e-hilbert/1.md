## Exercise 1

Compute $\|x\|_{L^2(0,1)}$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\|x\|_{L^2(0,1)}^2 &= \int_0^1 |x|^2\,dx \\
&= \int_0^1 x^2\,dx \\
&= \left[\frac{x^3}{3}\right]_0^1 \\
&= \frac{1^3}{3}-\frac{0^3}{3} \\
&= \frac{1}{3}. \\[6pt]
\|x\|_{L^2(0,1)} &= \sqrt{\frac{1}{3}} \\
&= \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}.
\end{aligned}
$$

::grafico{f="x^2;x^4" area="x^2;x^4;0;1" legenda="x^2;x^4" xmin=0 xmax=1.08 ymin=0 ymax=1.22 titolo="The areas under the two curves equal the squared norms. The difference between the areas is $\dfrac13-\dfrac15$."}

<div class="spiegazione">

The squared norm is the integral of the squared absolute value. The monomial is real and positive on the given interval, so the integrand is $x^2$. It extends continuously to $[0,1]$, so its Lebesgue and Riemann integrals agree and we may evaluate an antiderivative at the endpoints. The contribution at zero vanishes, while at one the antiderivative equals $\dfrac{1}{3}$.

The area under $y=x^2$ is therefore the **squared norm**. By definition the norm is the nonnegative square root of this area. Take the square root after computing the integral, as required by the order of operations in the norm.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Compute $\|x^{2}\|_{L^2(0,1)}$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\|x^2\|_{L^2(0,1)}^2 &= \int_0^1 |x^2|^2\,dx \\
&= \int_0^1 x^4\,dx \\
&= \left[\frac{x^5}{5}\right]_0^1 \\
&= \frac{1^5-0^5}{5} \\
&= \frac{1}{5}. \\[6pt]
\|x^2\|_{L^2(0,1)} &= \sqrt{\frac{1}{5}} \\
&= \frac{1}{\sqrt{5}}.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Here the given monomial already has degree two. Taking its squared absolute value doubles the degree, giving integrand $x^4$. Since $x^4$ is continuous on the closed interval, use the antiderivative obtained by increasing the exponent to five and dividing by five. Its value at the lower endpoint is zero. Its value at the upper endpoint gives the squared norm, so the last step is to take the square root.

The integrands give a direct comparison with the first exercise. For $0<x<1$ we have $x^4<x^2$, with a strict difference on an interval of positive measure. The integral of $x^4$ is therefore strictly smaller as well, and taking square roots preserves the inequality. In terms of areas the difference is $\dfrac{2}{15}$. This is the difference of the *squared* norms because each integral uses the square of its monomial.

</div>
