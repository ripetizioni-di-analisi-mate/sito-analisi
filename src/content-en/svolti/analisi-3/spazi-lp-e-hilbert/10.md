## Exercise 1

Let $e_1,e_2,e_3$ be orthonormal and let $v=e_1-2e_2+3e_3$. Find the projection onto $\operatorname{span}(e_1,e_3)$ and the squared distance.

$$
\begin{aligned}
M&=\operatorname{span}\{e_1,e_3\}. \\[4pt]
\langle v,e_1\rangle&=1-2\cdot0+3\cdot0 \\
&=1. \\[4pt]
\langle v,e_3\rangle&=0-2\cdot0+3\cdot1 \\
&=3. \\[4pt]
P_Mv &= \langle v,e_1\rangle e_1+\langle v,e_3\rangle e_3\\
&=e_1+3e_3. \\[4pt]
v-P_Mv&=-2e_2. \\[4pt]
d(v,M)^2&=\|-2e_2\|^2\\
&=|-2|^2\|e_2\|^2\\
&=4.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The span of $e_1$ and $e_3$ is closed because it is finite-dimensional. The generators are orthonormal, so each projection coefficient is the inner product of the input with its corresponding generator. In the expansion of $\langle v,e_1\rangle$, only the component along $e_1$ contributes. For $\langle v,e_3\rangle$ only the component along $e_3$ contributes.

Subtracting the projection removes these two components and leaves $-2e_2$. Since $e_2$ is orthogonal to both generators, the residual is orthogonal to every linear combination of them. Its norm is therefore the minimum distance. The norm of $e_2$ is one, so the absolute value of the coefficient $-2$ gives the distance. Its square is four.

</div>

## Exercise 2

For $f(t)=2\sin t+\cos(3t)$ in $L^2(-\pi,\pi)$, compute the squared norm and the squared error in projecting onto $\operatorname{span}(\sin t)$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\langle \sin t,\cos(3t)\rangle &=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\sin t\cos(3t)\,dt \\
&=0. \\[4pt]
\|\sin t\|_2^2 &=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\frac{1-\cos(2t)}2\,dt \\
&=\pi. \\[4pt]
\|\cos(3t)\|_2^2 &=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\frac{1+\cos(6t)}2\,dt \\
&=\pi. \\[4pt]
\|f\|_2^2 &= 4\|\sin t\|_2^2+\|\cos(3t)\|_2^2 \\
&=5\pi. \\[4pt]
P_M f&=2\sin t. \\[4pt]
d(f,M)^2&=\|f-P_M f\|_2^2\\
&=\|\cos(3t)\|_2^2\\
&=\pi.
\end{aligned}
$$

::grafico{f="2*sin(x)+cos(3*x);2*sin(x)" legenda="f;P_M f" xmin=-3.15 xmax=3.15 ymin=-3.24 ymax=3.26 titolo="The difference between the original function and its projection is the harmonic $\cos(3t)$." nomi="t,y"}

<div class="spiegazione">

The product $\sin t\cos(3t)$ is odd because sine is odd and cosine is even. Since the integration interval is symmetric, the inner product vanishes. Use the double-angle identities for the squared norms. The constant term contributes $\pi$ to both integrals. The antiderivatives of the cosines of frequencies two and six take equal values at the endpoints, so their contributions vanish.

The two modes are orthogonal, so the **Pythagorean identity** gives the sum of their squared norms. The span of $\sin t$ is one-dimensional and closed. The component $2\sin t$ belongs to it and the cosine is orthogonal to the generator. Thus the former is the projection and the squared norm of the cosine alone gives the required error.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Find the representing vector and norm of $\ell(f)=\int_0^1f(x)(1+ix)\,dx$ in the complex space $L^2(0,1)$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
h(x)&=1-ix. \\[4pt]
\langle f,h\rangle&=\int_0^1f(x)\overline{h(x)}\,dx \\
&=\ell(f). \\[4pt]
|h(x)|^2&=(1-ix)(1+ix) \\
&=1+x^2. \\[4pt]
\|h\|_2^2&=\int_0^1(1+x^2)\,dx \\
&=\left[x+\frac{x^3}{3}\right]_0^1 \\
&=1+\frac13 \\
&=\frac43. \\[4pt]
|\ell(f)|&\le\|f\|_2\|h\|_2. \\[4pt]
\left|\ell\left(\frac{h}{\|h\|_2}\right)\right|&=\|h\|_2. \\[4pt]
h(x)&=1-ix,\qquad \|\ell\|=\frac2{\sqrt3}.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The inner product is linear in the first argument and conjugate-linear in the second. To produce the weight $1+ix$ in the integral the representing vector must therefore equal $1-ix$. Its squared absolute value is $1+x^2$ since multiplication by the conjugate cancels the imaginary terms. The integral is finite, so $h$ belongs to $L^2$ and Cauchy–Schwarz gives continuity of the functional.

The **Riesz representation theorem** therefore applies to this continuous linear functional. The bound holds throughout the unit ball, and the normalized representing vector gives equality. The norm is the square root of $\dfrac43$. Conjugation determines the representing vector, but its norm equals that of the weight because conjugation preserves absolute value.

</div>

## Exercise 4

Let $(e_n)$ be the standard basis of $\ell^2$. Does $u_n=e_1+e_n$ for $n\ge2$ converge weakly or in norm to $e_1$?

$$
\begin{aligned}
u_n-e_1&=e_n\qquad(n\ge2). \\[4pt]
\langle u_n-e_1,y\rangle&=\langle e_n,y\rangle \\
&=\overline{y_n}\longrightarrow0\qquad(y\in\ell^2). \\[4pt]
u_n&\rightharpoonup e_1. \\[4pt]
\|u_n-e_1\|_{\ell^2}&=\|e_n\|_{\ell^2} \\
&=1. \\[4pt]
u_n&\text{ does not converge in norm to }e_1.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

For weak convergence, fix any vector $y\in\ell^2$ and take its inner product with the difference $u_n-e_1$, placing the difference in the first argument. The standard basis vector $e_n$ has only one nonzero component, so the result is the complex conjugate of coordinate $y_n$. The series $\sum_n|y_n|^2$ converges, so its terms tend to zero and $y_n$ also tends to zero.

The argument holds for every fixed test vector and thus verifies **weak convergence** in the Hilbert space. Norm convergence would require the norm of the entire difference to tend to zero. The difference is a standard basis vector at every index, so its norm is always one. Shifting the single unit coordinate suffices for weak convergence while leaving the distance unchanged.

</div>
