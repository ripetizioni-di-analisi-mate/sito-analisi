## Exercise 1

On a nonzero Banach space, let $A=\dfrac{1}{3}I$. Compute $\|(I-A)^{-1}\|$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\|A\|&=\frac{1}{3}\|I\|\\
&=\frac{1}{3}<1,\\
(I-A)^{-1}&=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}A^n\\
&=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}3^{-n}I\\
&=\frac{1}{1-\frac13}I\\
&=\frac{3}{2} I,\\
\|(I-A)^{-1}\|&=\frac{3}{2}.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Since the space is Banach, its bounded operators form a complete space in the operator norm. The original space is nonzero and contains a unit vector, whose norm the identity preserves. Therefore $\|I\|=1$. The operator $A$ is linear and bounded because it is a multiple of the identity. Its scalar coefficient has absolute value one third, so the strict norm bound for the **Neumann series** holds. Every power of $A$ is again a multiple of $I$, with the corresponding power of one third as coefficient. The series converges in norm to the multiple of the identity whose coefficient is the sum of the scalar series. The geometric series formula gives three halves. The inverse maps every unit vector to one of norm three halves. Thus the norm of the inverse equals this coefficient and is attained everywhere on the unit sphere.

</div>

## Exercise 2

On a nonzero Banach space, let $A=\dfrac{1}{4}I$. Compute $\|(I-A)^{-1}\|$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
I-A&=I-\frac{1}{4} I\\
&=\frac{3}{4} I,\\
\left(\frac{3}{4} I\right)\left(\frac{4}{3} I\right)&=I,\\
\left(\frac{4}{3} I\right)\left(\frac{3}{4} I\right)&=I,\\
(I-A)^{-1}&=\frac{4}{3} I.
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\|(I-A)^{-1}x\|&=\left\|\frac{4}{3}x\right\|\\
&=\frac{4}{3}\|x\|,\\
\|(I-A)^{-1}\|&=\frac{4}{3}.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The identity operator is bounded with norm one because it preserves every unit vector. Such vectors exist because the space is nonzero. The norm of $A$ is one quarter, and since the domain is Banach the Neumann series criterion applies. The inverse can also be found directly here. The difference $I-A$ multiplies every vector by three quarters. The reciprocal of this coefficient is four thirds. Composing the corresponding multiple of the identity with the original operator in either order gives the identity, so it is both a left and a right inverse. By homogeneity of the norm, the ratio of output norm to input norm is four thirds for every nonzero input. Its supremum over the unit vectors is therefore four thirds. The general Neumann bound is exact here because all powers are positive multiples of the same operator.

</div>
