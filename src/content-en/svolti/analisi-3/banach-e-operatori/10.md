## Exercise 1

On $C([0,1])$ with the $L^1$ norm, let $n\ge2$ index the function $f_n$ that is zero up to $\dfrac{1}{2}$, increases linearly to one between $\dfrac{1}{2}$ and $\dfrac{1}{2}+\dfrac{1}{n}$, and equals one thereafter. Does the sequence converge in this space?

$$
\begin{aligned}
g(x)&=\mathbf1_{(\frac{1}{2},1]}(x),\\
\|f_n-g\|_1&=\int_{\frac{1}{2}}^{\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{n}}
  \left(1-n\left(x-\frac{1}{2}\right)\right)\,dx\\
&=\int_0^{\frac{1}{n}}(1-nu)\,du\\
&=\left[u-\frac{n}{2}u^2\right]_0^{\frac{1}{n}}\\
&=\frac{1}{n}-\frac{1}{2n}\\
&=\frac{1}{2n}.
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\|f_n-f_m\|_1&\le\|f_n-g\|_1+\|f_m-g\|_1\\
&=\frac{1}{2n}+\frac{1}{2m}\longrightarrow0.
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
f_n\to h\ \text{in norm }L^1
&\Longrightarrow h=g\quad\text{almost everywhere}\\
&\Longrightarrow h\bigl(\frac{1}{2}-\bigr)=0,\quad h\bigl(\frac{1}{2}+\bigr)=1
\quad\text{if }h\in C([0,1]).
\end{aligned}
$$

Thus the sequence is Cauchy but has no limit in $C([0,1])$ with the $L^1$ norm.

::grafico{linee="0,0 0.5,0 0.75,1 1,1;0,0 0.5,0 0.625,1 1,1" segmenti="0.5,0,0.5,1" xmin=-0.06 xmax=1.1 ymin=-0.12 ymax=1.15 titolo="The ramps for $n=4$ and $n=8$ approximate a jump. The $L^1$ error is the area of the triangle between the ramp and the step function."}

<div class="spiegazione">

The ramp and the step function coincide except on the rising segment and at its endpoints, which do not affect the integral. On this segment the step equals one and the ramp increases from zero to one. Their absolute difference therefore decreases linearly from one to zero. Moving the origin to the midpoint gives a triangle of base $\dfrac{1}{n}$ whose area is the error. The distance in $L^1$ tends to zero, and the triangle inequality gives the Cauchy property explicitly. A possible continuous limit would have to agree almost everywhere with $g$, by uniqueness of the limit in $L^1$. A continuous function that is zero almost everywhere on an open interval is zero at every point there, since a nonzero value would persist on a neighborhood. The same argument applies to the value one to the right of the midpoint. The one-sided limits would differ, which rules out a continuous limit.

</div>

## Exercise 2

On $C([0,1])$ with the supremum norm, let $Tf(x)=\int_0^x (x-t)f(t)\,dt$. Find the norm of $T$ and verify that it is compact.

$$
\begin{aligned}
|Tf(x)|&\le \int_0^x (x-t)|f(t)|\,dt\\
&\le\|f\|_\infty\int_0^x(x-t)\,dt\\
&=\|f\|_\infty\left[xt-\frac{t^2}{2}\right]_0^x\\
&=\frac{x^2}{2}\|f\|_\infty,\\
\|Tf\|_\infty&\le\frac{1}{2}\|f\|_\infty,\\
(T1)(1)&=\frac{1}{2},\\
\|T\|&=\frac{1}{2} .
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
(Tf)'(x)&=(x-x)f(x)+\int_0^x f(t)\,dt\\
&=\int_0^x f(t)\,dt,\\
|(Tf)'(x)|&\le x\|f\|_\infty\\
&\le \|f\|_\infty,\\
|Tf(x)-Tf(y)|&\le |x-y|\qquad(\|f\|_\infty\le1).
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
T\bigl(\{f:\|f\|_\infty\le1\}\bigr)
\text{ equicontinuous and pointwise bounded}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad T\text{ compact}.
$$

::grafico{f="x^2/2;-x^2/2" area="x^2/2;-x^2/2;0;1" punti="1,0.5,pieno;1,-0.5,pieno" xmin=0 xmax=1.12 ymin=-0.63 ymax=0.63 titolo="The images of the unit ball lie between the curves $\dfrac{x^2}{2}$ and $-\dfrac{x^2}{2}$."}

<div class="spiegazione">

The weight $x-t$ is nonnegative between zero and $x$. The triangle inequality and the uniform bound on the input therefore allow us to replace $|f(t)|$ by $\|f\|_\infty$. The integral of the weight is $\dfrac{x^2}{2}$ and attains its maximum at the right endpoint. For the constant function one, the output at that endpoint equals the upper bound, so the norm of $T$ is one half. For compactness, consider the family of all images of unit inputs. The previous estimate makes this family pointwise bounded. Differentiation with respect to $x$ is justified because the kernel and its partial derivative are continuous and so is the input. The upper-endpoint contribution vanishes because the weight vanishes there. The derivative has absolute value at most one throughout the interval, so the mean value theorem gives an equicontinuity bound independent of $f$. Since $[0,1]$ is compact, the **Arzelà–Ascoli theorem** gives relative compactness of the image of the unit ball.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Let $S:\ell^2\to\ell^2$ be the right shift, $S(x_1,x_2,\ldots)=(0,x_1,x_2,\ldots)$. Compute $\|S\|$ and decide whether $S$ is compact.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\|Sx\|_2^2&=|0|^2+\sum_{k=1}^\infty|x_k|^2\\
&=\|x\|_2^2,\\
\|Sx\|_2&=\|x\|_2,\\
\|S\|&=1.
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\|e_k\|_2&=1,\\
Se_k&=e_{k+1},\\
\|Se_j-Se_k\|_2^2&=|1|^2+|-1|^2\qquad(j\ne k)\\
&=2,\\
\|Se_j-Se_k\|_2&=\sqrt2 .
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
(Se_k)_{k\ge1}\text{ with no Cauchy subsequence}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad S\text{ not compact}.
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Inserting a zero shifts each input coordinate one position to the right. The sum of squared coordinate magnitudes is unchanged, showing both that $S$ takes values in $\ell^2$ and that it is an isometry. The norm of $S$ is one because it maps every unit vector to a unit vector. To test sequential compactness, take the sequence of standard basis vectors. It is bounded since all its terms have norm one. Their images are again standard basis vectors, with indices increased by one. For distinct indices the difference has just two nonzero coordinates, one equal to one and one to minus one. The distance between any two such images is therefore $\sqrt2$. This fixed positive separation rules out a Cauchy subsequence. Since every convergent subsequence would be Cauchy, this bounded sequence proves that $S$ is not compact.

</div>

## Exercise 4

For $n\ge1$, define $T_n:\ell^2(\mathbb R)\to\mathbb R$ by $T_nx=nx_n$. Determine whether the family is uniformly bounded in norm and whether it is pointwise bounded.

$$
\begin{aligned}
|T_nx|&=n|x_n|\\
&\le n\|x\|_2,\\
\|e_n\|_2&=1,\\
T_ne_n&=n,\\
\|T_n\|&=n,\\
\sup_{n\ge1}\|T_n\|&=+\infty.
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
x_{2^k}&=2^{-\frac{k}{2}}\quad(k\ge1),\\
x_n&=0\quad(n\text{ distinct from every }2^k,\ k\ge1),\\
\|x\|_2^2&=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}2^{-k}\\
&=1,\\
T_{2^k}x&=2^k\,2^{-\frac{k}{2}}\\
&=2^{\frac{k}{2}}\longrightarrow+\infty,\\
\sup_{n\ge1}|T_nx|&=+\infty.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Each $T_n$ is a continuous linear functional, since the absolute value of coordinate $n$ is at most the input's square-summable norm. The standard basis vector $e_n$ has norm one and attains the bound $n$, so the functional norms are unbounded. The domain $\ell^2$ is a Banach space. If the family were pointwise bounded, the **uniform boundedness principle** would give a common bound on its norms. The family must therefore be unbounded on at least one fixed input. To find such an input, use only coordinates with indices that are powers of two. The squares of the chosen coordinates form a convergent geometric series, so the vector belongs to the domain and has norm one. The index multiplying each nonzero coordinate grows faster than its reciprocal. Consequently, on this same vector the values at indices $2^k$ tend to infinity, directly demonstrating failure of pointwise boundedness.

</div>
