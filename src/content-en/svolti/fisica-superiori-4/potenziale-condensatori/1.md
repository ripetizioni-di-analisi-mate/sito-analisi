## Exercise 1

A point charge $Q=+2.4\,\mathrm{nC}$ is in vacuum. Calculate the potential at $20\,\mathrm{cm}$ from the charge, with zero at infinity and $k_e=8.99\cdot10^9\,\mathrm{N\,m^2/C^2}$.

::grafico{linee="1,2 5,2" punti="1,2,pieno,Q;5,2,pieno,P" etichette="3,2.7,20 cm" xmin=0 xmax=6 ymin=0 ymax=4 assi=0 titolo="We calculate the potential of the source Q at the point P."}

$$
\begin{aligned}
V&=k_e\frac{Q}{r}, \\[6pt]
Q&=2.4\cdot10^{-9}\,\mathrm C,\qquad r=0.20\,\mathrm m, \\[6pt]
k_eQ&=8.99\cdot10^9\cdot2.4\cdot10^{-9}\,\mathrm{V\,m}=21.6\,\mathrm{V\,m}, \\[6pt]
V&=\frac{21.6\,\mathrm{V\,m}}{0.20\,\mathrm m}\simeq108\,\mathrm V.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The potential of a point source depends only on its charge and on the distance of the point, so we apply $V=k_e\dfrac{Q}{r}$. The formula holds with zero at infinity, which is the reference stated in the problem. Since $k_e$ is given in $\mathrm{N\,m^2/C^2}$, nanocoulombs are converted to coulombs and centimeters to meters.

In the product $k_eQ$ the factors $10^9$ and $10^{-9}$ cancel, so $21.6\,\mathrm{V\,m}$ remains, and the quotient by $0.20\,\mathrm m$ gives volts. The source is positive, so the potential is positive. The potential at the point $P$ is about $108\,\mathrm V$. As a check, at double the distance, $40\,\mathrm{cm}$, the potential would be half, about $54\,\mathrm V$, because $V$ is inversely proportional to $r$. The units work out, since $\mathrm{N\,m^2/C^2}\cdot\mathrm C/\mathrm m=\mathrm{N\,m/C}=\mathrm{J/C}=\mathrm V$. A test charge $+1.0\,\mathrm{nC}$ placed at $P$ would have potential energy $qV\simeq1.08\cdot10^{-7}\,\mathrm J$. The value of $U$ depends on the sign and the value of the test charge, while $V$ depends only on the source.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Two fixed point sources $Q_1=+3\,\mathrm{nC}$ and $Q_2=-6\,\mathrm{nC}$ are at distances $20$ and $60\,\mathrm{cm}$ from $P$, respectively. Calculate $V_P$ and the energy of a test charge $q=-2\,\mathrm{nC}$ at $P$. Use $k_e\simeq9\cdot10^9\,\mathrm{N\,m^2/C^2}$ and zero at infinity.

$$
\begin{aligned}
V_1&=k_e\frac{Q_1}{r_1}=\frac{9\cdot10^9\cdot3\cdot10^{-9}}{0.20}\,\mathrm V=135\,\mathrm V, \\[6pt]
V_2&=k_e\frac{Q_2}{r_2}=\frac{9\cdot10^9\cdot(-6\cdot10^{-9})}{0.60}\,\mathrm V=-90\,\mathrm V, \\[6pt]
V_P&=V_1+V_2=45\,\mathrm V, \\[6pt]
U&=qV_P=(-2\cdot10^{-9}\,\mathrm C)(45\,\mathrm V)=-9.0\cdot10^{-8}\,\mathrm J=-90\,\mathrm{nJ}.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The potential is a scalar quantity, so the potential at $P$ is the algebraic sum of the contributions of the two sources, each with its own sign. The contribution of $Q_1$ is positive and equals $135\,\mathrm V$. That of $Q_2$ is negative and equals $-90\,\mathrm V$. The second source has twice the charge in magnitude, but its distance is three times as large, so its contribution is smaller in magnitude.

Only after obtaining $V_P=45\,\mathrm V$ do we calculate the energy of the test charge, with $U=qV_P$. The test charge is negative and the potential is positive, so the product is negative. The energy of the test charge at $P$ is $-90\,\mathrm{nJ}$ relative to zero at infinity.

If the test charge were $+2\,\mathrm{nC}$, the energy would be $+90\,\mathrm{nJ}$. The potential $V_P$ stays the same, because it depends only on the sources, while $U$ is proportional to $q$ and changes sign with it. The product $\mathrm{C\cdot V}$ is a joule, so $2\cdot10^{-9}\cdot45=9.0\cdot10^{-8}\,\mathrm J$.

</div>
