## Exercise 1

A charge $q=+2\,\mathrm{\mu C}$ moves from $V_A=120\,\mathrm V$ to $V_B=40\,\mathrm V$. Calculate the electric work.

::grafico{linee="1,1 1,5;5,1 5,5" punti="1,2,pieno,A;5,4,pieno,B" etichette="1,5.35,120 V;5,5.35,40 V" segmenti="1,2,5,4" xmin=0 xmax=6 ymin=0 ymax=6 assi=0 titolo="The charge starts from the surface at 120 volts and arrives at the one at 40 volts."}

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta V&=V_B-V_A=(40-120)\,\mathrm V=-80\,\mathrm V, \\[6pt]
\Delta U&=q\,\Delta V=(2\,\mathrm{\mu C})(-80\,\mathrm V)=-160\,\mathrm{\mu J}, \\[6pt]
W_{A\to B}&=-\Delta U=+160\,\mathrm{\mu J}.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The potential difference is the final value minus the initial one, so $\Delta V=-80\,\mathrm V$. The charge is positive, so the potential energy varies like the potential and decreases by $160\,\mathrm{\mu J}$. The work of the electric force is the opposite of the change in potential energy, so it is positive.

The units are kept in microcoulombs and volts, because their product is a microjoule. The electric force is conservative, so the work depends only on the endpoints and any path from $A$ to $B$ gives the same result. If only the electric force acted and the charge started from rest, its final kinetic energy would be $160\,\mathrm{\mu J}$.

If the charge moved from $A$ to another point at $120\,\mathrm V$, on the same equipotential, we would have $\Delta V=0$ and the work would be zero, even along a long and winding path. A charge of $-2\,\mathrm{\mu C}$ making the same passage from $A$ to $B$ would have work $-160\,\mathrm{\mu J}$, because $W=-q\Delta V$ changes sign with $q$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

In a uniform field with $E=500\,\mathrm{V/m}$ pointing to the right, a charge $q=-3\,\mathrm{\mu C}$ moves $4\,\mathrm{cm}$ to the right and $3\,\mathrm{cm}$ up. Calculate $\Delta V$, $\Delta U$, and the electric work.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta V&=-E\,\Delta x=-500\cdot0.04\,\mathrm V=-20\,\mathrm V, \\[6pt]
\Delta U&=q\,\Delta V=(-3\,\mathrm{\mu C})(-20\,\mathrm V)=+60\,\mathrm{\mu J}, \\[6pt]
W_{A\to B}&=-\Delta U=-60\,\mathrm{\mu J}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Check with the force: $F=|q|E=3\cdot10^{-6}\cdot500\,\mathrm N=1.5\cdot10^{-3}\,\mathrm N$ to the left, and $W=F\cdot(-0.04\,\mathrm m)=-6.0\cdot10^{-5}\,\mathrm J$.

<div class="spiegazione">

In the uniform field the potential difference depends on the component of the displacement along the field. The vertical component is perpendicular to the field, so it does not change the potential and does no work. Over the $4\,\mathrm{cm}$ to the right the potential decreases by $20\,\mathrm V$, because the field points toward lower potentials.

The charge is negative, so $q\,\Delta V$ is positive and the potential energy increases by $60\,\mathrm{\mu J}$. The force on a negative charge has the direction opposite to the field, that is, it points to the left, while the useful component of the displacement is to the right. Force and displacement have opposite directions, so the work is negative and equals $-60\,\mathrm{\mu J}$, in agreement with the direct calculation of the force.

</div>
