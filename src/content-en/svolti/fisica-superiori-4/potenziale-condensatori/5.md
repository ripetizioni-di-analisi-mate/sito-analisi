## Exercise 1

A parallel-plate capacitor in vacuum has plates of area $120\,\mathrm{cm^2}$ separated by $1.0\,\mathrm{mm}$. Calculate the capacitance neglecting edge effects, with $\varepsilon_0=8.85\cdot10^{-12}\,\mathrm{F/m}$.

::grafico{assi=0 uguale=1 poligoni="1,3.5 5,3.5 5,3.8 1,3.8;1,0.2 5,0.2 5,0.5 1,0.5" segmenti="5.4,0.5,5.9,0.5;5.4,3.5,5.9,3.5;5.65,0.5,5.65,3.5" etichette="3,4.3,A = 120 cm²;6.5,2,d = 1 mm" xmin=0 xmax=7.8 ymin=0 ymax=4.8 titolo="Parallel plates with area $A$ and distance $d$ (figure not to scale)."}

$$
\begin{aligned}
C&=\varepsilon_0\frac{A}{d}, \\[6pt]
A&=120\,\mathrm{cm^2}=120\cdot10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m^2}=1.20\cdot10^{-2}\,\mathrm{m^2},\qquad d=1.0\cdot10^{-3}\,\mathrm m, \\[6pt]
\frac{A}{d}&=\frac{1.20\cdot10^{-2}\,\mathrm{m^2}}{1.0\cdot10^{-3}\,\mathrm m}=12\,\mathrm m, \\[6pt]
C&=8.85\cdot10^{-12}\,\mathrm{F/m}\cdot12\,\mathrm m=1.06\cdot10^{-10}\,\mathrm F=106\,\mathrm{pF}.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The capacitor is ideal and parallel-plate, with vacuum between the plates, so the capacitance is $C=\varepsilon_0\dfrac{A}{d}$. The area must be expressed in square meters. One square centimeter is $(10^{-2}\,\mathrm m)^2=10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m^2}$, so $120\,\mathrm{cm^2}$ equals $1.20\cdot10^{-2}\,\mathrm{m^2}$.

The ratio $\dfrac{A}{d}$ is a length, $12\,\mathrm m$. Multiplied by $\varepsilon_0$, expressed in farads per meter, it gives farads. The capacitance is about $106\,\mathrm{pF}$.

The uniform-field model is acceptable, because the distance of $1.0\,\mathrm{mm}$ is much smaller than the side of the plates, about $11\,\mathrm{cm}$. With a voltage of $12\,\mathrm V$ the plates would carry $Q=C\Delta V\simeq1.3\,\mathrm{nC}$ and the field between them would be $E=\dfrac{\Delta V}{d}=1.2\cdot10^4\,\mathrm{V/m}$. Doubling the distance would halve the capacitance, about $53\,\mathrm{pF}$, while doubling the area would double it, about $212\,\mathrm{pF}$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A parallel-plate capacitor has capacitance $C_0=90\,\mathrm{pF}$ in vacuum. The distance between the plates is halved and all the space is filled with a linear dielectric of $\varepsilon_r=4$. Calculate the new capacitance and the magnitude of the charge on one plate at $100\,\mathrm V$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
C_0&=\varepsilon_0\frac{A}{d},\qquad C'=\varepsilon_r\varepsilon_0\frac{A}{d/2}=2\varepsilon_r\,C_0, \\[6pt]
C'&=2\cdot4\cdot90\,\mathrm{pF}=720\,\mathrm{pF}, \\[6pt]
Q&=C'\,\Delta V=720\,\mathrm{pF}\cdot100\,\mathrm V=7.2\cdot10^{4}\,\mathrm{pC}=72\,\mathrm{nC}.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The capacitance formula contains the permittivity of the dielectric, $\varepsilon_r\varepsilon_0$, and the distance in the denominator. Halving the distance doubles the ratio $\dfrac{A}{d}$. The dielectric multiplies the permittivity by $4$. The two effects act in the same product, so the capacitance becomes $2\cdot4=8$ times the initial one.

The voltage of $100\,\mathrm V$ refers to the final situation, so the charge on one plate is $Q=C'\Delta V$. A picofarad times a volt is a picocoulomb, and $7.2\cdot10^4\,\mathrm{pC}$ is $72\,\mathrm{nC}$. At the same voltage, in the initial capacitor the charge would have been $Q_0=C_0\Delta V=90\,\mathrm{pF}\cdot100\,\mathrm V=9.0\,\mathrm{nC}$, eight times smaller, in agreement with the ratio of the capacitances. The field between the plates, at equal voltage, would instead be twice as large, because the distance is halved.

</div>
