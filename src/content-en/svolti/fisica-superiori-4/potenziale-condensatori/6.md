## Exercise 1

A capacitor of capacitance $2.8\,\mathrm{\mu F}$ is kept at $12\,\mathrm V$. Calculate the charge on each plate.

::schema{riferimento=0 linee="0,1.48 0,3 2.85,3;3.25,3 7,3 7,0 0,0 0,1.16;-0.44,1.48 0.44,1.48;-0.27,1.16 0.27,1.16;2.85,2.5 2.85,3.5;3.25,2.5 3.25,3.5" etichette="0.9,1.32,12 V;2.5,3.9,+Q;3.4,3.9,−Q;5,3.9,2.8 μF" titolo="The capacitor is connected to a $12\,\mathrm V$ source."}

$$
\begin{aligned}
Q&=C\,\Delta V=2.8\cdot10^{-6}\,\mathrm F\cdot12\,\mathrm V=3.36\cdot10^{-5}\,\mathrm C=33.6\,\mathrm{\mu C}, \\[6pt]
Q_+&=+33.6\,\mathrm{\mu C},\qquad Q_-=-33.6\,\mathrm{\mu C}, \\[6pt]
U_C&=\frac12 Q\,\Delta V=\frac12\cdot33.6\cdot12\,\mathrm{\mu J}\simeq202\,\mathrm{\mu J}.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Capacitance is defined as the ratio between the magnitude of the charge on one plate alone and the voltage between the plates, so $Q=C\Delta V$. A microfarad times a volt is a microcoulomb, so $2.8\cdot12=33.6$ gives microcoulombs directly.

The plate connected to the positive terminal of the source is at the higher potential and carries the positive charge. A charge of $33.6\,\mathrm{\mu C}$ corresponds to about $\dfrac{33.6\cdot10^{-6}}{1.60\cdot10^{-19}}\simeq2.1\cdot10^{14}$ elementary charges. The other plate carries the opposite charge. The two charges sum to zero, while the magnitude of the charge on each plate is $33.6\,\mathrm{\mu C}$.

The stored energy is $U_C=\dfrac{1}{2}Q\Delta V$, with the factor $\dfrac{1}{2}$ due to the growth of the voltage during charging. If the voltage doubled, to $24\,\mathrm V$, the charge would double to $67.2\,\mathrm{\mu C}$, because capacitance is a property of the capacitor and does not depend on the applied voltage.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A $3\,\mathrm{\mu F}$ parallel-plate capacitor initially at $12\,\mathrm V$ is disconnected. The plates are then moved apart to twice the initial distance. Calculate the magnitude of the charge on one plate, the new voltage, and the change in energy.

$$
\begin{aligned}
Q&=C\,\Delta V=3\cdot12\,\mathrm{\mu C}=36\,\mathrm{\mu C}, \\[6pt]
C'&=\varepsilon_0\frac{A}{2d}=\frac{C}{2}=1.5\,\mathrm{\mu F}, \\[6pt]
\Delta V'&=\frac{Q}{C'}=\frac{36\,\mathrm{\mu C}}{1.5\,\mathrm{\mu F}}=24\,\mathrm V, \\[6pt]
U_i&=\frac12 Q\,\Delta V=\frac12\cdot36\cdot12\,\mathrm{\mu J}=216\,\mathrm{\mu J},\qquad
U_f=\frac12\cdot36\cdot24\,\mathrm{\mu J}=432\,\mathrm{\mu J}, \\[6pt]
\Delta U&=U_f-U_i=216\,\mathrm{\mu J}.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The capacitor is disconnected, so the charge on the plates cannot change and stays $36\,\mathrm{\mu C}$. In the parallel-plate capacitor the capacitance is inversely proportional to the distance, so doubling $d$ halves the capacitance, which becomes $1.5\,\mathrm{\mu F}$.

At constant charge and halved capacitance the voltage doubles, and equals $24\,\mathrm V$. The energy also doubles, since $U=\dfrac{1}{2}Q\Delta V$ with $Q$ constant. The field $E=\dfrac{Q}{\varepsilon_0A}$ does not change, because $Q$ and $A$ are constant, and the voltage $\Delta V=Ed$ doubles only because the distance doubles. The increase of $216\,\mathrm{\mu J}$ comes from the external work needed to move the plates apart, which attract each other because they carry opposite charges.

</div>
