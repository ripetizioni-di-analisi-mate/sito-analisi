## Exercise 1

A mass $m=0.36\,\mathrm{kg}$ is attached to an ideal spring with $k=180\,\mathrm{N/m}$ on a frictionless surface. Find the period.

::grafico{f="-1.8*x" xmin=-4 xmax=4 ymin=-8 ymax=8 nomi="x,F (N)" punti="-2,3.6,pieno;0,0,pieno;2,-3.6,pieno" titolo="The spring force has the opposite sign to the displacement. Horizontal coordinates in centimeters."}

$$
\frac{m}{k}=\frac{0.36\,\mathrm{kg}}{180\,\mathrm{N/m}}=2.0\cdot10^{-3}\,\mathrm{s^2}.
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
T&=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{m}{k}}\\[6pt]
 &=2\pi\sqrt{2.0\cdot10^{-3}}\,\mathrm{s}\\[6pt]
 &=2\pi\cdot0.0447\,\mathrm{s}\simeq0.281\,\mathrm{s}.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

We assume the spring is ideal, with negligible mass and Hooke's law valid, and the surface frictionless, so the only horizontal force is the spring force, opposite to the displacement measured from equilibrium, as the graph shows. Newton's second law $ma=-kx$ gives $a=-\dfrac{k}{m}x$, so the motion is harmonic with $\omega^2=\dfrac{k}{m}$ and the period is $T=2\pi\sqrt{\dfrac{m}{k}}$. The ratio $\dfrac{m}{k}$ has units $\dfrac{\mathrm{kg}}{\mathrm{N/m}}=\mathrm{s^2}$, because $1\,\mathrm{N}=1\,\mathrm{kg\cdot m/s^2}$, and after the square root we obtain seconds. The period does not depend on the amplitude as long as the force remains linear. As a check, $\omega=\sqrt{\dfrac{180}{0.36}}\,\mathrm{rad/s}=\sqrt{500}\,\mathrm{rad/s}\simeq22.4\,\mathrm{rad/s}$ and $\dfrac{2\pi}{\omega}$ gives the same period.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A mass $m=0.45\,\mathrm{kg}$ is attached to an ideal spring with $k=180\,\mathrm{N/m}$. Find the period and the frequency in the absence of friction.

$$
\omega=\sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}=\sqrt{\frac{180}{0.45}}\,\mathrm{rad/s}=\sqrt{400}\,\mathrm{rad/s}=20\,\mathrm{rad/s}.
$$

$$
T=\frac{2\pi}{\omega}=\frac{2\pi}{20}\,\mathrm{s}=\frac{\pi}{10}\,\mathrm{s}\simeq0.314\,\mathrm{s}.
$$

$$
f=\frac{1}{T}=\frac{10}{\pi}\,\mathrm{Hz}\simeq3.18\,\mathrm{Hz}.
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The same assumptions hold: ideal spring, motion along the spring axis, and no friction. The angular frequency is $\omega=\sqrt{\dfrac{k}{m}}$ and the period is the time the phase takes to advance by $2\pi$. The frequency is computed as the reciprocal of the exact value $\dfrac{\pi}{10}\,\mathrm{s}$ before rounding. Compared with the previous exercise, the mass is larger for the same spring, so the angular frequency decreases, from $22.4\,\mathrm{rad/s}$ to $20\,\mathrm{rad/s}$, and the period increases, from $0.281\,\mathrm{s}$ to $0.314\,\mathrm{s}$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

For a mass of $0.45\,\mathrm{kg}$ the period with a $180\,\mathrm{N/m}$ spring is $T_1=\dfrac{\pi}{10}\,\mathrm{s}$. A $360\,\mathrm{N/m}$ spring is used. What mass produces a period twice as long?

$$
\left(\frac{T_2}{T_1}\right)^2=\frac{m_2\,k_1}{m_1\,k_2}=4.
$$

$$
m_2=4\,m_1\,\frac{k_2}{k_1}=4\cdot0.45\,\mathrm{kg}\cdot\frac{360}{180}=3.60\,\mathrm{kg}.
$$

$$
\frac{m_2}{k_2}=\frac{3.60}{360}\,\mathrm{s^2}=0.0100\,\mathrm{s^2}=4\cdot\frac{m_1}{k_1}=4\cdot0.0025\,\mathrm{s^2}.
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Both springs are ideal and the mass moves without friction along their axis. The factor $2\pi$ cancels in the ratio of the periods, which depends on the square root of $\dfrac{m}{k}$. To double the period, the ratio $\dfrac{m}{k}$ must quadruple, so $\dfrac{m_2}{k_2}=4\,\dfrac{m_1}{k_1}$. The new spring has twice the constant and, for the same mass, would halve $\dfrac{m}{k}$, so the mass must be multiplied by $2\cdot4=8$ and equals $8\cdot0.45\,\mathrm{kg}=3.60\,\mathrm{kg}$. In the last line $\dfrac{m_2}{k_2}=0.0100\,\mathrm{s^2}$ is four times $\dfrac{m_1}{k_1}=0.0025\,\mathrm{s^2}$. With only the stiffer spring and the same mass the period would be $2\pi\sqrt{\dfrac{0.45}{360}}\,\mathrm{s}\simeq0.222\,\mathrm{s}$, that is, $\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}$ of the initial one, so doubling requires a large increase in mass.

</div>
