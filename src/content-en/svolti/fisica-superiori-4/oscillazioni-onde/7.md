## Exercise 1

An ideal string has tension $F_T=72\,\mathrm{N}$ and linear density $\mu=0.012\,\mathrm{kg/m}$. Find the speed of transverse waves.

$$
\begin{aligned}
u&=\sqrt{\frac{F_T}{\mu}}\\[6pt]
 &=\sqrt{\frac{72\,\mathrm{N}}{0.012\,\mathrm{kg/m}}}\\[6pt]
 &=\sqrt{6000}\,\mathrm{m/s}\simeq77.5\,\mathrm{m/s}.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The string is ideal and uniform, the oscillations are small and transverse, and the tension remains constant, so $u=\sqrt{\dfrac{F_T}{\mu}}$ holds. The linear density is already in $\mathrm{kg/m}$ and is inserted directly. The ratio of newtons to $\mathrm{kg/m}$ has units $\mathrm{m^2/s^2}$, because $1\,\mathrm{N}=1\,\mathrm{kg\cdot m/s^2}$, so the root is a speed. The value found is the speed of the profile along the string, which is different from the speed of the individual points, which oscillate in the transverse direction. With four times the tension, $288\,\mathrm{N}$, the speed doubles to about $155\,\mathrm{m/s}$. With four times the linear density, $0.048\,\mathrm{kg/m}$, the speed halves to about $38.7\,\mathrm{m/s}$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

An ideal string of length $L=0.80\,\mathrm{m}$, fixed at both ends, has tension $F_T=90\,\mathrm{N}$ and linear density $\mu=0.012\,\mathrm{kg/m}$. Find the wave speed and the fundamental frequency.

$$
u=\sqrt{\frac{F_T}{\mu}}=\sqrt{\frac{90}{0.012}}\,\mathrm{m/s}=\sqrt{7500}\,\mathrm{m/s}\simeq86.6\,\mathrm{m/s}.
$$

$$
\lambda_1=2L=2\cdot0.80\,\mathrm{m}=1.60\,\mathrm{m}.
$$

$$
f_1=\frac{u}{\lambda_1}=\frac{86.6\,\mathrm{m/s}}{1.60\,\mathrm{m}}\simeq54.1\,\mathrm{Hz}.
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The string is ideal and uniform and the ends are fixed. Tension and linear density determine the speed $u$ of a wave on the string, while the fixed ends are nodes and impose the shape of the standing mode. In the fundamental there is a single antinode between the two nodes, so half a wavelength fits in $L$ and $\lambda_1=2L$. The frequency is $f_1=\dfrac{u}{2L}$. Dividing by $L$ would give twice that value, which is the frequency of the second harmonic. The result has units $\dfrac{\mathrm{m/s}}{\mathrm{m}}=\mathrm{s^{-1}}$. The second harmonic has frequency $f_2=2f_1\simeq108\,\mathrm{Hz}$ and has a node at the midpoint. The speed $86.6\,\mathrm{m/s}$ is the same for all modes.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A uniform piece of string $2.50\,\mathrm{m}$ long has mass $30\,\mathrm{g}$. What tension produces a speed of $60\,\mathrm{m/s}$? What tension is needed to double the speed, at constant linear density?

$$
\mu=\frac{m}{\ell}=\frac{0.030\,\mathrm{kg}}{2.50\,\mathrm{m}}=0.012\,\mathrm{kg/m}.
$$

$$
u=\sqrt{\frac{F_T}{\mu}}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad F_T=\mu u^2=0.012\cdot60^2\,\mathrm{N}=0.012\cdot3600\,\mathrm{N}=43.2\,\mathrm{N}.
$$

$$
F_{T,2}=\mu(2u)^2=4\mu u^2=4\cdot43.2\,\mathrm{N}=172.8\,\mathrm{N}.
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The string is uniform, so the linear density is the same in every segment. The mass must be expressed in kilograms, $30\,\mathrm{g}=0.030\,\mathrm{kg}$, and divided by the length. Squaring both sides of $u=\sqrt{\dfrac{F_T}{\mu}}$ isolates the tension, $F_T=\mu u^2$. At fixed density the tension is proportional to $u^2$, so for double the speed it must quadruple. As a check, $\sqrt{\dfrac{172.8}{0.012}}\,\mathrm{m/s}=\sqrt{14400}\,\mathrm{m/s}=120\,\mathrm{m/s}$. The product $\mu u^2$ has units $\dfrac{\mathrm{kg}}{\mathrm{m}}\cdot\dfrac{\mathrm{m^2}}{\mathrm{s^2}}=\mathrm{N}$. With tension $43.2\,\mathrm{N}$ a pulse travels the $2.50\,\mathrm{m}$ in $\dfrac{2.50}{60}\,\mathrm{s}\simeq0.042\,\mathrm{s}$.

</div>
