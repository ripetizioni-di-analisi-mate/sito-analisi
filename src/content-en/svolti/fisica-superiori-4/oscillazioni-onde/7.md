## Exercise 1

An ideal string has tension $F_T=72\,\mathrm{N}$ and linear density $\mu=0.012\,\mathrm{kg/m}$. Find the transverse wave speed.

$$
\begin{aligned}
u&=\sqrt{\frac{F_T}{\mu}}\\[6pt]
 &=\sqrt{\frac{72}{0.012}}\,\mathrm{m/s}\\[6pt]
 &=\sqrt{6000}\,\mathrm{m/s}\simeq77.5\,\mathrm{m/s}.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

For a uniform string with small transverse oscillations, the speed depends on tension and mass per unit length. The quantity in $\mathrm{kg/m}$ is already the linear density, so use it directly. Newtons divided by $\mathrm{kg/m}$ gives $\mathrm{m^2/s^2}$, and its square root gives a speed. The result describes the advance of the wave profile along the string.

</div>

## Exercise 2

An ideal string of length $L=0.80\,\mathrm{m}$ fixed at both ends has tension $F_T=90\,\mathrm{N}$ and linear density $\mu=0.012\,\mathrm{kg/m}$. Find its wave speed and fundamental frequency.

$$
u=\sqrt{\frac{90}{0.012}}\,\mathrm{m/s}=\sqrt{7500}\,\mathrm{m/s}\simeq86.6\,\mathrm{m/s}.
$$

$$
\lambda_1=2L=1.60\,\mathrm{m}.
$$

$$
f_1=\frac{\sqrt{7500}}{1.60}\,\mathrm{Hz}\simeq54.1\,\mathrm{Hz}.
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Tension and linear density determine the speed of a wave on the string. The ends instead set the shape of the standing mode. In the fundamental, there is one antinode between the two end nodes, so half a wavelength fits in length $L$. Its frequency is the speed divided by $2L$. Dividing by $L$ gives twice as much, the frequency of the second harmonic.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A uniform $2.50\,\mathrm{m}$ section of string has mass $30\,\mathrm{g}$. What tension gives a speed of $60\,\mathrm{m/s}$? What tension doubles the speed if the linear density stays constant?

$$
\mu=\frac{0.030}{2.50}\,\mathrm{kg/m}=0.012\,\mathrm{kg/m}.
$$

$$
F_T=\mu u^2=0.012\cdot60^2\,\mathrm{N}=43.2\,\mathrm{N}.
$$

$$
F_{T,2}=\mu(2u)^2=4F_T=172.8\,\mathrm{N}.
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

First express the total mass of the section in kilograms and divide by its length. Once $\mu$ is known, square the wave speed relation to solve for tension. At fixed density, tension is proportional to $u^2$, so twice the speed requires four times the tension. Substituting $172.8\,\mathrm{N}$ in the numerator under the square root gives $120\,\mathrm{m/s}$.

</div>
