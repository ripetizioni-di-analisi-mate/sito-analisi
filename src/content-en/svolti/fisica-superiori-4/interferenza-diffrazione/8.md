## Exercise 1

A grating has $320$ lines per millimeter. Find the spacing in meters.

$$
\begin{aligned}
d&=\frac{1\,\mathrm{mm}}{320}=\frac{10^{-3}}{320}\,\mathrm m
   =3.125\cdot10^{-6}\,\mathrm m.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The number of lines per millimeter is a linear density. To find the distance between adjacent lines, take its reciprocal. The spacing is therefore $\dfrac{1}{320}\,\mathrm{mm}$, which becomes meters after multiplication by $10^{-3}$. The same result follows by first writing the density as $320\,000$ lines per meter.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A grating has $400$ lines per millimeter and is illuminated at normal incidence with $\lambda=600\,\mathrm{nm}$. Find the spacing and the angle of the first positive maximum.

$$
\begin{aligned}
d&=\frac{10^{-3}}{400}\,\mathrm m
   =2.5\cdot10^{-6}\,\mathrm m\\[6pt]
\sin\theta_1&=\frac{600\cdot10^{-9}}{2.5\cdot10^{-6}}
             =0.24\\[6pt]
\theta_1&=\arcsin(0.24)\simeq13.9^\circ.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The line density first determines the distance used in the grating equation. After converting to meters, the ratio $\dfrac{\lambda}{d}$ is below one, so first order exists. A grating with more lines per millimeter would have a smaller spacing. At fixed wavelength this increases the sine of the first angle, as long as first order is still allowed.

</div>
