## Exercise 1

In vacuum, light has wavelength $\lambda_0=4.4\cdot10^{-7}\,\mathrm m$. Find the frequency using $c=3.00\cdot10^8\,\mathrm{m/s}$.

$$
c=f\lambda_0\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad f=\frac{c}{\lambda_0}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
f&=\frac{3.00\cdot10^8\,\mathrm{m/s}}{4.4\cdot10^{-7}\,\mathrm m}\\[6pt]
  &=\frac{3.00}{4.4}\cdot10^{15}\,\mathrm{Hz}
  =0.682\cdot10^{15}\,\mathrm{Hz}
  \simeq6.8\cdot10^{14}\,\mathrm{Hz}.
\end{aligned}
$$

::grafico{curve="t,3000/t,380,780" xmin=0 xmax=810 ymin=0 ymax=9 nomi="λ₀,f" punti="440,6.818,pieno" legenda="f=\dfrac{c}{\lambda_0}" titolo="Wavelength in nanometers and frequency in units of $10^{14}\,\mathrm{Hz}$."}

<div class="spiegazione">

In vacuum a periodic radiation travels the distance $c$ in one second, and in each second $f$ oscillations repeat, each of length $\lambda_0$. Hence $c=f\lambda_0$, and dividing both sides by $\lambda_0$ isolates $f$. The data are substituted with their units: the quotient of $\mathrm{m/s}$ and $\mathrm m$ has dimension $\mathrm{s^{-1}}$, that is, hertz. The ratio of the powers of ten is $10^8\cdot10^{7}=10^{15}$, because dividing by $10^{-7}$ is equivalent to multiplying by $10^{7}$. Since $0.682\cdot10^{15}=6.82\cdot10^{14}$, the frequency is **$6.8\cdot10^{14}\,\mathrm{Hz}$**. The point on the graph represents the pair $440\,\mathrm{nm}$ and $6.8\cdot10^{14}\,\mathrm{Hz}$ and lies on the part of the curve corresponding to the visible range, from $380\,\mathrm{nm}$ to $780\,\mathrm{nm}$. As a check, $440\,\mathrm{nm}$ is less than $750\,\mathrm{nm}$, the wavelength of red, so the frequency must exceed $4.00\cdot10^{14}\,\mathrm{Hz}$, and the value found does.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Light of vacuum wavelength $\lambda_0=5.5\cdot10^{-7}\,\mathrm m$ enters a stationary medium with $n=1.50$. Find its frequency and wavelength in the medium. Use $c=3.00\cdot10^8\,\mathrm{m/s}$.

$$
f=\frac{c}{\lambda_0},\qquad u=\frac{c}{n},\qquad \lambda=\frac{u}{f}=\frac{\lambda_0}{n}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
f&=\frac{3.00\cdot10^8}{5.5\cdot10^{-7}}\,\mathrm{Hz}
 \simeq5.5\cdot10^{14}\,\mathrm{Hz}\\[6pt]
u&=\frac{3.00\cdot10^8}{1.50}\,\mathrm{m/s}=2.00\cdot10^8\,\mathrm{m/s}\\[6pt]
\lambda&=\frac{\lambda_0}{n}=\frac{550}{1.50}\,\mathrm{nm}
 \simeq3.7\cdot10^2\,\mathrm{nm}.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The frequency is computed from the vacuum data with the same relation as in the previous exercise. At a stationary interface the frequency is conserved, so in the medium the light still oscillates $5.5\cdot10^{14}$ times per second. In a medium of index $n$ the phase speed drops to $u=\dfrac cn=2.00\cdot10^8\,\mathrm{m/s}$. Since $f$ is unchanged and $u$ is smaller, the distance covered in one oscillation decreases and equals $\lambda=\dfrac{u}{f}$, which coincides with $\dfrac{\lambda_0}{n}$. The conversion $5.5\cdot10^{-7}\,\mathrm m=550\,\mathrm{nm}$ makes the two wavelengths easy to compare. As a check, $\lambda f=3.67\cdot10^{-7}\,\mathrm m\cdot5.45\cdot10^{14}\,\mathrm{Hz}=2.00\cdot10^8\,\mathrm{m/s}$ coincides with $u$. A stretch of $1.0\,\mathrm{\mu m}$ contains $\dfrac{1000}{367}=2.7$ wavelengths in the medium, against $\dfrac{1000}{550}=1.8$ in vacuum. The wavelength in the medium is **about $3.7\cdot10^2\,\mathrm{nm}$**, smaller than the one in vacuum by a factor of $1.50$.

</div>
