## Exercise 1

A mass $m=0.144\,\mathrm{kg}$ of ice at $0\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$ must become water at $30\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$. Use $\ell_f=334\,\mathrm{kJ/kg}$ and $c=4180\,\mathrm{J/(kg\,K)}$. Calculate the total energy, with no losses.

Apply $Q_{\mathrm{fus}}=m\ell_f$ for melting at $0\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$ and $Q_{\mathrm{liq}}=mc\Delta T$ for heating the liquid water, with $Q=Q_{\mathrm{fus}}+Q_{\mathrm{liq}}$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
Q_{\mathrm{fus}}&=0.144\cdot334\,000\,\mathrm J\\
&=48\,096\,\mathrm J\\[5pt]
Q_{\mathrm{liq}}&=0.144\cdot4180\cdot30\,\mathrm J\\
&=18\,057.6\,\mathrm J\\[5pt]
Q&=Q_{\mathrm{fus}}+Q_{\mathrm{liq}}\\
&=66\,153.6\,\mathrm J\simeq66.2\,\mathrm{kJ}.
\end{aligned}
$$

::grafico{linee="0,0 48.096,0 66.1536,30" punti="48.096,0,pieno,A;66.1536,30,pieno,B" xmin=-8 xmax=72 ymin=-4 ymax=36 nomi="Q,t (°C)" titolo="The energy Q in kilojoules first melts the ice and then warms the water"}

<div class="spiegazione">

The initial temperature coincides with the melting temperature, so the first part of the energy turns the ice into water at $0\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$. This explains the horizontal segment of the curve. When the ice has melted completely, the subsequent energy raises the temperature of the water. The specific heat capacity of the liquid is then used with a rise of $30\,\mathrm K$. Both contributions are positive and add up. In this example more than two thirds of the total energy goes to melting, so starting with the formula $mc\Delta T$ alone would underestimate the heat by more than $48\,\mathrm{kJ}$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A heater delivers $500\,\mathrm W$ to $0.18\,\mathrm{kg}$ of ice that starts at $-10\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$. Use $c_s=2100\,\mathrm{J/(kg\,K)}$, $\ell_f=334\,\mathrm{kJ/kg}$, and $c=4180\,\mathrm{J/(kg\,K)}$. Calculate the total heat and the time to obtain water at $30\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$, with no losses.

Apply $Q_s=mc_s\Delta T$ for the ice, $Q_{\mathrm{fus}}=m\ell_f$ for melting, $Q_{\mathrm{liq}}=mc\Delta T$ for the water, and $\Delta t=\dfrac QP$ with $Q$ the sum of the three contributions.

$$
\begin{aligned}
Q_s&=0.18\cdot2100\cdot10\,\mathrm J=3780\,\mathrm J\\
Q_{\mathrm{fus}}&=0.18\cdot334\,000\,\mathrm J=60\,120\,\mathrm J\\
Q_{\mathrm{liq}}&=0.18\cdot4180\cdot30\,\mathrm J=22\,572\,\mathrm J\\[5pt]
Q&=3780\,\mathrm J+60\,120\,\mathrm J+22\,572\,\mathrm J\\
&=86\,472\,\mathrm J\\[5pt]
\Delta t&=\frac{86\,472}{500}\,\mathrm s\simeq173\,\mathrm s.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The first segment brings the solid from $-10$ to $0\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$ and requires a rise of $10\,\mathrm K$. Since the ice stays solid in that segment, $c_s$ is used. At the melting temperature the latent heat is added, because the energy needed for the transformation cannot be obtained from a change in temperature. The third contribution uses the specific heat capacity of water for a rise of $30\,\mathrm K$. The heater supplies the same power during all the stages, so the total duration is the total energy divided by $500\,\mathrm W$. Melting alone takes about $120\,\mathrm s$ of the $173\,\mathrm s$, since $\dfrac{60\,120}{500}\,\mathrm s\simeq120\,\mathrm s$. Heating the ice takes $\dfrac{3780}{500}\,\mathrm s\simeq7.6\,\mathrm s$ and heating the water $\dfrac{22\,572}{500}\,\mathrm s\simeq45\,\mathrm s$, and the sum of the three times is again $173\,\mathrm s$.

</div>
