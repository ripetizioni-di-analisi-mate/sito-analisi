## Exercise 1

A mass of water $m=0.28\,\mathrm{kg}$ is heated by $\Delta T=24\,\mathrm K$. Use $c=4180\,\mathrm{J/(kg\,K)}$ and assume that the water stays liquid. Calculate the heat absorbed.

Apply $Q=mc\Delta T=C\Delta T$, where $C=mc$ is the heat capacity of the sample. The units are consistent, since $\mathrm{kg}\cdot\mathrm{J/(kg\,K)}\cdot\mathrm K=\mathrm J$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
C&=mc\\
&=0.28\cdot4180\,\mathrm{J/K}\\
&=1170.4\,\mathrm{J/K}\\[6pt]
Q&=C\Delta T\\
&=1170.4\cdot24\,\mathrm J\\
&=28\,089.6\,\mathrm J\simeq28.1\,\mathrm{kJ}.
\end{aligned}
$$

::grafico{f="1.1704*x" punti="24,28.0896,pieno,A" xmin=-4 xmax=32 ymin=-5 ymax=40 nomi="ΔT,Q (kJ)" titolo="Heat absorbed by the water sample versus temperature rise in kelvin"}

<div class="spiegazione">

The mass stays in the same phase, so its specific heat capacity relates the temperature change directly to the energy absorbed. Multiplying $4180\,\mathrm{J/(kg\,K)}$ by $0.28\,\mathrm{kg}$ gives the heat capacity of the entire sample. This number is the energy required to raise the temperature of that amount of water by one kelvin. Since the required temperature rise is $24\,\mathrm K$, the result is multiplied by $24$. The product is in joules and is positive because the water receives energy. As a check on the order of magnitude, one kilogram of water would require about $4.2\,\mathrm{kJ}$ for each kelvin, so about $100\,\mathrm{kJ}$ for $24\,\mathrm K$. The mass is a little more than a quarter of a kilogram, and the result is about a quarter of that value. On the graph the heat capacity is the slope of the line. The marked point corresponds to about $28.1\,\mathrm{kJ}$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A mass of water $m=0.35\,\mathrm{kg}$ warms by $24\,\mathrm K$ without changing phase. A heater delivers a constant power of $600\,\mathrm W$ to it. With $c=4180\,\mathrm{J/(kg\,K)}$, calculate the heat absorbed and the time required.

Apply $Q=mc\Delta T$ for the heat absorbed and $P=\dfrac{Q}{\Delta t}$ for the constant power, which gives $\Delta t=\dfrac QP$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
Q&=mc\Delta T\\
&=0.35\cdot4180\cdot24\,\mathrm J\\
&=35\,112\,\mathrm J\simeq35.1\,\mathrm{kJ}\\[6pt]
\Delta t&=\frac QP\\
&=\frac{35\,112}{600}\,\mathrm s\\
&=58.52\,\mathrm s\simeq58.5\,\mathrm s.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The temperature rise and the value of $c$ determine how much energy the water must receive. The product $0.35\cdot4180=1463\,\mathrm{J/K}$ is the heat capacity of the sample, and multiplied by $24\,\mathrm K$ it gives $35\,112\,\mathrm J$. Power determines how long supplying that energy takes. Since the $600\,\mathrm W$ is already the power delivered to the water, $600\,\mathrm J$ enters each second. The heat absorbed is therefore divided by the power. The unit $\mathrm{J/W}$ reduces to seconds. One minute at that rate would supply $36\,000\,\mathrm J$, slightly more than the energy required. The time obtained is indeed slightly less than a minute, namely $58.5\,\mathrm s$. The result holds only if all of the $600\,\mathrm W$ reaches the water and if the temperature stays below the boiling point, because with a phase change the latent heat would also be required.

</div>
