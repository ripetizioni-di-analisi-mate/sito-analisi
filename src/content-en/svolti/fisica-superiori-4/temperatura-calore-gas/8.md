## Exercise 1

A uniform flat wall of area $6.4\,\mathrm{m^2}$ and thickness $0.12\,\mathrm m$ has conductivity $\kappa=0.050\,\mathrm{W/(m\,K)}$. Its faces are at $30$ and $6\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$. Neglect lateral losses and calculate the steady rate of heat transfer by conduction.

Apply conduction in steady state, $P=\kappa A\dfrac{T_h-T_c}{d}$, with the temperature difference expressed in kelvin.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta T &= (30-6)\,\mathrm K=24\,\mathrm K\\[4pt]
\frac{\Delta T}{d}&=\frac{24}{0.12}\,\mathrm{K/m}=200\,\mathrm{K/m}\\[4pt]
P&=\kappa A\frac{\Delta T}{d}\\
&=0.050\cdot6.4\cdot200\,\mathrm W=64\,\mathrm W.
\end{aligned}
$$

::grafico{linee="0,30 12,6" punti="0,30,pieno;12,6,pieno" xmin=-2 xmax=14 ymin=0 ymax=35 nomi="x,t (°C)" titolo="Temperature in the wall. Distance x from the warm face is in centimeters"}

<div class="spiegazione">

In steady state the wall does not accumulate energy, so the same power enters the warm face and leaves the cool face. In uniform material with no internal sources, the temperature profile is linear. A drop of $24\,\mathrm K$ distributed over $0.12\,\mathrm m$ gives a gradient of magnitude $200\,\mathrm{K/m}$. Multiplying by the conductivity gives the power per square meter, equal to $10\,\mathrm{W/m^2}$. Multiplying again by the area gives $64\,\mathrm W$. Net transfer is from the face at $30\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$ to the one at $6\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$. The units are consistent, since $\mathrm{W/(m\,K)}\cdot\mathrm{m^2}\cdot\mathrm{K/m}=\mathrm W$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A wall consists of two flat layers of area $8\,\mathrm{m^2}$, in perfect thermal contact. The layer on the warm side has thickness $0.12\,\mathrm m$ and conductivity $0.050\,\mathrm{W/(m\,K)}$. The cold one has thickness $0.08\,\mathrm m$ and conductivity $0.10\,\mathrm{W/(m\,K)}$. The outer faces are at $30$ and $6\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$. Neglecting lateral losses, calculate the temperature at the interface and the energy transferred in $600\,\mathrm s$ in steady state.

Apply the thermal resistance $R_i=\dfrac{d_i}{\kappa_iA}$ of each layer, the common power $P=\dfrac{T_h-T_c}{R_1+R_2}$, the drop $\Delta T_1=PR_1$ in the first layer, and the energy $Q=P\Delta t$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
R_1&=\frac{0.12}{0.050\cdot8}\,\mathrm{K/W}=0.30\,\mathrm{K/W}\\
R_2&=\frac{0.08}{0.10\cdot8}\,\mathrm{K/W}=0.10\,\mathrm{K/W}\\[5pt]
P&=\frac{24}{0.30+0.10}\,\mathrm W=60\,\mathrm W\\
\Delta T_1&=PR_1=18\,\mathrm K\\
t_{\mathrm{int}}&=(30-18)\,{}^\circ\mathrm C=12\,{}^\circ\mathrm C\\[5pt]
Q&=60\cdot600\,\mathrm J=36\,\mathrm{kJ}.
\end{aligned}
$$

::grafico{linee="0,30 12,12 20,6" punti="12,12,pieno,A" xmin=-3 xmax=27 ymin=0 ymax=35 nomi="x,t (°C)" titolo="Point A separates the layers. The distance x is in centimeters"}

<div class="spiegazione">

The power is the same in both layers, because the interface does not accumulate energy in steady conditions. Each produces a temperature drop equal to the power times the thermal resistance. Resistances in series add, so $60\,\mathrm W$ is obtained by dividing the total drop of $24\,\mathrm K$ by $0.40\,\mathrm{K/W}$. The warm layer has three quarters of the total resistance, so it loses $18\,\mathrm K$. From the value at the interface, a drop of $6\,\mathrm K$ remains along the second layer, consistent with $PR_2$. In $600\,\mathrm s$, $60\,\mathrm{J}$ crosses the wall every second, for a total of $36\,\mathrm{kJ}$.

</div>
