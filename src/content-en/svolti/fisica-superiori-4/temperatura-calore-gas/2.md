## Exercise 1

An unconstrained rod starts at length $L_0=2.0\,\mathrm m$. Its material has a linear expansion coefficient of $1.2\cdot10^{-5}\,\mathrm{K^{-1}}$. Calculate the increase in length for a temperature rise of $40\,\mathrm K$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\alpha\Delta T&=(1.2\cdot10^{-5})\cdot40\\
&=4.8\cdot10^{-4}\\[6pt]
\Delta L&=L_0\alpha\Delta T\\
&=2.0\cdot4.8\cdot10^{-4}\,\mathrm m\\
&=0.96\,\mathrm{mm}.
\end{aligned}
$$

::grafico{f="0.024*x" punti="40,0.96,pieno,A" xmin=-5 xmax=55 ymin=-0.2 ymax=1.45 nomi="ΔT,ΔL (mm)" titolo="Increase in length of the unconstrained rod for a 40 K rise"}

<div class="spiegazione">

The coefficient times the temperature change is the fraction of its initial length that the rod gains. Multiply this fraction by the initial length. The result in meters is $9.6\cdot10^{-4}\,\mathrm m$, which equals $0.96\,\mathrm{mm}$. Its positive sign agrees with heating and with $\alpha>0$. The linear model is appropriate because the fractional change $4.8\cdot10^{-4}$ is small. On the graph the point at $40\,\mathrm K$ lies just below $1\,\mathrm{mm}$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A $2.5\,\mathrm m$ rod is fixed at one end and free at the other, with a $2.0\,\mathrm{mm}$ gap between the free end and an obstacle. Its linear expansion coefficient is $1.2\cdot10^{-5}\,\mathrm{K^{-1}}$. After uniform heating by $40\,\mathrm K$, calculate the rod's length and the remaining gap.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta L&=L_0\alpha\Delta T\\
&=2.5\cdot(1.2\cdot10^{-5})\cdot40\,\mathrm m\\
&=1.2\cdot10^{-3}\,\mathrm m\\[6pt]
L&=2.5\,\mathrm m+0.0012\,\mathrm m\\
&=2.5012\,\mathrm m\\[6pt]
s&=(2.0-1.2)\,\mathrm{mm}\\
&=0.8\,\mathrm{mm}.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The free end can move forward as long as the increase in length is less than the available gap. First calculate how much the rod would lengthen without contact. The result of $1.2\,\mathrm{mm}$ is smaller than the available $2.0\,\mathrm{mm}$, so the assumption of free expansion holds throughout the heating. To find the final length, add the increase in length to $L_0$ after expressing both in meters. The remaining gap is found by subtracting the movement from the initial gap, with both in millimeters.

</div>
