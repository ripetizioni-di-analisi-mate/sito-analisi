## Exercise 1

A constant current of $0.28\,\mathrm{A}$ crosses a section of the wire for $150\,\mathrm{s}$. Calculate the net charge transported.

::grafico{f="0.28" xmin=0 xmax=150 ymin=0 ymax=0.5 nomi="t,I" area="0.28;0;0;150" punti="150,0.28,pieno" titolo="Time in seconds and current in amperes. The shaded area measures the charge."}

Definition of average current and the case of constant current:

$$
I=\frac{\Delta q}{\Delta t}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \Delta q=I\,\Delta t.
$$

Substituting the data gives

$$
\Delta q=0.28\,\mathrm{A}\cdot150\,\mathrm{s}=0.28\cdot150\,\mathrm{C}=42\,\mathrm{C}.
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

By definition, the current is the net charge crossing the section per unit time. Since the current is constant, the ratio $\dfrac{\Delta q}{\Delta t}$ has the same value in every interval, so we can solve for $\Delta q$ by multiplying $I$ by the duration $\Delta t$.

The value $0.28\,\mathrm{A}$ is equivalent to $0.28\,\mathrm{C}$ per second, so in $150\,\mathrm{s}$ a charge $150$ times as large passes. In the graph of $I$ versus $t$ the product $I\Delta t$ is the area of the rectangle of height $0.28\,\mathrm{A}$ and base $150\,\mathrm{s}$.

The product $\mathrm{A\cdot s}$ is a coulomb, so the net charge transported is **$42\,\mathrm{C}$**.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A constant current of $0.35\,\mathrm{A}$ crosses the wire for $150\,\mathrm{s}$. Calculate the charge and the corresponding net number of electrons with $e=1.60\cdot10^{-19}\,\mathrm{C}$.

We apply the charge transferred with constant current and the quantization of charge, where $N$ is the number of electrons and each carries a charge of magnitude $e$:

$$
\Delta q=I\,\Delta t,\qquad |\Delta q|=N\,e\quad\Longrightarrow\quad N=\frac{I\,\Delta t}{e}.
$$

Substituting the data gives

$$
\Delta q=0.35\,\mathrm{A}\cdot150\,\mathrm{s}=52.5\,\mathrm{C}.
$$

$$
N=\frac{52.5\,\mathrm{C}}{1.60\cdot10^{-19}\,\mathrm{C}}\simeq3.28\cdot10^{20}.
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The first relation gives the charge balance through the section, expressed in coulombs, because the current is constant and $\Delta q=I\Delta t$. We obtain $52.5\,\mathrm{C}$.

The charge is carried by electrons and each electron has a charge of magnitude $e$. Dividing the magnitude of the charge transferred by $e$ gives the number of electrons, because $N$ electrons carry the charge $Ne$. The coulombs in the numerator and denominator cancel, so $N$ is a pure number.

Electrons have negative charge and move on average in the direction opposite to conventional current. For the count only the magnitude of the charge matters, so we use the positive value $e$. About **$3.28\cdot10^{20}$ electrons** pass through the section, carrying $52.5\,\mathrm{C}$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

The current through a wire is $+0.60\,\mathrm{A}$ for $20\,\mathrm{s}$ and then $-0.20\,\mathrm{A}$ for $30\,\mathrm{s}$. Calculate the net charge and the average current.

Charge is summed over intervals in which the current is constant, with the sign of $I$, and the average current is the ratio of the net charge to the total duration:

$$
\Delta q=I_1\Delta t_1+I_2\Delta t_2,\qquad I_{\mathrm{avg}}=\frac{\Delta q}{\Delta t_1+\Delta t_2}.
$$

Substituting the data gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta q&=\big(0.60\cdot20+(-0.20)\cdot30\big)\,\mathrm{C}\\[6pt]
&=(12-6)\,\mathrm{C}=+6\,\mathrm{C}.
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
I_{\mathrm{avg}}=\frac{6\,\mathrm{C}}{(20+30)\,\mathrm{s}}=+0.12\,\mathrm{A}.
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

In each interval the current is constant, so the charge crossing the section is $I\Delta t$ with the sign of $I$. In the first interval $12\,\mathrm{C}$ pass in the positive direction. In the second the current is negative, so $6\,\mathrm{C}$ pass in the opposite direction and this contribution enters the sum with a minus sign.

The net charge is the algebraic sum of the two contributions and equals $+6\,\mathrm{C}$. The positive sign indicates that the net flow has the chosen positive direction.

The average current is calculated by dividing the net charge by the total duration of $50\,\mathrm{s}$, because the definition concerns the whole measurement interval. The two intervals have different durations, so the arithmetic mean of the two currents would give $0.20\,\mathrm{A}$, a different value. The average current is **$+0.12\,\mathrm{A}$**.

</div>
