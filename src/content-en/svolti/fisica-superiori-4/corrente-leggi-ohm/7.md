## Exercise 1

A device draws an average of $0.96\,\mathrm{kW}$ for $2.5\,\mathrm{h}$. Calculate the energy in kilowatt-hours.

The energy transferred is the product of the average power and the duration,

$$
E=P_{\mathrm{avg}}\,\Delta t.
$$

Substituting the data, keeping the units $\mathrm{kW}$ and $\mathrm{h}$ gives

$$
E=0.96\,\mathrm{kW}\cdot2.5\,\mathrm{h}=2.4\,\mathrm{kWh}.
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The average power is the constant power that would transfer the same energy in the same interval, so the product with the duration gives the total energy even if the actual power varies.

The power is expressed in kilowatts and the time in hours. The product of these units is the kilowatt-hour, so there is no need to convert to watts and seconds. The numerical product $0.96\cdot2.5$ equals $2.4$.

A device of $1\,\mathrm{kW}$ over the same time would consume $2.5\,\mathrm{kWh}$, slightly more than the result, consistent with an average power slightly below $1\,\mathrm{kW}$. The energy drawn is **$2.4\,\mathrm{kWh}$**.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A device draws $1.2\,\mathrm{kW}$ for $2.5\,\mathrm{h}$. Calculate the energy in kilowatt-hours and in joules.

::grafico{f="1.2*x" xmin=0 xmax=4 ymin=0 ymax=5 nomi="t,E" punti="2.5,3,pieno" titolo="Time in hours and energy in kilowatt-hours. At $2.5\,\mathrm{h}$ we read $3\,\mathrm{kWh}$."}

We apply the energy at constant power and the conversion factor between kilowatt-hours and joules:

$$
E=P\,\Delta t,\qquad 1\,\mathrm{kWh}=1000\,\mathrm{W}\cdot3600\,\mathrm{s}=3.6\cdot10^6\,\mathrm{J}.
$$

Substituting the data gives

$$
E=1.2\,\mathrm{kW}\cdot2.5\,\mathrm{h}=3.0\,\mathrm{kWh}.
$$

$$
E=3.0\cdot3.6\cdot10^6\,\mathrm{J}=1.08\cdot10^7\,\mathrm{J}.
$$

Check in International System units:

$$
E=1200\,\mathrm{W}\cdot9000\,\mathrm{s}=1.08\cdot10^7\,\mathrm{J}.
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The power is constant, so the energy grows linearly with time, as the line in the graph of slope $1.2\,\mathrm{kW}$ shows. The product with $2.5\,\mathrm{h}$ gives $3.0\,\mathrm{kWh}$.

To express the same energy in joules we multiply by the number of joules contained in one kilowatt-hour. This factor accounts both for the passage from kilowatts to watts, that is, $10^3$, and for that from hours to seconds, that is, $3600$. The product $3.0\cdot3.6\cdot10^6$ equals $1.08\cdot10^7$.

With a power of $1200\,\mathrm{W}$ and a duration of $9000\,\mathrm{s}$ we obtain the same number of joules. The energy is **$3.0\,\mathrm{kWh}$**, that is, **$1.08\cdot10^7\,\mathrm{J}$**.

</div>

## Exercise 3

An oven of $2.0\,\mathrm{kW}$ runs for $45\,\mathrm{min}$ at full power and for another $60\,\mathrm{min}$ at half power. Calculate the total energy in kilowatt-hours and in joules, the average power over the whole interval, and the cost at $0.25\,€/\mathrm{kWh}$.

The energy is summed over intervals of constant power, the average power is the ratio of the energy to the total duration, and the cost is the product of the energy and the unit price:

$$
E=P_1\Delta t_1+P_2\Delta t_2,\qquad P_{\mathrm{avg}}=\frac{E}{\Delta t_1+\Delta t_2},\qquad C=E\cdot c_u.
$$

Converting the times to hours and substituting the data:

$$
\Delta t_1=45\,\mathrm{min}=0.75\,\mathrm{h},\qquad \Delta t_2=1.0\,\mathrm{h},\qquad P_2=1.0\,\mathrm{kW}.
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
E&=2.0\cdot0.75\,\mathrm{kWh}+1.0\cdot1.0\,\mathrm{kWh}\\[6pt]
&=(1.5+1.0)\,\mathrm{kWh}=2.5\,\mathrm{kWh}.
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
E=2.5\cdot3.6\cdot10^6\,\mathrm{J}=9.0\cdot10^6\,\mathrm{J}.
$$

$$
P_{\mathrm{avg}}=\frac{2.5\,\mathrm{kWh}}{1.75\,\mathrm{h}}\simeq1.43\,\mathrm{kW},\qquad C=2.5\cdot0.25\,€=0.625\,€\simeq0.63\,€.
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The power changes during use, so the energy is not obtained with a single product. We add the energies of the two intervals, in each of which the power is constant. First we convert the $45\,\mathrm{min}$ to hours, because the power is in kilowatts and we want kilowatt-hours.

In the first interval the oven draws $1.5\,\mathrm{kWh}$. In the second the power is half of $2.0\,\mathrm{kW}$, so $1.0\,\mathrm{kW}$ for $1.0\,\mathrm{h}$ gives $1.0\,\mathrm{kWh}$. The sum is $2.5\,\mathrm{kWh}$ and in joules $9.0\cdot10^6\,\mathrm{J}$.

The average power is obtained by dividing the total energy by the whole duration of $1.75\,\mathrm{h}$. The value $1.43\,\mathrm{kW}$ lies between $1.0$ and $2.0\,\mathrm{kW}$, as it must for an average of two powers.

The cost is the product of the energy in $\mathrm{kWh}$ and the unit cost, because the rate refers to the kilowatt-hour. The oven draws **$2.5\,\mathrm{kWh}$**, equal to **$9.0\cdot10^6\,\mathrm{J}$**, with average power **$1.43\,\mathrm{kW}$** and a cost of about **$0.63\,€$**.

</div>
