## Exercise 1

An ohmic resistor of $20\,\Omega$ carries $0.40\,\mathrm{A}$. Calculate the power dissipated.

We apply the power absorbed by a resistor and Ohm's law, from which we eliminate the voltage:

$$
P=VI,\qquad V=RI\quad\Longrightarrow\quad P=RI^2.
$$

Substituting the data gives

$$
P=20\,\Omega\cdot(0.40\,\mathrm{A})^2=20\cdot0.16\,\mathrm{W}=3.2\,\mathrm{W}.
$$

Check with the voltage:

$$
V=RI=20\cdot0.40\,\mathrm{V}=8\,\mathrm{V},\qquad P=VI=8\cdot0.40\,\mathrm{W}=3.2\,\mathrm{W}.
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The power absorbed is $VI$, but the data are the resistance and the current, not the voltage. Since the resistor is ohmic, we can substitute $V=RI$ and obtain $P=RI^2$, in which only the data appear.

The square of the current is $0.16\,\mathrm{A^2}$ and the product with $20\,\Omega$ gives $3.2$. The unit is $\Omega\,\mathrm{A^2}=\mathrm{V\,A}=\mathrm{W}$.

The voltage drop across the resistor is $8\,\mathrm{V}$ and the product with $0.40\,\mathrm{A}$ reproduces the same value, that is, $3.2\,\mathrm{J}$ per second. The resistor dissipates **$3.2\,\mathrm{W}$**.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A $25\,\Omega$ resistor carries a constant current of $0.40\,\mathrm{A}$. Calculate the power and the energy dissipated in $90\,\mathrm{s}$.

We apply the power as a function of the current and the energy transferred at constant power:

$$
P=RI^2,\qquad E=P\,\Delta t.
$$

Substituting the data gives

$$
P=25\,\Omega\cdot(0.40\,\mathrm{A})^2=25\cdot0.16\,\mathrm{W}=4.0\,\mathrm{W}.
$$

$$
E=P\,\Delta t=4.0\,\mathrm{W}\cdot90\,\mathrm{s}=360\,\mathrm{J}.
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The current and the resistance are constant, so the power has the same value throughout the interval and follows from $P=RI^2$. The result is $4.0\,\mathrm{W}$, that is, $4.0\,\mathrm{J}$ dissipated each second.

Since the power is constant, the energy transferred in the interval is the product of the power and the duration. A watt times a second is a joule, so $E=360\,\mathrm{J}$.

If the duration doubles, the power stays $4.0\,\mathrm{W}$ and the dissipated energy doubles. The power dissipated is **$4.0\,\mathrm{W}$** and the energy in $90\,\mathrm{s}$ is **$360\,\mathrm{J}$**.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A $20\,\Omega$ resistor operates at $8\,\mathrm{V}$ with $0.40\,\mathrm{A}$. It is replaced with a $40\,\Omega$ one. Calculate the new power first with the voltage kept at $8\,\mathrm{V}$, then with the current kept at $0.40\,\mathrm{A}$.

::grafico{f="64/x;0.16*x" xmin=0 xmax=48 ymin=0 ymax=8 nomi="R,P (W)" legenda="V=8\,\mathrm{V};I=0.40\,\mathrm{A}" punti="20,3.2,pieno;40,1.6,pieno;40,6.4,pieno" titolo="Resistance in ohms and power in watts. At $R=20\,\Omega$ the curves meet. At $R=40\,\Omega$ they give different powers."}

With the voltage fixed we use $P=\dfrac{V^2}{R}$, with the current fixed we use $P=RI^2$:

$$
P_V=\frac{V^2}{R_2},\qquad P_I=R_2\,I^2.
$$

Substituting the data with $R_2=40\,\Omega$ gives

$$
P_V=\frac{(8\,\mathrm{V})^2}{40\,\Omega}=\frac{64}{40}\,\mathrm{W}=1.6\,\mathrm{W}.
$$

$$
P_I=40\,\Omega\cdot(0.40\,\mathrm{A})^2=40\cdot0.16\,\mathrm{W}=6.4\,\mathrm{W}.
$$

Initial value:

$$
P_1=\frac{8^2}{20}\,\mathrm{W}=3.2\,\mathrm{W}.
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The initial power is $3.2\,\mathrm{W}$. The comparison depends on which quantity is kept constant, because when the resistance changes, voltage and current cannot both stay the same.

If the voltage is fixed at $8\,\mathrm{V}$, the current in the new resistor falls to $\dfrac{8}{40}=0.20\,\mathrm{A}$. The power is the product $VI=8\cdot0.20=1.6\,\mathrm{W}$, equal to $\dfrac{V^2}{R_2}$, so it is halved.

If the current is fixed at $0.40\,\mathrm{A}$, the voltage across the new resistor rises to $RI=16\,\mathrm{V}$. The product $16\cdot0.40$ gives $6.4\,\mathrm{W}$, so the power doubles. In the graph the two curves meet at $R=20\,\Omega$ and separate for $R=40\,\Omega$. The new power is **$1.6\,\mathrm{W}$** with constant voltage and **$6.4\,\mathrm{W}$** with constant current.

</div>
