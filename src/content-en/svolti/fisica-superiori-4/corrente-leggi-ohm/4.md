## Exercise 1

A uniform wire has $\rho=1.7\cdot10^{-8}\,\Omega\,\mathrm{m}$, length $9.6\,\mathrm{m}$, and cross section $1.5\,\mathrm{mm^2}$. Calculate the resistance at fixed temperature.

::grafico{f="11.33*x" xmin=0 xmax=12 ymin=0 ymax=150 nomi="L,R (mΩ)" punti="9.6,108.8,pieno" titolo="Length in meters and resistance in milliohms. At $9.6\,\mathrm{m}$ we read $108.8\,\mathrm{m\Omega}$."}

Second law of Ohm for a homogeneous and uniform wire at fixed temperature:

$$
R=\rho\,\frac{L}{A}.
$$

Converting the cross section and substituting the data:

$$
A=1.5\,\mathrm{mm^2}=1.5\cdot10^{-6}\,\mathrm{m^2}.
$$

$$
R=\frac{1.7\cdot10^{-8}\,\Omega\,\mathrm{m}\cdot9.6\,\mathrm{m}}{1.5\cdot10^{-6}\,\mathrm{m^2}}=\frac{1.632\cdot10^{-7}}{1.5\cdot10^{-6}}\,\Omega=0.1088\,\Omega.
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The wire is homogeneous and uniform and the temperature is fixed, so we apply the second law of Ohm, in which the resistance is proportional to the length and inversely proportional to the cross section.

Since the cross section is an area, the factor $10^{-3}$ from millimeters to meters must be squared. We have $1\,\mathrm{mm^2}=10^{-6}\,\mathrm{m^2}$ and the cross section is $1.5\cdot10^{-6}\,\mathrm{m^2}$. With this conversion the units are consistent with those of the resistivity.

In the numerator the meters of the resistivity and those of the length give square meters, which cancel with those of the cross section. A resistance in ohms remains. The numerator is $1.632\cdot10^{-7}$ and the quotient with $1.5\cdot10^{-6}$ is $0.1088$.

In the graph the resistance grows linearly with the length, with coefficient $\dfrac{\rho}{A}=11.33\,\mathrm{m\Omega/m}$. The resistance of the wire is **$0.1088\,\Omega$**.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A wire with $\rho=1.7\cdot10^{-8}\,\Omega\,\mathrm{m}$ is $12\,\mathrm{m}$ long and has cross section $1.5\cdot10^{-6}\,\mathrm{m^2}$. Calculate the resistance and the power dissipated with $2.0\,\mathrm{A}$.

We apply the second law of Ohm and the power dissipated by a resistor carrying a known current:

$$
R=\rho\,\frac{L}{A},\qquad P=R\,I^2.
$$

Substituting the data gives

$$
R=\frac{1.7\cdot10^{-8}\cdot12}{1.5\cdot10^{-6}}\,\Omega=\frac{2.04\cdot10^{-7}}{1.5\cdot10^{-6}}\,\Omega=0.136\,\Omega.
$$

$$
P=RI^2=0.136\,\Omega\cdot(2.0\,\mathrm{A})^2=0.136\cdot4.0\,\mathrm{W}=0.544\,\mathrm{W}.
$$

Check with the voltage drop across the wire:

$$
V=RI=0.136\cdot2.0\,\mathrm{V}=0.272\,\mathrm{V},\qquad P=VI=0.272\cdot2.0\,\mathrm{W}=0.544\,\mathrm{W}.
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The geometry and the material fix the resistance of the wire, which follows from the second law of Ohm. The cross section is already in square meters, so no conversion is needed and we obtain $0.136\,\Omega$.

Since the current is known, the power dissipated follows from $P=RI^2$. This expression derives from $P=VI$ with $V=RI$, and for this reason the square of the current multiplies the resistance. With $I=2.0\,\mathrm{A}$ the square is $4.0\,\mathrm{A^2}$ and the product is $0.544\,\mathrm{W}$.

The voltage across the wire is $0.272\,\mathrm{V}$ and the product with the same current reproduces the power. So the wire dissipates **$0.544\,\mathrm{W}$** and has resistance **$0.136\,\Omega$**.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A $0.24\,\Omega$ wire is stretched to one and a half times its initial length, keeping volume and resistivity constant. Calculate the new resistance.

We apply the second law of Ohm and the conservation of the volume $\mathcal V=LA$, where $\mathcal V$ is the volume of the wire:

$$
R=\rho\frac{L}{A},\qquad L_1A_1=L_2A_2.
$$

With $L_2=1.5L_1$ the constant volume fixes the new cross section:

$$
A_2=\frac{L_1}{L_2}A_1=\frac{A_1}{1.5}.
$$

Symbolic solution and substitution of the data:

$$
\begin{aligned}
R_2&=\rho\frac{L_2}{A_2}=\rho\frac{1.5L_1}{\dfrac{A_1}{1.5}}\\[6pt]
&=1.5^2\,\rho\frac{L_1}{A_1}=1.5^2R_1\\[6pt]
&=2.25\cdot0.24\,\Omega=0.54\,\Omega.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

During the stretching the material does not change and the volume stays constant, so the product of length and cross section has the same value before and after. If the length is multiplied by $1.5$, the cross section is divided by the same factor.

In the second law of Ohm the length is in the numerator and the cross section in the denominator. The factor $1.5$ on the length therefore multiplies the resistance by $1.5$, and the reduced cross section multiplies it by another factor $1.5$. The ratio $\dfrac{L}{A}$ increases by the factor $1.5^2=2.25$.

The resistivity is the same, because it depends on the material and on the temperature. We obtain $R_2=2.25\cdot0.24\,\Omega=0.54\,\Omega$. Stretching the wire to one and a half times its length, the resistance becomes **$0.54\,\Omega$**, larger than the initial value by a factor $2.25$.

</div>
