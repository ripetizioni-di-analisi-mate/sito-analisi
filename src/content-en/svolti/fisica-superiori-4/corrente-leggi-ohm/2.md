## Exercise 1

A charge of magnitude $0.384\,\mathrm{C}$ passes through the section of a wire. Calculate the net number of electrons with $e=1.60\cdot10^{-19}\,\mathrm{C}$.

::grafico{f="0.16*x" xmin=0 xmax=4 ymin=0 ymax=0.7 nomi="N,q" punti="2.4,0.384,pieno" titolo="The count N is expressed in billions of billions and the charge q in coulombs."}

The charge is an integer multiple of the elementary charge, so

$$
|\Delta q|=N\,e\quad\Longrightarrow\quad N=\frac{|\Delta q|}{e}.
$$

Substituting the data gives

$$
N=\frac{0.384\,\mathrm{C}}{1.60\cdot10^{-19}\,\mathrm{C}}=2.40\cdot10^{18}.
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Every electron contributes the same charge of magnitude $e$, so $N$ electrons carry a charge magnitude $Ne$. Equating this quantity to the known magnitude $|\Delta q|$ and dividing by $e$ gives $N$.

In the calculation we divide $0.384$ by $1.60$ and multiply by $10^{19}$, because $10^{-19}$ appears in the denominator. The quotient is $0.24$, so $N=0.24\cdot10^{19}=2.40\cdot10^{18}$.

The coulombs in the numerator and denominator cancel and the result is a pure number. In the graph the charge is proportional to the number of electrons with coefficient $0.16\,\mathrm{C}$ per billion billion, and the marked point corresponds to **$2.40\cdot10^{18}$ electrons**.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A charge of magnitude $0.48\,\mathrm{C}$ passes through a wire with a constant current of $0.20\,\mathrm{A}$. Calculate the net number of electrons and the time, using $e=1.60\cdot10^{-19}\,\mathrm{C}$.

We apply the quantization of charge and the definition of constant current, from which we solve for the time:

$$
N=\frac{|\Delta q|}{e},\qquad I=\frac{\Delta q}{\Delta t}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \Delta t=\frac{\Delta q}{I}.
$$

Substituting the data gives

$$
N=\frac{0.48\,\mathrm{C}}{1.60\cdot10^{-19}\,\mathrm{C}}=3.00\cdot10^{18}.
$$

$$
\Delta t=\frac{0.48\,\mathrm{C}}{0.20\,\mathrm{A}}=\frac{0.48\,\mathrm{C}}{0.20\,\mathrm{C/s}}=2.4\,\mathrm{s}.
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The number of electrons depends only on the total charge and the charge of one electron. Dividing $0.48\,\mathrm{C}$ by $e$ gives $3.00\cdot10^{18}$, because $0.48$ divided by $1.60$ is $0.30$ and the factor $10^{19}$ leads to $3.00\cdot10^{18}$.

The time depends on how quickly the charge crosses the section. Since the current is constant, $I=\dfrac{\Delta q}{\Delta t}$ holds throughout the interval, so $\Delta t=\dfrac{\Delta q}{I}$. One ampere is one coulomb per second, so the quotient of coulombs and amperes is expressed in seconds.

The two denominators have different units, the charge $e$ for the count and the current for the time. Multiplying, $0.20\,\mathrm{A}$ times $2.4\,\mathrm{s}$ gives back $0.48\,\mathrm{C}$, in agreement with the data. **$3.00\cdot10^{18}$ electrons** pass in **$2.4\,\mathrm{s}$**.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A wire of cross section $2.0\,\mathrm{mm^2}$ contains $8.0\cdot10^{28}$ conduction electrons per cubic meter. Calculate the magnitude of the drift speed with current $2.56\,\mathrm{A}$ and $e=1.60\cdot10^{-19}\,\mathrm{C}$.

The current of a single type of carrier in a uniform section is $|I|=n\,e\,A\,v_d$, so

$$
v_d=\frac{I}{n\,e\,A}.
$$

Converting the cross section and substituting the data:

$$
A=2.0\,\mathrm{mm^2}=2.0\cdot10^{-6}\,\mathrm{m^2}.
$$

$$
v_d=\frac{2.56\,\mathrm{A}}{8.0\cdot10^{28}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}\cdot1.60\cdot10^{-19}\,\mathrm{C}\cdot2.0\cdot10^{-6}\,\mathrm{m^2}}=\frac{2.56}{2.56\cdot10^{4}}\,\mathrm{m/s}=1.0\cdot10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m/s}.
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The section of wire is uniform and the only type of carrier considered is the electron, so the relation $|I|=n e A v_d$ applies. Solving for $v_d$, we divide the current by the product $neA$.

First we convert the cross section to square meters. Since $1\,\mathrm{mm}=10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m}$, the factor must be squared and $1\,\mathrm{mm^2}=10^{-6}\,\mathrm{m^2}$. The carrier density is given per cubic meter, so the conversion is needed for the units to be consistent.

The product $neA$ is the charge of the carriers contained in one meter of wire and equals $2.56\cdot10^{4}\,\mathrm{C/m}$. The current divided by this quantity gives the meters traveled in one second, so the unit is $\mathrm{m/s}$.

The result is the average speed of the electrons in the direction opposite to conventional current, **$1.0\cdot10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m/s}$**. If the cross section doubles, with current and density fixed, the denominator doubles and each electron advances on average at half the speed.

</div>
