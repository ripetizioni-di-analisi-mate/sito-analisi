## Exercise 1

A wire has $R_0=12\,\Omega$ at $20\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$ and $\alpha=4.0\cdot10^{-3}\,\mathrm{K^{-1}}$. Calculate the resistance at $70\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$ in the linear model.

We use the linear model around the reference temperature $T_0=20\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$:

$$
R(T)=R_0\,[1+\alpha\,(T-T_0)].
$$

Temperature difference and substitution of the data:

$$
\Delta T=T-T_0=(70-20)\,\mathrm{K}=50\,\mathrm{K}.
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
R&=12\,\Omega\,\big(1+4.0\cdot10^{-3}\,\mathrm{K^{-1}}\cdot50\,\mathrm{K}\big)\\[6pt]
&=12\,\Omega\cdot(1+0.20)=12\cdot1.20\,\Omega=14.4\,\Omega.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The interval between $20\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$ and $70\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$ is limited and the wire is metallic, so the linear model with constant $\alpha$ can be applied. The law uses the difference between the final temperature and the one at which $R_0$ was measured.

For a temperature difference, degrees Celsius and kelvins have the same numerical value, so $\Delta T=50\,\mathrm{K}$. The product $\alpha\Delta T$ equals $0.20$ and has no units, because the coefficient is in $\mathrm{K^{-1}}$. It represents the relative increase in resistance, equal to $20\%$.

The factor $1+0.20$ multiplies the reference resistance. At $70\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$ the resistance of the wire is **$14.4\,\Omega$**.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A wire has $R_0=15\,\Omega$ at $20\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$ and $\alpha=4.0\cdot10^{-3}\,\mathrm{K^{-1}}$. Brought to $70\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$, it is kept at $12\,\mathrm{V}$. Calculate the resistance and the current.

::grafico{f="15*(1+0.004*(x-20))" xmin=0 xmax=80 ymin=0 ymax=20 nomi="T,R" punti="20,15,pieno;70,18,pieno" titolo="Temperature in degrees Celsius and resistance in ohms."}

We apply the linear model for resistance and Ohm's law at that temperature:

$$
R=R_0\,[1+\alpha\,(T-T_0)],\qquad I=\frac{V}{R}.
$$

Substituting the data gives

$$
R=15\,\Omega\,(1+0.004\cdot50)=15\cdot1.20\,\Omega=18\,\Omega.
$$

$$
I=\frac{12\,\mathrm{V}}{18\,\Omega}=\frac{2}{3}\,\mathrm{A}\simeq0.667\,\mathrm{A}.
$$

Comparison with the current at $20\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$:

$$
I_0=\frac{12\,\mathrm{V}}{15\,\Omega}=0.80\,\mathrm{A}.
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The final temperature exceeds the reference one by $50\,\mathrm{K}$, so the hot wire has greater resistance, and the graph shows the line rising from $15\,\Omega$ to $18\,\Omega$. The increase factor is $1.20$.

For the current we use the resistance at the operating temperature, because Ohm's law relates voltage and current through the resistance the wire has at that moment. With $R=18\,\Omega$ we obtain $\dfrac{12}{18}=0.667\,\mathrm{A}$.

When cold, with the same voltage, the current would be $0.80\,\mathrm{A}$. The ratio of the two currents is $\dfrac{15}{18}$, so at fixed voltage the current decreases by about $17\%$. The hot resistance is **$18\,\Omega$** and the current is **$0.667\,\mathrm{A}$**.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A copper winding has $R_0=50\,\Omega$ at $20\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$ and $\alpha=4.0\cdot10^{-3}\,\mathrm{K^{-1}}$. During operation its measured resistance is $58\,\Omega$. Determine the temperature reached and the relative change in current if the voltage stays $24\,\mathrm{V}$.

We use the linear model, from which we solve for the temperature difference:

$$
R=R_0[1+\alpha(T-T_0)]\quad\Longrightarrow\quad T-T_0=\frac{1}{\alpha}\left(\frac{R}{R_0}-1\right).
$$

Substituting the data gives

$$
T-T_0=\frac{1}{4.0\cdot10^{-3}\,\mathrm{K^{-1}}}\left(\frac{58}{50}-1\right)=\frac{0.16}{4.0\cdot10^{-3}}\,\mathrm{K}=40\,\mathrm{K}.
$$

$$
T=20\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}+40\,\mathrm{K}=60\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}.
$$

Currents at fixed voltage:

$$
I_0=\frac{24}{50}\,\mathrm{A}=0.48\,\mathrm{A},\qquad I=\frac{24}{58}\,\mathrm{A}\simeq0.414\,\mathrm{A}.
$$

$$
\frac{I_0-I}{I_0}=1-\frac{R_0}{R}=1-\frac{50}{58}\simeq0.138.
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The resistance is known and we look for the temperature, so we solve for $T-T_0$ in the linear model. The ratio $\dfrac{R}{R_0}$ equals $1.16$, so the relative change in resistance is $0.16$. Dividing by $\alpha$ gives $40\,\mathrm{K}$, which added to $20\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$ gives $60\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$. The interval is limited, so the linear model is applicable.

With constant voltage, the current is inversely proportional to the resistance. The ratio $\dfrac{I}{I_0}$ equals $\dfrac{R_0}{R}$, and the relative change in current is $1-\dfrac{50}{58}$.

The resistance increases by $16\%$ and the current decreases by $13.8\%$, a smaller value because the current depends on the reciprocal of the resistance. The currents confirm the value, since $\dfrac{0.48-0.414}{0.48}\simeq0.138$. The winding reaches **$60\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$** and the current falls by about **$13.8\%$**.

</div>
