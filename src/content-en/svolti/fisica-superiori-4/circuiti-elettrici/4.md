## Exercise 1

An unloaded divider is powered at $15\,\mathrm V$ and uses $R_1=2\,\mathrm{k\Omega}$ and $R_2=3\,\mathrm{k\Omega}$ in series. Calculate the voltage across $R_2$ and the current supplied.

$$
\begin{aligned}
R_{\mathrm{eq}} &= R_1+R_2=5\,\mathrm{k\Omega}, \\[6pt]
I &=\frac{\mathcal E}{R_{\mathrm{eq}}}=\frac{15\,\mathrm V}{5\,\mathrm{k\Omega}}=3\,\mathrm{mA}, \\[6pt]
V_2 &=IR_2=(3\,\mathrm{mA})(3\,\mathrm{k\Omega})=9\,\mathrm V, \\[6pt]
V_2 &=\mathcal E\,\frac{R_2}{R_1+R_2}=(15\,\mathrm V)\,\frac{3}{5}=9\,\mathrm V, \\[6pt]
V_1 &=IR_1=(3\,\mathrm{mA})(2\,\mathrm{k\Omega})=6\,\mathrm V.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The divider is **unloaded**, so no current is drawn from the node between $R_1$ and $R_2$ and the two resistors carry the same current. Ohm's law applied to the series gives $3\,\mathrm{mA}$. In the product with a resistance in $\mathrm{k\Omega}$ the factor $10^{-3}$ of the milliamperes cancels with the factor $10^3$ of the kilohms, so the result is directly in volts. The same voltage is obtained with the divider formula, since $R_2$ occupies three fifths of the total resistance and receives three fifths of $15\,\mathrm V$. $6\,\mathrm V$ remains across the resistor $R_1$, and the sum of the two voltages equals the supply voltage. There are $9\,\mathrm V$ across $R_2$ and the source supplies $3\,\mathrm{mA}$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

An ideal $12\,\mathrm V$ battery is connected to $R_1=2\,\mathrm{k\Omega}$ and $R_2=4\,\mathrm{k\Omega}$ in series. The output is measured across $R_2$. Connecting a $4\,\mathrm{k\Omega}$ load to the output, calculate the new voltage and the current in the load.

::schema{riferimento=0 linee="0,1.48 0,3 1.4,3;2.6,3 7,3 7,2.13;7,0.87 7,0 0,0 0,1.16;4.2,3 4.2,2.13;4.2,0.87 4.2,0;-0.44,1.48 0.44,1.48;-0.27,1.16 0.27,1.16;1.4,2.72 2.6,2.72 2.6,3.28 1.4,3.28 1.4,2.72;3.93,0.87 4.47,0.87 4.47,2.13 3.93,2.13 3.93,0.87;6.73,0.87 7.27,0.87 7.27,2.13 6.73,2.13 6.73,0.87" punti="4.2,3,A,pieno;4.2,0,B,pieno" etichette="0.98,1.32,12 V;1.7,3.76,2 kΩ;3.1,1.51,4 kΩ;5.7,1.51,load 4 kΩ" titolo="The output is measured between A and B, where the load is in parallel with $R_2$"}

$$
\begin{aligned}
V_{2,\mathrm{open}} &= \mathcal E\,\frac{R_2}{R_1+R_2}=(12\,\mathrm V)\,\frac{4}{6}=8\,\mathrm V, \\[6pt]
R_{\mathrm{p}} &= \frac{(4\,\mathrm{k\Omega})(4\,\mathrm{k\Omega})}{4\,\mathrm{k\Omega}+4\,\mathrm{k\Omega}}
               =2\,\mathrm{k\Omega}, \\[6pt]
V_{AB} &= \mathcal E\,\frac{R_{\mathrm p}}{R_1+R_{\mathrm p}}=(12\,\mathrm V)\,\frac{2}{4}=6\,\mathrm V, \\[6pt]
I_{\mathrm{L}} &= \frac{V_{AB}}{R_{\mathrm L}}=\frac{6\,\mathrm V}{4\,\mathrm{k\Omega}}
               =1.5\,\mathrm{mA}, \\[6pt]
I_2 &= \frac{V_{AB}}{R_2}=1.5\,\mathrm{mA}, \\[6pt]
I &= \frac{\mathcal E}{R_1+R_{\mathrm p}}=\frac{12\,\mathrm V}{4\,\mathrm{k\Omega}}=3\,\mathrm{mA}.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The load and $R_2$ have the same two nodes $A$ and $B$ across them, so they form a **parallel** combination. Since the two resistances are equal, the equivalent resistance is half of each, that is, $2\,\mathrm{k\Omega}$. The divider then becomes the series of $R_1$ and the equivalent, both of $2\,\mathrm{k\Omega}$, so the $12\,\mathrm V$ divides into equal parts. The $6\,\mathrm V$ across the equivalent is also across the load, and the current in the load is obtained by dividing by $4\,\mathrm{k\Omega}$. $R_2$ also carries $1.5\,\mathrm{mA}$, so the current supplied by the battery, $3\,\mathrm{mA}$, is the sum of the currents of the two branches and passes entirely through $R_1$. Unloaded, the output was $8\,\mathrm V$, because $R_2$ received two thirds of the voltage. The load reduces the output to $6\,\mathrm V$ and draws $1.5\,\mathrm{mA}$.

</div>
