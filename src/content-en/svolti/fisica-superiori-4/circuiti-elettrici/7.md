## Exercise 1

An initially uncharged $200\,\mathrm{\mu F}$ capacitor is connected in series with $5\,\mathrm{k\Omega}$ and a $12\,\mathrm V$ source. Find the voltage, charge, and current $1\,\mathrm s$ after the circuit closes.

::schema{riferimento=0 linee="0,1.48 0,3 2.2,3;4.2,3 7,3 7,1.64;7,1.34 7,0 0,0 0,1.16;-0.44,1.48 0.44,1.48;-0.27,1.16 0.27,1.16;2.2,2.72 4.2,2.72 4.2,3.28 2.2,3.28 2.2,2.72;6.55,1.64 7.45,1.64;6.55,1.34 7.45,1.34" frecce="3.8,3.9,5.6,3.9,I" etichette="1.0,1.32,12 V;3.2,2.2,5 kΩ;5.65,1.48,200 µF" titolo="Circuit in the charging configuration"}

$$
\begin{aligned}
\tau &=(5000\,\Omega)(2\cdot10^{-4}\,\mathrm F)=1\,\mathrm s,\\[6pt]
V_C &= (12\,\mathrm V)(1-e^{-1})\simeq7.59\,\mathrm V,\\[6pt]
q &= (200\,\mathrm{\mu F}) V_C\simeq1.52\,\mathrm{mC},\\[6pt]
I &= \frac{12\,\mathrm V}{5\,\mathrm{k\Omega}}e^{-1}
  \simeq0.883\,\mathrm{mA}.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

To locate this moment in the transient, express $R$ and $C$ in ohms and farads. Their product is $1\,\mathrm s$, so exactly one **time constant** has passed. The capacitor has reached $63.2\%$ of its final voltage, giving the charge from $q=CV_C$. The resistor has the remaining voltage, about $4.41\,\mathrm V$, so the current has already fallen below its initial $2.4\,\mathrm{mA}$. Dividing $4.41\,\mathrm V$ by $5\,\mathrm{k\Omega}$ gives the same current.

</div>

## Exercise 2

In an ideal charging circuit with an initially uncharged capacitor and a $20\,\mathrm{k\Omega}$ resistor, the voltage reaches half its final value after $(2\ln 2)\,\mathrm s$. Find the capacitance.

$$
\begin{aligned}
1-e^{-\frac{t}{\tau}}&=\frac{1}{2},\\[6pt]
\frac{t}{\tau}&=\ln 2,\\[6pt]
\tau&=\frac{(2\ln 2)\,\mathrm s}{\ln 2}=2\,\mathrm s,\\[6pt]
C&=\frac{2\,\mathrm s}{20\,000\,\Omega}
 =10^{-4}\,\mathrm F=100\,\mathrm{\mu F}.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The known voltage is a fraction of the final voltage, so $\mathcal E$ cancels from the charging law. At half charge, the exponential term also equals one-half. Taking the logarithm gives $\dfrac{t}{\tau}=\ln 2$. The measured time thus gives $\tau=2\,\mathrm s$. Seconds divided by ohms give farads, so $C=\dfrac{\tau}{R}$ gives the capacitance.

</div>
