## Exercise 1

A body has $N=3.2\cdot10^{12}$ excess electrons relative to neutrality. With $e=1.60\cdot10^{-19}\,\mathrm C$, find the net charge and write it also in microcoulombs.

$$
\begin{aligned}
q&=-Ne\\
&=-(3.2\cdot10^{12})(1.60\cdot10^{-19})\,\mathrm C\\
&=-(3.2\cdot1.60)\cdot10^{12-19}\,\mathrm C\\
&=-5.12\cdot10^{-7}\,\mathrm C\\
&=-\frac{5.12\cdot10^{-7}}{10^{-6}}\,\mathrm{\mu C}\\
&=-0.512\,\mathrm{\mu C}.
\end{aligned}
$$

::grafico{f="-160*x" punti="3.2,-512,pieno,A" xmin=0 xmax=4.6 ymin=-900 ymax=100 nomi="N,q (nC)" titolo="The $N$ axis counts the excess electrons in thousands of billions. The charge is in nanocoulombs"}

<div class="spiegazione">

In the neutral body the charge of the electrons already present balances that of the positive nuclei. Each added electron carries the charge $-e$, so the net charge is the product of their number $N$ and $-e$. The coefficients are multiplied, $3.2\cdot1.60=5.12$, and the powers of ten combine by adding the exponents, so $10^{12}\cdot10^{-19}=10^{-7}$. The sign is negative because the excess is of negative charges. Since $1\,\mathrm{\mu C}=10^{-6}\,\mathrm C$, the charge in microcoulombs is obtained by dividing by $10^{-6}$, and $5.12\cdot10^{-7}=0.512\cdot10^{-6}$. The body therefore has a net charge of $-0.512\,\mathrm{\mu C}$. The graph shows that the charge is proportional to the number of excess electrons, with coefficient $-1.60\cdot10^{-19}\,\mathrm C$ per electron, and the point $A$ corresponds to the data of the exercise.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A body $A$ has $4.0\cdot10^{12}$ excess electrons and a body $B$ is neutral. In isolation, half of the excess electrons are transferred from $A$ to $B$. With $e=1.60\cdot10^{-19}\,\mathrm C$, find the two final charges.

$$
\begin{aligned}
q_{A,i}&=-Ne=-(4.0\cdot10^{12})(1.60\cdot10^{-19})\,\mathrm C=-6.40\cdot10^{-7}\,\mathrm C\\[6pt]
N_{\mathrm{tr}}&=\frac{N}{2}=2.0\cdot10^{12}\\[6pt]
q_{B,f}&=-N_{\mathrm{tr}}\,e=-(2.0\cdot10^{12})(1.60\cdot10^{-19})\,\mathrm C=-3.20\cdot10^{-7}\,\mathrm C\\[6pt]
q_{A,f}&=q_{A,i}-q_{B,f}=(-6.40+3.20)\cdot10^{-7}\,\mathrm C=-3.20\cdot10^{-7}\,\mathrm C.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The transfer involves half of the excess electrons, so $B$ receives $N_{\mathrm{tr}}=2.0\cdot10^{12}$ electrons. Since $B$ was neutral, its final charge coincides with the charge received and is negative, $-3.20\cdot10^{-7}\,\mathrm C$. The system $A+B$ is isolated, so the total charge is conserved and remains equal to the initial charge of $A$, $-6.40\cdot10^{-7}\,\mathrm C$. Subtracting the final charge of $B$ from this total gives the final charge of $A$, which is $-3.20\cdot10^{-7}\,\mathrm C$. The same value is obtained by counting the electrons left on $A$, because $2.0\cdot10^{12}$ remain. The two final charges are equal because the two bodies split the initial excess equally. On $B$ the charge is negative because it has received electrons, and $A$ remains negative because it retains an excess of electrons relative to neutrality.

</div>
