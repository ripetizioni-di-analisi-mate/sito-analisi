## Exercise 1

An electron is in a uniform field of $96\,\mathrm{N/C}$ pointing to the right. Neglect other forces. With $e=1.60\cdot10^{-19}\,\mathrm C$ and $m_e=9.11\cdot10^{-31}\,\mathrm{kg}$, find the acceleration along the axis pointing to the right.

$$
\begin{aligned}
F_x&=qE_x=(-e)E_x\\[6pt]
&=-(1.60\cdot10^{-19}\,\mathrm C)(96\,\mathrm{N/C})\\[6pt]
&=-1.536\cdot10^{-17}\,\mathrm N\\[10pt]
a_x&=\frac{F_x}{m_e}\\[6pt]
&=\frac{-1.536\cdot10^{-17}\,\mathrm N}{9.11\cdot10^{-31}\,\mathrm{kg}}\\[6pt]
&\simeq-1.69\cdot10^{13}\,\mathrm{m/s^2}.
\end{aligned}
$$

::grafico{assi=0 punti="0,0,pieno,e⁻" vettori="0,0.45,2,0.45,E;0,-0.45,-2,-0.45,a" xmin=-3 xmax=3 ymin=-1.2 ymax=1.35 titolo="The acceleration of the electron is opposite to the field"}

<div class="spiegazione">

The force on a charge in a field is $\vec F=q\vec E$. The electron has negative charge and the field points to the right, so the component of the force is negative and the force points to the left. By Newton's second law the acceleration has the same direction as the force, because the mass is positive, and its magnitude is the ratio $\dfrac{F}{m_e}$. The magnitude is large because the mass in the denominator is very small. As for units, the ratio of newtons to kilograms is $\mathrm{m/s^2}$. If the electron started from rest, it would begin to move to the left with this acceleration. If it had an initial velocity to the right, it would slow to a stop and then reverse its motion. The acceleration is about $1.7\cdot10^{12}$ times $g=9.8\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$, so the weight of the electron is negligible. The same force would give a proton an acceleration smaller by a factor of about $1.8\cdot10^3$, because its mass is larger by that ratio.

</div>

## Exercise 2

An electron starts at the origin with $v_{0x}=4.0\cdot10^4\,\mathrm{m/s}$ and $v_{0y}=0$. The uniform field is $120\,\mathrm{N/C}$ pointing upward. Neglecting other forces, find $x$ and $y$ after $1.0\cdot10^{-9}\,\mathrm s$, with $y$ pointing upward. Use $e=1.60\cdot10^{-19}\,\mathrm C$ and $m_e=9.11\cdot10^{-31}\,\mathrm{kg}$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
a_x&=0,\qquad a_y=\frac{-eE_y}{m_e}=-\frac{(1.60\cdot10^{-19})(120)}{9.11\cdot10^{-31}}\,\mathrm{m/s^2}\simeq-2.11\cdot10^{13}\,\mathrm{m/s^2}\\[6pt]
x&=v_{0x}t=(4.0\cdot10^4)(1.0\cdot10^{-9})\,\mathrm m=4.0\cdot10^{-5}\,\mathrm m\\[6pt]
y&=\frac12 a_yt^2\\[6pt]
&\simeq-\frac{(2.11\cdot10^{13})(1.0\cdot10^{-9})^2}{2}\,\mathrm m\\[6pt]
&\simeq-1.05\cdot10^{-5}\,\mathrm m.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The electric force points downward, because the charge is negative and the field points upward. The horizontal component of the force is zero, so the velocity along $x$ remains constant and the horizontal displacement is $v_{0x}t$. Vertically the electron starts with zero velocity and moves with constant acceleration $a_y$, so the vertical displacement is $\dfrac12a_yt^2$ and depends on the square of the time. The negative sign of $y$ indicates that the point reached is below the origin. The vertical velocity after the given time has magnitude $|a_y|t\simeq2.11\cdot10^4\,\mathrm{m/s}$. Both velocity components are much smaller than the speed of light, so classical physics applies. The trajectory is an arc of a parabola, because $x$ grows linearly in time and $y$ with its square.

</div>

## Exercise 3

An electron with $v_0=2.0\cdot10^6\,\mathrm{m/s}$ crosses, perpendicular to the field lines, a region of length $L=2.0\,\mathrm{cm}$ in which the uniform field is $E=500\,\mathrm{N/C}$. Find the transverse displacement at the exit and the tangent of the deflection angle. Use $e=1.60\cdot10^{-19}\,\mathrm C$ and $m_e=9.11\cdot10^{-31}\,\mathrm{kg}$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
t_L&=\frac{L}{v_0}=\frac{0.020}{2.0\cdot10^6}\,\mathrm s=1.0\cdot10^{-8}\,\mathrm s\\[6pt]
a&=\frac{eE}{m_e}=\frac{(1.60\cdot10^{-19})(500)}{9.11\cdot10^{-31}}\,\mathrm{m/s^2}\simeq8.78\cdot10^{13}\,\mathrm{m/s^2}\\[6pt]
y_L&=\frac12at_L^2\simeq\frac{(8.78\cdot10^{13})(1.0\cdot10^{-8})^2}{2}\,\mathrm m\simeq4.39\cdot10^{-3}\,\mathrm m\\[6pt]
\tan\theta&=\frac{2y_L}{L}=\frac{2(4.39\cdot10^{-3})}{0.020}\simeq0.44.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The velocity along the initial direction remains $v_0$, because the force is perpendicular to it, so the crossing time is $\dfrac{L}{v_0}$. In the direction of the field the electron starts with zero velocity and has constant acceleration $\dfrac{eE}{m_e}$, so the transverse displacement is $\dfrac12at_L^2$. By the theorem on deflection in a uniform field, $\tan\theta=\dfrac{2y_L}{L}$, so the angle is about $24^\circ$. The electron leaves the region displaced by about $4.4\,\mathrm{mm}$ in the direction opposite to the field. After the exit it moves in a straight line, and the backward extension of its trajectory meets the initial direction at $\dfrac{L}{2}=1.0\,\mathrm{cm}$ from the entrance. With the data of the exercise the transverse velocity at the exit is $at_L\simeq8.78\cdot10^5\,\mathrm{m/s}$, smaller than $v_0$ and much smaller than the speed of light. The displacement $y_L$ is proportional to $E$ and to $L^2$ and inversely proportional to $v_0^2$, so with a doubled initial velocity it would become one fourth.

</div>
