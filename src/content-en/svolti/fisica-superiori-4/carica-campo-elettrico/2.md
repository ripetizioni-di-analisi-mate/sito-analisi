## Exercise 1

In vacuum, $q_1=+1.6\,\mathrm{nC}$ is $12\,\mathrm{cm}$ to the left of $q_2=-3.0\,\mathrm{nC}$. Use $k_e=8.99\cdot10^9\,\mathrm{N\,m^2/C^2}$ and find the horizontal component of the force on $q_2$, with the axis pointing to the right.

$$
\begin{aligned}
F_{1\to2,x}&=k_e\frac{q_1q_2}{r^2}\\[6pt]
r&=12\,\mathrm{cm}=0.12\,\mathrm m\\[6pt]
&=\frac{(8.99\cdot10^9)(1.6\cdot10^{-9})(-3.0\cdot10^{-9})}{(0.12)^2}\,\mathrm N\\[6pt]
&=\frac{-4.32\cdot10^{-8}}{1.44\cdot10^{-2}}\,\mathrm N\\[6pt]
&\simeq-3.0\cdot10^{-6}\,\mathrm N.
\end{aligned}
$$

::grafico{assi=0 punti="0,0,pieno,q1 (+);4,0,pieno,q2 (−)" vettori="0.4,0,1.8,0,F on q1;3.6,0,2.2,0,F on q2" xmin=-1 xmax=5 ymin=-1.2 ymax=1.4 titolo="Charges 12 cm apart. The forces point toward each other"}

<div class="spiegazione">

The force acts along the horizontal line joining the two charges. Since going from $q_1$ to $q_2$ means moving to the right, the unit vector $\hat r_{12}$ of Coulomb's law agrees with the positive axis and the component of the force has the sign of $q_1q_2$. The product of the charges is negative, so the component is negative and the force on $q_2$ points to the left, that is, toward the positive charge that attracts it. Before substituting, nanocoulombs are converted to coulombs and centimeters to meters, because $k_e$ is expressed in SI units. The numerator is $-4.32\cdot10^{-8}\,\mathrm{N\,m^2}$ and the denominator $1.44\cdot10^{-2}\,\mathrm{m^2}$, so the magnitude of the force is about $3.0\,\mathrm{\mu N}$. The force on $q_1$ has the same magnitude and points right, but it is applied to a different body. If the distance were doubled, $24\,\mathrm{cm}$, the denominator would be four times larger and the magnitude would drop to about $0.75\,\mathrm{\mu N}$. The result is of the order of micronewtons, as expected for charges of a few nanocoulombs at a distance of a few centimeters.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Two positive charges $q_1=2.0\,\mathrm{nC}$ and $q_2=3.0\,\mathrm{nC}$ are in vacuum at a distance of $0.12\,\mathrm m$. The second is to the right of the first. Find the field of $q_1$ at the position of $q_2$ and the force on $q_2$. Use $k_e=8.99\cdot10^9\,\mathrm{N\,m^2/C^2}$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
E_x&=k_e\frac{q_1}{r^2}\\[6pt]
&=\frac{(8.99\cdot10^9)(2.0\cdot10^{-9})}{(0.12)^2}\,\mathrm{N/C}\\[6pt]
&=\frac{17.98}{1.44\cdot10^{-2}}\,\mathrm{N/C}\simeq1.25\cdot10^3\,\mathrm{N/C}\\[10pt]
F_x&=q_2E_x\\[6pt]
&=(3.0\cdot10^{-9})(1.25\cdot10^3)\,\mathrm N\simeq3.75\cdot10^{-6}\,\mathrm N.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

To distinguish the field from the force, we first compute the effect of the source $q_1$ alone. At a point to the right of a positive source the field is outward, so it points right and its component is positive. Its magnitude is obtained by dividing the product $k_eq_1$ by the square of the distance, $r^2=1.44\cdot10^{-2}\,\mathrm{m^2}$. The force on $q_2$ is found by multiplying the field by the charge $q_2$, according to the relation $\vec F=q\vec E$. Since $q_2$ is positive, the force points along the field, that is, to the right, and is repulsive. The same magnitude is obtained by applying Coulomb's law directly, $k_e\dfrac{q_1q_2}{r^2}$, because the product $q_2\cdot\dfrac{k_eq_1}{r^2}$ coincides with that expression. The force on $q_2$ is therefore about $3.75\,\mathrm{\mu N}$ to the right. If $q_2$ were negative, the field of $q_1$ at its position would remain $1.25\cdot10^3\,\mathrm{N/C}$ to the right and the force would point the opposite way, that is, it would be attractive. The field $\dfrac{k_eq_1}{r^2}$ does not contain $q_2$, so the same field would act on any other charge placed at the same position.

</div>
