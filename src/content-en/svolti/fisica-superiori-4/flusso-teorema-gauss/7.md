## Exercise 1

An infinite insulating plane has uniform density $1.2\cdot10^{-8}\,\mathrm{C/m^2}$. Find the magnitude of the field on one side. Use $\varepsilon_0=8.85\cdot10^{-12}\,\mathrm{C^2/(N\,m^2)}$.

::grafico{assi=0 uguale=1 linee="-2.55,0 2.55,0;-1.15,-0.65 1.15,-0.65 1.15,0.65 -1.15,0.65 -1.15,-0.65" vettori="-0.42,0.68,-0.42,1.48,E;0.42,-0.68,0.42,-1.48,E" etichette="-1.99,0.18,+σ;0.34,0.78,A;-0.5,-0.82,A" xmin=-2.85 xmax=2.85 ymin=-1.65 ymax=1.65 titolo="The field has the same magnitude on the two end caps. The curved side is parallel to the field arrows."}

We choose a short cylinder crossing the plane, with two end caps of area $A$ parallel to the plane. The curved side has zero flux and each end cap has flux $EA$, so Gauss's law gives
$$
2EA=\frac{\sigma_qA}{\varepsilon_0}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
E=\frac{\sigma_q}{2\varepsilon_0}.
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
E&=\frac{1.2\cdot10^{-8}\,\mathrm{C/m^2}}{2\cdot8.85\cdot10^{-12}\,\mathrm{C^2/(N\,m^2)}}\\[6pt]
&=\frac{1.2\cdot10^{-8}}{1.77\cdot10^{-11}}\,\mathrm{N/C}\\[6pt]
&\simeq678\,\mathrm{N/C}.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The field is perpendicular to the plane, so it is parallel to the curved side of the cylinder and perpendicular to the two end caps. The density is positive, so the flux leaves through both end caps and the two contributions have the same sign. The enclosed charge is the product of the density and the cross-section $A$ of the cylinder, because only that portion of the plane lies inside the surface. In Gauss's law the area $A$ cancels, but the factor two from the two contributions remains. The denominator is $2\cdot8.85\cdot10^{-12}=1.77\cdot10^{-11}$. On each side the field points away from the plane and the magnitude is about $678\,\mathrm{N/C}$. Since the plane is infinite, the magnitude does not change with distance and at $1\,\mathrm{cm}$ as at $1\,\mathrm{m}$ the value is the same, $678\,\mathrm{N/C}$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

An infinite insulating plane has uniform positive density $1.5\cdot10^{-8}\,\mathrm{C/m^2}$. Find the magnitude of the field alone and when a second parallel plane of equal magnitude and opposite density is added. In the second case consider the region between the planes. Use $\varepsilon_0=8.85\cdot10^{-12}\,\mathrm{C^2/(N\,m^2)}$.

An isolated plane gives $E_1=\dfrac{\sigma_q}{2\varepsilon_0}$. With two planes of density $+\sigma_q$ and $-\sigma_q$, in the region between them the two fields have the same direction and add, so $E_2=E_1+E_1=\dfrac{\sigma_q}{\varepsilon_0}$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
E_1 &=\frac{1.5\cdot10^{-8}}{2\cdot8.85\cdot10^{-12}}\,\mathrm{N/C}
\simeq847\,\mathrm{N/C},\\[10pt]
E_2 &=\frac{1.5\cdot10^{-8}}{8.85\cdot10^{-12}}\,\mathrm{N/C}
\simeq1.69\cdot10^3\,\mathrm{N/C}\\[6pt]
&=2E_1.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Each isolated plane gives a field of about $847\,\mathrm{N/C}$ on both sides, pointing outward for the positive plane and inward for the negative one. In the region between the planes the field of the positive plane points toward the negative plane and that of the negative plane also points toward the negative plane, so the two fields have the same direction and add. The field is therefore $2E_1\simeq1.69\cdot10^3\,\mathrm{N/C}$. Outside, the magnitudes remain equal but the directions are opposite, so the sum is zero. The factor two does not depend on the value of $\sigma_q$ and holds for every pair of planes of opposite densities. As a check, outside the two planes $E=\dfrac{\sigma_q}{2\varepsilon_0}-\dfrac{\sigma_q}{2\varepsilon_0}=0$, and the field between the planes, $1.69\cdot10^3\,\mathrm{N/C}$, is double that of an isolated plane.

</div>
