## Exercise 1

A closed surface encloses $q_1=+2.4\,\mathrm{nC}$ and $q_2=-1.0\,\mathrm{nC}$. Outside it is $q_3=+5.0\,\mathrm{nC}$. Calculate the total flux. Use $\varepsilon_0=8.85\cdot10^{-12}\,\mathrm{C^2/(N\,m^2)}$.

::grafico{assi=0 uguale=1 curve="1.65*cos(t),1.65*sin(t),0,6.28318530718" punti="-0.52,0.54,pieno,q₁;0.45,-0.51,pieno,q₂;2.46,0.59,pieno,q₃" xmin=-2.5 xmax=3.3 ymin=-2 ymax=2 titolo="The surface encloses two of the three charges. The external charge contributes to the local field."}

$$
\begin{aligned}
Q_{\mathrm{int}}&=(2.4-1.0)\,\mathrm{nC}
=1.4\cdot10^{-9}\,\mathrm{C},\\[6pt]
\Phi_E&=\frac{1.4\cdot10^{-9}}{8.85\cdot10^{-12}}\,\mathrm{N\,m^2/C}
\simeq158\,\mathrm{N\,m^2/C}.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The surface balance includes the charges it encloses. Add $q_1$ and $q_2$ with their signs to obtain the net enclosed charge $+1.4\,\mathrm{nC}$. To apply Gauss's law, multiply the number of nanocoulombs by $10^{-9}$ and then divide by $\varepsilon_0$. The total flux is positive even though one enclosed charge is negative. Charge $q_3$ changes the field at each point, but its net contribution over the closed surface is exactly zero.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A closed surface contains $q_1=+3\,\mathrm{nC}$ and $q_2=-1\,\mathrm{nC}$. Charge $q_3=+5\,\mathrm{nC}$ is first outside, then ends up inside after being moved. Calculate the total flux in the two states. Use $\varepsilon_0=8.85\cdot10^{-12}\,\mathrm{C^2/(N\,m^2)}$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
Q_{\mathrm{int},1}&=(3-1)\,\mathrm{nC}=2\,\mathrm{nC},
&\Phi_1&\simeq226\,\mathrm{N\,m^2/C},\\[6pt]
Q_{\mathrm{int},2}&=(3-1+5)\,\mathrm{nC}=7\,\mathrm{nC},
&\Phi_2&\simeq791\,\mathrm{N\,m^2/C}.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Initially the external charge gives zero net flux. Dividing $2\cdot10^{-9}\,\mathrm{C}$ by $\varepsilon_0$ gives the first flux, about $226\,\mathrm{N\,m^2/C}$. Finally $q_3$ is also enclosed. Dividing $7\cdot10^{-9}\,\mathrm{C}$ by $\varepsilon_0$ gives about $791\,\mathrm{N\,m^2/C}$. As a check, the change in flux is the contribution of the charge that crossed the boundary, which is $5\cdot10^{-9}\,\mathrm{C}$ divided by $\varepsilon_0$.

</div>
