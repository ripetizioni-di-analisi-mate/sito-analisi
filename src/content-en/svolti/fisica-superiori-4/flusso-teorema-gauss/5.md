## Exercise 1

An insulating sphere of radius $0.10\,\mathrm{m}$ contains a uniform total charge of $0.8\,\mathrm{nC}$. Find the magnitude of the field at $0.040\,\mathrm{m}$ from the center. Use $\varepsilon_0=8.85\cdot10^{-12}\,\mathrm{C^2/(N\,m^2)}$.

The point is interior ($r<R$) and the density is uniform, so the enclosed charge is the fraction $\left(\dfrac{r}{R}\right)^3$ of the total charge. Gauss's law with spherical symmetry gives $E=\dfrac{Q_{\mathrm{int}}}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r^2}$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
Q_{\mathrm{int}}&=Q\left(\frac{r}{R}\right)^3
=0.8\cdot(0.40)^3\,\mathrm{nC}=0.0512\,\mathrm{nC},\\[10pt]
E&=\frac{0.0512\cdot10^{-9}\,\mathrm{C}}{4\pi\cdot8.85\cdot10^{-12}\cdot(0.040)^2\,\mathrm{C^2/N}}\\[6pt]
&\simeq288\,\mathrm{N/C}.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The point is inside the insulator. Since the volume density is uniform, the fraction of enclosed charge coincides with the fraction of volume, and the spheres have volumes in the ratio of the cubes of the radii. The radius of the Gaussian sphere is $0.4$ times the outer radius, so the enclosed charge is $0.4^3=0.064$ times the total, that is, $0.0512\,\mathrm{nC}$. Only this charge enters Gauss's law. We divide by $\varepsilon_0$ and by the area of the Gaussian sphere, which contains $(0.040)^2$. The magnitude is about $288\,\mathrm{N/C}$ and, with positive charge, the field points outward. The same value follows from $E=\dfrac{Qr}{4\pi\varepsilon_0R^3}$, a formula that collects the two powers of $r$ of the enclosed charge and of the area. As a check, the value is smaller than the field at the surface, $\dfrac{0.8\cdot10^{-9}}{4\pi\varepsilon_0(0.10)^2}\simeq719\,\mathrm{N/C}$, and is $40\%$ of it, in agreement with $\dfrac{r}{R}=0.40$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

An insulating sphere of radius $R=0.10\,\mathrm{m}$ has uniform total charge $1\,\mathrm{nC}$. Find the magnitude of the field at $r=0.040\,\mathrm{m}$ and at the surface. Use $\varepsilon_0=8.85\cdot10^{-12}\,\mathrm{C^2/(N\,m^2)}$.

::grafico{f="x<=10?89.918*x:89918/x^2" xmin=0 xmax=25 ymin=0 ymax=1000 nomi="r,E (N/C)" punti="4,359.67,pieno;10,899.18,pieno" legenda="E" titolo="The radius $r$ is expressed in centimeters. The interior point at $0.040\,\mathrm{m}$ and the surface at $0.10\,\mathrm{m}$ are highlighted."}

At the surface the Gaussian sphere encloses all the charge, so $E(R)=\dfrac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0R^2}$. Inside the sphere $E(r)=\dfrac{Qr}{4\pi\varepsilon_0R^3}=\dfrac{r}{R}E(R)$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
E(R)&=\frac{10^{-9}}{4\pi\cdot8.85\cdot10^{-12}\cdot(0.10)^2}\,\mathrm{N/C}\\[6pt]
&\simeq899\,\mathrm{N/C},\\[10pt]
E(r)&=\frac{0.040}{0.10}\cdot899\,\mathrm{N/C}\\[6pt]
&\simeq360\,\mathrm{N/C}.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

At the surface the Gaussian sphere encloses all the charge and the area is $4\pi R^2=0.126\,\mathrm{m^2}$, so we obtain about $899\,\mathrm{N/C}$. When we move to an interior sphere, the enclosed charge decreases as $r^3$ and the area as $r^2$, so the ratio decreases in proportion to $r$. At $0.040\,\mathrm{m}$ the field is forty percent of the value at the surface, that is, $0.40\cdot899\simeq360\,\mathrm{N/C}$. The result is consistent with the rising part of the graph, which reaches its maximum at $r=R$. Outside, for $r>R$, the field decreases as $\dfrac{1}{r^2}$, and at $r=2R$ it would be one fourth of $E(R)$. As a check, the field grows linearly from zero at the center to $899\,\mathrm{N/C}$ at the surface, and the point at $r=4\,\mathrm{cm}$ lies on the straight line of the graph at $40\%$ of its maximum height.

</div>
