## Exercise 1

The total flux leaving a closed surface is $+192\,\mathrm{N\,m^2/C}$. Find the net enclosed charge in nanocoulombs. Use $\varepsilon_0=8.85\cdot10^{-12}\,\mathrm{C^2/(N\,m^2)}$.

The surface is closed and the normals are outward, so Gauss's law applies,
$$
\Phi_E=\frac{Q_{\mathrm{int}}}{\varepsilon_0}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
Q_{\mathrm{int}}=\varepsilon_0\Phi_E.
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
Q_{\mathrm{int}}&=8.85\cdot10^{-12}\,\frac{\mathrm{C^2}}{\mathrm{N\,m^2}}\cdot192\,\frac{\mathrm{N\,m^2}}{\mathrm{C}}\\[6pt]
&=1.6992\cdot10^{-9}\,\mathrm{C}\\[6pt]
&\simeq1.70\,\mathrm{nC}.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The flux given is the net flux through the whole surface, so Gauss's law lets us find the enclosed charge by multiplying by $\varepsilon_0$. In the product of the units the newtons and square meters cancel, along with one factor of coulomb, so the coulomb remains. The numerical product $8.85\cdot192=1699.2$ gives $1.6992\cdot10^{-9}\,\mathrm{C}$. Since one nanocoulomb is $10^{-9}\,\mathrm{C}$, the charge is $1.70\,\mathrm{nC}$. The positive sign of the flux indicates that the algebraic sum of the enclosed charges is positive. From this datum we cannot find how many charges are inside or how they are arranged, because the flux depends only on their sum. As a check on the order of magnitude, $\varepsilon_0\sim10^{-11}$ and a flux of a few hundred $\mathrm{N\,m^2/C}$ correspond to a charge of a few nanocoulombs, a typical value for a small charged sphere.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A closed surface has net flux $240\,\mathrm{N\,m^2/C}$. Find the net enclosed charge. Then find the flux if the enclosed charge doubles. Use $\varepsilon_0=8.85\cdot10^{-12}\,\mathrm{C^2/(N\,m^2)}$.

Gauss's law relates the flux to the enclosed charge with $\varepsilon_0$ constant. We have $Q_{\mathrm{int}}=\varepsilon_0\Phi_E$ in the first situation and $\Phi_2=\dfrac{2Q_{\mathrm{int}}}{\varepsilon_0}$ in the second.

$$
\begin{aligned}
Q_{\mathrm{int}}&=8.85\cdot10^{-12}\,\frac{\mathrm{C^2}}{\mathrm{N\,m^2}}\cdot240\,\frac{\mathrm{N\,m^2}}{\mathrm{C}}\\[6pt]
&=2.124\cdot10^{-9}\,\mathrm{C}\simeq2.12\,\mathrm{nC},\\[10pt]
\Phi_2&=\frac{2Q_{\mathrm{int}}}{\varepsilon_0}
=\frac{2\cdot2.124\cdot10^{-9}}{8.85\cdot10^{-12}}\,\mathrm{N\,m^2/C}\\[6pt]
&=480\,\mathrm{N\,m^2/C}.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

In the first situation the positive flux leads to a positive net charge, $2.124\cdot10^{-9}\,\mathrm{C}$, that is, about $2.12\,\mathrm{nC}$. In the second, only the numerator of Gauss's formula changes, while $\varepsilon_0$ is the same constant. The flux is therefore proportional to the enclosed charge and, when this doubles, it doubles too, from $240$ to $480\,\mathrm{N\,m^2/C}$. The same result is obtained without computing the charge, because $2\cdot240=480$. The calculation concerns the total flux. The same charge could be distributed in different ways and produce different local values of the field, while the flux through the closed surface would not change. As a check, the ratio $\dfrac{Q_{\mathrm{int}}}{\varepsilon_0}=\dfrac{2.124\cdot10^{-9}}{8.85\cdot10^{-12}}=240\,\mathrm{N\,m^2/C}$ reproduces the assigned flux, and its double is the final value.

</div>
