## Exercise 1

Two parallel plates have densities $+\sigma_q$ and $-\sigma_q$, with $\sigma_q=6.4\cdot10^{-9}\,\mathrm{C/m^2}$. Neglecting edge effects, find the magnitude and direction of the field between them. Use $\varepsilon_0=8.85\cdot10^{-12}\,\mathrm{C^2/(N\,m^2)}$.

::grafico{assi=0 uguale=1 linee="-1.15,-1.2 -1.15,1.2;1.15,-1.2 1.15,1.2" vettori="-0.85,0.52,0.65,0.52,E₁;-0.85,-0.48,0.65,-0.48,E₂" etichette="-1.57,1.35,+σ;1.07,1.35,−σ" xmin=-2.8 xmax=2.8 ymin=-1.62 ymax=1.68 titolo="In the region between the plates both contributions point from the positive plate to the negative one."}

Each plate, considered as an infinite plane, produces a field of magnitude $\dfrac{\sigma_q}{2\varepsilon_0}$. Between the plates the two fields have the same direction, so they add.

$$
\begin{aligned}
E&=\frac{\sigma_q}{2\varepsilon_0}+\frac{\sigma_q}{2\varepsilon_0}
=\frac{\sigma_q}{\varepsilon_0}\\[6pt]
&=\frac{6.4\cdot10^{-9}\,\mathrm{C/m^2}}{8.85\cdot10^{-12}\,\mathrm{C^2/(N\,m^2)}}\\[6pt]
&\simeq723\,\mathrm{N/C}.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The field due to the positive plate points away from it, while that due to the negative plate points toward it. Between the plates both point from the positive to the negative plate, so they have the same direction and the magnitudes add. The two contributions are equal, each $\dfrac{\sigma_q}{2\varepsilon_0}$, so the factor two cancels and $\dfrac{\sigma_q}{\varepsilon_0}$ remains. Numerically $\dfrac{6.4\cdot10^{-9}}{8.85\cdot10^{-12}}\simeq723\,\mathrm{N/C}$. The density is uniform and the plates are approximated by infinite planes, so this value is the same throughout the gap and the field points from the positive plate to the negative one. As a check, the value $723\,\mathrm{N/C}$ is double that of a single plane, $\dfrac{6.4\cdot10^{-9}}{2\cdot8.85\cdot10^{-12}}\simeq362\,\mathrm{N/C}$, as superposition requires.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Two parallel plates with opposite densities of magnitude $8\cdot10^{-9}\,\mathrm{C/m^2}$ are $0.015\,\mathrm{m}$ apart. Neglecting edge effects, find the magnitude of the field and of the potential difference. Use $\varepsilon_0=8.85\cdot10^{-12}\,\mathrm{C^2/(N\,m^2)}$.

Between the plates the field is uniform, of magnitude $E=\dfrac{\sigma_q}{\varepsilon_0}$. In a uniform field the potential difference between two points on the same field line, at distance $d$, has magnitude $|\Delta V|=Ed$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
E&=\frac{8\cdot10^{-9}}{8.85\cdot10^{-12}}\,\mathrm{N/C}
\simeq904\,\mathrm{N/C},\\[10pt]
|\Delta V|&=Ed=904\,\mathrm{N/C}\cdot0.015\,\mathrm{m}\\[6pt]
&\simeq13.6\,\mathrm{V}.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The field in the gap is the sum of the two fields in the same direction and is about $904\,\mathrm{N/C}$. In a uniform field, along a direction parallel to the field, the potential changes by $E$ for every meter traveled. The distance between the two plates, measured perpendicular to them, is $0.015\,\mathrm{m}$, and multiplying it by the field magnitude gives about $13.6\,\mathrm{V}$, since $\mathrm{N/C}\cdot\mathrm{m}=\mathrm{V}$. The potential of the negative plate is lower than that of the positive one, because moving from the positive to the negative plate means moving in the direction of the field. The result holds far from the edges, where the field is uniform. As a dimensional check, the field can also be expressed in $\mathrm{V/m}$, and $904\,\mathrm{V/m}\cdot0.015\,\mathrm{m}=13.6\,\mathrm{V}$, in agreement with the result.

</div>
