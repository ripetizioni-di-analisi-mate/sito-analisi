## Exercise 1

The temperature of $0.64\,\mathrm{mol}$ of monatomic ideal gas increases by $45\,\mathrm{K}$. With $R=8.314\,\mathrm{J/(mol\,K)}$, calculate $\Delta U$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
C_V&=\frac32R=\frac32\cdot8.314\,\frac{\mathrm{J}}{\mathrm{mol\,K}} \simeq12.47\,\frac{\mathrm{J}}{\mathrm{mol\,K}}, \\[6pt]
nC_V&=0.64\,\mathrm{mol}\cdot12.47\,\frac{\mathrm{J}}{\mathrm{mol\,K}}\simeq7.98\,\frac{\mathrm{J}}{\mathrm{K}}, \\[6pt]
\Delta U&=nC_V\Delta T \\[6pt]
&=0.64\cdot\frac32\cdot8.314\cdot45\,\mathrm{J} \\[6pt]
&\simeq359\,\mathrm{J}.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The internal energy of an ideal gas depends only on temperature, so the problem statement allows $\Delta U$ to be found even without knowing how the gas was heated. For a monatomic ideal gas the molar heat capacity at constant volume is $C_V=\dfrac32R$, and its numerical value is about $12.47\,\mathrm{J/(mol\,K)}$.

The product $nC_V$ is the energy that must be supplied to the whole gas for each kelvin of temperature rise, and it is about $7.98\,\mathrm{J/K}$. Multiplying by $\Delta T=45\,\mathrm{K}$ gives an increase in internal energy of about **$359\,\mathrm{J}$**. The result is positive because the temperature increases. To know the heat exchanged, the work done would also be needed, which the problem does not provide.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A monatomic ideal gas goes from a state with $p_i=100\,\mathrm{kPa}$ and $V_i=2\,\mathrm{L}$ to one with $p_f=180\,\mathrm{kPa}$ and $V_f=3\,\mathrm{L}$. Calculate $\Delta U$ without knowing the number of moles. Also calculate the heat if during the process the gas does $140\,\mathrm{J}$ of work.

::grafico{punti="2,100,pieno,A;3,180,pieno,B" segmenti="2,0,2,100;0,100,2,100;3,0,3,180;0,180,3,180" xmin=0 xmax=4 ymin=0 ymax=250 nomi="V,p (kPa)" titolo="The initial state A and final state B in the $p$-$V$ plane. The path between the two states is not given. Volume is in liters."}

$$
\begin{aligned}
p_iV_i&=100\cdot2\,\mathrm{J}=200\,\mathrm{J}, \qquad p_fV_f=180\cdot3\,\mathrm{J}=540\,\mathrm{J}, \\[6pt]
nR\Delta T&=p_fV_f-p_iV_i \\[6pt]
&=(540-200)\,\mathrm{J}=340\,\mathrm{J}, \\[6pt]
\Delta U&=\frac32nR\Delta T=\frac32\cdot340\,\mathrm{J}=510\,\mathrm{J}, \\[6pt]
Q&=\Delta U+W=(510+140)\,\mathrm{J} \\[6pt]
&=650\,\mathrm{J}.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The equation of state $pV=nRT$ holds in each of the two states, so subtracting $p_iV_i=nRT_i$ from $p_fV_f=nRT_f$ directly gives the product $nR\Delta T$. This product is exactly what appears in $\Delta U=\dfrac32nR\Delta T$, so neither $n$ nor the two temperatures need be known.

The products $pV$ in $\mathrm{kPa}\cdot\mathrm{L}$ are already joules, because $10^3$ and $10^{-3}$ cancel. The gas gains $510\,\mathrm{J}$ of internal energy and delivers $140\,\mathrm{J}$ to the surroundings through work. From the first law $Q=\Delta U+W$ the heat entering must cover both contributions, so **the gas absorbs $650\,\mathrm{J}$**. The value of $\Delta U$ depends only on states A and B, whereas the heat also depends on the work, that is, on the path followed.

</div>
