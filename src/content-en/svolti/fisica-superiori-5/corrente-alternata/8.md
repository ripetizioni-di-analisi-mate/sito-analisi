## Exercise 1

A capacitor of capacitance $10\,\mu\mathrm{F}$ is charged by a $12\,\mathrm{V}$ battery, disconnected from the battery, and connected at time $t = 0$ to an ideal $40\,\mathrm{mH}$ inductor. The circuit resistance is negligible. Calculate the natural angular frequency, the frequency and period of the oscillations, the maximum charge and current, and the energy stored in the circuit.

$$
\omega_0 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{LC}} ,
\qquad
f_0 = \frac{\omega_0}{2\pi} ,
\qquad
T = \frac{1}{f_0} ,
\qquad
Q_0 = C V_0 ,
\qquad
I_0 = \omega_0 Q_0 ,
\qquad
U = \frac{1}{2} C V_0^2
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
\omega_0 &= \frac{1}{\sqrt{4.0 \cdot 10^{-2} \cdot 1.0 \cdot 10^{-5}}}\,\mathrm{rad/s} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{4.0 \cdot 10^{-7}}}\,\mathrm{rad/s} \approx 1.58 \cdot 10^3\,\mathrm{rad/s} \\[6pt]
f_0 &= \frac{1.58 \cdot 10^3}{2\pi}\,\mathrm{Hz} \approx 252\,\mathrm{Hz} ,
\qquad
T = \frac{1}{252}\,\mathrm{s} \approx 3.97\,\mathrm{ms} \\[6pt]
Q_0 &= 1.0 \cdot 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{F} \cdot 12\,\mathrm{V} = 1.2 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{C} \\[6pt]
I_0 &= 1.58 \cdot 10^3\,\mathrm{rad/s} \cdot 1.2 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{C} \approx 0.19\,\mathrm{A} \\[6pt]
U &= \frac{1}{2} \cdot 1.0 \cdot 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{F} \cdot (12\,\mathrm{V})^2 = 7.2 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{J}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The circuit consists only of a capacitor and an ideal inductor, and its resistance is negligible, so we can apply the result on **oscillations in an LC circuit**. Initially the capacitor holds the charge supplied by the battery, $Q_0 = C V_0$, and the current is zero. The charge oscillates according to $Q_0 \cos(\omega_0 t)$, with $\omega_0 = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{LC}}$.

The maximum current is $\omega_0 Q_0$ because the current is the derivative of charge. Conservation of energy, as described in the observation on **energy in an LC circuit**, gives the same value. Initially all the energy is in the capacitor. When the capacitor is discharged, all the energy is in the inductor, so $\dfrac{1}{2} L I_0^2 = 7.2 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{J}$. Solving gives $I_0 = \sqrt{\dfrac{2 \cdot 7.2 \cdot 10^{-4}}{4.0 \cdot 10^{-2}}}\,\mathrm{A} \approx 0.19\,\mathrm{A}$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

In an ideal $LC$ circuit with $L = 0.20\,\mathrm{H}$ and $C = 5.0\,\mu\mathrm{F}$, the capacitor has charge $Q_0 = 40\,\mu\mathrm{C}$ at time $t = 0$ and the current is zero.

1. Determine the first time at which the circuit’s energy is divided equally between the capacitor and the inductor, and find the current at that instant.
2. Determine the capacitance required to double the oscillation frequency while using the same inductor.

$$
\omega_0 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{LC}} ,
\qquad
\frac{q^2}{2C} = \frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{Q_0^2}{2C}
\;\Longrightarrow\;
\cos^2(\omega_0 t) = \frac{1}{2}
\;\Longrightarrow\;
t = \frac{\pi}{4\,\omega_0}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
\omega_0 &= \frac{1}{\sqrt{0.20 \cdot 5.0 \cdot 10^{-6}}}\,\mathrm{rad/s} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1.0 \cdot 10^{-6}}}\,\mathrm{rad/s} = 1.0 \cdot 10^3\,\mathrm{rad/s} \\[6pt]
t &= \frac{\pi}{4 \cdot 1.0 \cdot 10^3}\,\mathrm{s} \approx 7.9 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{s} \\[6pt]
|i| &= \omega_0 Q_0 \sin\frac{\pi}{4} = 1.0 \cdot 10^3 \cdot 4.0 \cdot 10^{-5} \cdot \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\,\mathrm{A} \approx 28\,\mathrm{mA}
\end{aligned}
$$
$$
f_0' = 2 f_0
\;\Longrightarrow\;
\frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{L C'}} = \frac{2}{2\pi\sqrt{L C}}
\;\Longrightarrow\;
\sqrt{C'} = \frac{\sqrt{C}}{2}
\;\Longrightarrow\;
C' = \frac{C}{4} = 1.25\,\mu\mathrm{F}
$$

::grafico{f="cos(x)^2;sin(x)^2" punti="0.785,0.5,pieno" segmenti="0.785,0,0.785,0.5" xmin=0 xmax=7 ymin=-0.2 ymax=1.3 nomi="ω₀t," legenda="\dfrac{U_C}{U};\dfrac{U_L}{U}" titolo="Fractions of the energy in the capacitor and inductor. The curves intersect for the first time at $\omega_0 t = \dfrac{\pi}{4}$, where each equals $\dfrac{1}{2}$"}

<div class="spiegazione">

By the result on **oscillations in an LC circuit**, the charge is $q(t) = Q_0 \cos(\omega_0 t)$, so the capacitor energy is $\dfrac{Q_0^2}{2C} \cos^2(\omega_0 t)$. The total energy, $\dfrac{Q_0^2}{2C}$, is constant. It is divided equally when the capacitor holds half of it, that is, when $\cos^2(\omega_0 t) = \dfrac{1}{2}$. The first positive time at which this happens satisfies $\omega_0 t = \dfrac{\pi}{4}$, one eighth of a period after the start.

At that instant the current is $-\omega_0 Q_0 \sin\dfrac{\pi}{4}$ and its magnitude is about $28\,\mathrm{mA}$. The negative sign indicates that the charge on the initially positive plate is decreasing.

The frequency is inversely proportional to $\sqrt{C}$. To double the frequency, $\sqrt{C}$ must fall by half, so the capacitance must be **one quarter** of its original value. The same behavior occurs for a mass on a spring, by the **analogy with a mass on a spring**. Capacitance corresponds to the reciprocal of the spring constant, and doubling the frequency of a spring requires quadrupling its spring constant.

</div>

## Exercise 3

An alternator consists of a coil of $100$ turns, each of area $0.010\,\mathrm{m^2}$, rotating at $50$ revolutions per second in a uniform magnetic field of $0.20\,\mathrm{T}$ perpendicular to the axis of rotation. The coil has negligible resistance and inductance. The alternator supplies a series circuit containing a $20\,\Omega$ resistor, a $0.10\,\mathrm{H}$ inductor, and a $200\,\mu\mathrm{F}$ capacitor.

1. Calculate the peak and rms values of the emf produced by the alternator.
2. Calculate the circuit impedance, the rms current, and the phase difference between voltage and current. Explain whether the circuit is inductive or capacitive.
3. Calculate the average power supplied by the alternator and the power factor.
4. Replace the capacitor so that the circuit resonates at the alternator frequency. Determine the new capacitance and the new average power.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\omega &= 2\pi \cdot 50\,\mathrm{rad/s} \approx 314\,\mathrm{rad/s} \\[6pt]
\mathcal{E}_0 &= N B S \omega = 100 \cdot 0.20\,\mathrm{T} \cdot 0.010\,\mathrm{m^2} \cdot 314\,\mathrm{rad/s} \approx 62.8\,\mathrm{V} \\[6pt]
V_{\text{eff}} &= \frac{\mathcal{E}_0}{\sqrt{2}} \approx 44.4\,\mathrm{V}
\end{aligned}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
X_L &= 314 \cdot 0.10\,\Omega \approx 31.4\,\Omega ,
\qquad
X_C = \frac{1}{314 \cdot 2.0 \cdot 10^{-4}}\,\Omega \approx 15.9\,\Omega \\[6pt]
Z &= \sqrt{20^2 + (31.4 - 15.9)^2}\,\Omega = \sqrt{20^2 + 15.5^2}\,\Omega \approx 25.3\,\Omega \\[6pt]
I_{\text{eff}} &= \frac{44.4\,\mathrm{V}}{25.3\,\Omega} \approx 1.76\,\mathrm{A} \\[6pt]
\varphi &= \arctan \frac{15.5}{20} \approx 38^\circ
\end{aligned}
$$
$$
\cos\varphi = \frac{R}{Z} = \frac{20}{25.3} \approx 0.79 ,
\qquad
P = R\, I_{\text{eff}}^2 = 20 \cdot 1.76^2\,\mathrm{W} \approx 62\,\mathrm{W}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
X_C' = X_L
\;\Longrightarrow\;
C' &= \frac{1}{\omega^2 L} = \frac{1}{314^2 \cdot 0.10}\,\mathrm{F} \approx 1.0 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{F} = 100\,\mu\mathrm{F} \\[6pt]
P' &= \frac{V_{\text{eff}}^2}{R} = \frac{44.4^2}{20}\,\mathrm{W} \approx 99\,\mathrm{W}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The field is uniform and perpendicular to the axis of rotation, so by the result on the **electromotive force of an alternator**, the emf is sinusoidal with peak value $N B S \omega$ and frequency equal to the rotational frequency, $50\,\mathrm{Hz}$. Its rms value is the peak value divided by $\sqrt{2}$. The coil has negligible resistance and inductance, so the voltage across the external circuit equals the alternator emf.

Calculate the reactances at the angular frequency of the alternator. The result on the **impedance of a series RLC circuit** then gives the impedance and the rms current. The inductive reactance exceeds the capacitive reactance, so $\varphi > 0$, the current lags the voltage by about $38^\circ$, and the circuit is **inductive**.

In the series $RLC$ circuit all the average power is dissipated in the resistor, by the definition of power factor, so $P = R\,I_{\text{eff}}^2$. The result for average power, $44.4 \cdot 1.76 \cdot 0.79\,\mathrm{W} \approx 62\,\mathrm{W}$, gives the same value. This power comes from the work done by the turbine that drives the coil.

By the result on **resonance in a series RLC circuit**, at resonance the capacitive reactance equals the inductive reactance, $\dfrac{1}{\omega C'} = \omega L$, giving $C' = \dfrac{1}{\omega^2 L}$. The new capacitor has about half the previous capacitance, so its reactance doubles and equals $31.4\,\Omega$. At resonance the impedance equals $R$ and the power factor is $1$, so the rms current rises to $\dfrac{44.4}{20}\,\mathrm{A} \approx 2.2\,\mathrm{A}$ and the average power to about $99\,\mathrm{W}$. This is the greatest power attainable by changing only the capacitor, because the impedance is smallest at resonance.

</div>
