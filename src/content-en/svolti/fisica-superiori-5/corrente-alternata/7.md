## Exercise 1

An ideal transformer for a battery charger has a primary of $2000$ turns connected to the grid at $V_{\text{eff}} = 230\,\mathrm{V}$. The required secondary rms voltage is $9.2\,\mathrm{V}$. Calculate the number of secondary turns. If the charger draws an rms current of $1.5\,\mathrm{A}$, determine the power delivered and the rms current in the primary.

$$
\frac{V_2}{V_1} = \frac{N_2}{N_1}
\;\Longrightarrow\;
N_2 = N_1\, \frac{V_2}{V_1} ,
\qquad
P = V_2\, I_2 ,
\qquad
I_1 = \frac{P}{V_1}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
N_2 &= 2000 \cdot \frac{9.2}{230} = 80 \\[6pt]
P &= 9.2\,\mathrm{V} \cdot 1.5\,\mathrm{A} = 13.8\,\mathrm{W} \\[6pt]
I_1 &= \frac{13.8\,\mathrm{W}}{230\,\mathrm{V}} = 0.060\,\mathrm{A}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The transformer is ideal, so by the **ideal transformer** relation, the ratio of the rms voltages equals the turns ratio. Multiplying both sides by $N_1$ gives $N_2 = 80$. The ratio $\dfrac{N_2}{N_1} = \dfrac{1}{25}$ shows that the transformer steps down the voltage by a factor of $25$.

An ideal transformer loses no energy, so the power supplied to the primary equals the power delivered to the charger. Dividing the power by the primary voltage gives the rms primary current, $0.060\,\mathrm{A}$. The second transformer relation, $I_1 = I_2\, \dfrac{N_2}{N_1} = 1.5 \cdot \dfrac{80}{2000}\,\mathrm{A}$, gives the same value. The primary voltage exceeds the secondary voltage by a factor of $25$, so the primary current is smaller by a factor of $25$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

At the output of a power plant, a transformer, assumed ideal, steps up the rms voltage from $15\,\mathrm{kV}$ to $300\,\mathrm{kV}$ and delivers $60\,\mathrm{MW}$ of power to the line. Calculate the ratio of secondary to primary turns and the rms current in each winding.

$$
\frac{N_2}{N_1} = \frac{V_2}{V_1} ,
\qquad
I_1 = \frac{P}{V_1} ,
\qquad
I_2 = \frac{P}{V_2}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{N_2}{N_1} &= \frac{300\,\mathrm{kV}}{15\,\mathrm{kV}} = 20 \\[6pt]
I_1 &= \frac{6.0 \cdot 10^7\,\mathrm{W}}{1.5 \cdot 10^4\,\mathrm{V}} = 4.0 \cdot 10^3\,\mathrm{A} \\[6pt]
I_2 &= \frac{6.0 \cdot 10^7\,\mathrm{W}}{3.0 \cdot 10^5\,\mathrm{V}} = 200\,\mathrm{A}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

By the **ideal transformer** relation, the turns ratio equals the voltage ratio, so the secondary has twenty times as many turns as the primary. The power is the same in both windings, so with a power factor of $1$, each rms current equals the power divided by the voltage across that winding.

The line current is $200\,\mathrm{A}$, a factor of twenty smaller than the $4000\,\mathrm{A}$ current produced by the plant’s generator. By the observation on **high-voltage electric power transmission**, power dissipated in a line of fixed resistance is proportional to the square of the current. The power loss is therefore smaller by a factor of $400$ than it would be if the same power were transmitted at $15\,\mathrm{kV}$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A power plant must transmit $5.0\,\mathrm{MW}$ of power to a city over a line of total resistance $10\,\Omega$ at a power factor of $1$.

1. Calculate the line current, power dissipated, and percentage of power lost if the transmission voltage is $25\,\mathrm{kV}$.
2. Repeat the calculation for a transmission voltage of $250\,\mathrm{kV}$.
3. In the city, an ideal transformer must convert $250\,\mathrm{kV}$ to $10\,\mathrm{kV}$. Calculate the ratio of primary to secondary turns.

$$
I = \frac{P}{V} ,
\qquad
P_{\text{loss}} = R_\ell\, I^2 ,
\qquad
\frac{N_1}{N_2} = \frac{V_1}{V_2}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{with } V = 25\,\mathrm{kV} ,
\quad
I &= \frac{5.0 \cdot 10^6\,\mathrm{W}}{2.5 \cdot 10^4\,\mathrm{V}} = 200\,\mathrm{A} ,
\qquad
P_{\text{loss}} = 10\,\Omega \cdot (200\,\mathrm{A})^2 = 4.0 \cdot 10^5\,\mathrm{W} \\[6pt]
\frac{P_{\text{loss}}}{P} &= \frac{4.0 \cdot 10^5}{5.0 \cdot 10^6} = 0.080 = 8.0\%
\end{aligned}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{with } V = 250\,\mathrm{kV} ,
\quad
I &= \frac{5.0 \cdot 10^6\,\mathrm{W}}{2.5 \cdot 10^5\,\mathrm{V}} = 20\,\mathrm{A} ,
\qquad
P_{\text{loss}} = 10\,\Omega \cdot (20\,\mathrm{A})^2 = 4.0 \cdot 10^3\,\mathrm{W} \\[6pt]
\frac{P_{\text{loss}}}{P} &= \frac{4.0 \cdot 10^3}{5.0 \cdot 10^6} = 8.0 \cdot 10^{-4} = 0.080\%
\end{aligned}
$$
$$
\frac{N_1}{N_2} = \frac{250\,\mathrm{kV}}{10\,\mathrm{kV}} = 25
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

With a power factor of $1$, transmitted power equals voltage times current, so the line current is $\dfrac{P}{V}$. The line behaves as a resistor carrying this current and dissipates power $R_\ell\, I^2$ through Joule heating. At $25\,\mathrm{kV}$, the line loses $8\%$ of the power produced.

Multiplying the voltage by $10$ reduces the current by a factor of $10$ and the power loss by a factor of $100$, in agreement with the formula $P_{\text{loss}} = \dfrac{R_\ell\, P^2}{V^2}$ in the observation on **high-voltage electric power transmission**. At $250\,\mathrm{kV}$, less than one thousandth of the power is lost. The voltage drop along the line, $R_\ell\, I$, decreases from $2000\,\mathrm{V}$ to $200\,\mathrm{V}$, or from $8\%$ to $0.08\%$ of the transmission voltage.

The city’s transformer steps down the voltage, so the primary has more turns than the secondary. By the **ideal transformer** relation, the turns ratio equals the voltage ratio, $25$.

</div>
