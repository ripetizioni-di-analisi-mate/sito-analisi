## Exercise 1

An ideal inductor of inductance $L = 0.50\,\mathrm{H}$ is connected to the power grid, with $V_{\text{eff}} = 230\,\mathrm{V}$ and $f = 50\,\mathrm{Hz}$. Calculate the inductive reactance, the rms current, and the peak current. Explain which quantity peaks first, the voltage or the current.

$$
X_L = 2\pi f L ,
\qquad
I_{\text{eff}} = \frac{V_{\text{eff}}}{X_L} ,
\qquad
I_0 = \sqrt{2}\, I_{\text{eff}}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
X_L &= 2\pi \cdot 50\,\mathrm{Hz} \cdot 0.50\,\mathrm{H} \approx 157\,\Omega \\[6pt]
I_{\text{eff}} &= \frac{230\,\mathrm{V}}{157\,\Omega} \approx 1.46\,\mathrm{A} \\[6pt]
I_0 &= \sqrt{2} \cdot 1.46\,\mathrm{A} \approx 2.07\,\mathrm{A}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The inductor is ideal, meaning its resistance is negligible, so the result for a **purely inductive circuit** applies. The inductive reactance is $X_L = \omega L$, with $\omega = 2\pi f$, and the ratio of rms voltage to rms current equals $X_L$. Dividing the rms voltage by the reactance gives the rms current. Multiplying that current by $\sqrt{2}$ gives its peak value.

In a purely inductive circuit the voltage leads the current by $\dfrac{\pi}{2}$. The voltage therefore peaks **first**, and the current peaks a quarter of a period later, after $5\,\mathrm{ms}$, since the period is $\dfrac{1}{50}\,\mathrm{s} = 20\,\mathrm{ms}$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A capacitor connected to the power grid at $V_{\text{eff}} = 230\,\mathrm{V}$ and $f = 50\,\mathrm{Hz}$ carries an rms current of $0.50\,\mathrm{A}$. Determine the capacitive reactance and the capacitance. Then calculate the rms current if the same capacitor is connected to a source with the same rms voltage and frequency $100\,\mathrm{Hz}$.

$$
X_C = \frac{V_{\text{eff}}}{I_{\text{eff}}} ,
\qquad
X_C = \frac{1}{2\pi f C}
\;\Longrightarrow\;
C = \frac{1}{2\pi f X_C} ,
\qquad
I'_{\text{eff}} = \frac{V_{\text{eff}}}{X'_C} = 2\pi f' C\, V_{\text{eff}}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
X_C &= \frac{230\,\mathrm{V}}{0.50\,\mathrm{A}} = 460\,\Omega \\[6pt]
C &= \frac{1}{2\pi \cdot 50\,\mathrm{Hz} \cdot 460\,\Omega} \approx 6.9 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\mathrm{F} = 6.9\,\mu\mathrm{F} \\[6pt]
X'_C &= \frac{460\,\Omega}{2} = 230\,\Omega ,
\qquad
I'_{\text{eff}} = \frac{230\,\mathrm{V}}{230\,\Omega} = 1.0\,\mathrm{A}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

By the result for a **purely capacitive circuit**, the ratio of rms voltage to rms current equals the capacitive reactance. To solve $X_C = \dfrac{1}{2\pi f C}$ for the capacitance, multiply both sides by $C$ and divide by $X_C$.

Capacitive reactance is inversely proportional to frequency, so doubling the frequency halves the reactance, giving $230\,\Omega$. The rms voltage is unchanged, so the rms current **doubles** to $1.0\,\mathrm{A}$. At the higher frequency, the plates charge and discharge more times per second and more charge passes through the wires in the same time.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A coil of negligible resistance connected to a source of rms voltage $12\,\mathrm{V}$ and frequency $50\,\mathrm{Hz}$ carries an rms current of $0.30\,\mathrm{A}$. Calculate the inductance of the coil. If the source frequency doubles while its rms voltage stays the same, does the rms current double, halve, or stay the same? Explain and calculate its value.

$$
X_L = \frac{V_{\text{eff}}}{I_{\text{eff}}} ,
\qquad
L = \frac{X_L}{2\pi f} ,
\qquad
X'_L = 2\pi (2f) L = 2 X_L ,
\qquad
I'_{\text{eff}} = \frac{V_{\text{eff}}}{X'_L}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
X_L &= \frac{12\,\mathrm{V}}{0.30\,\mathrm{A}} = 40\,\Omega \\[6pt]
L &= \frac{40\,\Omega}{2\pi \cdot 50\,\mathrm{Hz}} \approx 0.13\,\mathrm{H} \\[6pt]
X'_L &= 80\,\Omega ,
\qquad
I'_{\text{eff}} = \frac{12\,\mathrm{V}}{80\,\Omega} = 0.15\,\mathrm{A}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The coil has negligible resistance and therefore behaves as an ideal inductor. We can apply the result for a **purely inductive circuit**. Dividing the rms voltage by the rms current gives the inductive reactance. Dividing that by the angular frequency $2\pi f$ gives the inductance. The units agree because $\dfrac{\Omega}{\mathrm{s^{-1}}} = \Omega \cdot \mathrm{s} = \mathrm{H}$.

Inductive reactance is proportional to frequency, so at twice the frequency it is $80\,\Omega$. With the same rms voltage, the rms current **falls by half** to $0.15\,\mathrm{A}$. This is the opposite of the behavior of a capacitor, for which current increases with frequency, as described in the observation on reactance and frequency.

</div>
