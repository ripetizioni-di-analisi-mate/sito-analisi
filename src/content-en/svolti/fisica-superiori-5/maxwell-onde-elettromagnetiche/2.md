## Exercise 1

A parallel-plate capacitor in a vacuum has plates of area $S = 0.040\,\mathrm{m^2}$ separated by a distance $d = 2.0\,\mathrm{mm}$ and charges at a constant current $i = 0.20\,\mathrm{A}$. Neglecting edge effects, calculate the displacement current between the plates, the rate at which the electric field increases, and the rate at which the potential difference between the plates increases ($\varepsilon_0 = 8.85 \cdot 10^{-12}\,\mathrm{C^2/(N\cdot m^2)}$).

$$
i_s = \varepsilon_0 \frac{d\Phi(\vec{E})}{dt} ,
\qquad
\Phi(\vec{E}) = E S = \frac{q}{\varepsilon_0}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
i_s = \frac{dq}{dt} = i
$$
$$
i_s = \varepsilon_0 S \frac{dE}{dt}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\frac{dE}{dt} = \frac{i}{\varepsilon_0 S} ,
\qquad
\Delta V = E d
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\frac{d(\Delta V)}{dt} = d\,\frac{dE}{dt}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
i_s &= 0.20\,\mathrm{A} \\[6pt]
\frac{dE}{dt} &= \frac{0.20}{8.85 \cdot 10^{-12} \cdot 0.040}\,\mathrm{V/(m\cdot s)} \approx 5.6 \cdot 10^{11}\,\mathrm{V/(m\cdot s)} \\[6pt]
\frac{d(\Delta V)}{dt} &= 2.0 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m} \cdot 5.65 \cdot 10^{11}\,\mathrm{V/(m\cdot s)} \approx 1.1 \cdot 10^{9}\,\mathrm{V/s}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Edge effects are negligible, so the field between the plates is uniform, perpendicular to the plates, and of magnitude $E = \dfrac{q}{\varepsilon_0 S}$, while outside it is zero. The flux through a surface between and parallel to the plates is therefore $E S = \dfrac{q}{\varepsilon_0}$. Multiplying by $\varepsilon_0$ and differentiating gives $i_s = \dfrac{dq}{dt}$, which is the current in the wires. Hence the **displacement current** equals $0.20\,\mathrm{A}$ regardless of the plate area or separation.

Writing the same formula as $i_s = \varepsilon_0 S \dfrac{dE}{dt}$ gives the rate of increase of the field, about $5.6 \cdot 10^{11}\,\mathrm{V/(m\cdot s)}$. In a uniform field, the potential difference between the plates is $\Delta V = E d$, and the separation $d$ is constant. Thus $\Delta V$ increases at a rate $d\,\dfrac{dE}{dt} \approx 1.1 \cdot 10^{9}\,\mathrm{V/s}$. The capacitance $C = \dfrac{\varepsilon_0 S}{d} \approx 1.8 \cdot 10^{-10}\,\mathrm{F}$ gives the same result because $\dfrac{d(\Delta V)}{dt} = \dfrac{i}{C}$. The rate is very large, so in one microsecond the voltage increases by about $1.1 \cdot 10^{3}\,\mathrm{V}$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A parallel-plate capacitor in a vacuum has circular plates of radius $R = 5.0\,\mathrm{cm}$. During charging, the uniform electric field between the plates increases from zero to $3.0 \cdot 10^{5}\,\mathrm{V/m}$ in $2.0\,\mu\mathrm{s}$ at a constant rate. Calculate the displacement current between the plates, the charge accumulated by the end of the interval, and the circulation of the magnetic field around a circle enclosing the supply wire ($\varepsilon_0 = 8.85 \cdot 10^{-12}\,\mathrm{C^2/(N\cdot m^2)}$, $\mu_0 = 4\pi \cdot 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{T\cdot m/A}$).

$$
i_s = \varepsilon_0 \pi R^2 \frac{dE}{dt} ,
\qquad
q = \varepsilon_0 \pi R^2 E_{\text{final}} ,
\qquad
\Gamma(\vec{B}) = \mu_0\, i
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{dE}{dt} &= \frac{3.0 \cdot 10^{5}\,\mathrm{V/m}}{2.0 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\mathrm{s}} = 1.5 \cdot 10^{11}\,\mathrm{V/(m\cdot s)} \\[6pt]
i_s &= 8.85 \cdot 10^{-12} \cdot \pi \cdot 0.050^2 \cdot 1.5 \cdot 10^{11}\,\mathrm{A} \approx 1.0 \cdot 10^{-2}\,\mathrm{A} \\[6pt]
q &= 8.85 \cdot 10^{-12} \cdot \pi \cdot 0.050^2 \cdot 3.0 \cdot 10^{5}\,\mathrm{C} \approx 2.1 \cdot 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{C} \\[6pt]
\Gamma(\vec{B}) &= 4\pi \cdot 10^{-7} \cdot 1.04 \cdot 10^{-2}\,\mathrm{T\cdot m} \approx 1.3 \cdot 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{T\cdot m}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The field between the plates is uniform and perpendicular to them, so the flux through the disk of radius $R$ is $E\,\pi R^2$. The field increases at a constant rate, so its time derivative is the change $3.0 \cdot 10^{5}\,\mathrm{V/m}$ divided by the time interval. By the definition of **displacement current**, multiplying the flux derivative by $\varepsilon_0$ gives about $1.0 \cdot 10^{-2}\,\mathrm{A}$.

The electric flux between the plates is $\dfrac{q}{\varepsilon_0}$, so the final charge is $\varepsilon_0$ times the final flux, or about $2.1 \cdot 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{C}$. As a check, the current in the wires is constant and equals $i_s$, and in time $2.0\,\mu\mathrm{s}$ it transfers charge $1.04 \cdot 10^{-2}\,\mathrm{A} \cdot 2.0 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\mathrm{s} \approx 2.1 \cdot 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{C}$.

By the **Ampère–Maxwell law**, the circulation around a circle enclosing the wire is $\mu_0 (i_{\text{enc}} + i_s)$. For a flat surface crossed by the wire, $i_{\text{enc}} = i$ and $i_s = 0$. For a surface extending between the plates, $i_{\text{enc}} = 0$ and $i_s = i$. Thus both choices give the same value $\mu_0 i \approx 1.3 \cdot 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{T\cdot m}$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A parallel-plate capacitor in a vacuum has plates of area $S = 0.020\,\mathrm{m^2}$ separated by a distance $d = 1.0\,\mathrm{mm}$. It is connected to an alternating voltage source $\Delta V(t) = V_0 \sin(\omega t)$ with $V_0 = 50\,\mathrm{V}$ and frequency $f = 1.0\,\mathrm{kHz}$. Neglecting edge effects, write the displacement current between the plates as a function of time and calculate its maximum value ($\varepsilon_0 = 8.85 \cdot 10^{-12}\,\mathrm{C^2/(N\cdot m^2)}$).

$$
E(t) = \frac{\Delta V(t)}{d} = \frac{V_0}{d} \sin(\omega t) ,
\qquad
\Phi(\vec{E}) = E(t)\,S
$$
$$
i_s(t) = \varepsilon_0 S \frac{dE}{dt} = \frac{\varepsilon_0 S}{d}\, V_0\, \omega \cos(\omega t) ,
\qquad
i_{s,\max} = \frac{\varepsilon_0 S}{d}\, V_0\, \omega
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
\omega &= 2\pi f = 2\pi \cdot 1.0 \cdot 10^{3}\,\mathrm{rad/s} \approx 6.28 \cdot 10^{3}\,\mathrm{rad/s} \\[6pt]
\frac{\varepsilon_0 S}{d} &= \frac{8.85 \cdot 10^{-12} \cdot 0.020}{1.0 \cdot 10^{-3}}\,\mathrm{F} \approx 1.77 \cdot 10^{-10}\,\mathrm{F} \\[6pt]
i_{s,\max} &= 1.77 \cdot 10^{-10} \cdot 50 \cdot 6.28 \cdot 10^{3}\,\mathrm{A} \approx 5.6 \cdot 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{A}
\end{aligned}
$$

::grafico{f="sin(x);cos(x)" xmin=0 xmax=13 ymin=-2 ymax=2 nomi="t," legenda="\Delta V(t);i_s(t)" titolo="Voltage across the capacitor and displacement current, in units of their respective maximum values, with time in units of $\dfrac{1}{\omega}$. The displacement current is greatest when the voltage passes through zero"}

<div class="spiegazione">

Edge effects are negligible, so at every instant the field between the plates is uniform and equals $E = \dfrac{\Delta V}{d}$. Its flux through a surface parallel to the plates is $E S$. By the definition of **displacement current**, multiply the flux derivative by $\varepsilon_0$. By the chain rule, the derivative of $\sin(\omega t)$ is $\omega \cos(\omega t)$, so $i_s(t) = \dfrac{\varepsilon_0 S}{d}\, V_0\, \omega \cos(\omega t)$.

The factor $\dfrac{\varepsilon_0 S}{d}$ is the capacitance $C$ of the capacitor, and the maximum value $C V_0 \omega$ equals the current amplitude in the capacitive circuit, as discussed in the chapter on alternating current. The maximum value of the cosine is $1$, so the displacement current has an amplitude of about $5.6 \cdot 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{A}$. The displacement current and voltage differ in phase by a quarter of a period. The current therefore reaches its maximum when the voltage is zero, since the electric field is then changing most rapidly.

</div>
