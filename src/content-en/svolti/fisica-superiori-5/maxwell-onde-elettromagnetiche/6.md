## Exercise 1

A laser pointer emits a beam of power $P = 3.0\,\mathrm{mW}$ with a circular cross section of diameter $D = 1.5\,\mathrm{mm}$. Assume the power is distributed uniformly across the beam and the beam is a sinusoidal plane wave in a vacuum. Calculate the beam intensity, rms electric field, electric field amplitude, magnetic field amplitude, and average energy density ($c = 3.00 \cdot 10^{8}\,\mathrm{m/s}$, $\varepsilon_0 = 8.85 \cdot 10^{-12}\,\mathrm{C^2/(N\cdot m^2)}$).

$$
I = \frac{P}{\pi \left( \dfrac{D}{2} \right)^2} ,
\qquad
I = c\,\varepsilon_0 E_{\text{rms}}^2
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
E_{\text{rms}} = \sqrt{\frac{I}{c\,\varepsilon_0}}
$$
$$
E_0 = \sqrt{2}\, E_{\text{rms}} ,
\qquad
B_0 = \frac{E_0}{c} ,
\qquad
\bar{u} = \frac{I}{c}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
I &= \frac{3.0 \cdot 10^{-3}}{\pi \cdot \left( 7.5 \cdot 10^{-4} \right)^2}\,\mathrm{W/m^2} \approx 1.7 \cdot 10^{3}\,\mathrm{W/m^2} \\[6pt]
E_{\text{rms}} &= \sqrt{\frac{1.70 \cdot 10^{3}}{3.00 \cdot 10^{8} \cdot 8.85 \cdot 10^{-12}}}\,\mathrm{V/m} \approx 8.0 \cdot 10^{2}\,\mathrm{V/m} \\[6pt]
E_0 &= \sqrt{2} \cdot 800\,\mathrm{V/m} \approx 1.1 \cdot 10^{3}\,\mathrm{V/m} \\[6pt]
B_0 &= \frac{1.13 \cdot 10^{3}}{3.00 \cdot 10^{8}}\,\mathrm{T} \approx 3.8 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\mathrm{T} \\[6pt]
\bar{u} &= \frac{1.70 \cdot 10^{3}}{3.00 \cdot 10^{8}}\,\mathrm{J/m^3} \approx 5.7 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\mathrm{J/m^3}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The power is spread uniformly over the beam cross section, a disk of radius $\dfrac{D}{2} = 0.75\,\mathrm{mm} = 7.5 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m}$. By the definition of **intensity**, divide the power by the cross-sectional area. The result is about $1.7 \cdot 10^{3}\,\mathrm{W/m^2}$, comparable to the intensity of sunlight.

For a sinusoidal wave, intensity and electric field are related by $I = c\,\varepsilon_0 E_{\text{rms}}^2$. Divide by $c\,\varepsilon_0$ and take the square root to obtain $E_{\text{rms}} \approx 800\,\mathrm{V/m}$. The amplitude is the rms value multiplied by $\sqrt{2}$ because $E_{\text{rms}} = \dfrac{E_0}{\sqrt{2}}$, and the magnetic field amplitude follows from $E = cB$.

Intensity is the average energy density times the speed $c$, so $\bar{u} = \dfrac{I}{c} \approx 5.7 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\mathrm{J/m^3}$. The same value follows from $\bar{u} = \varepsilon_0 E_{\text{rms}}^2$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

An antenna can be treated as a point source radiating average power $P = 25\,\mathrm{kW}$ uniformly in all directions. Neglecting absorption by the air, calculate the intensity and rms electric field at a distance $r = 5.0\,\mathrm{km}$. A receiver operates only if the rms field is at least $5.0 \cdot 10^{-2}\,\mathrm{V/m}$. Calculate the greatest distance from the antenna at which it operates ($c = 3.00 \cdot 10^{8}\,\mathrm{m/s}$, $\varepsilon_0 = 8.85 \cdot 10^{-12}\,\mathrm{C^2/(N\cdot m^2)}$).

$$
I = \frac{P}{4\pi r^2} ,
\qquad
E_{\text{rms}} = \sqrt{\frac{I}{c\,\varepsilon_0}} = \frac{1}{r} \sqrt{\frac{P}{4\pi c\,\varepsilon_0}}
$$
$$
E_{\text{rms}}\,r = \text{constant}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
r_{\max} = r\,\frac{E_{\text{rms}}(r)}{E_{\min}}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
I &= \frac{2.5 \cdot 10^{4}}{4\pi \cdot \left( 5.0 \cdot 10^{3} \right)^2}\,\mathrm{W/m^2} \approx 8.0 \cdot 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{W/m^2} \\[6pt]
E_{\text{rms}} &= \sqrt{\frac{7.96 \cdot 10^{-5}}{3.00 \cdot 10^{8} \cdot 8.85 \cdot 10^{-12}}}\,\mathrm{V/m} \approx 0.17\,\mathrm{V/m} \\[6pt]
r_{\max} &= 5.0\,\mathrm{km} \cdot \frac{0.173}{5.0 \cdot 10^{-2}} \approx 17\,\mathrm{km}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The point source radiates uniformly, and the air absorbs no energy. The formula for the **intensity of a point source** applies because the power spreads over a sphere of radius $r$ and area $4\pi r^2$. From this intensity, find the rms field using $I = c\,\varepsilon_0 E_{\text{rms}}^2$, as in the previous exercise.

Substituting the expression for $I$ into the formula for $E_{\text{rms}}$ shows that the rms field is proportional to $\dfrac{1}{r}$ because $\sqrt{r^2} = r$. Thus the product $E_{\text{rms}}\,r$ is the same at all distances, and the field falls to the minimum value $5.0 \cdot 10^{-2}\,\mathrm{V/m}$ at the distance $r_{\max}$ such that $E_{\min}\,r_{\max} = E_{\text{rms}}(r)\,r$. The field must decrease by a factor of $\dfrac{0.173}{0.050} \approx 3.5$, so the distance must increase by the same factor, giving $r_{\max} \approx 17\,\mathrm{km}$. At this distance, the intensity has decreased by a factor of $3.5^2 \approx 12$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A solar panel of area $S = 1.6\,\mathrm{m^2}$ is perpendicular to the Sun's rays, which reach the ground with intensity $I = 950\,\mathrm{W/m^2}$. The panel converts $20\%$ of the power it receives into electrical power. Calculate the electrical power output and the electrical energy produced in one hour. Then calculate the rms electric field of a sinusoidal wave of the same intensity and the average energy density of the radiation ($c = 3.00 \cdot 10^{8}\,\mathrm{m/s}$, $\varepsilon_0 = 8.85 \cdot 10^{-12}\,\mathrm{C^2/(N\cdot m^2)}$).

$$
P_{\text{inc}} = I\,S ,
\qquad
P_{\text{el}} = 0.20\,P_{\text{inc}} ,
\qquad
U = P_{\text{el}}\,\Delta t
$$
$$
E_{\text{rms}} = \sqrt{\frac{I}{c\,\varepsilon_0}} ,
\qquad
\bar{u} = \frac{I}{c}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
P_{\text{inc}} &= 950\,\mathrm{W/m^2} \cdot 1.6\,\mathrm{m^2} = 1.52 \cdot 10^{3}\,\mathrm{W} \\[6pt]
P_{\text{el}} &= 0.20 \cdot 1.52 \cdot 10^{3}\,\mathrm{W} \approx 3.0 \cdot 10^{2}\,\mathrm{W} \\[6pt]
U &= 304\,\mathrm{W} \cdot 3600\,\mathrm{s} \approx 1.1 \cdot 10^{6}\,\mathrm{J} \\[6pt]
E_{\text{rms}} &= \sqrt{\frac{950}{3.00 \cdot 10^{8} \cdot 8.85 \cdot 10^{-12}}}\,\mathrm{V/m} \approx 6.0 \cdot 10^{2}\,\mathrm{V/m} \\[6pt]
\bar{u} &= \frac{950}{3.00 \cdot 10^{8}}\,\mathrm{J/m^3} \approx 3.2 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\mathrm{J/m^3}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The panel is perpendicular to the rays, and intensity is power per unit area, so the power striking the panel is the product $I\,S$, about $1.5\,\mathrm{kW}$. The panel converts $20\%$ of this, giving an electrical power of about $300\,\mathrm{W}$. The power is constant, so the energy produced in one hour is the power multiplied by $3600\,\mathrm{s}$, about $1.1 \cdot 10^{6}\,\mathrm{J}$, or $0.30\,\mathrm{kWh}$.

Sunlight contains many frequencies, so the field calculated from $I = c\,\varepsilon_0 E_{\text{rms}}^2$ is that of a sinusoidal wave of the same intensity. It gives the order of magnitude of the electric field in sunlight, about $600\,\mathrm{V/m}$. The average energy density is the intensity divided by $c$. Thus one cubic meter of space illuminated by sunlight contains only $3.2\,\mu\mathrm{J}$ of energy, but that energy is continually replenished because the radiation arrives at speed $c$.

</div>

## Exercise 4

A solar sail of area $S = 800\,\mathrm{m^2}$ and total mass $m = 50\,\mathrm{kg}$, including the probe it carries, is in space at Earth's distance from the Sun, where the solar radiation has intensity $I = 1360\,\mathrm{W/m^2}$. The sail is perpendicular to the rays and reflects all the radiation. Neglect gravity and assume that the intensity remains constant. Calculate the radiation pressure, the force on the sail, the acceleration of the probe, and the speed the probe reaches in $30$ days starting from rest ($c = 3.00 \cdot 10^{8}\,\mathrm{m/s}$).

$$
p_{\text{rad}} = \frac{2I}{c} ,
\qquad
F = p_{\text{rad}}\,S ,
\qquad
a = \frac{F}{m} ,
\qquad
v = a\,\Delta t
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
p_{\text{rad}} &= \frac{2 \cdot 1360}{3.00 \cdot 10^{8}}\,\mathrm{Pa} \approx 9.1 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\mathrm{Pa} \\[6pt]
F &= 9.07 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\mathrm{Pa} \cdot 800\,\mathrm{m^2} \approx 7.3 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{N} \\[6pt]
a &= \frac{7.25 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{N}}{50\,\mathrm{kg}} \approx 1.5 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m/s^2} \\[6pt]
v &= 1.45 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m/s^2} \cdot 30 \cdot 86\,400\,\mathrm{s} \approx 3.8 \cdot 10^{2}\,\mathrm{m/s}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The sail reflects all the radiation, and the rays arrive at normal incidence, so the **radiation pressure** is $\dfrac{2I}{c}$. The factor $2$ arises because the reflected radiation travels back with opposite momentum, and the sail receives twice as much momentum as it would if it absorbed the radiation. Pressure is force divided by area, so the force equals the pressure times the sail area, about $7.3\,\mathrm{mN}$.

Gravity is neglected, so the radiation force is the only force acting on the probe. By Newton's second law, its acceleration is $\dfrac{F}{m}$. The intensity is assumed constant, so the acceleration is constant as well. Starting from rest, the probe reaches speed $a\,\Delta t$, with $\Delta t = 30 \cdot 86\,400\,\mathrm{s}$. The force is very small but acts continuously without consuming fuel, and after a month the probe has gained a speed of about $380\,\mathrm{m/s}$.

</div>
