## Exercise 1

Calculate the rest energy of $1.0\,\mathrm{g}$ of matter. A household uses $2700\,\mathrm{kWh}$ of electrical energy in a year. For how many years could its energy needs be met if the rest energy of $1.0\,\mathrm{g}$ were converted entirely to electrical energy? Use $c = 3.00 \cdot 10^{8}\,\mathrm{m/s}$ and $1\,\mathrm{kWh} = 3.6 \cdot 10^{6}\,\mathrm{J}$.

$$
E_0 = m c^2 = 1.0 \cdot 10^{-3} \cdot \left( 3.00 \cdot 10^{8} \right)^2\,\mathrm{J} = 9.0 \cdot 10^{13}\,\mathrm{J}
$$
$$
E_{\text{year}} = 2700 \cdot 3.6 \cdot 10^{6}\,\mathrm{J} \approx 9.7 \cdot 10^{9}\,\mathrm{J}
$$
$$
n = \frac{E_0}{E_{\text{year}}} = \frac{9.0 \cdot 10^{13}}{9.72 \cdot 10^{9}} \approx 9.3 \cdot 10^{3}\ \text{years}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

By **mass–energy equivalence**, each mass corresponds to a rest energy $m c^2$. The mass must be expressed in kilograms, so $1.0\,\mathrm{g} = 1.0 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{kg}$. Multiplying by $c^2 = 9.00 \cdot 10^{16}\,\mathrm{m^2/s^2}$ gives $9.0 \cdot 10^{13}\,\mathrm{J}$.

Annual consumption is given in kilowatt-hours. Multiplication by $3.6 \cdot 10^{6}$ converts it to joules. Dividing the rest energy by the household’s annual energy consumption gives about **nine thousand years**. This calculation assumes complete conversion of mass into electrical energy, which does not occur in any power plant: in nuclear reactions, less than $1\%$ of the mass is converted.

</div>

## Exercise 2

The Sun radiates power $P = 3.83 \cdot 10^{26}\,\mathrm{W}$. Calculate the mass the Sun loses each second and each year due to this emission, and the fraction of its mass $M = 1.99 \cdot 10^{30}\,\mathrm{kg}$ that it loses in one year. Use $c = 3.00 \cdot 10^{8}\,\mathrm{m/s}$ and take one year to be $3.15 \cdot 10^{7}\,\mathrm{s}$.

$$
\Delta m = \frac{\Delta E}{c^2} = \frac{P\, \Delta t}{c^2}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\Delta m}{\Delta t} &= \frac{3.83 \cdot 10^{26}}{9.00 \cdot 10^{16}}\,\mathrm{kg/s} \approx 4.26 \cdot 10^{9}\,\mathrm{kg/s} \\[6pt]
\Delta m_{\text{year}} &= 4.26 \cdot 10^{9} \cdot 3.15 \cdot 10^{7}\,\mathrm{kg} \approx 1.34 \cdot 10^{17}\,\mathrm{kg} \\[6pt]
\frac{\Delta m_{\text{year}}}{M} &= \frac{1.34 \cdot 10^{17}}{1.99 \cdot 10^{30}} \approx 6.7 \cdot 10^{-14}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Power is the energy emitted per unit time, so in time $\Delta t$ the Sun emits energy $P\,\Delta t$. By **mass–energy equivalence**, a system that gives up energy $\Delta E$ loses mass $\dfrac{\Delta E}{c^2}$. The mass lost each second is therefore $\dfrac{P}{c^2}$, about four million metric tons.

Multiplying by the number of seconds in a year gives an annual mass loss of about $1.3 \cdot 10^{17}\,\mathrm{kg}$. Dividing by the Sun's mass gives a fraction on the order of $10^{-13}$ per year. Even over billions of years, the Sun therefore loses only a very small fraction of its mass through radiation.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A $1.0\,\mathrm{kg}$ sample of water is heated from $20\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ to $80\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$. Calculate the heat absorbed and the increase in mass. Explain why a balance cannot measure this increase. Use the specific heat capacity of water $c_s = 4186\,\mathrm{J/(kg\cdot K)}$ and $c = 3.00 \cdot 10^{8}\,\mathrm{m/s}$.

$$
Q = m\, c_s\, \Delta T = 1.0 \cdot 4186 \cdot 60\,\mathrm{J} \approx 2.5 \cdot 10^{5}\,\mathrm{J}
$$
$$
\Delta m = \frac{Q}{c^2} = \frac{2.51 \cdot 10^{5}}{9.00 \cdot 10^{16}}\,\mathrm{kg} \approx 2.8 \cdot 10^{-12}\,\mathrm{kg}
$$
$$
\frac{\Delta m}{m} \approx 2.8 \cdot 10^{-12}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The heat absorbed is given by the calorimetry equation $Q = m\, c_s\, \Delta T$, with $\Delta T = 60\,\mathrm{K}$. The specific heat capacity is denoted by $c_s$ to distinguish it from the speed of light $c$.

The water gains energy $Q$, so by **mass–energy equivalence** its rest energy increases by $Q$ and its mass increases by $\dfrac{Q}{c^2}$, approximately $2.8 \cdot 10^{-12}\,\mathrm{kg}$, or 2.8 millionths of a milligram. The relative increase is on the order of $10^{-12}$, while the most precise balances measure relative changes of about $10^{-9}$ or $10^{-10}$. Mass is therefore treated as constant in thermal and chemical processes. Mass–energy equivalence can be tested in nuclear processes, where relative changes are on the order of $10^{-3}$.

</div>
