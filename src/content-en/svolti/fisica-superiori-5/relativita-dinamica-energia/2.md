## Exercise 1

Calculate the kinetic energy of an electron moving at $0.10\,c$, $0.50\,c$, and $0.90\,c$, using both the relativistic formula and the classical formula $\dfrac{1}{2} m v^2$. For each speed, determine the percentage error incurred by using the classical formula. Use $m_e c^2 = 511\,\mathrm{keV}$.

$$
K = (\gamma - 1)\, m_e c^2 ,
\qquad
K_{\text{cl}} = \frac{1}{2}\, m_e v^2 = \frac{1}{2}\, \beta^2\, m_e c^2 ,
\qquad
\text{error} = \frac{K - K_{\text{cl}}}{K}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
\beta = 0.10 &: \quad \gamma \approx 1.00504 , \quad K \approx 2.57\,\mathrm{keV} , \quad K_{\text{cl}} \approx 2.56\,\mathrm{keV} , \quad \text{error} \approx 0.75\% \\[6pt]
\beta = 0.50 &: \quad \gamma \approx 1.155 , \quad K \approx 79.1\,\mathrm{keV} , \quad K_{\text{cl}} \approx 63.9\,\mathrm{keV} , \quad \text{error} \approx 19\% \\[6pt]
\beta = 0.90 &: \quad \gamma \approx 2.294 , \quad K \approx 661\,\mathrm{keV} , \quad K_{\text{cl}} \approx 207\,\mathrm{keV} , \quad \text{error} \approx 69\%
\end{aligned}
$$

::grafico{f="511*(1/sqrt(1-x^2)-1);511*x^2/2" punti="0.1,2.57,pieno;0.5,79.1,pieno;0.9,661,pieno" asintoti="x=1" xmin=0 xmax=1.1 ymin=0 ymax=1000 nomi="β,K" legenda="K = (\gamma - 1)\,m_e c^2;K_{\text{cl}} = \dfrac{1}{2} m_e v^2" titolo="Electron kinetic energy in keV as a function of $\beta$. The points mark the three relativistic values in the exercise. Their separation from the classical curve increases with speed"}

<div class="spiegazione">

For each speed, first calculate $\gamma = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1 - \beta^2}}$. At $\beta = 0.10$ the radicand is $0.99$ and $\gamma$ exceeds $1$ by only about $0.005$. At $\beta = 0.50$ the radicand is $0.75$. At $\beta = 0.90$ the radicand is $0.19$, and $\gamma$ exceeds $2$.

The relativistic kinetic energy is $(\gamma - 1)$ times $511\,\mathrm{keV}$. In the classical formula, write $v = \beta c$ so that $\dfrac{1}{2} m_e v^2 = \dfrac{1}{2} \beta^2\, m_e c^2$. This gives the classical energy in $\mathrm{keV}$ as well. For example, with $\beta = 0.50$ we have $K_{\text{cl}} = \dfrac{1}{2} \cdot 0.25 \cdot 511\,\mathrm{keV} \approx 63.9\,\mathrm{keV}$.

The relative error is the difference between the two values divided by the relativistic value. At $0.10\,c$ it is less than $1\%$, so the classical formula is a good approximation, as the **classical limit of kinetic energy** predicts. At $0.50\,c$ the error is about $19\%$, and at $0.90\,c$ the classical formula gives less than one-third of the actual kinetic energy.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A proton is accelerated from $v_1 = 0.50\,c$ to $v_2 = 0.90\,c$. Calculate the work $W$ done on the proton by the accelerating force, in $\mathrm{MeV}$, and compare it with the prediction of classical mechanics. Use $m_p c^2 = 938\,\mathrm{MeV}$.

$$
W = \Delta K = K_2 - K_1 = (\gamma_2 - 1)\, m_p c^2 - (\gamma_1 - 1)\, m_p c^2 = (\gamma_2 - \gamma_1)\, m_p c^2
$$
$$
\gamma_1 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - 0.25}} \approx 1.155 ,
\qquad
\gamma_2 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - 0.81}} \approx 2.294
$$
$$
W = (2.294 - 1.155) \cdot 938\,\mathrm{MeV} \approx 1.07 \cdot 10^{3}\,\mathrm{MeV}
$$
$$
W_{\text{cl}} = \frac{1}{2}\, m_p c^2 \left( \beta_2^2 - \beta_1^2 \right) = \frac{1}{2} \cdot 938 \cdot (0.81 - 0.25)\,\mathrm{MeV} \approx 263\,\mathrm{MeV}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

By the work–energy theorem, the work $W$ done by the net force equals the change in kinetic energy. The theorem also holds in relativity with $K = (\gamma - 1)\, m c^2$. In the difference $K_2 - K_1$ the two $-m_p c^2$ terms cancel, so the work is $(\gamma_2 - \gamma_1)\, m_p c^2$, the change in total energy.

The two Lorentz factors are approximately $1.155$ and $2.294$, and their difference multiplied by $938\,\mathrm{MeV}$ gives about $1.07 \cdot 10^{3}\,\mathrm{MeV}$. Classical mechanics would give about $263\,\mathrm{MeV}$, so the work required is **about four times** the classical value. The difference is large because $\gamma$ increases rapidly as the speed approaches $c$, as shown on the graph of kinetic energy as a function of $\beta$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A proton initially at rest is accelerated through a potential difference of $10.0\,\mathrm{MV}$. Calculate its final speed using the relativistic and classical formulas and determine the percentage difference. Use $m_p c^2 = 938\,\mathrm{MeV}$ and $c = 3.00 \cdot 10^{8}\,\mathrm{m/s}$.

$$
K = e\, \Delta V = 10.0\,\mathrm{MeV}
$$
$$
\gamma = 1 + \frac{K}{m_p c^2} = 1 + \frac{10.0}{938} \approx 1.01066
$$
$$
\beta = \sqrt{1 - \frac{1}{\gamma^2}} \approx 0.1449
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
v \approx 0.1449 \cdot 3.00 \cdot 10^{8}\,\mathrm{m/s} \approx 4.35 \cdot 10^{7}\,\mathrm{m/s}
$$
$$
\frac{1}{2}\, m_p v_{\text{cl}}^2 = K
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\beta_{\text{cl}} = \sqrt{\frac{2K}{m_p c^2}} = \sqrt{\frac{2 \cdot 10.0}{938}} \approx 0.1460
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
v_{\text{cl}} \approx 4.38 \cdot 10^{7}\,\mathrm{m/s}
$$
$$
\frac{v_{\text{cl}} - v}{v} = \frac{0.1460 - 0.1449}{0.1449} \approx 0.8\%
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The proton has charge $e$ and moves through the potential difference $\Delta V$, so the electric force does work $e\,\Delta V$. By the definition of the electronvolt this work equals $10.0\,\mathrm{MeV}$, and since the proton starts at rest, it equals the proton's final kinetic energy.

The equation $K = (\gamma - 1)\, m_p c^2$ gives $\gamma = 1 + \dfrac{K}{m_p c^2}$. From the definition of $\gamma$, we have $1 - \beta^2 = \dfrac{1}{\gamma^2}$, so $\beta = \sqrt{1 - \dfrac{1}{\gamma^2}}$. With $\gamma \approx 1.01066$, this gives $\dfrac{1}{\gamma^2} \approx 0.97901$ and $\beta \approx 0.1449$. The calculation requires at least five digits in $\gamma$ because $\gamma$ is very close to $1$.

In the classical formula, solve for $v$ in $\dfrac{1}{2} m_p v^2 = K$ and divide by $c$ to obtain the ratio $\dfrac{K}{m_p c^2}$. The two speeds differ by less than $1\%$ because the kinetic energy is about $1\%$ of the rest energy. Protons with energies of a few $\mathrm{MeV}$ can therefore be treated using **classical mechanics**.

</div>
