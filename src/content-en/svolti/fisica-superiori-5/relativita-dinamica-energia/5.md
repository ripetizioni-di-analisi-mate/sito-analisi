## Exercise 1

A laser emits green light. Its photons have energy $E = 2.50\,\mathrm{eV}$ and the beam power is $P = 2.0\,\mathrm{W}$. Calculate the momentum of one photon, the number of photons emitted per second, and the momentum carried by the beam each second. Use $c = 3.00 \cdot 10^{8}\,\mathrm{m/s}$ and $1\,\mathrm{eV} = 1.60 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{J}$.

$$
p = \frac{E}{c} ,
\qquad
n = \frac{P}{E} ,
\qquad
\frac{\Delta p_{\text{tot}}}{\Delta t} = n\, p = \frac{P}{c}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
E &= 2.50 \cdot 1.60 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{J} = 4.00 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{J} \\[6pt]
p &= \frac{4.00 \cdot 10^{-19}}{3.00 \cdot 10^{8}}\,\mathrm{kg\cdot m/s} \approx 1.33 \cdot 10^{-27}\,\mathrm{kg\cdot m/s} \\[6pt]
n &= \frac{2.0}{4.00 \cdot 10^{-19}}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}} = 5.0 \cdot 10^{18}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}} \\[6pt]
\frac{\Delta p_{\text{tot}}}{\Delta t} &= \frac{2.0}{3.00 \cdot 10^{8}}\,\mathrm{N} \approx 6.7 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\mathrm{N}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The photon has zero mass, so the relation for **massless particles** gives its momentum as $\dfrac{E}{c}$. First convert the photon energy to joules.

Power is the energy emitted per second, so the number of photons emitted per second equals the power divided by the energy of one photon. The momentum carried per second is this number times the momentum of one photon, which simplifies to $\dfrac{P}{c}$. Its unit is $\mathrm{kg\cdot m/s^2}$, or newtons. This is the force the beam would exert on a surface that absorbed it completely, about $7\,\mathrm{nN}$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A photon of energy $E_\gamma = 2.50\,\mathrm{MeV}$ passes near a heavy nucleus and produces an electron–positron pair. Neglecting the nucleus's kinetic energy, calculate the total kinetic energy of the pair. If the electron and positron have equal kinetic energies, calculate the Lorentz factor and speed of each. Use $m_e c^2 = 0.511\,\mathrm{MeV}$.

$$
E_\gamma = 2\, m_e c^2 + K_{-} + K_{+}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
K_{-} + K_{+} = E_\gamma - 2\, m_e c^2
$$
$$
K_{-} + K_{+} = 2.50 - 1.022 = 1.478\,\mathrm{MeV} ,
\qquad
K_{-} = K_{+} = 0.739\,\mathrm{MeV}
$$
$$
\gamma = 1 + \frac{K}{m_e c^2} = 1 + \frac{0.739}{0.511} \approx 2.45 ,
\qquad
\beta = \sqrt{1 - \frac{1}{\gamma^2}} \approx 0.913
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Because the nucleus is very heavy, it absorbs some of the photon's momentum while acquiring negligible kinetic energy. By **conservation of total energy**, the energy of the photon equals the total energy of the pair, the sum of the two rest energies and the two kinetic energies. The electron and positron have equal masses, so the pair's rest energy is $2\, m_e c^2 = 1.022\,\mathrm{MeV}$.

Subtracting the rest energy from the photon energy gives the total kinetic energy, about $1.48\,\mathrm{MeV}$, shared equally by the two particles. Each has a Lorentz factor $1 + \dfrac{K}{m_e c^2} \approx 2.45$ and a speed of about $0.913\,c$. The process is possible because the photon energy exceeds the **threshold** of $1.022\,\mathrm{MeV}$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

An electron and a positron, both essentially at rest, annihilate to produce two photons. Explain why the photons have equal energies and travel in opposite directions. Calculate the energy of each photon in $\mathrm{MeV}$ and its momentum in $\mathrm{kg\cdot m/s}$. Use $m_e c^2 = 0.511\,\mathrm{MeV}$, $1\,\mathrm{MeV} = 1.60 \cdot 10^{-13}\,\mathrm{J}$, and $c = 3.00 \cdot 10^{8}\,\mathrm{m/s}$.

$$
\vec{p}_1 + \vec{p}_2 = \vec{0} ,
\qquad
E_1 + E_2 = 2\, m_e c^2
$$
$$
p_1 = p_2
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
E_1 = p_1 c = p_2 c = E_2 = m_e c^2 = 0.511\,\mathrm{MeV}
$$
$$
p = \frac{E}{c} = \frac{0.511 \cdot 1.60 \cdot 10^{-13}}{3.00 \cdot 10^{8}}\,\mathrm{kg\cdot m/s} \approx 2.73 \cdot 10^{-22}\,\mathrm{kg\cdot m/s}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Both particles are initially at rest, so the initial momentum is zero and the initial energy is the sum of their rest energies, $1.022\,\mathrm{MeV}$. By **conservation of momentum**, the two photon momenta must add to zero. They therefore have equal magnitudes and opposite directions. Photons have zero mass, so $E = pc$ for each, and equal momentum magnitudes imply equal energies.

By conservation of energy, the two equal energies add up to $2\, m_e c^2$, so each photon has energy $m_e c^2 = 0.511\,\mathrm{MeV}$. Dividing the energy, converted to joules, by $c$ gives the momentum. PET detects precisely such pairs of $0.511\,\mathrm{MeV}$ photons arriving simultaneously from opposite directions.

</div>

## Exercise 4

A positron with kinetic energy $K = 1.00\,\mathrm{MeV}$ annihilates with an electron at rest. Two photons are observed traveling along the positron's original line of motion, one in its direction of travel and the other in the opposite direction. Calculate the energy of each photon. Use $m_e c^2 = 0.511\,\mathrm{MeV}$.

::grafico{assi=0 uguale=1 vettori="-3.6,0,-0.15,0,p₊;0.15,0,3.6,0,E₁;-0.15,-0.9,-1.9,-0.9,E₂" punti="0,0,pieno" etichette="-0.35,0.35,e⁻;-3.9,0.35,e⁺" xmin=-4.3 xmax=4.3 ymin=-1.5 ymax=0.9 legenda=0 titolo="The positron approaches the stationary electron with momentum $\vec{p}_{+}$. After annihilation the photon of energy $E_1$ continues forward and the photon of energy $E_2$ travels backward"}

$$
\begin{cases}
E_1 + E_2 = K + 2\, m_e c^2 \\[6pt]
\dfrac{E_1}{c} - \dfrac{E_2}{c} = p_{+}
\end{cases}
\qquad
p_{+} c = \sqrt{\left( K + m_e c^2 \right)^2 - m_e^2 c^4}
$$
$$
E_1 + E_2 = 1.00 + 1.022 = 2.022\,\mathrm{MeV} ,
\qquad
p_{+} c = \sqrt{1.511^2 - 0.511^2}\,\mathrm{MeV} \approx 1.422\,\mathrm{MeV}
$$
$$
E_1 = \frac{2.022 + 1.422}{2}\,\mathrm{MeV} \approx 1.72\,\mathrm{MeV} ,
\qquad
E_2 = \frac{2.022 - 1.422}{2}\,\mathrm{MeV} \approx 0.300\,\mathrm{MeV}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The total initial energy is the sum of the positron's total energy, $K + m_e c^2$, and the rest energy of the stationary electron, giving $K + 2\, m_e c^2$. The initial momentum is that of the positron, found from the **energy–momentum relation**. With $E_{+} = 1.511\,\mathrm{MeV}$ we obtain $p_{+} c \approx 1.422\,\mathrm{MeV}$.

Choose the positive direction along the positron's motion. The first photon has momentum of magnitude $\dfrac{E_1}{c}$ in the positive direction, and the second has momentum of magnitude $\dfrac{E_2}{c}$ in the negative direction. The two conservation laws must hold simultaneously, giving the system of equations above. Multiplying the second equation by $c$ gives equations for the sum and difference of $E_1$ and $E_2$. Adding the equations and dividing by $2$ gives $E_1$. Subtracting them gives $E_2$.

The forward photon has **about six times as much energy** as the backward photon. As a check, if the positron were at rest, we would have $p_{+} = 0$ and therefore $E_1 = E_2 = 0.511\,\mathrm{MeV}$, as in the preceding exercise.

</div>
