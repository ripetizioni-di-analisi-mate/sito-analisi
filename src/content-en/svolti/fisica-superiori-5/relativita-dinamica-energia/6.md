## Exercise 1

Two grains, each of mass $m = 1.0\,\mathrm{mg}$, travel along the same line in opposite directions, each at speed $0.80\,c$ in the laboratory frame. They collide and stick together. Calculate the mass of the resulting body and the kinetic energy converted to rest energy. Use $c = 3.00 \cdot 10^{8}\,\mathrm{m/s}$.

$$
\vec{p}_{\text{tot}} = \gamma m \vec{v} - \gamma m \vec{v} = \vec{0}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\text{final body at rest}
$$
$$
M c^2 = 2 \gamma\, m c^2
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
M = 2 \gamma\, m
$$
$$
\gamma = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - 0.64}} = \frac{1}{0.60} = \frac{5}{3} ,
\qquad
M = 2 \cdot \frac{5}{3} \cdot 1.0\,\mathrm{mg} \approx 3.3\,\mathrm{mg}
$$
$$
K_{\text{initial}} = 2 (\gamma - 1)\, m c^2 = 2 \cdot \frac{2}{3} \cdot 1.0 \cdot 10^{-6} \cdot 9.00 \cdot 10^{16}\,\mathrm{J} \approx 1.2 \cdot 10^{11}\,\mathrm{J}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The two grains have equal masses and opposite velocities, so they have opposite momenta and their total momentum is zero. By **conservation of momentum**, the final body also has zero momentum. It is therefore at rest, and its total energy equals its rest energy $M c^2$.

By conservation of total energy, $M c^2$ equals the sum of the initial total energies, $2 \gamma\, m c^2$. With $\gamma = \dfrac{5}{3}$, the final mass is about $3.3\,\mathrm{mg}$, which is **greater** than the sum $2.0\,\mathrm{mg}$ of the initial masses.

The final body is at rest, so all the initial kinetic energy has been converted to rest energy. The energy associated with the increase in mass is $(M - 2m)\, c^2$. For two one-milligram grains, this energy is about $1.2 \cdot 10^{11}\,\mathrm{J}$. This explains why speeds this high are attained only for elementary particles in such experiments.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A body of mass $m$ moving at velocity $0.60\,c$ collides with an identical body at rest, and they stick together. Determine the velocity of the final body, its mass in terms of $m$, and the fraction of initial kinetic energy converted into rest energy. Compare the final velocity with the prediction of classical mechanics.

$$
E_{\text{tot}} = \gamma\, m c^2 + m c^2 ,
\qquad
p_{\text{tot}} c = \gamma\, m v c = \gamma \beta\, m c^2
$$
$$
\gamma = 1.25
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
E_{\text{tot}} = 2.25\, m c^2 ,
\qquad
p_{\text{tot}} c = 1.25 \cdot 0.60\, m c^2 = 0.75\, m c^2
$$
$$
\beta_f = \frac{p_{\text{tot}} c}{E_{\text{tot}}} = \frac{0.75}{2.25} = \frac{1}{3} \approx 0.333
$$
$$
M c^2 = \sqrt{E_{\text{tot}}^2 - p_{\text{tot}}^2 c^2} = \sqrt{2.25^2 - 0.75^2}\, m c^2 = \sqrt{4.5}\, m c^2 \approx 2.12\, m c^2
$$
$$
\frac{K_i - K_f}{K_i} = \frac{0.25 - (2.25 - 2.121)}{0.25} \approx 0.49 ,
\qquad
\beta_{f,\text{cl}} = \frac{0.60}{2} = 0.30
$$

After the collision the body of mass $M \approx 2.12\,m$ moves at velocity $V = \dfrac{c}{3}$"}

<div class="spiegazione">

The two-body system is isolated, so total energy and momentum are conserved. The initial energy is the total energy of the moving body plus the rest energy of the stationary body. Only the moving body contributes to the initial momentum. Multiplying the momentum by $c$ allows both quantities to be expressed in units of $m c^2$.

The final body has the same energy and momentum as the initial system. Its velocity follows from the ratio $\beta = \dfrac{pc}{E}$ and its mass from the **energy–momentum relation**, since $M^2 c^4 = E^2 - p^2 c^2$. The final body has mass approximately $2.12\, m$, greater than $2m$.

The initial kinetic energy is $(\gamma - 1)\, m c^2 = 0.25\, m c^2$ and the final kinetic energy is $E_{\text{tot}} - M c^2 \approx 0.13\, m c^2$. About half the initial kinetic energy becomes rest energy. Classical mechanics would distribute the momentum $m v$ over the mass $2m$, giving a final velocity of $0.30\,c$, whereas the correct velocity is $\dfrac{c}{3}$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A neutral kaon at rest with rest energy $497.6\,\mathrm{MeV}$ decays into two charged pions, each with rest energy $139.6\,\mathrm{MeV}$. Calculate the total energy, kinetic energy, momentum, and speed of each pion.

$$
E_1 = E_2 = \frac{M c^2}{2} = \frac{497.6}{2}\,\mathrm{MeV} = 248.8\,\mathrm{MeV}
$$
$$
K = E_1 - m_\pi c^2 = 248.8 - 139.6 = 109.2\,\mathrm{MeV}
$$
$$
p c = \sqrt{248.8^2 - 139.6^2}\,\mathrm{MeV} \approx 206\,\mathrm{MeV}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
p \approx 206\,\mathrm{MeV}/c
$$
$$
\beta = \frac{p c}{E_1} = \frac{205.9}{248.8} \approx 0.828
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The kaon is at rest, so the two pions have opposite momenta of equal magnitude. In the formula for the **two-body decay of a particle at rest**, $m_1 = m_2$ makes the terms $m_1^2$ and $m_2^2$ cancel, leaving $E_1 = \dfrac{M c^2}{2}$. The two pions therefore share the rest energy of the kaon equally.

The kinetic energy of each pion is its total energy minus its rest energy. The total energy released is $2 \cdot 109.2 = 218.4\,\mathrm{MeV}$, the difference between the rest energy of the kaon and the combined rest energies of the two pions. The momentum follows from the energy–momentum relation and the speed from the ratio $\dfrac{pc}{E}$.

</div>

## Exercise 4

A $\Sigma^0$ particle at rest with rest energy $1192.6\,\mathrm{MeV}$ decays into a $\Lambda$ particle with rest energy $1115.7\,\mathrm{MeV}$ and a photon. Calculate the photon energy, the kinetic energy of the $\Lambda$ particle, and the momenta of the two products.

$$
E_\gamma = \frac{M^2 + 0 - m_\Lambda^2}{2M}\, c^2 = \frac{1192.6^2 - 1115.7^2}{2 \cdot 1192.6}\,\mathrm{MeV} \approx 74.4\,\mathrm{MeV}
$$
$$
E_\Lambda = M c^2 - E_\gamma \approx 1192.6 - 74.4 = 1118.2\,\mathrm{MeV}
$$
$$
K_\Lambda = E_\Lambda - m_\Lambda c^2 \approx 1118.2 - 1115.7 \approx 2.5\,\mathrm{MeV}
$$
$$
p = \frac{E_\gamma}{c} \approx 74.4\,\mathrm{MeV}/c
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Apply the formula for the **two-body decay of a particle at rest**, taking the first particle to be the massless photon and the second to be the $\Lambda$. With $m_1 = 0$ the formula directly gives the photon energy, about $74.4\,\mathrm{MeV}$.

By energy conservation the total energy of the $\Lambda$ is the rest energy of the $\Sigma^0$ minus the photon energy. Subtracting the rest energy of the $\Lambda$ then gives its kinetic energy. The two products have opposite momenta of equal magnitude, which is $\dfrac{E_\gamma}{c}$ for the photon. As a check, $\sqrt{1115.7^2 + 74.4^2}\,\mathrm{MeV} \approx 1118.2\,\mathrm{MeV}$ agrees with the energy found for the $\Lambda$.

The $\Lambda$ is much heavier than the photon and carries away the same magnitude of momentum but only about $2.5\,\mathrm{MeV}$ of kinetic energy. Almost all the energy released, $76.9\,\mathrm{MeV}$, therefore goes to the photon.

</div>
