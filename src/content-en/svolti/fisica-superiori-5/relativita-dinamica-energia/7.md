## Exercise 1

The deuterium nucleus, or deuteron, consists of one proton and one neutron and has mass $m_d = 2.013553\,\mathrm{u}$. Calculate its mass defect, binding energy, and binding energy per nucleon. Use $m_p = 1.007276\,\mathrm{u}$, $m_n = 1.008665\,\mathrm{u}$, and $1\,\mathrm{u} \cdot c^2 = 931.5\,\mathrm{MeV}$.

$$
\Delta m = m_p + m_n - m_d ,
\qquad
B = \Delta m\, c^2 ,
\qquad
\frac{B}{A}
$$
$$
\Delta m = (1.007276 + 1.008665 - 2.013553)\,\mathrm{u} = 0.002388\,\mathrm{u}
$$
$$
B = 0.002388 \cdot 931.5\,\mathrm{MeV} \approx 2.22\,\mathrm{MeV} ,
\qquad
\frac{B}{A} = \frac{2.22}{2}\,\mathrm{MeV} \approx 1.11\,\mathrm{MeV}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

By the definition of **mass defect**, subtract the mass of the nucleus from the sum of the masses of its separate nucleons. The sum is $2.015941\,\mathrm{u}$ and the difference is $0.002388\,\mathrm{u}$. The binding energy is the energy equivalent of this mass, obtained by multiplying by $931.5\,\mathrm{MeV/u}$.

The deuteron contains $A = 2$ nucleons, so its binding energy per nucleon is about $1.11\,\mathrm{MeV}$, much smaller than the roughly $8\,\mathrm{MeV}$ for medium-mass nuclei. The deuteron is therefore a **weakly bound** nucleus. A photon with an energy of at least $2.22\,\mathrm{MeV}$ can split the deuteron into a proton and a neutron.

</div>

## Exercise 2

An iron-56 atom has $26$ protons, $30$ neutrons, and mass $55.934936\,\mathrm{u}$. Using the mass of the hydrogen atom, calculate the binding energy of the nucleus and its binding energy per nucleon. Use $m_{\text{H}} = 1.007825\,\mathrm{u}$, $m_n = 1.008665\,\mathrm{u}$, and $1\,\mathrm{u} \cdot c^2 = 931.5\,\mathrm{MeV}$.

$$
\Delta m = Z\, m_{\text{H}} + N\, m_n - m_{\text{atom}}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta m &= (26 \cdot 1.007825 + 30 \cdot 1.008665 - 55.934936)\,\mathrm{u} \\[6pt]
&= (56.463400 - 55.934936)\,\mathrm{u} = 0.528464\,\mathrm{u}
\end{aligned}
$$
$$
B = 0.528464 \cdot 931.5\,\mathrm{MeV} \approx 492\,\mathrm{MeV} ,
\qquad
\frac{B}{A} = \frac{492.3}{56}\,\mathrm{MeV} \approx 8.79\,\mathrm{MeV}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The given mass is that of the neutral atom, including $26$ electrons. As explained in **calculations using atomic masses**, use $26$ hydrogen atoms in place of $26$ protons. The electrons are then counted once in the hydrogen atom masses and once in the iron atom mass, so their masses cancel in the subtraction.

The binding energy is about $492\,\mathrm{MeV}$. Dividing by the $56$ nucleons gives about $8.79\,\mathrm{MeV}$ per nucleon. This value is close to the **maximum** of the binding-energy-per-nucleon curve, so iron-56 is among the most stable nuclei and energy cannot be obtained from it by either fission or fusion.

</div>

## Exercise 3

In the fusion reaction $\,^{2}\mathrm{H} + {}^{2}\mathrm{H} \to {}^{3}\mathrm{He} + n$, two deuterium nuclei form a helium-3 nucleus and a neutron. Calculate the energy released. Assuming the initial deuterium nuclei have negligible kinetic energy, calculate how the helium-3 nucleus and the neutron share the energy released. Use the atomic masses $m(^{2}\mathrm{H}) = 2.014102\,\mathrm{u}$ and $m(^{3}\mathrm{He}) = 3.016029\,\mathrm{u}$, the neutron mass $m_n = 1.008665\,\mathrm{u}$, and $1\,\mathrm{u} \cdot c^2 = 931.5\,\mathrm{MeV}$.

$$
Q = \left[ 2\, m(^{2}\mathrm{H}) - m(^{3}\mathrm{He}) - m_n \right] c^2
$$
$$
Q = (4.028204 - 4.024694) \cdot 931.5\,\mathrm{MeV} = 0.003510 \cdot 931.5\,\mathrm{MeV} \approx 3.27\,\mathrm{MeV}
$$
$$
\begin{cases}
K_{\text{He}} + K_n = Q \\[6pt]
p_{\text{He}} = p_n
\end{cases}
\qquad
K = \frac{p^2}{2m}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\frac{K_n}{K_{\text{He}}} = \frac{m_{\text{He}}}{m_n}
$$
$$
K_n = Q\, \frac{m_{\text{He}}}{m_{\text{He}} + m_n} \approx 3.27 \cdot \frac{3.016}{4.025}\,\mathrm{MeV} \approx 2.45\,\mathrm{MeV} ,
\qquad
K_{\text{He}} = Q - K_n \approx 0.82\,\mathrm{MeV}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Use atomic masses. On the left are two deuterium atoms with $2$ electrons in total. On the right are a helium-3 atom with $2$ electrons and a neutron. The electron counts match, so their masses cancel. The mass decreases by $0.003510\,\mathrm{u}$, and by **mass–energy equivalence** the energy released is about $3.27\,\mathrm{MeV}$.

The initial nuclei have negligible kinetic energy and momentum, so the released energy becomes kinetic energy of the products. The products have opposite momenta of equal magnitude $p$. Their kinetic energies, a few $\mathrm{MeV}$, are much smaller than their rest energies, about $10^{3}\,\mathrm{MeV}$ or more, so the classical formula $K = \dfrac{p^2}{2m}$ applies. For equal $p$, the kinetic energies are inversely proportional to the masses. The neutron has about one-third the mass of the helium nucleus and receives about three-quarters of the energy, or $2.45\,\mathrm{MeV}$.

</div>

## Exercise 4

The fission reaction $\,^{235}\mathrm{U} + n \to {}^{141}\mathrm{Ba} + {}^{92}\mathrm{Kr} + 3n$ releases energy $Q = 173\,\mathrm{MeV}$, as calculated from the atomic masses. Calculate the number of nuclei in $1.00\,\mathrm{g}$ of uranium-235 and the energy released if all these nuclei undergo fission. Then calculate the mass of methane that releases the same energy when burned. Use the molar mass of uranium-235, $235\,\mathrm{g/mol}$, Avogadro's number $N_A = 6.02 \cdot 10^{23}\,\mathrm{mol^{-1}}$, $1\,\mathrm{MeV} = 1.60 \cdot 10^{-13}\,\mathrm{J}$, and the heating value of methane $5.5 \cdot 10^{7}\,\mathrm{J/kg}$.

$$
N = \frac{m}{M_{\text{mol}}}\, N_A = \frac{1.00}{235} \cdot 6.02 \cdot 10^{23} \approx 2.56 \cdot 10^{21}
$$
$$
E = N\, Q = 2.56 \cdot 10^{21} \cdot 173 \cdot 1.60 \cdot 10^{-13}\,\mathrm{J} \approx 7.1 \cdot 10^{10}\,\mathrm{J}
$$
$$
m_{\text{methane}} = \frac{E}{5.5 \cdot 10^{7}\,\mathrm{J/kg}} = \frac{7.09 \cdot 10^{10}}{5.5 \cdot 10^{7}}\,\mathrm{kg} \approx 1.3 \cdot 10^{3}\,\mathrm{kg}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The number of moles in $1.00\,\mathrm{g}$ equals the mass divided by the molar mass. Multiplying by Avogadro's number gives the number of nuclei, about $2.56 \cdot 10^{21}$. Each fission releases $173\,\mathrm{MeV}$, so the total energy is the number of nuclei times $Q$, converted to joules.

One gram of uranium-235 releases about $7 \cdot 10^{10}\,\mathrm{J}$, the same energy as burning about **1,300 kilograms** of methane. The two masses differ by a factor on the order of a million because nuclear energies, on the order of one $\mathrm{MeV}$ per nucleon, are about a million times larger than chemical bond energies of a few $\mathrm{eV}$ per molecule. In both cases the energy released corresponds to a decrease in mass, but the decrease is measurable only in nuclear reactions.

</div>
