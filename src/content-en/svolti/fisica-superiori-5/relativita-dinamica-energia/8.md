## Exercise 1

A linear accelerator produces a beam of electrons with kinetic energy $K = 4.50\,\mathrm{MeV}$ and beam current $I = 2.00\,\mu\mathrm{A}$. The beam strikes a target at normal incidence, and the target absorbs all the electrons. Calculate the number of electrons reaching the target each second, the beam power, the momentum of one electron, and the average force the beam exerts on the target. Use $e = 1.60 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{C}$, $m_e c^2 = 0.511\,\mathrm{MeV}$, $1\,\mathrm{MeV} = 1.60 \cdot 10^{-13}\,\mathrm{J}$, and $1\,\mathrm{MeV}/c = 5.33 \cdot 10^{-22}\,\mathrm{kg\cdot m/s}$.

$$
n = \frac{I}{e} ,
\qquad
P = n K ,
\qquad
p c = \sqrt{\left( K + m_e c^2 \right)^2 - m_e^2 c^4} ,
\qquad
F = \frac{\Delta p_{\text{tot}}}{\Delta t} = n\, p
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
n &= \frac{2.00 \cdot 10^{-6}}{1.60 \cdot 10^{-19}}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}} = 1.25 \cdot 10^{13}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}} \\[6pt]
P &= 1.25 \cdot 10^{13} \cdot 4.50 \cdot 1.60 \cdot 10^{-13}\,\mathrm{W} = 9.00\,\mathrm{W} \\[6pt]
p c &= \sqrt{5.011^2 - 0.511^2}\,\mathrm{MeV} \approx 4.98\,\mathrm{MeV}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
p \approx 4.98 \cdot 5.33 \cdot 10^{-22}\,\mathrm{kg\cdot m/s} \approx 2.66 \cdot 10^{-21}\,\mathrm{kg\cdot m/s} \\[6pt]
F &= 1.25 \cdot 10^{13} \cdot 2.66 \cdot 10^{-21}\,\mathrm{N} \approx 3.32 \cdot 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{N}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Current is the charge crossing a section of the beam per second. Each electron carries a charge of magnitude $e$, so the number of electrons per second is $\dfrac{I}{e}$. Each electron transfers its kinetic energy $K$ to the target, so the beam power is the product $nK$, expressed in watts. The same result follows from the product $I \cdot \Delta V$ with $\Delta V = 4.50\,\mathrm{MV}$.

The total energy of an electron is $K + m_e c^2 = 5.011\,\mathrm{MeV}$, and the **energy–momentum relation** gives $pc \approx 4.98\,\mathrm{MeV}$. The kinetic energy is about nine times the rest energy, so $pc$ is nearly equal to the total energy and the electron travels at about $0.995\,c$.

The target absorbs the electrons, so each second it receives the momentum of $n$ electrons. By **Newton's second law in relativity**, the average force equals the momentum received divided by the elapsed time. The result, about $3.3 \cdot 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{N}$, is a very small force even though the beam carries $9\,\mathrm{W}$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

An experiment measures the time that electrons of known kinetic energy take to travel a straight distance $d = 8.40\,\mathrm{m}$ in vacuum. An adjustable accelerator produces the electrons, and their kinetic energy $K$ is measured from the heat they transfer to a target. Use $m_e c^2 = 0.511\,\mathrm{MeV}$ and $c = 3.00 \cdot 10^{8}\,\mathrm{m/s}$. The table gives the measured times:

| $K\ (\mathrm{MeV})$ | $0.50$ | $1.00$ | $1.50$ | $4.50$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $t\ (\mathrm{ns})$ | $32.5$ | $29.8$ | $29.0$ | $28.1$ |

1. Use the experimental data to calculate the speed of the electrons in units of $c$ at each energy.
2. For the same energies, calculate the speeds predicted by the classical formula $K = \dfrac{1}{2} m_e v^2$ and explain why the experimental data contradict classical mechanics.
3. Calculate the speeds predicted by special relativity and compare them with the data by plotting $\beta$ against $K$.
4. Determine the lower limit on the time an electron needs to travel the given distance, regardless of its energy. Use relativity to determine the kinetic energy of an electron that takes $29.0\,\mathrm{ns}$.

$$
\beta_{\text{exp}} = \frac{d}{c\, t} ,
\qquad
\beta_{\text{cl}} = \sqrt{\frac{2K}{m_e c^2}} ,
\qquad
\beta_{\text{rel}} = \sqrt{1 - \frac{1}{\gamma^2}} \quad \text{with} \quad \gamma = 1 + \frac{K}{m_e c^2}
$$

| $K\ (\mathrm{MeV})$ | $0.50$ | $1.00$ | $1.50$ | $4.50$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $\beta_{\text{exp}}$ | $0.862$ | $0.940$ | $0.966$ | $0.996$ |
| $\beta_{\text{cl}}$ | $1.40$ | $1.98$ | $2.42$ | $4.20$ |
| $\gamma$ | $1.98$ | $2.96$ | $3.94$ | $9.81$ |
| $\beta_{\text{rel}}$ | $0.863$ | $0.941$ | $0.967$ | $0.995$ |

::grafico{f="sqrt(1-1/(1+x/0.511)^2);sqrt(2*x/0.511)" punti="0.5,0.862,pieno;1.0,0.940,pieno;1.5,0.966,pieno;4.5,0.996,pieno" asintoti="y=1" xmin=0 xmax=5 ymin=0 ymax=3 nomi="K,β" legenda="\beta_{\text{rel}};\beta_{\text{cl}}" titolo="Electron speed in units of $c$ as a function of kinetic energy in MeV. The experimental points lie on the relativistic curve, which stays below the line $\beta = 1$. The classical curve already exceeds $1$ at $K \approx 0.26\,\mathrm{MeV}$"}

$$
t_{\min} = \frac{d}{c} = \frac{8.40}{3.00 \cdot 10^{8}}\,\mathrm{s} = 2.80 \cdot 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{s} = 28.0\,\mathrm{ns}
$$
$$
\beta = \frac{8.40}{3.00 \cdot 10^{8} \cdot 29.0 \cdot 10^{-9}} \approx 0.9655 ,
\qquad
\gamma = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - 0.9655^2}} \approx 3.84
$$
$$
K = (\gamma - 1)\, m_e c^2 \approx 2.84 \cdot 0.511\,\mathrm{MeV} \approx 1.45\,\mathrm{MeV}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

**Question 1.** The electrons move in vacuum with no forces acting on them along the path, so they travel in a straight line at constant speed and their speed is the ratio $\dfrac{d}{t}$. Dividing by $c$ gives $\beta$. For example, with $t = 32.5\,\mathrm{ns}$ we obtain $\beta = \dfrac{8.40}{3.00 \cdot 10^{8} \cdot 32.5 \cdot 10^{-9}} \approx 0.862$. As the energy increases, the increases in speed become smaller and the speed stays below $c$.

**Question 2.** The classical formula $K = \dfrac{1}{2} m_e v^2$ gives $v = \sqrt{\dfrac{2K}{m_e}}$. Dividing by $c$ and writing $m_e c^2$ in the denominator gives $\beta_{\text{cl}} = \sqrt{\dfrac{2K}{m_e c^2}}$, which can be calculated with energies in $\mathrm{MeV}$. All the classical speeds exceed $c$, and the classical speed increases without bound in proportion to $\sqrt{K}$. Yet the measured times are all greater than $28.0\,\mathrm{ns}$, the time light would take. Thus **classical mechanics contradicts** the data. The formula $\dfrac{1}{2} m v^2$ applies only for $v \ll c$, whereas here the kinetic energy is comparable to or greater than the rest energy.

**Question 3.** In relativity the kinetic energy is $(\gamma - 1)\, m_e c^2$, so $\gamma = 1 + \dfrac{K}{m_e c^2}$. The definition of $\gamma$ gives $1 - \beta^2 = \dfrac{1}{\gamma^2}$, from which the formula for $\beta_{\text{rel}}$ follows. The relativistic speeds differ from the measured speeds by less than $0.2\%$. This difference is consistent with the uncertainty in the times, which were measured to the nearest tenth of a nanosecond. In the graph the experimental points lie on the relativistic curve, which approaches the line $\beta = 1$ without reaching it, as the result on the **limiting speed** predicts.

**Question 4.** An electron cannot travel at or above $c$, so its travel time is always greater than $\dfrac{d}{c} = 28.0\,\mathrm{ns}$ and approaches that value as its energy increases. For the electron that takes $29.0\,\mathrm{ns}$, first calculate $\beta$ as in question 1, then $\gamma$, and finally the kinetic energy $(\gamma - 1)\, m_e c^2 \approx 1.45\,\mathrm{MeV}$, in agreement with the third entry in the table. Keep four digits in $\beta$ in this calculation because $1 - \beta^2$ is small and an error in $\beta$ has a large effect on $\gamma$.

</div>
