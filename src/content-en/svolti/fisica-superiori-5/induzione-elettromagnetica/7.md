## Exercise 1

Two vertical conducting rails a distance $\ell = 25\,\mathrm{cm}$ apart are connected at the top by a resistor $R = 0.20\,\mathrm{\Omega}$. They are in a uniform horizontal magnetic field $B = 0.40\,\mathrm{T}$ perpendicular to the plane of the rails. A horizontal rod of mass $m = 20\,\mathrm{g}$ is released from rest and slides down the rails, staying in contact with them. Friction and the resistances of the rails and rod are negligible. Use $g = 9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$.

a) Explain why the rod reaches a terminal speed and calculate that speed.

b) Calculate the current and the emf once the rod has reached terminal speed.

c) Verify that at terminal speed the power supplied by gravity equals the power dissipated in the resistor.

::grafico{assi=0 uguale=1 linee="0,0 0,5;3,0 3,5;0,5 0.5,5 0.6,5.3 0.8,4.7 1.0,5.3 1.2,4.7 1.4,5.3 1.6,4.7 1.8,5.3 2.0,4.7 2.2,5.3 2.4,4.7 2.5,5 3,5" poligoni="-0.3,2.42 3.3,2.42 3.3,2.58 -0.3,2.58" vettori="1.0,2.3,1.0,1.1,mg;2.0,2.7,2.0,3.9,F;3.5,3,3.5,1.8,v;0.6,2.75,2.4,2.75" etichette="1.5,5.7,R;0.5,4,⊗;2.4,4,⊗;0.5,0.6,⊗;2.4,0.6,⊗;1.5,0.6,⊗;1.3,3.05,I;3.6,4.6,B ⊗;1.4,-0.4,ℓ" xmin=-1 xmax=4.4 ymin=-0.7 ymax=6.1 titolo="A rod falling along vertical rails in a field pointing into the page ($\otimes$). The induced current $I$ flows to the right along the rod. The magnetic force $\vec{F}$ points upward, opposing the rod’s weight"}

$$
F = \frac{B^2 \ell^2 v}{R} , \qquad
\frac{B^2 \ell^2 v_{\text{lim}}}{R} = m g
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
v_{\text{lim}} = \frac{m g R}{B^2 \ell^2}
$$
$$
I = \frac{m g}{B \ell} , \qquad
\mathcal{E} = B \ell v_{\text{lim}} , \qquad
P_{\text{weight}} = m g\,v_{\text{lim}} , \qquad
P_{\text{diss}} = R I^2
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
v_{\text{lim}} &= \frac{0.020 \cdot 9.81 \cdot 0.20}{0.40^2 \cdot 0.25^2}\,\mathrm{m/s} \approx 3.9\,\mathrm{m/s} \\[6pt]
I &= \frac{0.020 \cdot 9.81}{0.40 \cdot 0.25}\,\mathrm{A} \approx 2.0\,\mathrm{A} \\[6pt]
\mathcal{E} &= 0.40 \cdot 0.25 \cdot 3.92\,\mathrm{V} \approx 0.39\,\mathrm{V} \\[6pt]
P_{\text{weight}} &= 0.020 \cdot 9.81 \cdot 3.92\,\mathrm{W} \approx 0.77\,\mathrm{W} \\[6pt]
P_{\text{diss}} &= 0.20 \cdot 1.96^2\,\mathrm{W} \approx 0.77\,\mathrm{W}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

As the rod falls, the rod, its velocity, and the field are mutually perpendicular. There is therefore a **motional emf** $B \ell v$ across its ends, and a current $\dfrac{B \ell v}{R}$ flows in the circuit. The rod then experiences a magnetic force of magnitude $\dfrac{B^2 \ell^2 v}{R}$ that, by Lenz’s law, opposes the motion and therefore points upward.

Initially the velocity is zero, so the magnetic force is zero and the rod accelerates with acceleration $g$. As the speed increases, the magnetic force increases while the weight remains constant, so the net force decreases. When the magnetic force equals the weight, the net force is zero, and from that point on the velocity remains constant. The corresponding speed is the **terminal speed**. Equating the two forces gives $v_{\text{lim}} \approx 3.9\,\mathrm{m/s}$.

At terminal speed the magnetic force $I \ell B$ equals the weight, so the current is $\dfrac{m g}{B \ell} \approx 2.0\,\mathrm{A}$ and the emf is $B \ell v_{\text{lim}} \approx 0.39\,\mathrm{V}$. As a check, $\dfrac{\mathcal{E}}{R} = \dfrac{0.392}{0.20}\,\mathrm{A} = 1.96\,\mathrm{A}$.

The power supplied by gravity is $m g\,v_{\text{lim}}$ because the weight and velocity are parallel. It is about $0.77\,\mathrm{W}$, the same as the power dissipated in the resistor. At terminal speed the kinetic energy is constant, so all the potential energy lost by the rod is converted to heat in the resistor, in agreement with the **energy balance**.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Two parallel conducting rails a distance $\ell = 50\,\mathrm{cm}$ apart are inclined at $30^\circ$ to the horizontal and connected at the bottom by a resistor $R = 0.45\,\mathrm{\Omega}$. The magnetic field is uniform, has magnitude $0.60\,\mathrm{T}$, and is perpendicular to the plane of the rails. A rod of mass $40\,\mathrm{g}$, perpendicular to the rails, starts from rest and slides along them without friction. The resistances of the rails and rod are negligible. Use $g = 9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$. Calculate the terminal speed, the current at terminal speed, and the power dissipated in the resistor.

$$
m g \sin\alpha = \frac{B^2 \ell^2 v_{\text{lim}}}{R}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
v_{\text{lim}} = \frac{m g R \sin\alpha}{B^2 \ell^2} , \qquad
I = \frac{m g \sin\alpha}{B \ell} , \qquad
P = R I^2
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
v_{\text{lim}} &= \frac{0.040 \cdot 9.81 \cdot 0.45 \cdot 0.50}{0.60^2 \cdot 0.50^2}\,\mathrm{m/s} \approx 0.98\,\mathrm{m/s} \\[6pt]
I &= \frac{0.040 \cdot 9.81 \cdot 0.50}{0.60 \cdot 0.50}\,\mathrm{A} \approx 0.65\,\mathrm{A} \\[6pt]
P &= 0.45 \cdot 0.654^2\,\mathrm{W} \approx 0.19\,\mathrm{W}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The field is perpendicular to the plane of the rails, so the rod, its velocity along the rails, and the field are mutually perpendicular. The motional emf is therefore $B \ell v$. The magnetic force on the rod has magnitude $\dfrac{B^2 \ell^2 v}{R}$ and points along the rails up the incline because it opposes the motion.

The force pulling the rod down the incline is the component of its weight parallel to the incline, $m g \sin\alpha$. The normal force from the rails balances the perpendicular component. The rod reaches terminal speed when the magnetic force balances $m g \sin\alpha$. With $\sin 30^\circ = 0.50$, this gives about $0.98\,\mathrm{m/s}$.

At terminal speed the magnetic force $I \ell B$ equals $m g \sin\alpha$. This gives a current of about $0.65\,\mathrm{A}$ and a dissipated power of about $0.19\,\mathrm{W}$. As a check, the power supplied by the component of the weight along the incline is $m g \sin\alpha \cdot v_{\text{lim}} = 0.196 \cdot 0.981\,\mathrm{W} \approx 0.19\,\mathrm{W}$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Two horizontal rails a distance $\ell = 40\,\mathrm{cm}$ apart are connected by a resistor $R = 0.25\,\mathrm{\Omega}$. A rod of mass $m = 50\,\mathrm{g}$ is given an initial velocity $v_0 = 4.0\,\mathrm{m/s}$ along the rails and then allowed to move freely. The magnetic field is uniform, has magnitude $0.50\,\mathrm{T}$, and is perpendicular to the plane of the rails. Friction and the resistances of the rails and rod are negligible.

a) Calculate the current, the braking force, and the rod’s acceleration immediately after release.

b) Calculate the heat dissipated in the resistor by the time the speed has fallen to half its initial value, and by the time the rod stops.

c) Calculate the total charge that passes through the resistor and the distance traveled by the rod.

$$
I_0 = \frac{B \ell v_0}{R} , \qquad
F_0 = I_0 \ell B , \qquad
a_0 = \frac{F_0}{m}
$$
$$
Q_{\frac{1}{2}} = \frac{1}{2} m v_0^2 - \frac{1}{2} m \left( \frac{v_0}{2} \right)^2 = \frac{3}{8} m v_0^2 , \qquad
Q_{\text{tot}} = \frac{1}{2} m v_0^2
$$
$$
B \ell\,q = m v_0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
q = \frac{m v_0}{B \ell} , \qquad
q = \frac{B \ell\,x}{R}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
x = \frac{q R}{B \ell}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
I_0 &= \frac{0.50 \cdot 0.40 \cdot 4.0}{0.25}\,\mathrm{A} = 3.2\,\mathrm{A} \\[6pt]
F_0 &= 3.2 \cdot 0.40 \cdot 0.50\,\mathrm{N} = 0.64\,\mathrm{N} \\[6pt]
a_0 &= \frac{0.64}{0.050}\,\mathrm{m/s^2} = 12.8\,\mathrm{m/s^2} \approx 13\,\mathrm{m/s^2} \\[6pt]
Q_{\frac{1}{2}} &= \frac{3}{8} \cdot 0.050 \cdot 4.0^2\,\mathrm{J} = 0.30\,\mathrm{J} , \qquad
Q_{\text{tot}} = \frac{1}{2} \cdot 0.050 \cdot 4.0^2\,\mathrm{J} = 0.40\,\mathrm{J} \\[6pt]
q &= \frac{0.050 \cdot 4.0}{0.50 \cdot 0.40}\,\mathrm{C} = 1.0\,\mathrm{C} \\[6pt]
x &= \frac{1.0 \cdot 0.25}{0.50 \cdot 0.40}\,\mathrm{m} = 1.25\,\mathrm{m}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Immediately after release the speed is $v_0$, so the motional emf is $B \ell v_0$ and the current is $3.2\,\mathrm{A}$. The magnetic force on the rod has magnitude $I_0 \ell B = 0.64\,\mathrm{N}$ and opposes its velocity. It is the only force along the rails because the weight and the normal force from the rails are vertical. By Newton’s second law the deceleration is $\dfrac{F_0}{m} \approx 13\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$. The braking force is proportional to speed, so the deceleration decreases as the rod slows down.

The magnetic force is the only force that does work on the rod, and by the **energy balance**, all of the kinetic energy lost is converted to heat in the resistor. When the speed has fallen to half its initial value, the kinetic energy is one-quarter of its initial value, so the heat dissipated is three-quarters of the initial energy, $0.30\,\mathrm{J}$. By the time the rod stops, all the initial kinetic energy, $0.40\,\mathrm{J}$, has been dissipated.

Use the impulse–momentum theorem to find the charge. During a short interval $\Delta t$, the magnetic force has magnitude $B \ell\,i$ and the magnitude of its impulse is $B \ell\,i\,\Delta t = B \ell\,\Delta q$, where $\Delta q$ is the charge that passes during that interval. Summing over the entire motion gives a total impulse of magnitude $B \ell\,q$. This must equal the magnitude of the initial momentum $m v_0$, because the rod finally comes to rest. This gives $q = 1.0\,\mathrm{C}$.

Finally, apply the formula for the **charge passing through the circuit**. If the rod travels a distance $x$, the circuit area increases by $\ell x$ and the flux by $B \ell x$, with the flux increasing throughout. Thus $q = \dfrac{B \ell x}{R}$, giving $x = 1.25\,\mathrm{m}$. The rod’s speed becomes smaller and smaller and approaches zero, but the total distance traveled is finite.

</div>
