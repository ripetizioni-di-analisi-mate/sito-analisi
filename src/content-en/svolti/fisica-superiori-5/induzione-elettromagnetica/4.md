## Exercise 1

A coil of $50$ turns, each of area $20\,\mathrm{cm^2}$, forms a closed circuit of total resistance $2.0\,\mathrm{\Omega}$. It is perpendicular to a uniform magnetic field whose magnitude varies over time according to
$$
B(t) = 0.30 + 0.40\,t ,
$$
with $B$ in teslas and $t$ in seconds. Write the flux linkage as a function of time and calculate the induced emf and the induced current.

$$
\Phi_B(t) = N S\,B(t) , \qquad
\mathcal{E}(t) = -\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt} , \qquad
i = \frac{|\mathcal{E}|}{R}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
\Phi_B(t) &= 50 \cdot 20 \cdot 10^{-4} \cdot (0.30 + 0.40\,t) \\[6pt]
&= 0.030 + 0.040\,t \qquad (\Phi_B \text{ in } \mathrm{Wb})
\end{aligned}
$$
$$
\mathcal{E} = -0.040\,\mathrm{V} , \qquad
i = \frac{0.040}{2.0}\,\mathrm{A} = 0.020\,\mathrm{A}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The field is uniform and perpendicular to the turns, so the flux linkage of the coil is $N S B$. Convert the area to square meters, $20\,\mathrm{cm^2} = 20 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m^2}$. The product $N S$ equals $0.10\,\mathrm{m^2}$. Multiplying by $B(t)$ gives a flux that increases linearly with time.

By the **Faraday–Neumann–Lenz law**, the instantaneous emf is the negative of the derivative of the flux. The derivative of $0.030 + 0.040\,t$ is the constant $0.040\,\mathrm{Wb/s}$, so the emf is constant and equals $-0.040\,\mathrm{V}$. The minus sign indicates that the current flows in the direction that opposes the increase in flux. Dividing the magnitude of the emf by the resistance gives the current, $20\,\mathrm{mA}$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

The flux linkage of a loop varies over time according to
$$
\Phi_B(t) = 10\,t - 2\,t^2 , \qquad 0 \leq t \leq 5\,\mathrm{s} ,
$$
with $\Phi_B$ in milliwebers and $t$ in seconds.

a) Write the induced emf as a function of time and calculate it for $t = 1\,\mathrm{s}$ and $t = 4\,\mathrm{s}$.

b) Find the instant at which the emf is zero and the value of the flux at that instant.

c) Calculate the average emf between $t = 0$ and $t = 5\,\mathrm{s}$.

::grafico{f="(x>=0&&x<=5?1:NaN)*(10*x-2*x^2);(x>=0&&x<=5?1:NaN)*(4*x-10)" punti="2.5,12.5,pieno" segmenti="2.5,0,2.5,12.5" xmin=-0.5 xmax=6 ymin=-12 ymax=14 nomi="t,y" legenda="\Phi_B(t) \text{ in mWb};\mathcal{E}(t) \text{ in mV}" titolo="Flux linkage and induced emf as functions of time $t$ in $\mathrm{s}$. The emf is zero at $t = 2.5\,\mathrm{s}$, where the flux reaches its maximum"}

$$
\mathcal{E}(t) = -\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt} = -(10 - 4\,t) = 4\,t - 10 \qquad (\mathcal{E} \text{ in } \mathrm{mV})
$$
$$
\mathcal{E}(1\,\mathrm{s}) = 4 - 10 = -6\,\mathrm{mV} , \qquad
\mathcal{E}(4\,\mathrm{s}) = 16 - 10 = 6\,\mathrm{mV}
$$
$$
4\,t - 10 = 0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
t = 2.5\,\mathrm{s} , \qquad
\Phi_B(2.5\,\mathrm{s}) = 25 - 12.5 = 12.5\,\mathrm{mWb}
$$
$$
\mathcal{E}_m = -\frac{\Phi_B(5) - \Phi_B(0)}{5 - 0} = -\frac{0 - 0}{5}\,\mathrm{mV} = 0
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

By the **Faraday–Neumann–Lenz law**, the instantaneous emf is the negative of the derivative of the flux. The derivative of $10\,t - 2\,t^2$ is $10 - 4\,t$, so $\mathcal{E}(t) = 4\,t - 10$. The flux is in milliwebers and the time in seconds, so the resulting emf is in millivolts. Substitution gives $-6\,\mathrm{mV}$ for $t = 1\,\mathrm{s}$ and $6\,\mathrm{mV}$ for $t = 4\,\mathrm{s}$. The signs are opposite because the flux is increasing at the first instant and decreasing at the second.

The emf vanishes when the derivative of the flux is zero, that is, at $t = 2.5\,\mathrm{s}$. At this instant the flux reaches its maximum value, $12.5\,\mathrm{mWb}$, and the tangent to the flux graph is horizontal. The emf is therefore zero precisely when the flux reaches its maximum.

The average emf between $0$ and $5\,\mathrm{s}$ is the negative of the change in flux divided by the time interval. The flux is zero both at the beginning and at the end, so the average emf is **zero**. This agrees with the graph, which shows negative emf in the first half of the interval and positive emf in the second, with opposite values at times equally spaced on either side of $2.5\,\mathrm{s}$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A square loop of side $20\,\mathrm{cm}$ and resistance $0.50\,\mathrm{\Omega}$ is perpendicular to a uniform magnetic field. The graph shows the flux linkage of the loop as a function of time. Calculate the emf and the induced current on each of the three intervals, and the magnetic field at $t = 4\,\mathrm{s}$ and $t = 8\,\mathrm{s}$.

::grafico{f="x<0||x>8?NaN:(x<=3?4*x:(x<=5?12:12-6*(x-5)))" punti="3,12,pieno;5,12,pieno;8,-6,pieno" segmenti="8,0,8,-6" xmin=-0.5 xmax=9 ymin=-9 ymax=15 nomi="t,Φ" legenda="\Phi_B(t)" titolo="Flux linkage $\Phi_B$ in $\mathrm{mWb}$ as a function of time $t$ in $\mathrm{s}$. It increases from $0$ to $12\,\mathrm{mWb}$ between $0$ and $3\,\mathrm{s}$, remains constant until $5\,\mathrm{s}$, and decreases to $-6\,\mathrm{mWb}$ between $5$ and $8\,\mathrm{s}$"}

$$
\mathcal{E} = -\frac{\Delta\Phi_B}{\Delta t} , \qquad
i = \frac{\mathcal{E}}{R} , \qquad
B = \frac{\Phi_B}{S}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
0 < t < 3\,\mathrm{s} : &\quad \mathcal{E} = -\frac{12\,\mathrm{mWb}}{3\,\mathrm{s}} = -4.0\,\mathrm{mV} , \quad i = -\frac{4.0\,\mathrm{mV}}{0.50\,\mathrm{\Omega}} = -8.0\,\mathrm{mA} \\[6pt]
3\,\mathrm{s} < t < 5\,\mathrm{s} : &\quad \mathcal{E} = 0 , \quad i = 0 \\[6pt]
5\,\mathrm{s} < t < 8\,\mathrm{s} : &\quad \mathcal{E} = -\frac{(-6 - 12)\,\mathrm{mWb}}{3\,\mathrm{s}} = 6.0\,\mathrm{mV} , \quad i = \frac{6.0\,\mathrm{mV}}{0.50\,\mathrm{\Omega}} = 12\,\mathrm{mA}
\end{aligned}
$$
$$
S = 0.20^2\,\mathrm{m^2} = 0.040\,\mathrm{m^2} , \qquad
B(4\,\mathrm{s}) = \frac{12 \cdot 10^{-3}}{0.040}\,\mathrm{T} = 0.30\,\mathrm{T} , \qquad
B(8\,\mathrm{s}) = \frac{-6 \cdot 10^{-3}}{0.040}\,\mathrm{T} = -0.15\,\mathrm{T}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Each part of the flux graph is a straight line segment, so the flux changes uniformly and the emf is constant on each interval. By the **Faraday–Neumann–Lenz law**, the emf is the negative of the slope of the segment, that is, the negative of the change in flux divided by the duration of the interval.

On the first interval the flux increases by $12\,\mathrm{mWb}$ in $3\,\mathrm{s}$, so the emf is $-4.0\,\mathrm{mV}$. On the second interval the flux is constant and the emf is zero. On the third, the flux goes from $12\,\mathrm{mWb}$ to $-6\,\mathrm{mWb}$, a change of $-18\,\mathrm{mWb}$ in $3\,\mathrm{s}$, so the emf is $6.0\,\mathrm{mV}$. Divide by $0.50\,\mathrm{\Omega}$ to obtain the currents. The opposite signs on the first and third intervals mean that the current flows in opposite directions.

The field is perpendicular to the loop, so the flux is $B S$. Dividing the flux by the area, $0.040\,\mathrm{m^2}$, gives the field. At $t = 4\,\mathrm{s}$ the field is $0.30\,\mathrm{T}$. At $t = 8\,\mathrm{s}$ the flux is negative, so the field has magnitude $0.15\,\mathrm{T}$ and points **opposite** to its initial direction. On the third interval the field drops to zero and then reverses direction, while the emf remains constant.

</div>

## Exercise 4

A loop of area $0.020\,\mathrm{m^2}$ and resistance $0.50\,\mathrm{\Omega}$ is perpendicular to a uniform magnetic field that varies over time according to
$$
B(t) = B_0 \sin(\omega t) ,
$$
with $B_0 = 0.050\,\mathrm{T}$ and $\omega = 100\pi\,\mathrm{rad/s}$. Write the induced emf as a function of time, calculate the maximum current, and find the emf at $t = 0$, $t = 5.0\,\mathrm{ms}$, and $t = 10\,\mathrm{ms}$.

$$
\Phi_B(t) = S B_0 \sin(\omega t) , \qquad
\mathcal{E}(t) = -\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt} = -S B_0 \omega \cos(\omega t)
$$
$$
\mathcal{E}_{\max} = S B_0 \omega , \qquad
i_{\max} = \frac{S B_0 \omega}{R}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{E}_{\max} &= 0.020 \cdot 0.050 \cdot 100\pi\,\mathrm{V} \approx 0.31\,\mathrm{V} \\[6pt]
i_{\max} &= \frac{0.314}{0.50}\,\mathrm{A} \approx 0.63\,\mathrm{A}
\end{aligned}
$$
$$
\mathcal{E}(0) = -0.31\,\mathrm{V} , \qquad
\mathcal{E}(5.0\,\mathrm{ms}) = -0.31 \cos\frac{\pi}{2}\,\mathrm{V} = 0 , \qquad
\mathcal{E}(10\,\mathrm{ms}) = -0.31 \cos\pi\,\mathrm{V} = 0.31\,\mathrm{V}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The field is perpendicular to the loop, so the flux is $S B(t) = S B_0 \sin(\omega t)$. By the **Faraday–Neumann–Lenz law**, the emf is the negative of the derivative of the flux. By the chain rule, the derivative of $\sin(\omega t)$ is $\omega \cos(\omega t)$, so $\mathcal{E}(t) = -S B_0 \omega \cos(\omega t)$.

The cosine ranges from $-1$ to $1$, so the maximum magnitude of the emf is $S B_0 \omega \approx 0.31\,\mathrm{V}$ and the maximum current is about $0.63\,\mathrm{A}$.

At $t = 5.0\,\mathrm{ms}$, the argument of the cosine is $100\pi \cdot 0.0050 = \dfrac{\pi}{2}$, and $\cos\dfrac{\pi}{2} = 0$. At $t = 10\,\mathrm{ms}$, the argument is $\pi$ and $\cos\pi = -1$. At $t = 5.0\,\mathrm{ms}$ the field reaches its maximum value $B_0$ and the emf is **zero**, whereas the emf has maximum magnitude at $t = 0$ and $t = 10\,\mathrm{ms}$, when the field is zero and changing most rapidly.

</div>
