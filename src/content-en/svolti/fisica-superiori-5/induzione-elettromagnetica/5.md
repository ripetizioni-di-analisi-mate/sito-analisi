## Exercise 1

A square loop of side $8.0\,\mathrm{cm}$ and resistance $0.040\,\mathrm{\Omega}$ is perpendicular to a uniform magnetic field of $0.50\,\mathrm{T}$ and is pulled completely out of the field region. Calculate the charge that passes through the loop. Then calculate the average current if the loop is removed in $0.10\,\mathrm{s}$ and if it is removed in $0.40\,\mathrm{s}$.

$$
q = \frac{|\Delta\Phi_B|}{R} = \frac{B L^2}{R} , \qquad
i_m = \frac{q}{\Delta t}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
q &= \frac{0.50 \cdot 0.080^2}{0.040}\,\mathrm{C} = 8.0 \cdot 10^{-2}\,\mathrm{C} \\[6pt]
i_m(0.10\,\mathrm{s}) &= \frac{8.0 \cdot 10^{-2}}{0.10}\,\mathrm{A} = 0.80\,\mathrm{A} \\[6pt]
i_m(0.40\,\mathrm{s}) &= \frac{8.0 \cdot 10^{-2}}{0.40}\,\mathrm{A} = 0.20\,\mathrm{A}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Initially the loop is perpendicular to the field, so the flux is $B L^2$, where $L$ is the side length in meters. At the end, the loop is outside the field and the flux is zero. The flux decreases throughout the removal, so the formula for the **charge passing through the circuit** applies. The charge is therefore the magnitude of the change in flux divided by the resistance, or $8.0 \cdot 10^{-2}\,\mathrm{C}$.

The charge is independent of the time taken to remove the loop. The average current, however, depends on the duration because it is the charge divided by the time. When removal takes four times as long, the average current is therefore one-quarter as large, decreasing from $0.80\,\mathrm{A}$ to $0.20\,\mathrm{A}$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

To measure a uniform magnetic field, a small coil of $200$ circular turns of radius $1.5\,\mathrm{cm}$ is connected to a charge-measuring instrument. The total resistance of the circuit is $25\,\mathrm{\Omega}$. The coil is initially perpendicular to the field and is rotated rapidly through $180^\circ$. The instrument measures a charge of $4.5 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{C}$. Determine the magnitude of the magnetic field.

$$
\Phi_1 = N B S , \qquad
\Phi_2 = -N B S , \qquad
|\Delta\Phi_B| = 2 N B S
$$
$$
q = \frac{2 N B S}{R}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
B = \frac{q R}{2 N S} = \frac{q R}{2 N \pi r^2}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
S &= \pi \cdot 0.015^2\,\mathrm{m^2} \approx 7.07 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m^2} \\[6pt]
B &= \frac{4.5 \cdot 10^{-4} \cdot 25}{2 \cdot 200 \cdot 7.07 \cdot 10^{-4}}\,\mathrm{T} \approx 4.0 \cdot 10^{-2}\,\mathrm{T}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Initially the normal to the turns is parallel to the field, so the flux linkage of the coil is $N B S$. After a rotation through $180^\circ$, the normal points opposite to the field. The angle is $180^\circ$ and $\cos 180^\circ = -1$, so the flux is $-N B S$. The magnitude of the change in flux is therefore $2 N B S$.

During rotation the angle goes from $0^\circ$ to $180^\circ$ and the cosine decreases throughout, so the flux changes in only one direction. The formula for the **charge passing through the circuit**, $q = \dfrac{2 N B S}{R}$, therefore applies. Solving this relation for the field by dividing $q R$ by $2 N S$ gives approximately $40\,\mathrm{mT}$.

This method requires only a measurement of the charge, because the result is independent of how quickly the coil rotates.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A long solenoid with $1000$ turns per meter carries a current of $4.0\,\mathrm{A}$. Inside it, far from the ends, is a small coil of $40$ turns, each of area $3.0\,\mathrm{cm^2}$, with its axis parallel to the axis of the solenoid. The coil forms a closed circuit of total resistance $0.50\,\mathrm{\Omega}$. The current in the solenoid is switched off. Calculate the charge that passes through the coil. Use $\mu_0 = 4\pi \cdot 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{T\cdot m/A}$.

$$
B = \mu_0 n I , \qquad
\Phi_B = N_2 S_2 B , \qquad
q = \frac{\Phi_B - 0}{R} = \frac{N_2 S_2\,\mu_0 n I}{R}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
B &= 4\pi \cdot 10^{-7} \cdot 1000 \cdot 4.0\,\mathrm{T} \approx 5.03 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{T} \\[6pt]
\Phi_B &= 40 \cdot 3.0 \cdot 10^{-4} \cdot 5.03 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{Wb} \approx 6.03 \cdot 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{Wb} \\[6pt]
q &= \frac{6.03 \cdot 10^{-5}}{0.50}\,\mathrm{C} \approx 1.2 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{C}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Inside a long solenoid, far from its ends, the field is uniform, is parallel to its axis, and has magnitude $\mu_0 n I$, as discussed in the chapter on sources of magnetic fields. Substituting the values gives about $5.0\,\mathrm{mT}$.

The axis of the coil is parallel to the field, so the flux linkage of the coil is $N_2 S_2 B$. When the current in the solenoid is switched off, the field drops to zero and the flux decreases from about $6.0 \cdot 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{Wb}$ to zero without reversing direction. By the formula for the **charge passing through the circuit**, the charge is the magnitude of this change divided by $0.50\,\mathrm{\Omega}$, or about $1.2 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{C}$.

The coil and solenoid are electrically isolated, so the current in the coil is due entirely to the change in the field produced by the solenoid, as in Faraday’s experiment with two coupled circuits.

</div>
