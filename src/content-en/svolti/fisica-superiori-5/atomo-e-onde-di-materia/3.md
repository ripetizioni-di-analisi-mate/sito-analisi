## Exercise 1

A hydrogen atom goes from level $n = 4$ to level $n = 2$. Calculate the energy of the emitted photon in electronvolts, its frequency, and its wavelength. Use $h = 6.63 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\cdot s}$, $c = 3.00 \cdot 10^{8}\,\mathrm{m/s}$, and $1\,\mathrm{eV} = 1.60 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{J}$.

::grafico{assi=0 linee="1,-13.6 7,-13.6;1,-3.4 7,-3.4;1,-1.51 7,-1.51;1,-0.85 7,-0.85;1,0 7,0" vettori="4,-0.85,4,-3.4,hf" etichette="7.2,-13.8,n = 1   −13.6 eV;7.2,-3.6,n = 2   −3.40 eV;7.2,-1.71,n = 3;7.2,-0.75,n = 4   −0.85 eV;7.2,0.25,E = 0" xmin=0 xmax=10.5 ymin=-14.5 ymax=1 titolo="The transition from $n = 4$ to $n = 2$ on the hydrogen energy-level diagram. The emitted photon carries away the energy difference between the two levels"}

$$
E_n = -\frac{13.6\,\mathrm{eV}}{n^2} ,
\qquad
E_{\text{photon}} = E_4 - E_2 ,
\qquad
f = \frac{E_{\text{photon}}}{h} ,
\qquad
\lambda = \frac{c}{f}
$$
$$
E_4 = -\frac{13.6}{16}\,\mathrm{eV} = -0.850\,\mathrm{eV} ,
\qquad
E_2 = -\frac{13.6}{4}\,\mathrm{eV} = -3.40\,\mathrm{eV}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
E_{\text{photon}} &= -0.850\,\mathrm{eV} - (-3.40\,\mathrm{eV}) = 2.55\,\mathrm{eV} \\[6pt]
f &= \frac{2.55 \cdot 1.60 \cdot 10^{-19}}{6.63 \cdot 10^{-34}}\,\mathrm{Hz} \approx 6.15 \cdot 10^{14}\,\mathrm{Hz} \\[6pt]
\lambda &= \frac{3.00 \cdot 10^{8}}{6.15 \cdot 10^{14}}\,\mathrm{m} \approx 4.88 \cdot 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{m} = 488\,\mathrm{nm}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The electron drops from level $n = 4$ to level $n = 2$, which has a lower energy, so the atom emits a photon. By **Bohr’s third postulate**, the photon’s energy is the difference between the initial and final energy levels, namely $2.55\,\mathrm{eV}$. The energy is positive, as required, because $E_4$ is less negative than $E_2$.

To find the frequency, convert the energy to joules by multiplying by $1.60 \cdot 10^{-19}$, then divide by $h$. The wavelength follows from $c = \lambda f$ and is approximately $488\,\mathrm{nm}$, corresponding to blue light. The result agrees to within $0.4\%$ with the $\mathrm{H}_\beta$ line measured at $486.1\,\mathrm{nm}$. The small difference comes from rounding the value of $13.6\,\mathrm{eV}$ and the constants.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A hydrogen atom in its ground state absorbs a photon of wavelength $\lambda = 102.8\,\mathrm{nm}$. Determine the electron’s level after absorption and list the wavelengths of the photons the atom can emit as it returns to its ground state. Use $h = 6.63 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\cdot s}$, $c = 3.00 \cdot 10^{8}\,\mathrm{m/s}$, and $1\,\mathrm{eV} = 1.60 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{J}$.

$$
E_{\text{photon}} = \frac{h c}{\lambda} = \frac{6.63 \cdot 10^{-34} \cdot 3.00 \cdot 10^{8}}{1.028 \cdot 10^{-7} \cdot 1.60 \cdot 10^{-19}}\,\mathrm{eV} \approx 12.09\,\mathrm{eV}
$$
$$
E_f = E_1 + E_{\text{photon}} = -13.6\,\mathrm{eV} + 12.09\,\mathrm{eV} = -1.51\,\mathrm{eV} ,
\qquad
n^2 = \frac{13.6}{1.51} \approx 9
\quad \Longrightarrow \quad
n = 3
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
3 \to 1 : \quad & E = 12.09\,\mathrm{eV} , && \lambda = 102.8\,\mathrm{nm} \\[6pt]
3 \to 2 : \quad & E = -1.51\,\mathrm{eV} - (-3.40\,\mathrm{eV}) = 1.89\,\mathrm{eV} , && \lambda = \frac{h c}{E} \approx 658\,\mathrm{nm} \\[6pt]
2 \to 1 : \quad & E = -3.40\,\mathrm{eV} - (-13.6\,\mathrm{eV}) = 10.2\,\mathrm{eV} , && \lambda = \frac{h c}{E} \approx 122\,\mathrm{nm}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

An atom absorbs a photon only if its energy equals the difference between two levels. The photon’s energy, $12.09\,\mathrm{eV}$, is added to the ground-state energy, taking the electron to a level with energy $-1.51\,\mathrm{eV}$. The formula for the **energy levels** gives $n^2 = 9$, so the electron is in level $n = 3$.

An electron in level $n = 3$ can return to the ground state in two ways. It can fall directly to $n = 1$ and emit a photon with the same energy as the one absorbed, or fall first to $n = 2$ and then to $n = 1$, emitting two photons. There are therefore three possible wavelengths: $102.8\,\mathrm{nm}$ and $122\,\mathrm{nm}$, which belong to the Lyman series, and $658\,\mathrm{nm}$, the red $\mathrm{H}_\alpha$ line of the Balmer series. The energies of the two photons in the second route, $1.89\,\mathrm{eV}$ and $10.2\,\mathrm{eV}$, add up to $12.09\,\mathrm{eV}$, as required by conservation of energy.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A hydrogen atom is in level $n = 2$.

1. Calculate the ionization energy from this level and the maximum wavelength of a photon capable of ionizing the atom.
2. The atom absorbs an ultraviolet photon of wavelength $300\,\mathrm{nm}$. Calculate the kinetic energy and speed of the freed electron, neglecting the recoil of the proton.

Use $h = 6.63 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\cdot s}$, $c = 3.00 \cdot 10^{8}\,\mathrm{m/s}$, $m_e = 9.11 \cdot 10^{-31}\,\mathrm{kg}$, and $1\,\mathrm{eV} = 1.60 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{J}$.

$$
E_{\text{ion}} = 0 - E_2 = \frac{13.6\,\mathrm{eV}}{4} = 3.40\,\mathrm{eV}
$$
$$
\lambda_{\max} = \frac{h c}{E_{\text{ion}}} = \frac{6.63 \cdot 10^{-34} \cdot 3.00 \cdot 10^{8}}{3.40 \cdot 1.60 \cdot 10^{-19}}\,\mathrm{m} \approx 3.66 \cdot 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{m} = 366\,\mathrm{nm}
$$
$$
E_{\text{photon}} = \frac{6.63 \cdot 10^{-34} \cdot 3.00 \cdot 10^{8}}{3.00 \cdot 10^{-7} \cdot 1.60 \cdot 10^{-19}}\,\mathrm{eV} \approx 4.14\,\mathrm{eV}
$$
$$
K = E_{\text{photon}} - E_{\text{ion}} \approx 4.14\,\mathrm{eV} - 3.40\,\mathrm{eV} = 0.74\,\mathrm{eV}
$$
$$
v = \sqrt{\frac{2K}{m_e}} = \sqrt{\frac{2 \cdot 0.744 \cdot 1.60 \cdot 10^{-19}}{9.11 \cdot 10^{-31}}}\,\mathrm{m/s} \approx 5.1 \cdot 10^{5}\,\mathrm{m/s}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The **ionization energy** from level $n = 2$ is the energy required to take the electron from $E_2 = -3.40\,\mathrm{eV}$ to energy $0$, so it equals $3.40\,\mathrm{eV}$. A photon’s energy is $\dfrac{h c}{\lambda}$ and decreases as $\lambda$ increases. The longest-wavelength photon capable of ionizing the atom is therefore the one with energy equal to $3.40\,\mathrm{eV}$. Its wavelength is $366\,\mathrm{nm}$, in the near ultraviolet.

The $300\,\mathrm{nm}$ photon has a wavelength shorter than $366\,\mathrm{nm}$ and therefore has sufficient energy, $4.14\,\mathrm{eV}$, to ionize the atom. By conservation of energy, the excess over the ionization energy becomes kinetic energy of the freed electron, namely $0.74\,\mathrm{eV}$. Converting to joules and solving $K = \dfrac{1}{2} m_e v^2$ for speed gives approximately $5.1 \cdot 10^{5}\,\mathrm{m/s}$, much smaller than $c$. The classical kinetic energy formula therefore applies.

</div>
