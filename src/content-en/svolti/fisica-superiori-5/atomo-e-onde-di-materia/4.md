## Exercise 1

An electron initially at rest is accelerated through a potential difference $V = 150\,\mathrm{V}$. Calculate its momentum, speed, and de Broglie wavelength. Use $m_e = 9.11 \cdot 10^{-31}\,\mathrm{kg}$, $e = 1.60 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{C}$, and $h = 6.63 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\cdot s}$.

$$
K = e V ,
\qquad
K = \frac{p^2}{2 m_e}
\quad \Longrightarrow \quad
p = \sqrt{2 m_e e V} ,
\qquad
v = \frac{p}{m_e} ,
\qquad
\lambda = \frac{h}{p}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
p &= \sqrt{2 \cdot 9.11 \cdot 10^{-31} \cdot 1.60 \cdot 10^{-19} \cdot 150}\,\mathrm{kg\cdot m/s} \approx 6.61 \cdot 10^{-24}\,\mathrm{kg\cdot m/s} \\[6pt]
v &= \frac{6.61 \cdot 10^{-24}}{9.11 \cdot 10^{-31}}\,\mathrm{m/s} \approx 7.26 \cdot 10^{6}\,\mathrm{m/s} \\[6pt]
\lambda &= \frac{6.63 \cdot 10^{-34}}{6.61 \cdot 10^{-24}}\,\mathrm{m} \approx 1.00 \cdot 10^{-10}\,\mathrm{m} = 0.100\,\mathrm{nm}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The electric field does work $eV$ on the electron. By the work–energy theorem, this becomes the electron’s kinetic energy because it starts from rest. The kinetic energy can be written as $\dfrac{1}{2} m_e v^2 = \dfrac{p^2}{2 m_e}$, so the momentum is $\sqrt{2 m_e e V}$.

The speed is about $7.26 \cdot 10^{6}\,\mathrm{m/s}$, or $2.4\%$ of $c$, so $v \ll c$ and the classical expressions for $K$ and $p$ apply. By **de Broglie’s hypothesis**, the wavelength is the ratio $\dfrac{h}{p}$, which is $0.100\,\mathrm{nm}$. This is comparable to the spacing between atoms in a solid, so electrons with this energy are diffracted by crystals.

</div>

## Exercise 2

An electron and a proton have the same kinetic energy, $K = 1.0\,\mathrm{keV}$. Calculate their de Broglie wavelengths and the ratio of the two. Use $m_e = 9.11 \cdot 10^{-31}\,\mathrm{kg}$, $m_p = 1.67 \cdot 10^{-27}\,\mathrm{kg}$, $h = 6.63 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\cdot s}$, and $1\,\mathrm{eV} = 1.60 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{J}$.

$$
\lambda = \frac{h}{p} = \frac{h}{\sqrt{2 m K}} ,
\qquad
K = 1.0 \cdot 10^{3} \cdot 1.60 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{J} = 1.60 \cdot 10^{-16}\,\mathrm{J}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
\lambda_e &= \frac{6.63 \cdot 10^{-34}}{\sqrt{2 \cdot 9.11 \cdot 10^{-31} \cdot 1.60 \cdot 10^{-16}}}\,\mathrm{m} \approx 3.9 \cdot 10^{-11}\,\mathrm{m} \\[6pt]
\lambda_p &= \frac{6.63 \cdot 10^{-34}}{\sqrt{2 \cdot 1.67 \cdot 10^{-27} \cdot 1.60 \cdot 10^{-16}}}\,\mathrm{m} \approx 9.1 \cdot 10^{-13}\,\mathrm{m}
\end{aligned}
$$
$$
\frac{\lambda_e}{\lambda_p} = \sqrt{\frac{m_p}{m_e}} = \sqrt{\frac{1.67 \cdot 10^{-27}}{9.11 \cdot 10^{-31}}} \approx 43
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

For both particles, the kinetic energy is much smaller than the rest energy, so their speeds are much smaller than $c$ and $p = \sqrt{2 m K}$ holds. Substituting into **de Broglie’s relation** gives $\lambda = \dfrac{h}{\sqrt{2 m K}}$.

At the same kinetic energy, wavelength is inversely proportional to the square root of mass. In the ratio $\dfrac{\lambda_e}{\lambda_p}$, the factors $h$, $2$, and $K$ cancel, leaving $\sqrt{\dfrac{m_p}{m_e}}$. A proton has about $1830$ times the mass of an electron, so its wavelength is **shorter by a factor of about 43**. To observe proton diffraction therefore requires much smaller structures than those sufficient for electrons of the same energy.

</div>

## Exercise 3

An electron has de Broglie wavelength $\lambda = 0.10\,\mathrm{nm}$.

1. Calculate its speed and its kinetic energy in electronvolts.
2. Calculate the energy of a photon with the same wavelength and compare it with the electron’s kinetic energy.
3. Calculate the de Broglie wavelength of a tennis ball of mass $58\,\mathrm{g}$ traveling at $50\,\mathrm{m/s}$ and explain why the ball does not exhibit wave effects.

Use $m_e = 9.11 \cdot 10^{-31}\,\mathrm{kg}$, $h = 6.63 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\cdot s}$, $c = 3.00 \cdot 10^{8}\,\mathrm{m/s}$, and $1\,\mathrm{eV} = 1.60 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{J}$.

$$
p = \frac{h}{\lambda} = \frac{6.63 \cdot 10^{-34}}{1.0 \cdot 10^{-10}}\,\mathrm{kg\cdot m/s} = 6.63 \cdot 10^{-24}\,\mathrm{kg\cdot m/s}
$$
$$
v = \frac{p}{m_e} = \frac{6.63 \cdot 10^{-24}}{9.11 \cdot 10^{-31}}\,\mathrm{m/s} \approx 7.3 \cdot 10^{6}\,\mathrm{m/s} ,
\qquad
K = \frac{p^2}{2 m_e} = \frac{\left( 6.63 \cdot 10^{-24} \right)^2}{2 \cdot 9.11 \cdot 10^{-31}}\,\mathrm{J} \approx 2.41 \cdot 10^{-17}\,\mathrm{J} \approx 151\,\mathrm{eV}
$$
$$
E_{\text{photon}} = \frac{h c}{\lambda} = \frac{6.63 \cdot 10^{-34} \cdot 3.00 \cdot 10^{8}}{1.0 \cdot 10^{-10}}\,\mathrm{J} \approx 1.99 \cdot 10^{-15}\,\mathrm{J} \approx 1.24 \cdot 10^{4}\,\mathrm{eV} ,
\qquad
\frac{E_{\text{photon}}}{K} \approx 82
$$
$$
\lambda_{\text{ball}} = \frac{h}{m v} = \frac{6.63 \cdot 10^{-34}}{0.058 \cdot 50}\,\mathrm{m} \approx 2.3 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\mathrm{m}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

**De Broglie’s hypothesis** gives the momentum $p = \dfrac{h}{\lambda}$. The resulting speed, approximately $7.3 \cdot 10^{6}\,\mathrm{m/s}$, is much smaller than $c$, so $p = m_e v$ and $K = \dfrac{p^2}{2 m_e}$ can be used. The kinetic energy is approximately $151\,\mathrm{eV}$, consistent with an electron accelerated through $150\,\mathrm{V}$ having a wavelength of about $0.10\,\mathrm{nm}$.

A photon with the same wavelength has the same momentum magnitude $\dfrac{h}{\lambda}$. Its energy, however, is $p c$, because a photon is massless. This energy, about $12.4\,\mathrm{keV}$, corresponds to an X-ray photon and is about **82 times** the electron’s kinetic energy. Thus electrons require much less energy than photons to attain the same wavelength. The electron microscope uses this property.

The tennis ball has about $4 \cdot 10^{23}$ times the momentum of the electron and a wavelength of approximately $2.3 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\mathrm{m}$. Wave effects can be observed only with obstacles or slits comparable in size to the wavelength. No object is that small, so the ball moves according to the laws of classical mechanics.

</div>
