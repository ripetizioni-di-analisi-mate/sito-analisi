## Exercise 1

In a laboratory, four wavelengths in the hydrogen Balmer series are measured. The results appear in the table, where $n_i$ is the quantum number of the initial level.

| $n_i$ | $\lambda$ (nm) | $\dfrac{1}{n_i^2}$ | $\dfrac{1}{\lambda}$ ($10^{5}\,\mathrm{m^{-1}}$) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $3$ | $656.3$ | $0.1111$ | $15.24$ |
| $4$ | $486.1$ | $0.0625$ | $20.57$ |
| $5$ | $434.0$ | $0.0400$ | $23.04$ |
| $6$ | $410.2$ | $0.0278$ | $24.38$ |

1. Explain why the graph of $\dfrac{1}{\lambda}$ as a function of $\dfrac{1}{n_i^2}$ is a straight line and use it to determine the Rydberg constant.
2. Determine the limiting wavelength of the series.
3. Find the ionization energies of the atom from level $n = 2$ and from level $n = 1$.

Use $h = 6.63 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\cdot s}$, $c = 3.00 \cdot 10^{8}\,\mathrm{m/s}$, and $1\,\mathrm{eV} = 1.60 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{J}$.

::grafico{f="27.43-1.097*x" punti="11.11,15.24,pieno;6.25,20.57,pieno;4.0,23.04,pieno;2.778,24.38,pieno" segmenti="2.778,24.38,2.778,15.24;2.778,15.24,11.11,15.24" xmin=0 xmax=12 ymin=0 ymax=30 nomi="x,y" legenda=0 titolo="Balmer-series data with $x = \dfrac{100}{n_i^2}$ and $y = \dfrac{1}{\lambda}$ in $10^{5}\,\mathrm{m^{-1}}$. The points lie on a straight line whose intercept on the $y$-axis gives the series limit"}

$$
\frac{1}{\lambda} = \frac{R_H}{4} - R_H \cdot \frac{1}{n_i^2}
\qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad
y = q + m\, x \quad \text{with} \quad m = -R_H , \quad q = \frac{R_H}{4}
$$
$$
m = \frac{(24.38 - 15.24) \cdot 10^{5}\,\mathrm{m^{-1}}}{0.0278 - 0.1111} \approx -1.097 \cdot 10^{7}\,\mathrm{m^{-1}}
\qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad
R_H \approx 1.097 \cdot 10^{7}\,\mathrm{m^{-1}}
$$
$$
q = \frac{1}{\lambda_\infty} = 15.24 \cdot 10^{5}\,\mathrm{m^{-1}} + 1.097 \cdot 10^{7} \cdot 0.1111\,\mathrm{m^{-1}} \approx 2.743 \cdot 10^{6}\,\mathrm{m^{-1}}
\qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad
\lambda_\infty \approx 364.6\,\mathrm{nm}
$$
$$
E_{\text{ion},2} = \frac{h c}{\lambda_\infty} = \frac{6.63 \cdot 10^{-34} \cdot 3.00 \cdot 10^{8}}{3.646 \cdot 10^{-7} \cdot 1.60 \cdot 10^{-19}}\,\mathrm{eV} \approx 3.41\,\mathrm{eV} ,
\qquad
E_{\text{ion},1} = 4\, E_{\text{ion},2} \approx 13.6\,\mathrm{eV}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

From the **Rydberg formula** with $n_f = 2$, we have $\dfrac{1}{\lambda} = R_H \left( \dfrac{1}{4} - \dfrac{1}{n_i^2} \right)$. Expanding the product expresses $\dfrac{1}{\lambda}$ as the sum of the constant $\dfrac{R_H}{4}$ and the term $-R_H \cdot \dfrac{1}{n_i^2}$, so it is a linear function of $\dfrac{1}{n_i^2}$. The graph is therefore a straight line of slope $-R_H$, and the four measured points lie on it.

Use the two points farthest apart, those with $n_i = 6$ and $n_i = 3$, to calculate the slope. It is $-1.097 \cdot 10^{7}\,\mathrm{m^{-1}}$, and the Rydberg constant is its negative. The vertical intercept corresponds to $\dfrac{1}{n_i^2} = 0$, that is, to the series limit $n_i \to +\infty$. Starting at the point with $n_i = 3$ and adding $R_H \cdot 0.1111$ gives the intercept $\lambda_\infty \approx 364.6\,\mathrm{nm}$.

The series limit corresponds to an electron initially at rest at infinity arriving at level $n = 2$, with initial energy $0$. The limiting photon energy therefore equals the **ionization energy** from level $n = 2$, namely $3.41\,\mathrm{eV}$. The energies of the hydrogen levels are proportional to $\dfrac{1}{n^2}$, so the ionization energy from level $n = 1$ is four times as large, or about $13.6\,\mathrm{eV}$, in agreement with the Bohr model.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A beam of electrons, each with kinetic energy $K = 12.5\,\mathrm{eV}$, passes through a gas of hydrogen atoms in the ground state.

1. Calculate the speed and de Broglie wavelength of the beam electrons.
2. Explain which levels a hydrogen atom can reach in a collision with one of these electrons, neglecting the recoil energy of the atom.
3. Determine the wavelengths of the lines emitted by the gas and identify which are visible.
4. Calculate the kinetic energy remaining to the electron after it raises an atom to the highest possible level.

Use $m_e = 9.11 \cdot 10^{-31}\,\mathrm{kg}$, $h = 6.63 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\cdot s}$, $c = 3.00 \cdot 10^{8}\,\mathrm{m/s}$, and $1\,\mathrm{eV} = 1.60 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{J}$.

$$
v = \sqrt{\frac{2K}{m_e}} = \sqrt{\frac{2 \cdot 12.5 \cdot 1.60 \cdot 10^{-19}}{9.11 \cdot 10^{-31}}}\,\mathrm{m/s} \approx 2.10 \cdot 10^{6}\,\mathrm{m/s} ,
\qquad
\lambda = \frac{h}{m_e v} \approx \frac{6.63 \cdot 10^{-34}}{9.11 \cdot 10^{-31} \cdot 2.095 \cdot 10^{6}}\,\mathrm{m} \approx 0.347\,\mathrm{nm}
$$
$$
E_n - E_1 = 13.6\,\mathrm{eV} \left( 1 - \frac{1}{n^2} \right) :
\qquad
n = 2 : \ 10.2\,\mathrm{eV} ,
\qquad
n = 3 : \ 12.09\,\mathrm{eV} ,
\qquad
n = 4 : \ 12.75\,\mathrm{eV} > 12.5\,\mathrm{eV}
$$
$$
\lambda = \frac{h c}{\Delta E} :
\qquad
3 \to 1 : \ 102.8\,\mathrm{nm} ,
\qquad
2 \to 1 : \ 122\,\mathrm{nm} ,
\qquad
3 \to 2 : \ 658\,\mathrm{nm}
$$
$$
K' = K - (E_3 - E_1) = 12.5\,\mathrm{eV} - 12.09\,\mathrm{eV} \approx 0.41\,\mathrm{eV}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The electrons’ speed, about $2.1 \cdot 10^{6}\,\mathrm{m/s}$, is less than $1\%$ of $c$, so we can use the classical formulas $K = \dfrac{1}{2} m_e v^2$ and $p = m_e v$. By **de Broglie’s hypothesis**, the wavelength is $\dfrac{h}{m_e v}$, approximately $0.35\,\mathrm{nm}$.

In a collision, an electron can transfer to the atom only an energy equal to the difference between the ground state and an excited level, as illustrated by the **Franck–Hertz experiment**. The differences for levels $n = 2$ and $n = 3$ are less than $12.5\,\mathrm{eV}$, while the difference for $n = 4$ is $12.75\,\mathrm{eV}$, greater than the available energy. Thus the collision can raise the atom only to levels $n = 2$ and $n = 3$. Higher levels and ionization require still more energy.

The excited atoms return to the ground state by emitting photons. From level $3$, the possible transitions are $3 \to 1$, $3 \to 2$, and $2 \to 1$. From level $2$, the only transition is $2 \to 1$, which is already included. Three lines are therefore emitted. The lines at $102.8\,\mathrm{nm}$ and $122\,\mathrm{nm}$ belong to the Lyman series, in the ultraviolet. The only visible line is at $658\,\mathrm{nm}$, the red $\mathrm{H}_\alpha$ line. Finally, by conservation of energy, an electron that raises an atom to level $n = 3$ retains kinetic energy $12.5\,\mathrm{eV} - 12.09\,\mathrm{eV} \approx 0.41\,\mathrm{eV}$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

The electron in a hydrogen atom occupies the Bohr orbit with $n = 4$. The Bohr radius $r_1 = 5.3 \cdot 10^{-11}\,\mathrm{m}$ and the constants $m_e = 9.11 \cdot 10^{-31}\,\mathrm{kg}$, $h = 6.63 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\cdot s}$, $c = 3.00 \cdot 10^{8}\,\mathrm{m/s}$, and $1\,\mathrm{eV} = 1.60 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{J}$ are known.

1. Calculate the orbital radius, the electron’s speed, and its de Broglie wavelength.
2. Verify that the circumference of the orbit contains an integer number of wavelengths and explain the meaning of this result.
3. Use the uncertainty principle to estimate the minimum uncertainty in the electron’s velocity, assuming that its position uncertainty equals the orbital diameter. Compare the uncertainty with the speed you calculated.
4. The electron goes from orbit $n = 4$ to orbit $n = 2$. Calculate the energy and wavelength of the emitted photon.

$$
r_4 = 16\, r_1 = 16 \cdot 5.3 \cdot 10^{-11}\,\mathrm{m} \approx 8.5 \cdot 10^{-10}\,\mathrm{m}
$$
$$
v_4 = \frac{4 h}{2\pi m_e r_4} = \frac{4 \cdot 6.63 \cdot 10^{-34}}{2\pi \cdot 9.11 \cdot 10^{-31} \cdot 8.48 \cdot 10^{-10}}\,\mathrm{m/s} \approx 5.46 \cdot 10^{5}\,\mathrm{m/s}
$$
$$
\lambda_4 = \frac{h}{m_e v_4} = \frac{6.63 \cdot 10^{-34}}{9.11 \cdot 10^{-31} \cdot 5.46 \cdot 10^{5}}\,\mathrm{m} \approx 1.33 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\mathrm{m}
$$
$$
\frac{2\pi r_4}{\lambda_4} = \frac{2\pi \cdot 8.48 \cdot 10^{-10}}{1.332 \cdot 10^{-9}} \approx 4.0
$$
$$
\Delta x = 2 r_4 \approx 1.70 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\mathrm{m} ,
\qquad
\Delta v_x \geq \frac{h}{4\pi\, m_e\, \Delta x} = \frac{6.63 \cdot 10^{-34}}{4\pi \cdot 9.11 \cdot 10^{-31} \cdot 1.70 \cdot 10^{-9}}\,\mathrm{m/s} \approx 3.4 \cdot 10^{4}\,\mathrm{m/s} ,
\qquad
\frac{\Delta v_x}{v_4} \approx 0.063
$$
$$
E_{\text{photon}} = E_4 - E_2 = -\frac{13.6}{16}\,\mathrm{eV} + \frac{13.6}{4}\,\mathrm{eV} = 2.55\,\mathrm{eV} ,
\qquad
\lambda = \frac{h c}{E_{\text{photon}}} = \frac{6.63 \cdot 10^{-34} \cdot 3.00 \cdot 10^{8}}{2.55 \cdot 1.60 \cdot 10^{-19}}\,\mathrm{m} \approx 488\,\mathrm{nm}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

From the formula for the **radii of the Bohr orbits**, the radius of orbit $n = 4$ is $16 r_1$, or approximately $0.85\,\mathrm{nm}$. The speed follows from the second postulate, $m_e v_4 r_4 = 4 \dfrac{h}{2\pi}$, and is one-quarter of $v_1$. Since $v_4 \ll c$, the momentum is $m_e v_4$ and the de Broglie wavelength is about $1.33\,\mathrm{nm}$.

The circumference of the orbit, $2\pi r_4 \approx 5.33\,\mathrm{nm}$, contains four de Broglie wavelengths. The electron wave therefore matches up with itself and forms a **standing wave**. This is the wave interpretation of Bohr’s quantization condition. The number of wavelengths in the circumference equals the quantum number $n$.

The electron is on the orbit, so the uncertainty in its position along a diameter is at most the diameter, about $1.7\,\mathrm{nm}$. By the **uncertainty principle**, the minimum velocity uncertainty is $\dfrac{h}{4\pi m_e\, \Delta x}$, or about $3.4 \cdot 10^{4}\,\mathrm{m/s}$, which is $6\%$ of the speed $v_4$. This uncertainty is small but appreciable. For orbits closer to the nucleus, the ratio increases, and for $n = 1$ it becomes approximately $0.25$. Thus a precise orbit is an increasingly poor approximation for the electron the closer it is to the nucleus.

In the transition from orbit $n = 4$ to orbit $n = 2$, the photon’s energy is the difference between the levels, $2.55\,\mathrm{eV}$, by Bohr’s third postulate. The corresponding wavelength is about $488\,\mathrm{nm}$, the blue $\mathrm{H}_\beta$ line of the Balmer series.

</div>
