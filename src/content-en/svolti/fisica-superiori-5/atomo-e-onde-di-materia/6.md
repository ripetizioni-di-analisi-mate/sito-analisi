## Exercise 1

An electron is confined to a region of width $\Delta x = 0.50\,\mathrm{nm}$. Calculate the minimum uncertainties in its momentum and velocity. Repeat the calculation for a dust grain of mass $1.0 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\mathrm{kg}$ with the same position uncertainty. Use $m_e = 9.11 \cdot 10^{-31}\,\mathrm{kg}$ and $h = 6.63 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\cdot s}$.

$$
\Delta x \cdot \Delta p_x \geq \frac{h}{4\pi}
\quad \Longrightarrow \quad
\Delta p_x \geq \frac{h}{4\pi\, \Delta x} ,
\qquad
\Delta v_x = \frac{\Delta p_x}{m}
$$
$$
\Delta p_x \geq \frac{6.63 \cdot 10^{-34}}{4\pi \cdot 5.0 \cdot 10^{-10}}\,\mathrm{kg\cdot m/s} \approx 1.06 \cdot 10^{-25}\,\mathrm{kg\cdot m/s}
$$
$$
\Delta v_{x,\text{electron}} \geq \frac{1.06 \cdot 10^{-25}}{9.11 \cdot 10^{-31}}\,\mathrm{m/s} \approx 1.2 \cdot 10^{5}\,\mathrm{m/s} ,
\qquad
\Delta v_{x,\text{grain}} \geq \frac{1.06 \cdot 10^{-25}}{1.0 \cdot 10^{-9}}\,\mathrm{m/s} \approx 1.1 \cdot 10^{-16}\,\mathrm{m/s}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

By the **uncertainty principle**, the product of the uncertainties in position and momentum is at least $\dfrac{h}{4\pi}$. The minimum momentum uncertainty is therefore $\dfrac{h}{4\pi\, \Delta x}$. It depends only on $\Delta x$, so it is the same for the electron and the dust grain.

Dividing by the mass gives the velocity uncertainty, which differs greatly in the two cases. For the electron the uncertainty is about $1.2 \cdot 10^{5}\,\mathrm{m/s}$, comparable to typical electron speeds in atoms, so the principle has appreciable effects. For the grain it is about $10^{-16}\,\mathrm{m/s}$, well below the precision of any measurement. Thus the principle has **negligible effects** for the grain.

</div>

## Exercise 2

An electron has a speed of $v = 3.00 \cdot 10^{5}\,\mathrm{m/s}$ measured with an uncertainty of $0.10\%$. Calculate the minimum uncertainty with which its position along the direction of motion can be known at the same instant. Use $m_e = 9.11 \cdot 10^{-31}\,\mathrm{kg}$ and $h = 6.63 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\cdot s}$.

$$
\Delta p_x = m_e\, \Delta v_x ,
\qquad
\Delta x \geq \frac{h}{4\pi\, \Delta p_x}
$$
$$
\Delta v_x = 0.0010 \cdot 3.00 \cdot 10^{5}\,\mathrm{m/s} = 300\,\mathrm{m/s} ,
\qquad
\Delta p_x = 9.11 \cdot 10^{-31} \cdot 300\,\mathrm{kg\cdot m/s} \approx 2.73 \cdot 10^{-28}\,\mathrm{kg\cdot m/s}
$$
$$
\Delta x \geq \frac{6.63 \cdot 10^{-34}}{4\pi \cdot 2.73 \cdot 10^{-28}}\,\mathrm{m} \approx 1.9 \cdot 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{m}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

An uncertainty of $0.10\%$ corresponds to a fraction $0.0010$ of the speed, or $300\,\mathrm{m/s}$. The speed is much smaller than $c$, so $p_x = m_e v_x$ and the momentum uncertainty is $m_e\, \Delta v_x$. The **uncertainty principle** for position and momentum then gives the minimum position uncertainty.

The result, about $1.9 \cdot 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{m}$, is roughly two thousand times the diameter of an atom. Knowing the electron’s speed precisely therefore entails a large uncertainty in its position.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A proton is confined to a nucleus, a region of width $\Delta x = 5.0 \cdot 10^{-15}\,\mathrm{m}$. Estimate the minimum uncertainty in its momentum, assuming the proton’s momentum to be of the same order of magnitude as that uncertainty. Estimate the proton’s speed and its kinetic energy in $\mathrm{MeV}$, and check that the use of classical mechanics is justified. Use $m_p = 1.67 \cdot 10^{-27}\,\mathrm{kg}$, $h = 6.63 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\cdot s}$, $c = 3.00 \cdot 10^{8}\,\mathrm{m/s}$, and $1\,\mathrm{eV} = 1.60 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{J}$.

$$
p \approx \Delta p_x = \frac{h}{4\pi\, \Delta x} = \frac{6.63 \cdot 10^{-34}}{4\pi \cdot 5.0 \cdot 10^{-15}}\,\mathrm{kg\cdot m/s} \approx 1.06 \cdot 10^{-20}\,\mathrm{kg\cdot m/s}
$$
$$
v = \frac{p}{m_p} = \frac{1.06 \cdot 10^{-20}}{1.67 \cdot 10^{-27}}\,\mathrm{m/s} \approx 6.3 \cdot 10^{6}\,\mathrm{m/s} ,
\qquad
\frac{v}{c} \approx 0.021
$$
$$
K = \frac{p^2}{2 m_p} = \frac{\left( 1.06 \cdot 10^{-20} \right)^2}{2 \cdot 1.67 \cdot 10^{-27}}\,\mathrm{J} \approx 3.3 \cdot 10^{-14}\,\mathrm{J} \approx 0.21\,\mathrm{MeV}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The proton cannot leave the nuclear region, so its position uncertainty is at most the width of the nucleus. By the **uncertainty principle**, its momentum uncertainty is therefore at least $\dfrac{h}{4\pi\, \Delta x}$. For this estimate we take a particle with this momentum uncertainty to have a typical momentum of the same order, on the assumption that its typical momentum is at least of the order of its uncertainty.

The resulting speed is about $2\%$ of $c$, so $v \ll c$ and the classical formulas $p = m_p v$ and $K = \dfrac{p^2}{2 m_p}$ apply. The estimated kinetic energy is of order $0.2\,\mathrm{MeV}$, much greater than the energies of electrons in atoms, which are a few $\mathrm{eV}$. This explains why nuclear processes involve energies of order $\mathrm{MeV}$.

</div>

## Exercise 4

The excited state $n = 3$ of the hydrogen atom has a mean lifetime of about $\Delta t = 5.0 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\mathrm{s}$. Estimate the minimum energy uncertainty of this level, in electronvolts, and the corresponding frequency uncertainty of photons emitted in the transition $3 \to 2$. Compare the result with the frequency of the $\mathrm{H}_\alpha$ line, of wavelength $656.3\,\mathrm{nm}$. Use $h = 6.63 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\cdot s}$, $c = 3.00 \cdot 10^{8}\,\mathrm{m/s}$, and $1\,\mathrm{eV} = 1.60 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{J}$.

$$
\Delta E \geq \frac{h}{4\pi\, \Delta t} ,
\qquad
\Delta f = \frac{\Delta E}{h} = \frac{1}{4\pi\, \Delta t} ,
\qquad
f = \frac{c}{\lambda}
$$
$$
\Delta E \geq \frac{6.63 \cdot 10^{-34}}{4\pi \cdot 5.0 \cdot 10^{-9}}\,\mathrm{J} \approx 1.06 \cdot 10^{-26}\,\mathrm{J} \approx 6.6 \cdot 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{eV}
$$
$$
\Delta f = \frac{1}{4\pi \cdot 5.0 \cdot 10^{-9}}\,\mathrm{Hz} \approx 1.6 \cdot 10^{7}\,\mathrm{Hz} ,
\qquad
f = \frac{3.00 \cdot 10^{8}}{6.563 \cdot 10^{-7}}\,\mathrm{Hz} \approx 4.57 \cdot 10^{14}\,\mathrm{Hz} ,
\qquad
\frac{\Delta f}{f} \approx 3.5 \cdot 10^{-8}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The electron remains in level $n = 3$ for a time of the order of its mean lifetime, so by the **energy–time uncertainty principle** the energy uncertainty of the level is at least $\dfrac{h}{4\pi\, \Delta t}$, about $6.6 \cdot 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{eV}$. The final level $n = 2$ also has a finite mean lifetime. This estimate includes only the uncertainty of the initial level.

The energy of the emitted photon is $h f$, so an energy uncertainty $\Delta E$ corresponds to a frequency uncertainty $\dfrac{\Delta E}{h}$, or approximately $1.6 \cdot 10^{7}\,\mathrm{Hz}$. The line frequency is about $4.57 \cdot 10^{14}\,\mathrm{Hz}$, and the ratio of the uncertainty to this frequency is of order $10^{-8}$. The line’s natural width is therefore very small relative to its frequency, and it appears **very narrow** in the spectrum.

</div>
