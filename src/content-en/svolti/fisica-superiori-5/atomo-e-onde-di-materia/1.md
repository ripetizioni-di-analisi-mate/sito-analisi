## Exercise 1

Calculate the wavelength and frequency of the hydrogen Balmer line corresponding to $n_i = 5$, and identify the region of the spectrum in which it lies. Use $R_H = 1.097 \cdot 10^{7}\,\mathrm{m^{-1}}$ and $c = 3.00 \cdot 10^{8}\,\mathrm{m/s}$.

$$
\frac{1}{\lambda} = R_H \left( \frac{1}{n_f^2} - \frac{1}{n_i^2} \right) ,
\qquad
n_f = 2 ,
\qquad
n_i = 5
$$
$$
\frac{1}{4} - \frac{1}{25} = \frac{25 - 4}{100} = \frac{21}{100}
$$
$$
\lambda = \frac{100}{21\, R_H} = \frac{100}{21 \cdot 1.097 \cdot 10^{7}}\,\mathrm{m} \approx 4.341 \cdot 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{m} = 434.1\,\mathrm{nm}
$$
$$
f = \frac{c}{\lambda} = \frac{3.00 \cdot 10^{8}}{4.341 \cdot 10^{-7}}\,\mathrm{Hz} \approx 6.91 \cdot 10^{14}\,\mathrm{Hz}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The lines of the Balmer series have $n_f = 2$, so set $n_f = 2$ and $n_i = 5$ in the **Rydberg formula**. Expressing the difference $\dfrac{1}{4} - \dfrac{1}{25}$ over the common denominator $100$ gives $\dfrac{21}{100}$. The formula gives the reciprocal of the wavelength, so $\lambda$ is obtained by taking the reciprocal of the product $R_H \cdot \dfrac{21}{100}$.

The wavelength is $434.1\,\mathrm{nm}$, between $380\,\mathrm{nm}$ and $750\,\mathrm{nm}$, so the line lies in the **visible** spectrum and is violet. The frequency follows from $c = \lambda f$ and is approximately $6.91 \cdot 10^{14}\,\mathrm{Hz}$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A line in the Balmer series is measured at wavelength $\lambda = 486\,\mathrm{nm}$ in the emission spectrum of hydrogen gas. Determine the number $n_i$ corresponding to the line. Use $R_H = 1.097 \cdot 10^{7}\,\mathrm{m^{-1}}$.

$$
\frac{1}{\lambda} = R_H \left( \frac{1}{4} - \frac{1}{n_i^2} \right)
\qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad
\frac{1}{n_i^2} = \frac{1}{4} - \frac{1}{\lambda R_H}
$$
$$
\lambda R_H = 4.86 \cdot 10^{-7} \cdot 1.097 \cdot 10^{7} \approx 5.331 ,
\qquad
\frac{1}{\lambda R_H} \approx 0.1876
$$
$$
\frac{1}{n_i^2} \approx 0.2500 - 0.1876 = 0.0624
\qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad
n_i^2 \approx 16.0
\qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad
n_i = 4
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The line belongs to the Balmer series, so set $n_f = 2$ in the **Rydberg formula**. The unknown is $n_i$. Divide the formula by $R_H$ and isolate the term $\dfrac{1}{n_i^2}$ by moving $\dfrac{1}{\lambda R_H}$ to the right-hand side.

The product $\lambda R_H$ is dimensionless because $\lambda$ is measured in meters and $R_H$ in $\mathrm{m^{-1}}$. Subtracting its reciprocal from $0.25$ gives $\dfrac{1}{n_i^2} \approx 0.0624$ and hence $n_i^2 \approx 16.0$. Since $n_i$ is a positive integer, the small difference between its calculated square and $16$ results from rounding the measurement. The line corresponds to $n_i = 4$. It is the blue $\mathrm{H}_\beta$ line.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Calculate the wavelength of the first line and the limiting wavelength for the Lyman series ($n_f = 1$), the Balmer series ($n_f = 2$), and the Paschen series ($n_f = 3$). Explain why lines in any one of these series do not overlap the other two series. Use $R_H = 1.097 \cdot 10^{7}\,\mathrm{m^{-1}}$.

$$
\text{first line: } n_i = n_f + 1 ,
\qquad
\text{limit: } n_i \to +\infty ,
\quad
\lambda_\infty = \frac{n_f^2}{R_H}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{Lyman:} \quad & \frac{1}{\lambda} = R_H \left( 1 - \frac{1}{4} \right) = \frac{3}{4} R_H , && \lambda = \frac{4}{3 R_H} \approx 121.5\,\mathrm{nm} , && \lambda_\infty = \frac{1}{R_H} \approx 91.2\,\mathrm{nm} \\[8pt]
\text{Balmer:} \quad & \frac{1}{\lambda} = R_H \left( \frac{1}{4} - \frac{1}{9} \right) = \frac{5}{36} R_H , && \lambda = \frac{36}{5 R_H} \approx 656.3\,\mathrm{nm} , && \lambda_\infty = \frac{4}{R_H} \approx 364.6\,\mathrm{nm} \\[8pt]
\text{Paschen:} \quad & \frac{1}{\lambda} = R_H \left( \frac{1}{9} - \frac{1}{16} \right) = \frac{7}{144} R_H , && \lambda = \frac{144}{7 R_H} \approx 1875\,\mathrm{nm} , && \lambda_\infty = \frac{9}{R_H} \approx 820.4\,\mathrm{nm}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

In each series, the line with the longest wavelength has the smallest difference $\dfrac{1}{n_f^2} - \dfrac{1}{n_i^2}$, which occurs for $n_i = n_f + 1$. As $n_i$ increases, the term $\dfrac{1}{n_i^2}$ approaches $0$, so $\dfrac{1}{\lambda}$ approaches $\dfrac{R_H}{n_f^2}$ and the wavelength approaches the limit $\lambda_\infty = \dfrac{n_f^2}{R_H}$, the shortest wavelength of the series.

The Lyman lines lie between $91.2\,\mathrm{nm}$ and $121.5\,\mathrm{nm}$, in the ultraviolet. The Balmer lines lie between $364.6\,\mathrm{nm}$ and $656.3\,\mathrm{nm}$, and the Paschen lines lie between $820.4\,\mathrm{nm}$ and $1875\,\mathrm{nm}$, in the infrared. The three intervals are separate because the limiting wavelength of each series is longer than the wavelength of the first line of the preceding series. For example, the Balmer limit, $364.6\,\mathrm{nm}$, is longer than $121.5\,\mathrm{nm}$. The three series therefore **do not overlap**. For series with larger $n_f$, this property no longer holds. For example, the first Paschen line, at $1875\,\mathrm{nm}$, lies beyond the limit for the series with $n_f = 4$, which is $1459\,\mathrm{nm}$.

</div>
