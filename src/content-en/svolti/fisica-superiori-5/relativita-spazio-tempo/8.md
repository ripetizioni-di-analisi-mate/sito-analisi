## Exercise 1

An astronaut leaves Earth for a star $3.0$ light-years away in Earth’s frame. She travels at $v = 0.60\,c$ and immediately returns at the same speed. Her twin brother stays on Earth. Neglect the acceleration phases and treat Earth as an inertial frame. Calculate the trip duration for the twin on Earth and for the astronaut, and their age difference on her return.

$$
\Delta t = \frac{2 \cdot 3.0\ \text{light-years}}{0.60\,c} = 10\ \text{years}
$$
$$
\gamma = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - 0.60^2}} = \frac{1}{0.80} = 1.25
$$
$$
\Delta t_0 = \frac{\Delta t}{\gamma} = \frac{10\ \text{years}}{1.25} = 8.0\ \text{years} ,
\qquad
\Delta t - \Delta t_0 = 2.0\ \text{years}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

In Earth’s frame the astronaut travels $3.0$ light-years on the outward leg and $3.0$ on the return, at speed $0.60\,c$. A light-year is the distance light travels in a year, so the trip lasts $\dfrac{6.0}{0.60} = 10$ years for the twin on Earth.

The astronaut is present at departure and at arrival at the star, so her clock measures the proper time for the outward leg. The same holds for the return leg. The calculation is made in Earth’s frame, which is inertial throughout the trip. In that frame the astronaut’s clock moves at speed $0.60\,c$ on both legs. By **time dilation**, the total time recorded on board is $\dfrac{10\ \text{years}}{1.25} = 8.0$ years. When the astronaut returns, she is therefore **2.0 years younger** than her twin.

Reversing the calculation in the astronaut’s frame is invalid. At turnaround she changes inertial frames, so the time-dilation formula cannot be applied to the whole trip in a single astronaut frame. This resolves the **twin paradox**.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A spacecraft leaves Earth for a station at rest relative to Earth and $6.0$ light-years away as measured in Earth’s frame. The spacecraft travels at $v = 0.80\,c$, reaches the station, immediately reverses direction, and returns to Earth at the same speed. One of two twin sisters is on board, while the other remains on Earth, which is treated as an inertial frame. Neglect the acceleration phases.

1. Calculate the duration of the round trip for the twin on Earth and the traveling twin, and find their age difference upon reunion.
2. Calculate the Earth–station distance measured by the traveling twin on the outward leg, and verify that the time for that leg measured on board agrees with the first part.
3. Draw the Minkowski diagram for Earth’s frame, with $x$ and $ct$ in light-years, including the worldline of the spacecraft. Determine the Earth times that the traveler considers simultaneous with turnaround before and after reversing direction.
4. Explain how the traveling twin also concludes that $15$ years have passed on Earth.

$$
\gamma = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - 0.80^2}} = \frac{1}{0.60} = \frac{5}{3}
$$
$$
\Delta t = \frac{2 \cdot 6.0\ \text{light-years}}{0.80\,c} = 15\ \text{years} ,
\qquad
\Delta t_0 = \frac{\Delta t}{\gamma} = \frac{3}{5} \cdot 15\ \text{years} = 9.0\ \text{years} ,
\qquad
\Delta t - \Delta t_0 = 6.0\ \text{years}
$$
$$
L = \frac{L_0}{\gamma} = \frac{3}{5} \cdot 6.0\ \text{light-years} = 3.6\ \text{light-years} ,
\qquad
\Delta t_{0,\text{outbound}} = \frac{3.6\ \text{light-years}}{0.80\,c} = 4.5\ \text{years}
$$
$$
G: \ x = 6.0 ,\ ct = 7.5
\qquad
\begin{aligned}
\text{outbound: } ct &= 7.5 - 0.80 \cdot 6.0 = 2.7 \\[6pt]
\text{return: } ct &= 7.5 + 0.80 \cdot 6.0 = 12.3
\end{aligned}
$$
$$
\frac{4.5\ \text{years}}{\gamma} = 2.7\ \text{years} ,
\qquad
2.7 + (12.3 - 2.7) + 2.7 = 2.7 + 9.6 + 2.7 = 15\ \text{years}
$$

From turnaround $G$, the dashed lines with slopes $0.8$ and $-0.8$ intersect Earth’s worldline at times $2.7$ and $12.3$ years"}

<div class="spiegazione">

**Part one.** In Earth’s frame the spacecraft covers $12$ light-years at speed $0.80\,c$, so the twin on Earth waits $15$ years. The traveling twin is present at both ends of each leg, so her clock measures proper time. By **time dilation**, calculated in Earth’s inertial frame, this time totals $\dfrac{15}{\gamma} = 9.0$ years. On her return the traveler is therefore **6.0 years younger**.

**Part two.** The distance of $6.0$ light-years is measured in the frame where Earth and the station are at rest, so it is a proper length. On the outward leg the traveler sees Earth and the station move at speed $0.80\,c$, so by **length contraction** she measures $3.6$ light-years. The station approaches her at speed $0.80\,c$, so for her the outward leg lasts $4.5$ years. The return leg is symmetric, and the total of $9.0$ years agrees with the result in the first part.

**Part three.** In Earth’s frame, turnaround $G$ takes place at $x = 6.0$ light-years at time $t = 7.5$ years, halfway through the $15$-year trip, so in the diagram it has $ct = 7.5$ light-years. In the **Minkowski diagram**, the events simultaneous with $G$ for an observer moving at velocity $v$ lie on the line through $G$ parallel to the observer’s $x'$-axis. The line has slope $\beta = 0.80$. On the outward leg this line intersects the $ct$-axis, Earth’s worldline, at $ct = 7.5 - 0.80 \cdot 6.0 = 2.7$ light-years, corresponding to Earth time $t = 2.7$ years. On the return leg the velocity is $-0.80\,c$, the line has slope $-0.80$, and it intersects the $ct$-axis at time $t = 12.3$ years. By the **relativity of simultaneity**, the Earth time simultaneous with turnaround therefore changes from $2.7$ to $12.3$ years when the traveler changes frames.

**Part four.** On each leg the traveler is in an inertial frame relative to which Earth’s clock moves at speed $0.80\,c$. Thus, by time dilation, she calculates that during $4.5$ years on board, Earth’s clock advances by only $\dfrac{4.5}{\gamma} = 2.7$ years. The two legs account for a total of $5.4$ Earth years. At turnaround, however, the change of frame shifts the Earth time considered simultaneous from $2.7$ to $12.3$ years, adding $9.6$ years. Summing the three contributions, the traveler obtains **15 years**, the same as her twin on Earth. The situation is asymmetric because the traveler changes inertial frames while the twin on Earth stays in the same one.

</div>
