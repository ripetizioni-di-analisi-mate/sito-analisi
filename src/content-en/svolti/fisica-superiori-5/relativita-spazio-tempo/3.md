## Exercise 1

A spacecraft moves away from Earth at speed $v = 0.50\,c$ and launches a probe forward at speed $u' = 0.60\,c$ relative to the spacecraft. Calculate the probe’s speed relative to Earth and compare it with the result of Galilean addition.

$$
u = \frac{u' + v}{1 + \dfrac{u'\, v}{c^2}}
$$
$$
u = \frac{0.60\,c + 0.50\,c}{1 + 0.60 \cdot 0.50} = \frac{1.10\,c}{1.30} \approx 0.846\,c
$$
$$
u_{\text{gal}} = u' + v = 1.10\,c
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Orient the $x$-axis along the spacecraft’s direction of travel. Take $S$ to be Earth’s frame and $S'$ to be the spacecraft frame, which moves relative to $S$ at velocity $v = 0.50\,c$. The probe has velocity $u' = 0.60\,c$ relative to $S'$ in the same direction, so both velocities are positive. By **relativistic velocity addition**, the probe’s velocity relative to Earth is the sum $u' + v$ divided by $1 + \dfrac{u'\, v}{c^2}$.

The product $\dfrac{u'\, v}{c^2}$ is $0.60 \cdot 0.50 = 0.30$ because the velocities are expressed in units of $c$. The probe thus moves relative to Earth at **approximately** $0.85\,c$, less than $c$. Galilean addition would instead give $1.10\,c$, a speed greater than that of light and incompatible with Einstein’s postulates.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Two spacecraft $A$ and $B$ approach Earth from opposite directions, each at speed $0.80\,c$ relative to Earth. Spacecraft $A$ approaches from the left and $B$ from the right. Calculate the velocity of $B$ relative to spacecraft $A$.

::grafico{assi=0 uguale=1 punti="0,0,pieno,Earth;-4,0,pieno,A;4,0,pieno,B" vettori="-4,0.5,-2.4,0.5;4,0.5,2.4,0.5" etichette="-3.6,1.15,0.80 c;2.7,1.15,0.80 c" linee="-5,-0.5 5,-0.5" xmin=-5.4 xmax=5.4 ymin=-0.9 ymax=1.6 titolo="The two spacecraft approach Earth from opposite directions, each at speed $0.80\,c$. The $x$-axis points from $A$ toward $B$"}

$$
v = +0.80\,c ,
\qquad
u = -0.80\,c
$$
$$
u = \frac{u' + v}{1 + \dfrac{u'\, v}{c^2}}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
u' = \frac{u - v}{1 - \dfrac{u\, v}{c^2}}
$$
$$
u' = \frac{-0.80\,c - 0.80\,c}{1 - (-0.80) \cdot 0.80} = \frac{-1.60\,c}{1.64} \approx -0.976\,c
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Orient the $x$-axis from $A$ toward $B$. Frame $S$ is Earth’s frame, and frame $S'$ is that of spacecraft $A$, which moves to the right, so $v = +0.80\,c$. Spacecraft $B$ moves to the left, so its velocity relative to Earth is $u = -0.80\,c$. The unknown is the velocity $u'$ of $B$ relative to $A$.

The **relativistic velocity-addition** formula gives $u$ in terms of $u'$. To find $u'$, use the same formula with the roles of the frames interchanged. Earth moves relative to $A$ at velocity $-v$, so replace $v$ with $-v$ and interchange $u$ and $u'$. The denominator is $1 - (-0.80)(0.80) = 1 + 0.64 = 1.64$.

The result is $u' \approx -0.976\,c$. The minus sign means that for the crew of $A$, spacecraft $B$ approaches from right to left at a speed of **approximately** $0.98\,c$. Galilean addition would give $1.60\,c$, whereas relativistic addition gives a speed less than $c$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A spacecraft moves at velocity $v = 0.50\,c$ to the right relative to a space station. It emits a pulse of light and a beam of electrons to the left, that is, backward. The electrons have velocity $0.90\,c$ relative to the spacecraft. Calculate the velocities of the light pulse and the electrons relative to the station.

$$
u'_{\text{light}} = -c ,
\qquad
u'_{\text{el}} = -0.90\,c ,
\qquad
v = +0.50\,c
$$
$$
u_{\text{light}} = \frac{-c + 0.50\,c}{1 + \dfrac{(-c) \cdot 0.50\,c}{c^2}} = \frac{-0.50\,c}{0.50} = -c
$$
$$
u_{\text{el}} = \frac{-0.90\,c + 0.50\,c}{1 + (-0.90) \cdot 0.50} = \frac{-0.40\,c}{0.55} \approx -0.727\,c
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

With the $x$-axis pointing to the right, the velocity of the spacecraft relative to the station is $v = +0.50\,c$. The pulse and electrons are emitted backward, so their velocities relative to the spacecraft are negative. To apply **relativistic velocity addition**, substitute these values with their signs into the formula.

For the light, the numerator is $-0.50\,c$ and the denominator is $1 - 0.50 = 0.50$, so the pulse has velocity $-c$ relative to the station. The pulse therefore travels to the left at **exactly** $c$, as required by the postulate of invariance of the speed of light. Galilean addition would have given $0.50\,c$.

For the electrons the numerator is $-0.40\,c$ and the denominator is $1 - 0.45 = 0.55$, so their velocity relative to the station is approximately $-0.73\,c$. The electrons also move to the left relative to the station, with a speed greater than the Galilean value $0.40\,c$.

</div>

## Exercise 4

A spacecraft moves relative to Earth at velocity $v = 0.70\,c$ and launches a probe forward. Determine the velocity the probe must have relative to the spacecraft for its velocity relative to Earth to be $0.90\,c$.

$$
u = \frac{u' + v}{1 + \dfrac{u'\, v}{c^2}}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
u \left( 1 + \frac{u'\, v}{c^2} \right) = u' + v
$$
$$
u'\left( \frac{u\, v}{c^2} - 1 \right) = v - u
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
u' = \frac{u - v}{1 - \dfrac{u\, v}{c^2}}
$$
$$
u' = \frac{0.90\,c - 0.70\,c}{1 - 0.90 \cdot 0.70} = \frac{0.20\,c}{0.37} \approx 0.54\,c
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The unknown is the probe’s velocity $u'$ relative to the spacecraft, while $u = 0.90\,c$ and $v = 0.70\,c$ are known. Multiply both sides of the **relativistic velocity-addition** formula by the denominator $1 + \dfrac{u'\, v}{c^2}$, which is positive. Move the terms containing $u'$ to the left and the remaining terms to the right, then factor out $u'$.

Dividing by $\dfrac{u\, v}{c^2} - 1$ and changing the signs of the numerator and denominator gives $u' = \dfrac{u - v}{1 - \dfrac{u\, v}{c^2}}$. Substituting the data shows that the probe must move at **approximately** $0.54\,c$ relative to the spacecraft, whereas Galilean addition would give only $0.20\,c$.

As a check, combine $0.54\,c$ and $0.70\,c$ using the direct formula. Using the more precise value $u' = \dfrac{20}{37}\,c \approx 0.5405\,c$ gives $\dfrac{0.5405 + 0.70}{1 + 0.5405 \cdot 0.70}\,c = \dfrac{1.2405}{1.3784}\,c \approx 0.900\,c$, the required velocity.

</div>
