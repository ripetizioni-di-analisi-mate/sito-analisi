## Exercise 1

In frame $S$, two events are separated by $\Delta x = 2400\,\mathrm{m}$ and $\Delta t = 10\,\mu\mathrm{s}$. Calculate the interval between the events and determine its type. Then find the velocity of an object moving in a straight line at constant speed that is present at both events, and the time its clock measures between them. Use $c = 3.00 \cdot 10^{8}\,\mathrm{m/s}$.

$$
c\, \Delta t = 3.00 \cdot 10^{8} \cdot 1.0 \cdot 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{m} = 3000\,\mathrm{m}
$$
$$
(\Delta s)^2 = (c\, \Delta t)^2 - (\Delta x)^2 = \left( 3000^2 - 2400^2 \right) \mathrm{m^2} = 3.24 \cdot 10^{6}\,\mathrm{m^2} > 0 ,
\qquad
\Delta s = 1800\,\mathrm{m}
$$
$$
v = \frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t} = \frac{2400}{1.0 \cdot 10^{-5}}\,\mathrm{m/s} = 2.4 \cdot 10^{8}\,\mathrm{m/s} = 0.80\,c
$$
$$
\Delta t_0 = \frac{\Delta s}{c} = \frac{1800}{3.00 \cdot 10^{8}}\,\mathrm{s} = 6.0\,\mu\mathrm{s}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

To compare time with distance, multiply $\Delta t$ by $c$ to obtain $c\,\Delta t = 3000\,\mathrm{m}$. The **interval** is the difference between the squares of $c\,\Delta t$ and $\Delta x$. This difference is positive, so the interval is **timelike** and equals $\Delta s = 1800\,\mathrm{m}$. In other words, in $10\,\mu\mathrm{s}$ light travels farther than the $2400\,\mathrm{m}$ separating the two locations, so an object moving more slowly than light can be present at both events.

An object moving at constant velocity from the first event to the second covers $2400\,\mathrm{m}$ in $10\,\mu\mathrm{s}$, so its velocity is $0.80\,c$. In the frame of this object the two events occur at the same point, so its clock measures the **proper time** $\Delta t_0 = \dfrac{\Delta s}{c} = 6.0\,\mu\mathrm{s}$.

Time dilation gives the same result. For $\beta = 0.80$ we have $\gamma = \dfrac{5}{3}$, and the proper time is $\dfrac{\Delta t}{\gamma} = \dfrac{3}{5} \cdot 10\,\mu\mathrm{s} = 6.0\,\mu\mathrm{s}$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

In frame $S$, event $A$ occurs at the origin at time $t = 0$. Events $B$, $C$, and $D$ have coordinates
$$
B:\ x = 900\,\mathrm{m},\ t = 2.0\,\mu\mathrm{s} ;
\qquad
C:\ x = 450\,\mathrm{m},\ t = 1.5\,\mu\mathrm{s} ;
\qquad
D:\ x = 300\,\mathrm{m},\ t = 4.0\,\mu\mathrm{s} .
$$
Classify the interval between $A$ and each of the other events, and determine which events can be effects of $A$. For the timelike interval, calculate the proper time. For the spacelike interval, calculate the distance in the frame in which the events are simultaneous. Use $c = 3.00 \cdot 10^{8}\,\mathrm{m/s}$.

$$
c\, t_B = 600\,\mathrm{m} ,
\qquad
c\, t_C = 450\,\mathrm{m} ,
\qquad
c\, t_D = 1200\,\mathrm{m}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
(\Delta s_{AB})^2 &= \left( 600^2 - 900^2 \right) \mathrm{m^2} = -4.5 \cdot 10^{5}\,\mathrm{m^2} < 0 \\[6pt]
(\Delta s_{AC})^2 &= \left( 450^2 - 450^2 \right) \mathrm{m^2} = 0 \\[6pt]
(\Delta s_{AD})^2 &= \left( 1200^2 - 300^2 \right) \mathrm{m^2} = 1.35 \cdot 10^{6}\,\mathrm{m^2} > 0
\end{aligned}
$$
$$
\Delta t_0 = \frac{\sqrt{1.35 \cdot 10^{6}}}{3.00 \cdot 10^{8}}\,\mathrm{s} \approx \frac{1162}{3.00 \cdot 10^{8}}\,\mathrm{s} \approx 3.9\,\mu\mathrm{s} ,
\qquad
d_{AB} = \sqrt{900^2 - 600^2}\,\mathrm{m} \approx 671\,\mathrm{m}
$$

::grafico{f="x" area="14;abs(x);-2;14" segmenti="0,0,-2,2" punti="9,6,pieno,B;4.5,4.5,pieno,C;3,12,pieno,D;0,0,pieno,A" xmin=-2 xmax=14 ymin=0 ymax=14 uguale=1 nomi="x,ct" legenda="ct = x" titolo="Events on a Minkowski diagram, with $x$ and $ct$ in hundreds of meters. Event $D$ lies in the future of $A$, $C$ on the light cone of $A$, and $B$ in the elsewhere region"}

<div class="spiegazione">

For each event, calculate $c\,t$, the distance light travels in the time separating that event from $A$, and compare it with the distance $x$. The sign of the difference of the squares determines the **type of interval**. For $B$, light emitted at $A$ travels only $600\,\mathrm{m}$ of the required $900\,\mathrm{m}$, so the interval is **spacelike**. For $C$ the distances are equal, so the interval is **lightlike**, and a flash emitted at $A$ arrives exactly at $C$. For $D$, light travels $1200\,\mathrm{m}$, more than the required $300\,\mathrm{m}$, so the interval is **timelike**.

An event can be an effect of $A$ only if a signal emitted at $A$ can reach it, that is, if it lies in the future of $A$ or on its **light cone**. Hence $C$ and $D$ can be effects of $A$, whereas $B$ cannot. This result holds in all inertial frames because the interval is invariant.

For $D$, the proper time is $\dfrac{\Delta s}{c}$, and $\sqrt{1.35 \cdot 10^{6}}\,\mathrm{m} \approx 1162\,\mathrm{m}$. Thus a clock moving at constant velocity from $A$ to $D$ measures approximately $3.9\,\mu\mathrm{s}$. For $B$, there is a frame in which $A$ and $B$ are simultaneous, and in that frame the distance between them is $\sqrt{(\Delta x)^2 - (c\,\Delta t)^2} \approx 671\,\mathrm{m}$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

In frame $S$, two events are separated by $\Delta x = 3600\,\mathrm{m}$ and $\Delta t = 15\,\mu\mathrm{s}$. In a second inertial frame $S'$ moving along the $x$-axis, their spatial separation is $\Delta x' = 1200\,\mathrm{m}$. Without determining the velocity of $S'$, calculate the time interval between the events in $S'$. Then determine the frame in which the two events occur at the same point and find the time interval in that frame. Use $c = 3.00 \cdot 10^{8}\,\mathrm{m/s}$.

$$
c\, \Delta t = 3.00 \cdot 10^{8} \cdot 1.5 \cdot 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{m} = 4500\,\mathrm{m} ,
\qquad
(\Delta s)^2 = \left( 4500^2 - 3600^2 \right) \mathrm{m^2} = 7.29 \cdot 10^{6}\,\mathrm{m^2}
$$
$$
(c\, \Delta t')^2 - (\Delta x')^2 = (\Delta s)^2
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
c\, \Delta t' = \sqrt{(\Delta s)^2 + (\Delta x')^2} = \sqrt{7.29 \cdot 10^{6} + 1.44 \cdot 10^{6}}\,\mathrm{m} \approx 2955\,\mathrm{m}
$$
$$
\Delta t' = \frac{2955}{3.00 \cdot 10^{8}}\,\mathrm{s} \approx 9.85\,\mu\mathrm{s}
$$
$$
v = \frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t} = \frac{3600}{1.5 \cdot 10^{-5}}\,\mathrm{m/s} = 2.4 \cdot 10^{8}\,\mathrm{m/s} ,
\qquad
\Delta t_0 = \frac{\Delta s}{c} = \frac{2700}{3.00 \cdot 10^{8}}\,\mathrm{s} = 9.0\,\mu\mathrm{s}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

By the theorem on the **invariant interval**, the difference $(c\,\Delta t')^2 - (\Delta x')^2$ in $S'$ equals the difference calculated in $S$, which is $7.29 \cdot 10^{6}\,\mathrm{m^2}$. Since $\Delta x'$ is known, we can find $c\,\Delta t'$ and then $\Delta t'$, approximately $9.9\,\mu\mathrm{s}$. The velocity of $S'$ is unnecessary because the interval is the same in every inertial frame.

The interval is positive and therefore timelike, so there is a frame in which the two events occur at the same point. This is the frame of an object moving at constant velocity from the first event to the second, with velocity $\dfrac{\Delta x}{\Delta t} = 2.4 \cdot 10^{8}\,\mathrm{m/s} = 0.80\,c$. In this frame $\Delta x_0 = 0$, so $(c\,\Delta t_0)^2 = (\Delta s)^2$, and the proper time is $\Delta t_0 = \dfrac{2700\,\mathrm{m}}{c} = 9.0\,\mu\mathrm{s}$.

The proper time is the shortest of the time intervals measured in different frames because $(c\,\Delta t)^2 = (\Delta s)^2 + (\Delta x)^2$ is smallest for $\Delta x = 0$. Indeed, $9.0\,\mu\mathrm{s}$ is less than both $9.85\,\mu\mathrm{s}$ and $15\,\mu\mathrm{s}$.

</div>
