## Exercise 1

A spacecraft of length $120\,\mathrm{m}$ as measured by its crew passes a space station at speed $v = 0.80\,c$. Calculate the length of the spacecraft as measured by the station and the time, in the station frame, that the spacecraft takes to pass one of the station windows. Use $c = 3.00 \cdot 10^{8}\,\mathrm{m/s}$.

::grafico{assi=0 uguale=1 poligoni="0,0 6,0 7,0.6 6,1.2 0,1.2" vettori="2.5,1.9,4.5,1.9" linee="-1,-0.6 9,-0.6" punti="3,-0.6,pieno,P" etichette="3.3,2.2,v;2.8,0.4,L" xmin=-1.3 xmax=9.3 ymin=-1.1 ymax=2.5 titolo="The spacecraft passes the station window at speed $v$. The window is at $P$, and the length of the spacecraft in the station frame is $L = \dfrac{L_0}{\gamma}$"}

$$
L = \frac{L_0}{\gamma} = L_0 \sqrt{1 - \frac{v^2}{c^2}} ,
\qquad
\Delta t = \frac{L}{v}
$$
$$
L = 120\,\mathrm{m} \cdot \sqrt{1 - 0.80^2} = 120\,\mathrm{m} \cdot 0.60 = 72\,\mathrm{m}
$$
$$
\Delta t = \frac{72}{0.80 \cdot 3.00 \cdot 10^{8}}\,\mathrm{s} = 3.0 \cdot 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{s}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The length $L_0 = 120\,\mathrm{m}$ is measured by the crew in the rest frame of the spacecraft, so it is the **proper length**. In the station frame the spacecraft moves in the direction of its length, so by **length contraction** the station measures $L = \dfrac{L_0}{\gamma}$. With $\beta = 0.80$, the square root $\sqrt{1 - \beta^2}$ equals $0.60$, and the length measured by the station is $72\,\mathrm{m}$.

In the station frame the spacecraft has length $72\,\mathrm{m}$ and moves at speed $v$, so the interval between its front and rear passing the window is $\dfrac{L}{v}$, equal to $0.30\,\mu\mathrm{s}$. Both events occur at the same point $P$ on the station, so this interval is a proper time for the station.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A rod of length $1.00\,\mathrm{m}$ in its rest frame moves along its length relative to a laboratory. Determine its speed if its length measured in the laboratory is $50\,\mathrm{cm}$. Use $c = 3.00 \cdot 10^{8}\,\mathrm{m/s}$.

$$
\frac{L}{L_0} = \sqrt{1 - \beta^2}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
1 - \beta^2 = \left( \frac{L}{L_0} \right)^2
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\beta = \sqrt{1 - \left( \frac{L}{L_0} \right)^2}
$$
$$
\beta = \sqrt{1 - 0.50^2} = \sqrt{0.75} = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} \approx 0.866
$$
$$
v = \beta c \approx 0.866 \cdot 3.00 \cdot 10^{8}\,\mathrm{m/s} \approx 2.60 \cdot 10^{8}\,\mathrm{m/s}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The proper length is $L_0 = 1.00\,\mathrm{m}$, measured where the rod is at rest, and the length measured in the laboratory is $L = 0.50\,\mathrm{m}$. From the **length-contraction** formula, the ratio $\dfrac{L}{L_0}$ equals $\sqrt{1 - \beta^2}$, the reciprocal of the Lorentz factor. Thus a rod contracted to half its proper length corresponds to $\gamma = 2$.

Squaring gives $1 - \beta^2 = 0.25$, so $\beta^2 = 0.75$. Since $\beta$ is positive, $\beta = \dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$. The rod therefore travels at **approximately** $2.60 \cdot 10^{8}\,\mathrm{m/s}$, or $87\%$ of the speed of light.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A star is $4.2$ light-years from Earth as measured in Earth’s frame, and can be considered at rest relative to Earth. A spacecraft leaves Earth and travels to the star at constant speed $v = 0.90\,c$. Neglect the acceleration and braking phases.

- Calculate the duration of the trip for observers on Earth.
- Calculate the distance between Earth and the star as measured by the crew.
- Calculate the duration of the trip for the crew, first using length contraction and then using time dilation.

$$
\gamma = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - 0.90^2}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{0.19}} \approx 2.294
$$
$$
\Delta t = \frac{L_0}{v} = \frac{4.2\ \text{light-years}}{0.90\,c} \approx 4.67\ \text{years}
$$
$$
L = \frac{L_0}{\gamma} = \frac{4.2}{2.294}\ \text{light-years} \approx 1.83\ \text{light-years}
$$
$$
\Delta t_0 = \frac{L}{v} = \frac{1.83\ \text{light-years}}{0.90\,c} \approx 2.0\ \text{years} ,
\qquad
\Delta t_0 = \frac{\Delta t}{\gamma} = \frac{4.67}{2.294}\ \text{years} \approx 2.0\ \text{years}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

A light-year is the distance light travels in one year, so a distance of $4.2$ light-years at speed $0.90\,c$ takes $\dfrac{4.2}{0.90}$ years to cover. For observers on Earth, the trip thus lasts **about 4.7 years**. With these units, there is no need to convert to meters and seconds.

The distance of $L_0 = 4.2$ light-years is measured in Earth’s frame, in which Earth and the star are at rest, so it is a **proper length**. In the spacecraft frame Earth and the star move at speed $v$ along the line joining them. By **length contraction**, the crew measures a shorter distance of $\dfrac{L_0}{\gamma} \approx 1.8$ light-years.

For the crew, the star approaches at speed $v$ and must cover $1.83$ light-years, so the journey lasts about $2.0$ years. The same result follows in Earth’s frame. Departure and arrival both occur at the location of the spacecraft, so the interval measured by the onboard clock is a proper time. By **time dilation**, it equals $\dfrac{\Delta t}{\gamma}$, again **about 2.0 years**. The two methods agree because they describe the same journey in different frames.

</div>
