## Exercise 1

A light clock consists of two parallel mirrors a distance $d = 1.2\,\mathrm{m}$ apart. A light pulse travels back and forth between them. The clock is aboard a spacecraft moving at velocity $v = 0.80\,c$ relative to Earth, perpendicular to the line joining the mirrors. Calculate the duration of one round trip of the pulse in the spacecraft frame and in Earth’s frame. Use $c = 3.00 \cdot 10^{8}\,\mathrm{m/s}$.

::grafico{assi=0 uguale=1 linee="-0.6,0 0.6,0;-0.6,3 0.6,3;2.4,0 3.6,0;4.4,3 5.6,3;6.4,0 7.6,0" vettori="0,0.05,0,2.95;3,0.05,4.97,2.95;5.03,2.95,7,0.05;3.4,-0.75,6.6,-0.75" segmenti="5,0,5,3" etichette="0.2,1.4,d;5.15,1.4,d;3.4,1.75,ℓ;6.2,1.75,ℓ;4.75,-1.2,v Δt" xmin=-1 xmax=8 ymin=-1.5 ymax=3.5 legenda=0 titolo="The light clock in the spacecraft frame (left) and in Earth’s frame (right), where the pulse travels along two sloping sides, each of length $\ell = \dfrac{c\,\Delta t}{2}$"}

$$
\Delta t_0 = \frac{2d}{c} ,
\qquad
\gamma = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - \dfrac{v^2}{c^2}}} ,
\qquad
\Delta t = \gamma\, \Delta t_0
$$
$$
\Delta t_0 = \frac{2 \cdot 1.2}{3.00 \cdot 10^{8}}\,\mathrm{s} = 8.0 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\mathrm{s}
$$
$$
\gamma = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - 0.80^2}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{0.36}} = \frac{1}{0.60} = \frac{5}{3} \approx 1.67
$$
$$
\Delta t = \frac{5}{3} \cdot 8.0 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\mathrm{s} \approx 1.3 \cdot 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{s}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

In the spacecraft frame the clock is at rest, so the pulse departs and returns at the same point on the lower mirror. The interval measured on board between these events is therefore a **proper time**. It equals the time light takes to cover the distance $2d$ at speed $c$, namely $8.0\,\mathrm{ns}$.

In Earth’s frame the clock moves at speed $v$, and the two events occur at different points. By **time dilation**, the interval measured on Earth is the proper time multiplied by the Lorentz factor. With $\beta = 0.80$ we have $1 - \beta^2 = 0.36$, whose square root is $0.60$, so $\gamma = \dfrac{5}{3}$. On Earth the round trip therefore lasts **about 13 ns**, longer than the proper time.

Use the figure as a check. In $13.3\,\mathrm{ns}$ light travels $c\,\Delta t = 4.0\,\mathrm{m}$, so each sloping side is $2.0\,\mathrm{m}$ long. In the meantime the clock advances by $v\,\Delta t = 3.2\,\mathrm{m}$, or $1.6\,\mathrm{m}$ for each side. The right triangle with legs $1.2\,\mathrm{m}$ and $1.6\,\mathrm{m}$ has hypotenuse $\sqrt{1.44 + 2.56}\,\mathrm{m} = 2.0\,\mathrm{m}$, as expected.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Charged pions are unstable particles with a mean lifetime of $\tau = 2.6 \cdot 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{s}$, measured in the pion rest frame. In an accelerator, a beam of pions moves at speed $v = 0.95\,c$. Calculate the mean lifetime measured in the laboratory and the mean distance the pions travel in the laboratory before decaying. Compare the result with the distance that would be obtained by neglecting time dilation. Use $c = 3.00 \cdot 10^{8}\,\mathrm{m/s}$.

$$
\gamma = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - 0.95^2}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{0.0975}} \approx 3.20
$$
$$
\Delta t = \gamma\, \tau = 3.20 \cdot 2.6 \cdot 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{s} \approx 8.3 \cdot 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{s}
$$
$$
d = v\, \Delta t = 0.95 \cdot 3.00 \cdot 10^{8} \cdot 8.33 \cdot 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{m} \approx 24\,\mathrm{m}
$$
$$
d_{\text{class}} = v\, \tau = 0.95 \cdot 3.00 \cdot 10^{8} \cdot 2.6 \cdot 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{m} \approx 7.4\,\mathrm{m}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The mean lifetime $\tau$ is measured in the pion rest frame, where production and decay occur at the same point, so it is a **proper time**. In the laboratory the pion moves, so by **time dilation** its mean lifetime is $\gamma\, \tau$. Calculate the Lorentz factor using $1 - 0.95^2 = 1 - 0.9025 = 0.0975$, which gives approximately $3.20$. Thus the mean lifetime in the laboratory is $83\,\mathrm{ns}$.

In the laboratory the pion moves at constant speed $v$ for a mean time $\gamma\, \tau$, traveling **about 24 m** on average. If time dilation were neglected, multiplying $v$ by the proper time $\tau$ would give approximately $7.4\,\mathrm{m}$, a distance smaller by a factor of $\gamma$. Accelerator measurements do in fact show the longer distances predicted by relativity.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Determine the speed, in units of $c$ and in meters per second, at which a clock must move relative to Earth so that while $60$ minutes elapse on Earth, the clock records $20$ minutes. Then calculate how many minutes the clock records during one Earth day. Use $c = 3.00 \cdot 10^{8}\,\mathrm{m/s}$.

$$
\Delta t = \gamma\, \Delta t_0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\gamma = \frac{\Delta t}{\Delta t_0} = \frac{60\,\mathrm{min}}{20\,\mathrm{min}} = 3
$$
$$
\frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - \beta^2}} = \gamma
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
1 - \beta^2 = \frac{1}{\gamma^2}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\beta = \sqrt{1 - \frac{1}{\gamma^2}}
$$
$$
\beta = \sqrt{1 - \frac{1}{9}} = \sqrt{\frac{8}{9}} = \frac{2\sqrt{2}}{3} \approx 0.943 ,
\qquad
v = \beta c \approx 2.83 \cdot 10^{8}\,\mathrm{m/s}
$$
$$
\Delta t_0 = \frac{\Delta t}{\gamma} = \frac{1440\,\mathrm{min}}{3} = 480\,\mathrm{min}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The $20$ minutes are recorded by the moving clock, which is at the same point in its own frame at the start and end of the interval. This is therefore the **proper time** $\Delta t_0$, while the $60$ minutes are the interval $\Delta t$ measured on Earth. From the **time-dilation** formula, the Lorentz factor is the ratio of the two intervals, giving $\gamma = 3$.

To find $\beta$, square the definition of $\gamma$ and take the reciprocal, giving $1 - \beta^2 = \dfrac{1}{\gamma^2} = \dfrac{1}{9}$. Then $\beta^2 = \dfrac{8}{9}$, and because $\beta$ is positive, $\beta = \dfrac{2\sqrt{2}}{3} \approx 0.943$. Thus the speed is **approximately** $2.83 \cdot 10^{8}\,\mathrm{m/s}$, less than $c$, as required.

A day is $24 \cdot 60 = 1440$ minutes. The Lorentz factor is unchanged because it depends only on the speed, so the moving clock records one-third of that time, or $480$ minutes, equal to $8$ hours.

</div>
