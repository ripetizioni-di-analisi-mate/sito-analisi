## Exercise 1

Muons are produced in the upper atmosphere at an altitude of $h = 9.0\,\mathrm{km}$ and move vertically downward toward the ground at speed $v = 0.998\,c$. A muon at rest has a mean lifetime $\tau = 2.2\,\mu\mathrm{s}$. Of an initial group of muons, after a proper time $t$, the fraction $e^{-\frac{t}{\tau}}$ survives.

- Calculate the duration of the descent in Earth’s frame and in the muon frame.
- Calculate the thickness of atmosphere crossed, as measured in the muon frame.
- Calculate the fraction of muons that reach the ground and the fraction predicted if time dilation were neglected.

Use $c = 3.00 \cdot 10^{8}\,\mathrm{m/s}$.

$$
\gamma = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - 0.998^2}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{0.003996}} \approx 15.8
$$
$$
\Delta t = \frac{h}{v} = \frac{9000}{0.998 \cdot 3.00 \cdot 10^{8}}\,\mathrm{s} \approx 3.01 \cdot 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{s} = 30.1\,\mu\mathrm{s}
$$
$$
\Delta t_0 = \frac{\Delta t}{\gamma} = \frac{30.1\,\mu\mathrm{s}}{15.8} \approx 1.90\,\mu\mathrm{s}
$$
$$
h' = \frac{h}{\gamma} = \frac{9000\,\mathrm{m}}{15.8} \approx 570\,\mathrm{m}
$$
$$
e^{-\frac{\Delta t_0}{\tau}} = e^{-\frac{1.90}{2.2}} = e^{-0.864} \approx 0.42 ,
\qquad
e^{-\frac{\Delta t}{\tau}} = e^{-\frac{30.1}{2.2}} = e^{-13.7} \approx 1.2 \cdot 10^{-6}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

To calculate the Lorentz factor, use $1 - 0.998^2 = 1 - 0.996004 = 0.003996$, whose square root is about $0.0632$, so $\gamma \approx 15.8$. In Earth’s frame the muons travel $9.0\,\mathrm{km}$ at constant speed $v$, and the descent takes about $30\,\mu\mathrm{s}$.

The production of a muon and its arrival at the ground both occur at the position of the muon. In the muon’s frame the interval between the events is therefore a **proper time**. By **time dilation** it equals the interval measured on Earth divided by $\gamma$, approximately $1.9\,\mu\mathrm{s}$, less than one mean lifetime.

In the muon frame, Earth moves upward at speed $v$, and the thickness of the atmosphere, whose proper length in Earth’s frame is $9.0\,\mathrm{km}$, is contracted. By **length contraction**, the ground is only about $570\,\mathrm{m}$ from the muon when it forms. Moving at speed $v$, the ground reaches the muon in $\dfrac{570}{0.998 \cdot 3.00 \cdot 10^{8}}\,\mathrm{s} \approx 1.9\,\mu\mathrm{s}$, the same proper time as before.

The decay law uses proper time because the mean lifetime is measured in the muon rest frame. Thus **about 42%** of the muons reach the ground. If Earth time were used, the exponent would be approximately $13.7$, and about $1.2$ muons in every million would reach the ground. The muon flux measured at ground level agrees with the relativistic prediction.

</div>

## Exercise 2

In a laboratory, an unstable particle is observed to travel an average distance of $12\,\mathrm{m}$ before decaying. Its mean lifetime in the particle rest frame is $\tau = 25\,\mathrm{ns}$. Determine the particle’s speed in the laboratory. Use $c = 3.00 \cdot 10^{8}\,\mathrm{m/s}$.

$$
d = v\, \gamma\, \tau = \beta\, \gamma\, c\, \tau
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\beta \gamma = \frac{d}{c\, \tau} = \frac{12}{3.00 \cdot 10^{8} \cdot 25 \cdot 10^{-9}} = \frac{12}{7.5} = 1.6
$$
$$
\frac{\beta^2}{1 - \beta^2} = (\beta\gamma)^2
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\beta^2 = \frac{(\beta\gamma)^2}{1 + (\beta\gamma)^2}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\beta = \frac{\beta\gamma}{\sqrt{1 + (\beta\gamma)^2}}
$$
$$
\beta = \frac{1.6}{\sqrt{1 + 2.56}} = \frac{1.6}{\sqrt{3.56}} \approx 0.848 ,
\qquad
v \approx 2.54 \cdot 10^{8}\,\mathrm{m/s}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

In the laboratory the particle has mean lifetime $\gamma\, \tau$, by **time dilation**, because $\tau$ is a proper time. During this interval, it moves at constant speed $v = \beta c$ through a distance $d = \beta c\, \gamma\, \tau$. The unknown appears in both $\beta$ and $\gamma$, so first find the product $\beta\gamma$, which is $1.6$.

Squaring gives $(\beta\gamma)^2 = \dfrac{\beta^2}{1 - \beta^2}$. Multiply both sides by $1 - \beta^2$ and move the terms containing $\beta^2$ to the left to obtain $\beta^2 \left( 1 + (\beta\gamma)^2 \right) = (\beta\gamma)^2$. Dividing and taking the positive root gives $\beta \approx 0.848$, a speed of **approximately** $2.54 \cdot 10^{8}\,\mathrm{m/s}$.

As a check, with $\beta = 0.848$ the Lorentz factor is $\dfrac{1.6}{0.848} \approx 1.89$. The mean lifetime in the laboratory is therefore $1.89 \cdot 25\,\mathrm{ns} \approx 47\,\mathrm{ns}$, and the particle covers $0.848 \cdot 3.00 \cdot 10^{8} \cdot 4.7 \cdot 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{m} \approx 12\,\mathrm{m}$ in that time. Without time dilation the same particle would have to move at $v = \dfrac{12}{25 \cdot 10^{-9}}\,\mathrm{m/s} = 4.8 \cdot 10^{8}\,\mathrm{m/s}$, faster than light.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A beam of pions with speed $v = 0.90\,c$ travels through a tube of length $20\,\mathrm{m}$, measured in the laboratory. The mean lifetime of pions at rest is $\tau = 26\,\mathrm{ns}$, and the fraction surviving after a proper time $t$ is $e^{-\frac{t}{\tau}}$. Calculate the fraction of pions that reach the far end of the tube and the fraction predicted by classical physics. Use $c = 3.00 \cdot 10^{8}\,\mathrm{m/s}$.

$$
\gamma = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - 0.90^2}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{0.19}} \approx 2.29
$$
$$
\Delta t = \frac{20}{0.90 \cdot 3.00 \cdot 10^{8}}\,\mathrm{s} \approx 7.41 \cdot 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{s} = 74.1\,\mathrm{ns} ,
\qquad
\Delta t_0 = \frac{\Delta t}{\gamma} \approx 32.3\,\mathrm{ns}
$$
$$
e^{-\frac{32.3}{26}} = e^{-1.24} \approx 0.29 ,
\qquad
e^{-\frac{74.1}{26}} = e^{-2.85} \approx 0.058
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The tube is at rest in the laboratory with length $20\,\mathrm{m}$. The pions travel along it at speed $v$ in about $74\,\mathrm{ns}$. The entry of a pion into the tube and its exit both occur at the pion’s position, so in the pion frame the interval between the events is a proper time. By **time dilation** this equals $\dfrac{\Delta t}{\gamma}$, or approximately $32\,\mathrm{ns}$.

Use proper time in the decay law because the mean lifetime $\tau$ is measured in the pion rest frame. Thus **about 29%** of the pions reach the far end of the tube. Classical physics would use the laboratory time and the exponent $\dfrac{74.1}{26} \approx 2.85$, predicting that only about $5.8\%$ of the pions reach the end, one-fifth of the observed fraction.

</div>
