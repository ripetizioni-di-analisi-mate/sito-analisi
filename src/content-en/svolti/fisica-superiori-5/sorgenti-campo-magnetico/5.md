## Exercise 1

A circular loop of radius $6.0\,\mathrm{cm}$ is placed in a uniform magnetic field of magnitude $B = 0.40\,\mathrm{T}$. Calculate the magnetic flux through the loop when the angle $\theta$ between $\vec{B}$ and the normal to the loop is $0^\circ$, $60^\circ$, $90^\circ$, and $180^\circ$.

$$
S = \pi r^2 = \pi \cdot 0.060^2\,\mathrm{m^2} \approx 1.13 \cdot 10^{-2}\,\mathrm{m^2}
$$
$$
\Phi(\vec{B}) = B S \cos\theta
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
\theta = 0^\circ &: \quad \Phi = 0.40 \cdot 1.13 \cdot 10^{-2}\,\mathrm{Wb} \approx 4.5 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{Wb} \\[6pt]
\theta = 60^\circ &: \quad \Phi = 4.52 \cdot 10^{-3} \cdot \frac{1}{2}\,\mathrm{Wb} \approx 2.3 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{Wb} \\[6pt]
\theta = 90^\circ &: \quad \Phi = 0 \\[6pt]
\theta = 180^\circ &: \quad \Phi \approx -4.5 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{Wb}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The loop is flat and the field is uniform, so the **flux** is the product $B S \cos\theta$. The area of the loop is the area of a disk of radius $6.0\,\mathrm{cm} = 0.060\,\mathrm{m}$.

For $\theta = 0^\circ$, the field is perpendicular to the plane of the loop and points along the normal, so the flux is at its maximum, $4.5 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{Wb}$. For $\theta = 60^\circ$, we have $\cos 60^\circ = \dfrac{1}{2}$, so the flux is half the maximum. For $\theta = 90^\circ$, the field is parallel to the plane of the loop. No field lines pass through it, so the flux is zero. For $\theta = 180^\circ$, the loop has been turned over and the field passes through it opposite to the normal. Since $\cos 180^\circ = -1$, the flux has the same magnitude as in the case $\theta = 0^\circ$ but a **negative sign**.

</div>

## Exercise 2

At a point on Earth’s surface, Earth’s magnetic field has magnitude $B = 4.6 \cdot 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{T}$. It lies in the vertical plane containing the north–south direction and points downward at an angle of $58^\circ$ below the horizontal. A rectangular loop of sides $30\,\mathrm{cm}$ and $20\,\mathrm{cm}$ lies on the floor.

1. Calculate the flux of Earth’s magnetic field through the loop, taking the normal downward.
2. Calculate the flux when the loop hangs on a vertical, north-facing wall, with its normal pointing horizontally north.

$$
S = 0.30 \cdot 0.20\,\mathrm{m^2} = 0.060\,\mathrm{m^2}
$$
$$
B_{\text{vert}} = B \sin 58^\circ \approx 3.90 \cdot 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{T} ,
\qquad
B_{\text{horiz}} = B \cos 58^\circ \approx 2.44 \cdot 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{T}
$$
$$
\Phi_1 = B_{\text{vert}}\,S = 3.90 \cdot 10^{-5} \cdot 0.060\,\mathrm{Wb} \approx 2.3 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\mathrm{Wb}
$$
$$
\Phi_2 = B_{\text{horiz}}\,S = 2.44 \cdot 10^{-5} \cdot 0.060\,\mathrm{Wb} \approx 1.5 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\mathrm{Wb}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The dimensions of the loop are small compared with the scale over which Earth’s field varies. The field can therefore be treated as uniform over the loop, and the flux can be calculated from $B S \cos\theta$. Resolve the field into vertical and horizontal components. Since the field makes an angle $58^\circ$ with the horizontal, its vertical component has magnitude $B \sin 58^\circ$ and its horizontal component, directed north, has magnitude $B \cos 58^\circ$.

When the loop is on the floor, its normal is vertical, so the horizontal component of the field is parallel to the loop and contributes no flux. The flux is the vertical component times the area, $2.3 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\mathrm{Wb}$, and is positive because the field, like the normal, points downward. When the loop is on the wall, the normal points horizontally north, so only the horizontal component contributes and the flux is $1.5 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\mathrm{Wb}$. The same result follows from $\Phi = B S \cos\theta$, where $\theta$ is the angle between the field and the normal. It equals $32^\circ$ in the first case and $58^\circ$ in the second.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A closed surface consists of a hemisphere of radius $R = 5.0\,\mathrm{cm}$ and the disk forming its base. The surface is in a uniform magnetic field of magnitude $B = 0.20\,\mathrm{T}$ perpendicular to the base and directed from the base toward the hemisphere.

1. Calculate the flux through the base, taking the normal outward from the closed surface.
2. Use Gauss’s law for magnetism to find the flux through the hemisphere, taking the normal outward.

$$
\Phi_{\text{base}} = B S \cos 180^\circ = -B\,\pi R^2 = -0.20 \cdot \pi \cdot 0.050^2\,\mathrm{Wb} \approx -1.6 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{Wb}
$$
$$
\Phi_{\text{base}} + \Phi_{\text{hemisphere}} = 0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\Phi_{\text{hemisphere}} = -\Phi_{\text{base}} \approx 1.6 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{Wb}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The base is a flat disk in a uniform field, so the flux follows from $B S \cos\theta$. At the base, the outward normal points away from the hemisphere, while the field points from the base toward the hemisphere. The angle between the two vectors is therefore $180^\circ$. The flux is negative because the field enters the closed surface through the base.

The hemisphere is curved, and the angle between the field and the normal changes from point to point, so the formula $B S \cos\theta$ cannot be used directly. By **Gauss’s law for magnetism**, the total outward flux through the closed surface is zero. The flux through the hemisphere is therefore the negative of the flux through the base, $1.6 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{Wb}$. This agrees with the field-line picture. All the lines that enter through the base leave through the hemisphere, because magnetic field lines have neither a beginning nor an end.

</div>
