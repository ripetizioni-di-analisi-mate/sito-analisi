## Exercise 1

A very long straight wire carries current $I = 8.0\,\mathrm{A}$. Calculate the magnitude of the magnetic field at distances $r_1 = 4.0\,\mathrm{cm}$ and $r_2 = 12\,\mathrm{cm}$ from the wire. ($\mu_0 = 4\pi \cdot 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{T\cdot m/A}$)

$$
B = \frac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi r}
$$
$$
\frac{\mu_0}{2\pi} = \frac{4\pi \cdot 10^{-7}}{2\pi}\,\mathrm{T\cdot m/A} = 2 \cdot 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{T\cdot m/A}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
B_1 &= \frac{2 \cdot 10^{-7} \cdot 8.0}{0.040}\,\mathrm{T} = 4.0 \cdot 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{T} \\[6pt]
B_2 &= \frac{2 \cdot 10^{-7} \cdot 8.0}{0.12}\,\mathrm{T} \approx 1.3 \cdot 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{T}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The wire is very long compared with the distances considered, so it can be treated as infinitely long and the **Biot–Savart law** applies. The quotient $\dfrac{\mu_0}{2\pi}$ equals $2 \cdot 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{T\cdot m/A}$ because $\pi$ cancels. This quotient appears in every calculation of the field of a straight wire. Convert the distances to meters before substituting.

The second distance is three times the first, and the field is inversely proportional to distance. Therefore $B_2$ is one-third of $B_1$, as confirmed by $\dfrac{4.0 \cdot 10^{-5}}{3} \approx 1.3 \cdot 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{T}$. Both values are of the same order of magnitude as Earth’s magnetic field.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A long straight cable carries current $I = 15\,\mathrm{A}$.

1. Find the distance from the cable at which the magnetic field has magnitude $1.0 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{T}$.
2. Find the current the cable would have to carry for the field to have magnitude $1.0 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{T}$ at a distance of $5.0\,\mathrm{cm}$.

($\mu_0 = 4\pi \cdot 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{T\cdot m/A}$)

$$
B = \frac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi r}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
r = \frac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi B} ,
\qquad
I = \frac{2\pi r B}{\mu_0}
$$
$$
r = \frac{2 \cdot 10^{-7} \cdot 15}{1.0 \cdot 10^{-4}}\,\mathrm{m} = 3.0 \cdot 10^{-2}\,\mathrm{m} = 3.0\,\mathrm{cm}
$$
$$
I = \frac{2\pi \cdot 0.050 \cdot 1.0 \cdot 10^{-4}}{4\pi \cdot 10^{-7}}\,\mathrm{A} = \frac{0.050 \cdot 1.0 \cdot 10^{-4}}{2 \cdot 10^{-7}}\,\mathrm{A} = 25\,\mathrm{A}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The cable can be treated as an infinitely long wire, so the **Biot–Savart law** applies. In the first question, the unknown is the distance. Multiplying both sides by $r$ and dividing by $B$ gives $r = \dfrac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi B}$. The result is $3.0\,\mathrm{cm}$.

In the second question, the unknown is the current. Multiplying both sides by $2\pi r$ and dividing by $\mu_0$ gives $I = \dfrac{2\pi r B}{\mu_0}$. In the calculation, $2\pi$ divided by $4\pi$ gives $\dfrac{1}{2}$, so the current is $25\,\mathrm{A}$. This is consistent with the first result. To produce the same field at a greater distance, $5.0\,\mathrm{cm}$ instead of $3.0\,\mathrm{cm}$, the current must increase by the same factor, $15 \cdot \dfrac{5.0}{3.0}\,\mathrm{A} = 25\,\mathrm{A}$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A very long vertical straight wire carries current $I = 6.0\,\mathrm{A}$ upward. A small compass rests on a horizontal table north of the wire. The horizontal component of Earth’s magnetic field has magnitude $B_T = 2.0 \cdot 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{T}$ and points north.

1. Determine the direction of the field produced by the wire at the compass.
2. Find the distance from the wire to the compass if the needle makes an angle of $45^\circ$ with north.

($\mu_0 = 4\pi \cdot 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{T\cdot m/A}$)

::grafico{assi=0 uguale=1 vettori="0,0,0,2,B Earth;0,0,-2,0,B wire;0,0,-2,2,B" segmenti="-2,0,-2,2;0,2,-2,2" punti="0,0,pieno" etichette="0.25,2.15,north;-2.6,-0.3,west;-1.05,0.45,45°" xmin=-3 xmax=1.2 ymin=-0.6 ymax=2.6 titolo="Top view. Earth’s field points north and the wire’s field points west. Their magnitudes are equal, so the needle aligns with $\vec{B}$, at $45^\circ$ west of north"}

$$
\tan 45^\circ = \frac{B_{\text{wire}}}{B_T} = 1
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
B_{\text{wire}} = B_T
$$
$$
\frac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi r} = B_T
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
r = \frac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi B_T}
$$
$$
r = \frac{2 \cdot 10^{-7} \cdot 6.0}{2.0 \cdot 10^{-5}}\,\mathrm{m} = 6.0 \cdot 10^{-2}\,\mathrm{m} = 6.0\,\mathrm{cm}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

View the table from above, with north at the top of the figure. The current flows upward toward the observer, hence out of the page. By the **right-hand rule**, the field lines are counterclockwise circles. At the point north of the wire, the tangent to a counterclockwise circle points west. At the compass, the field of the wire is therefore **horizontal and directed west**, perpendicular to Earth’s field.

The compass needle aligns with the total field, the vector sum of Earth’s field and the field of the wire. The two vectors are perpendicular, so the angle $\alpha$ between the needle and north satisfies $\tan\alpha = \dfrac{B_{\text{wire}}}{B_T}$. Since $\tan 45^\circ = 1$, the fields have equal magnitudes. Solving the Biot–Savart law for $r$ with $B_{\text{wire}} = B_T$ gives a compass position $6.0\,\mathrm{cm}$ from the wire. Closer to the wire, the needle would turn more than $45^\circ$ toward the west. Farther away, it would turn less.

</div>
