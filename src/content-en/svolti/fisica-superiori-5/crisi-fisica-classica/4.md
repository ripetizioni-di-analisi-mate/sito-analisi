## Exercise 1

The sodium cathode of a photocell ($W_0 = 2.30\,\mathrm{eV}$) is illuminated by light of wavelength $\lambda = 400\,\mathrm{nm}$, and the saturation current is $I_s = 2.0\,\mu\mathrm{A}$. Calculate the number of electrons emitted in one second and the stopping potential. Determine the saturation current and stopping potential when the light intensity is halved at constant wavelength. Use $h = 6.63 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\cdot s}$, $c = 3.00 \cdot 10^{8}\,\mathrm{m/s}$, and $e = 1.60 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{C}$.

$$
I_s = \frac{N e}{\Delta t}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\frac{N}{\Delta t} = \frac{I_s}{e} ,
\qquad
e V_0 = K_{\max} = \frac{h c}{\lambda} - W_0
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{N}{\Delta t} &= \frac{2.0 \cdot 10^{-6}}{1.60 \cdot 10^{-19}}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}} = 1.25 \cdot 10^{13}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}} \\[6pt]
\frac{h c}{\lambda} &= \frac{6.63 \cdot 10^{-34} \cdot 3.00 \cdot 10^{8}}{4.00 \cdot 10^{-7} \cdot 1.60 \cdot 10^{-19}}\,\mathrm{eV} \approx 3.11\,\mathrm{eV} \\[6pt]
K_{\max} &= 3.11\,\mathrm{eV} - 2.30\,\mathrm{eV} \approx 0.81\,\mathrm{eV}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
V_0 \approx 0.81\,\mathrm{V}
\end{aligned}
$$
$$
I_s' = \frac{I_s}{2} = 1.0\,\mu\mathrm{A} ,
\qquad
V_0' = V_0 \approx 0.81\,\mathrm{V}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

At saturation, all the electrons emitted by the cathode reach the anode. The current is the charge passing per second, and each electron carries charge of magnitude $e$. The number of electrons emitted per second is therefore $\dfrac{I_s}{e}$, or $1.25 \cdot 10^{13}$.

The stopping potential stops even the fastest electrons, so $eV_0 = K_{\max}$. By **Einstein’s equation**, $K_{\max}$ equals the photon energy, $3.11\,\mathrm{eV}$, minus the work function. An energy of $0.81\,\mathrm{eV}$ corresponds to a stopping potential of $0.81\,\mathrm{V}$ because an electron crossing a potential difference of $1\,\mathrm{V}$ changes its energy by $1\,\mathrm{eV}$.

Halving the intensity halves the number of photons arriving per second while the energy of each photon remains $hf$. Half as many electrons are emitted per second, and the saturation current becomes $1.0\,\mu\mathrm{A}$. The maximum kinetic energy depends only on $hf$ and $W_0$, so the **stopping potential stays the same**.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A photocell is illuminated with blue light of wavelength $\lambda = 450\,\mathrm{nm}$ and power $P = 1.5\,\mathrm{mW}$, all of which is absorbed by the cathode. The saturation current is $I_s = 54\,\mu\mathrm{A}$. Calculate the number of photons arriving per second, the number of electrons emitted per second, and the fraction of photons that eject an electron, called the quantum efficiency. Use $h = 6.63 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\cdot s}$, $c = 3.00 \cdot 10^{8}\,\mathrm{m/s}$, and $e = 1.60 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{C}$.

$$
\frac{N_f}{\Delta t} = \frac{P}{h f} = \frac{P \lambda}{h c} ,
\qquad
\frac{N_e}{\Delta t} = \frac{I_s}{e} ,
\qquad
\eta = \frac{N_e}{N_f}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{N_f}{\Delta t} &= \frac{1.5 \cdot 10^{-3} \cdot 4.50 \cdot 10^{-7}}{6.63 \cdot 10^{-34} \cdot 3.00 \cdot 10^{8}}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}} \approx 3.39 \cdot 10^{15}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}} \\[6pt]
\frac{N_e}{\Delta t} &= \frac{54 \cdot 10^{-6}}{1.60 \cdot 10^{-19}}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}} \approx 3.38 \cdot 10^{14}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}} \\[6pt]
\eta &= \frac{3.38 \cdot 10^{14}}{3.39 \cdot 10^{15}} \approx 0.10 = 10\%
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The light consists of photons of energy $hf = \dfrac{hc}{\lambda}$. The number arriving per second is the power divided by the energy of one photon, $\dfrac{P \lambda}{hc}$, giving about $3.39 \cdot 10^{15}$.

The number of electrons emitted per second follows from the saturation current, as in the preceding exercise, and is about $3.38 \cdot 10^{14}$. Each emitted electron absorbed a photon, but many absorbed photons transfer their energy to electrons that do not reach the surface. The ratio of emitted electrons to incident photons is the **quantum efficiency**, about $10\%$, or roughly one electron per ten photons.

</div>

## Exercise 3

The cesium cathode of a photocell ($W_0 = 2.10\,\mathrm{eV}$) is illuminated by light of wavelength $\lambda_1 = 400\,\mathrm{nm}$, and the saturation current is $I_1 = 8.0\,\mu\mathrm{A}$. The source is replaced with an ultraviolet source of wavelength $\lambda_2 = 300\,\mathrm{nm}$ and the *same power*. Assuming the same quantum efficiency at both wavelengths, calculate the two stopping potentials and the new saturation current, and sketch current versus voltage in both cases. Use $h = 6.63 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\cdot s}$, $c = 3.00 \cdot 10^{8}\,\mathrm{m/s}$, and $1\,\mathrm{eV} = 1.60 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{J}$.

$$
V_0 = \frac{1}{e} \left( \frac{h c}{\lambda} - W_0 \right) ,
\qquad
\frac{N_f}{\Delta t} = \frac{P \lambda}{h c}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\frac{I_2}{I_1} = \frac{\lambda_2}{\lambda_1}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{h c}{\lambda_1} &\approx 3.11\,\mathrm{eV} ,
\qquad
V_{0,1} \approx 3.11\,\mathrm{V} - 2.10\,\mathrm{V} \approx 1.01\,\mathrm{V} \\[6pt]
\frac{h c}{\lambda_2} &\approx 4.14\,\mathrm{eV} ,
\qquad
V_{0,2} \approx 4.14\,\mathrm{V} - 2.10\,\mathrm{V} \approx 2.04\,\mathrm{V} \\[6pt]
I_2 &= 8.0\,\mu\mathrm{A} \cdot \frac{300}{400} = 6.0\,\mu\mathrm{A}
\end{aligned}
$$

::grafico{f="max(0,8*(1-exp(-(x+1.01)/0.5)));max(0,6*(1-exp(-(x+2.04)/0.5)))" punti="-1.01,0,pieno;-2.04,0,pieno" xmin=-3 xmax=4 ymin=-1 ymax=9 nomi="V,I" legenda="\lambda_1 = 400\,\mathrm{nm};\lambda_2 = 300\,\mathrm{nm}" titolo="Current $I$ in microamperes versus voltage $V$ in volts. With ultraviolet light the stopping potential is higher ($2.04\,\mathrm{V}$ instead of $1.01\,\mathrm{V}$), but the saturation current is lower because fewer photons arrive at the same power"}

<div class="spiegazione">

By **Einstein’s equation** and the relation $eV_0 = K_{\max}$, the stopping potential in volts equals the photon energy in electronvolts minus the work function in electronvolts. Ultraviolet photons have more energy, so the stopping potential rises from about $1.01\,\mathrm{V}$ to about $2.04\,\mathrm{V}$.

Both sources have the same power, but ultraviolet photons have more energy, so the ultraviolet source emits *fewer* photons per second. The number of photons per second is $\dfrac{P \lambda}{hc}$, proportional to $\lambda$, and the ratio of the two photon rates is $\dfrac{300}{400} = 0.75$. With the same quantum efficiency, the number of electrons emitted per second also scales by the factor $0.75$, and the saturation current becomes $6.0\,\mu\mathrm{A}$.

In the graph the ultraviolet curve starts at a more negative potential, $-2.04\,\mathrm{V}$, and reaches a lower saturation current. This shows that electron energy depends on photon energy, whereas current depends on the number of photons arriving.

</div>
