## Exercise 1

A sodium lamp emits yellow light of wavelength $\lambda = 589\,\mathrm{nm}$. Calculate the frequency of the light and the energy of a photon in joules and electronvolts. Use $h = 6.63 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\cdot s}$, $c = 3.00 \cdot 10^{8}\,\mathrm{m/s}$, and $1\,\mathrm{eV} = 1.60 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{J}$.

$$
f = \frac{c}{\lambda} ,
\qquad
E = h f = \frac{h c}{\lambda}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
f &= \frac{3.00 \cdot 10^{8}}{5.89 \cdot 10^{-7}}\,\mathrm{Hz} \approx 5.09 \cdot 10^{14}\,\mathrm{Hz} \\[6pt]
E &= 6.63 \cdot 10^{-34} \cdot 5.09 \cdot 10^{14}\,\mathrm{J} \approx 3.38 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{J} \\[6pt]
E &= \frac{3.38 \cdot 10^{-19}}{1.60 \cdot 10^{-19}}\,\mathrm{eV} \approx 2.11\,\mathrm{eV}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Light is an electromagnetic wave traveling at speed $c$, so its frequency and wavelength are related by $c = \lambda f$. Express the wavelength in meters, $589\,\mathrm{nm} = 5.89 \cdot 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{m}$, and divide $c$ by $\lambda$.

According to Einstein’s **photon hypothesis**, light of frequency $f$ consists of photons of energy $hf$. The product gives about $3.38 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{J}$. Since $1\,\mathrm{eV} = 1.60 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{J}$, divide by $1.60 \cdot 10^{-19}$ to convert to electronvolts and obtain $2.11\,\mathrm{eV}$. The shortcut formula $E \approx \dfrac{1240}{589}\,\mathrm{eV}$ gives the same value.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A green laser pointer emits light of wavelength $\lambda = 532\,\mathrm{nm}$ with power $P = 5.0\,\mathrm{mW}$. Calculate the energy of one photon, the number of photons emitted in one second, and the number emitted in two minutes. Use $h = 6.63 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\cdot s}$ and $c = 3.00 \cdot 10^{8}\,\mathrm{m/s}$.

$$
E = \frac{h c}{\lambda} ,
\qquad
P = \frac{N E}{\Delta t}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\frac{N}{\Delta t} = \frac{P}{E}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
E &= \frac{6.63 \cdot 10^{-34} \cdot 3.00 \cdot 10^{8}}{5.32 \cdot 10^{-7}}\,\mathrm{J} \approx 3.74 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{J} \\[6pt]
\frac{N}{\Delta t} &= \frac{5.0 \cdot 10^{-3}}{3.74 \cdot 10^{-19}}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}} \approx 1.3 \cdot 10^{16}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}} \\[6pt]
N_{120} &= 1.34 \cdot 10^{16} \cdot 120 \approx 1.6 \cdot 10^{18}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Each photon carries energy $E = \dfrac{hc}{\lambda}$, calculated using the wavelength in meters. If the laser emits $N$ photons in an interval $\Delta t$, the emitted energy is $NE$, and the power is energy divided by time. Solving the relation $P = \dfrac{NE}{\Delta t}$ for $\dfrac{N}{\Delta t}$ gives the number of photons per second: the power divided by the energy of one photon.

The laser emits about $1.3 \cdot 10^{16}$ photons per second. In two minutes, or $120\,\mathrm{s}$, this number is multiplied by $120$, giving about $1.6 \cdot 10^{18}$. Such large numbers explain why a beam of light appears continuous and the individual photons go unnoticed.

</div>

## Exercise 3

An object of mass $m = 0.20\,\mathrm{kg}$ suspended from a spring oscillates with frequency $f = 2.0\,\mathrm{Hz}$ and amplitude $A = 5.0\,\mathrm{cm}$. According to Planck’s hypothesis, the oscillator’s energy can take only the values $E = n h f$. Calculate its mechanical energy, the energy quantum $hf$, and the quantum number $n$ of its state. Explain why the quantization is not observed. Use $h = 6.63 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\cdot s}$.

$$
E = \frac{1}{2} m \omega^2 A^2 ,
\qquad
\omega = 2\pi f ,
\qquad
n = \frac{E}{h f}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
\omega &= 2\pi \cdot 2.0\,\mathrm{rad/s} \approx 12.6\,\mathrm{rad/s} \\[6pt]
E &= \frac{1}{2} \cdot 0.20 \cdot 12.57^2 \cdot 0.050^2\,\mathrm{J} \approx 3.9 \cdot 10^{-2}\,\mathrm{J} \\[6pt]
h f &= 6.63 \cdot 10^{-34} \cdot 2.0\,\mathrm{J} \approx 1.3 \cdot 10^{-33}\,\mathrm{J} \\[6pt]
n &= \frac{3.95 \cdot 10^{-2}}{1.33 \cdot 10^{-33}} \approx 3.0 \cdot 10^{31}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The object on the spring is a harmonic oscillator. At the equilibrium position all its energy is kinetic and its speed is greatest, $\omega A$, so the mechanical energy is $\dfrac{1}{2} m (\omega A)^2$. The angular frequency is $\omega = 2\pi f \approx 12.6\,\mathrm{rad/s}$ and the amplitude in meters is $0.050\,\mathrm{m}$, giving an energy of about $3.9 \cdot 10^{-2}\,\mathrm{J}$.

By **Planck’s hypothesis**, the energy can change only in steps of $hf \approx 1.3 \cdot 10^{-33}\,\mathrm{J}$. The quantum number is the ratio of the energy to one quantum, about $3.0 \cdot 10^{31}$. A step of one quantum therefore changes the energy by one part in $3 \cdot 10^{31}$. No measurement of amplitude or speed has comparable precision, so for a macroscopic oscillator the energy **appears continuous**.

</div>

## Exercise 4

A lamp emits power $P = 60\,\mathrm{W}$ uniformly in all directions, with $5.0\%$ emitted as visible light. Take the wavelength of all the visible light to be its mean wavelength $\lambda = 550\,\mathrm{nm}$. Calculate the number of visible photons emitted per second and the number entering an eye each second. The pupil has diameter $d = 4.0\,\mathrm{mm}$ and is at a distance $r = 4.0\,\mathrm{m}$ from the lamp. Use $h = 6.63 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\cdot s}$ and $c = 3.00 \cdot 10^{8}\,\mathrm{m/s}$.

$$
P_{\text{vis}} = 0.050\, P ,
\qquad
E = \frac{h c}{\lambda} ,
\qquad
\frac{N}{\Delta t} = \frac{P_{\text{vis}}}{E}
$$
$$
\frac{S_{\text{pupil}}}{S_{\text{sphere}}} = \frac{\pi \left( \dfrac{d}{2} \right)^2}{4\pi r^2} = \frac{d^2}{16\, r^2}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
P_{\text{vis}} &= 0.050 \cdot 60\,\mathrm{W} = 3.0\,\mathrm{W} \\[6pt]
E &= \frac{6.63 \cdot 10^{-34} \cdot 3.00 \cdot 10^{8}}{5.50 \cdot 10^{-7}}\,\mathrm{J} \approx 3.62 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{J} \\[6pt]
\frac{N}{\Delta t} &= \frac{3.0}{3.62 \cdot 10^{-19}}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}} \approx 8.3 \cdot 10^{18}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}} \\[6pt]
\frac{S_{\text{pupil}}}{S_{\text{sphere}}} &= \frac{\left( 4.0 \cdot 10^{-3} \right)^2}{16 \cdot 4.0^2} = 6.25 \cdot 10^{-8} \\[6pt]
\frac{N_{\text{eye}}}{\Delta t} &= 8.30 \cdot 10^{18} \cdot 6.25 \cdot 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}} \approx 5.2 \cdot 10^{11}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The visible power is $5.0\%$ of the total power, or $3.0\,\mathrm{W}$. As in the laser exercise, the number of photons per second is the visible power divided by the energy of one photon, giving about $8.3 \cdot 10^{18}$ photons per second.

The lamp emits uniformly in all directions, so at a distance $r$ the photons are distributed uniformly over a sphere of area $4\pi r^2$. The pupil is a circle of radius $\dfrac{d}{2}$ and area $\pi \dfrac{d^2}{4}$. The fraction of photons it receives therefore equals the ratio of its area to that of the sphere. Canceling $\pi$ gives $\dfrac{d^2}{16 r^2}$, which equals $6.25 \cdot 10^{-8}$.

Thus about $5.2 \cdot 10^{11}$ photons per second enter the eye. Even this tiny fraction of the emitted light contains an enormous number of photons.

</div>
