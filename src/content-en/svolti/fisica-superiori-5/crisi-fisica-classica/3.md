## Exercise 1

A sodium plate with work function $W_0 = 2.30\,\mathrm{eV}$ is illuminated with violet light of wavelength $\lambda = 410\,\mathrm{nm}$. Calculate the photon energy, the maximum kinetic energy of the photoelectrons, and their maximum speed. Use $h = 6.63 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\cdot s}$, $c = 3.00 \cdot 10^{8}\,\mathrm{m/s}$, $1\,\mathrm{eV} = 1.60 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{J}$, and $m_e = 9.11 \cdot 10^{-31}\,\mathrm{kg}$.

::grafico{assi=0 uguale=1 linee="0,0 3,0;0,2.3 3,2.3;0,3.03 3,3.03" vettori="0.8,0,0.8,3.03,hf;2.2,0,2.2,2.3,W₀;2.2,2.3,2.2,3.03" etichette="3.7,0,electron in metal;3.55,2.3,escape from metal;2.75,2.67,Kₘₐₓ" xmin=-0.5 xmax=5 ymin=-0.4 ymax=3.4 legenda=0 titolo="Energy balance for the least tightly bound electron. Of the photon energy $hf = 3.03\,\mathrm{eV}$, an amount $W_0 = 2.30\,\mathrm{eV}$ is needed to leave the metal and the rest is kinetic energy $K_{\max}$"}

$$
E = \frac{h c}{\lambda} ,
\qquad
K_{\max} = h f - W_0 ,
\qquad
K_{\max} = \frac{1}{2} m_e v_{\max}^2
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
v_{\max} = \sqrt{\frac{2 K_{\max}}{m_e}}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
E &= \frac{6.63 \cdot 10^{-34} \cdot 3.00 \cdot 10^{8}}{4.10 \cdot 10^{-7}}\,\mathrm{J} \approx 4.85 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{J} \approx 3.03\,\mathrm{eV} \\[6pt]
K_{\max} &= 3.03\,\mathrm{eV} - 2.30\,\mathrm{eV} = 0.73\,\mathrm{eV} \approx 1.17 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{J} \\[6pt]
v_{\max} &= \sqrt{\frac{2 \cdot 1.17 \cdot 10^{-19}}{9.11 \cdot 10^{-31}}}\,\mathrm{m/s} \approx 5.1 \cdot 10^{5}\,\mathrm{m/s}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Calculate the photon energy using $E = \dfrac{hc}{\lambda}$ and divide by $1.60 \cdot 10^{-19}$ to convert it to electronvolts for comparison with the work function. The photon carries $3.03\,\mathrm{eV}$, more than the work function of sodium, so the photoelectric effect occurs.

By **Einstein’s equation**, the least tightly bound electron receives all the photon energy and uses $W_0$ to escape the metal. The energy left over is the maximum kinetic energy, $0.73\,\mathrm{eV}$. To calculate the speed, express this energy in joules, $0.73 \cdot 1.60 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{J} \approx 1.17 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{J}$.

The speed is about $5.1 \cdot 10^{5}\,\mathrm{m/s}$, or about $0.17\%$ of $c$. Since $v \ll c$, the classical kinetic energy formula $\dfrac{1}{2} m_e v^2$ is justified.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Cesium has a work function of $W_0 = 2.10\,\mathrm{eV}$. Determine its threshold frequency and threshold wavelength. Decide whether red light with $\lambda_1 = 680\,\mathrm{nm}$ and green light with $\lambda_2 = 520\,\mathrm{nm}$ produce the photoelectric effect and, if they do, calculate the maximum kinetic energy of the photoelectrons. Use $h = 6.63 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\cdot s}$, $c = 3.00 \cdot 10^{8}\,\mathrm{m/s}$, and $1\,\mathrm{eV} = 1.60 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{J}$.

$$
f_0 = \frac{W_0}{h} ,
\qquad
\lambda_0 = \frac{c}{f_0} = \frac{h c}{W_0}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
f_0 &= \frac{2.10 \cdot 1.60 \cdot 10^{-19}}{6.63 \cdot 10^{-34}}\,\mathrm{Hz} \approx 5.07 \cdot 10^{14}\,\mathrm{Hz} \\[6pt]
\lambda_0 &= \frac{3.00 \cdot 10^{8}}{5.07 \cdot 10^{14}}\,\mathrm{m} \approx 5.92 \cdot 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{m} = 592\,\mathrm{nm}
\end{aligned}
$$
$$
\lambda_1 = 680\,\mathrm{nm} > \lambda_0 ,
\qquad
E_1 = \frac{h c}{\lambda_1} \approx 1.83\,\mathrm{eV} < W_0
$$
$$
\lambda_2 = 520\,\mathrm{nm} < \lambda_0 ,
\qquad
E_2 = \frac{h c}{\lambda_2} \approx 2.39\,\mathrm{eV} ,
\qquad
K_{\max} = 2.39\,\mathrm{eV} - 2.10\,\mathrm{eV} \approx 0.29\,\mathrm{eV}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

An electron can leave the metal only if the photon energy is at least equal to the work function, that is, if $hf \ge W_0$. The **threshold frequency** is therefore $f_0 = \dfrac{W_0}{h}$, with $W_0$ expressed in joules: $2.10\,\mathrm{eV} = 3.36 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{J}$. The threshold wavelength is $\lambda_0 = \dfrac{c}{f_0} \approx 592\,\mathrm{nm}$, in the orange region. Since photon energy decreases as wavelength increases, the effect occurs for $\lambda \le \lambda_0$.

The red light has $\lambda_1 > \lambda_0$. Its photons have energy $1.83\,\mathrm{eV}$, less than $W_0$, so they do **not** eject electrons, regardless of intensity. The green light has $\lambda_2 < \lambda_0$ and therefore produces the effect. By **Einstein’s equation**, the maximum kinetic energy is the difference between photon energy and work function, about $0.29\,\mathrm{eV}$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

An unknown metal is illuminated by ultraviolet light of wavelength $\lambda = 250\,\mathrm{nm}$, and its fastest photoelectrons have kinetic energy $K_{\max} = 0.67\,\mathrm{eV}$. Determine the work function and threshold wavelength of the metal. Compare the work function with those of zinc ($4.3\,\mathrm{eV}$) and copper ($4.7\,\mathrm{eV}$) to identify the metal. Use $h = 6.63 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\cdot s}$, $c = 3.00 \cdot 10^{8}\,\mathrm{m/s}$, and $1\,\mathrm{eV} = 1.60 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{J}$.

$$
K_{\max} = \frac{h c}{\lambda} - W_0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
W_0 = \frac{h c}{\lambda} - K_{\max} ,
\qquad
\lambda_0 = \frac{h c}{W_0}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{h c}{\lambda} &= \frac{6.63 \cdot 10^{-34} \cdot 3.00 \cdot 10^{8}}{2.50 \cdot 10^{-7}}\,\mathrm{J} \approx 7.96 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{J} \approx 4.97\,\mathrm{eV} \\[6pt]
W_0 &= 4.97\,\mathrm{eV} - 0.67\,\mathrm{eV} = 4.30\,\mathrm{eV} \\[6pt]
\lambda_0 &= \frac{6.63 \cdot 10^{-34} \cdot 3.00 \cdot 10^{8}}{4.30 \cdot 1.60 \cdot 10^{-19}}\,\mathrm{m} \approx 2.89 \cdot 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{m} = 289\,\mathrm{nm}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The unknown is the work function. In **Einstein’s equation**, isolate $W_0$ by moving $K_{\max}$ to the left. Thus $W_0$ equals the photon energy minus the maximum kinetic energy. The photon has energy $4.97\,\mathrm{eV}$ and the electron emerges with at most $0.67\,\mathrm{eV}$, so the work function is $4.30\,\mathrm{eV}$.

The value matches that for zinc and differs from the value for copper, so the metal is **zinc**. Its threshold wavelength, $\lambda_0 = \dfrac{hc}{W_0} \approx 289\,\mathrm{nm}$, lies in the ultraviolet. This explains why zinc emits electrons only under ultraviolet illumination in the electroscope experiment.

</div>
