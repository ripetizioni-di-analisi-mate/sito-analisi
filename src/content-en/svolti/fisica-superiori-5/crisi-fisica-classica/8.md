## Exercise 1

The tungsten filament of an incandescent bulb has temperature $T = 2900\,\mathrm{K}$ and emitting surface area $A = 0.50\,\mathrm{cm^2}$ and can be treated as a blackbody. Calculate the peak emission wavelength, the energy in electronvolts of photons at that wavelength, and the power emitted by the filament. Determine whether photons at the emission peak can eject electrons from a cesium cathode ($W_0 = 2.10\,\mathrm{eV}$). Use Wien’s constant $2.90 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m\cdot K}$, $\sigma = 5.67 \cdot 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{W/(m^2\cdot K^4)}$, $h = 6.63 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\cdot s}$, $c = 3.00 \cdot 10^{8}\,\mathrm{m/s}$, and $1\,\mathrm{eV} = 1.60 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{J}$.

$$
\lambda_{\max} = \frac{2.90 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m\cdot K}}{T} ,
\qquad
E = \frac{h c}{\lambda_{\max}} ,
\qquad
P = \sigma A T^4 ,
\qquad
\lambda_0 = \frac{h c}{W_0}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
\lambda_{\max} &= \frac{2.90 \cdot 10^{-3}}{2900}\,\mathrm{m} = 1.00 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\mathrm{m} = 1.00\,\mu\mathrm{m} \\[6pt]
E &= \frac{6.63 \cdot 10^{-34} \cdot 3.00 \cdot 10^{8}}{1.00 \cdot 10^{-6} \cdot 1.60 \cdot 10^{-19}}\,\mathrm{eV} \approx 1.24\,\mathrm{eV} \\[6pt]
P &= 5.67 \cdot 10^{-8} \cdot 0.50 \cdot 10^{-4} \cdot 2900^4\,\mathrm{W} \approx 2.0 \cdot 10^{2}\,\mathrm{W} \\[6pt]
\lambda_0 &= \frac{6.63 \cdot 10^{-34} \cdot 3.00 \cdot 10^{8}}{2.10 \cdot 1.60 \cdot 10^{-19}}\,\mathrm{m} \approx 592\,\mathrm{nm}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The filament is treated as a blackbody, so by **Wien’s law** its emission peaks at $1.00\,\mu\mathrm{m}$, in the near infrared. The bulb therefore emits most of its power as infrared radiation and only a small fraction as visible light. Photons at this wavelength have energy $\dfrac{hc}{\lambda_{\max}} \approx 1.24\,\mathrm{eV}$.

The area in square meters is $0.50 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m^2}$ because $1\,\mathrm{cm^2} = 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m^2}$. By the **Stefan–Boltzmann law**, the emitted power is about $200\,\mathrm{W}$.

Photons at the peak have less energy than the work function of cesium, $1.24\,\mathrm{eV} < 2.10\,\mathrm{eV}$. Equivalently, $1.00\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ exceeds the threshold wavelength $592\,\mathrm{nm}$. These photons therefore do **not** eject electrons. The lamp nevertheless produces the photoelectric effect in cesium because the blackbody spectrum also contains photons of wavelength shorter than $592\,\mathrm{nm}$, in the green, blue, and violet regions.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A laboratory studies the photoelectric effect using a photocell. Four filters pass light of different wavelengths onto the cathode. The stopping potential $V_0$ is measured at each wavelength.

| $\lambda$ ($\mathrm{nm}$) | $400$ | $450$ | $500$ | $550$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $V_0$ ($\mathrm{V}$) | $1.11$ | $0.76$ | $0.49$ | $0.26$ |

Use $c = 3.00 \cdot 10^{8}\,\mathrm{m/s}$, $e = 1.60 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{C}$, and $m_e = 9.11 \cdot 10^{-31}\,\mathrm{kg}$, and from part b) onward use $h = 6.63 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\cdot s}$ together with the work function found in part a).

a) Calculate the frequencies corresponding to the four wavelengths, plot $V_0$ versus $f$, and verify that the points lie on a straight line. From the graph, determine Planck’s constant, the cathode work function, the threshold frequency, and the threshold wavelength.

b) Determine whether a red laser pointer with $\lambda = 650\,\mathrm{nm}$ produces the photoelectric effect in this cathode.

c) A violet laser of wavelength $\lambda = 405\,\mathrm{nm}$ and power $P = 2.0\,\mathrm{mW}$ illuminates the cathode, which absorbs all the light. The quantum efficiency is $8.0\%$. Calculate the saturation current, the stopping potential, and the maximum speed of the photoelectrons.

d) Double the power of the violet laser. Determine the new saturation current and stopping potential, and explain why the result conflicts with classical wave theory.

**a)** Frequencies and graph.

$$
f = \frac{c}{\lambda} ,
\qquad
f_{400} = 7.50 \cdot 10^{14}\,\mathrm{Hz} ,
\quad
f_{450} \approx 6.67 \cdot 10^{14}\,\mathrm{Hz} ,
\quad
f_{500} = 6.00 \cdot 10^{14}\,\mathrm{Hz} ,
\quad
f_{550} \approx 5.45 \cdot 10^{14}\,\mathrm{Hz}
$$

::grafico{f="(x>=4.83?0.4156*x-2.007:NaN)" segmenti="4.83,0,0,-2.007" punti="7.5,1.11,pieno;6.667,0.76,pieno;6,0.49,pieno;5.455,0.26,pieno;4.83,0,vuoto,f₀" xmin=-0.5 xmax=8.5 ymin=-2.5 ymax=1.5 nomi="f,V₀" legenda=0 titolo="Stopping potential $V_0$ in volts versus frequency $f$ in units of $10^{14}\,\mathrm{Hz}$. The measured points lie on a straight line that crosses the frequency axis at $f_0 \approx 4.83 \cdot 10^{14}\,\mathrm{Hz}$"}

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{h}{e} &= \frac{1.11 - 0.26}{(7.50 - 5.455) \cdot 10^{14}}\,\mathrm{V\cdot s} \approx 4.16 \cdot 10^{-15}\,\mathrm{V\cdot s} \\[6pt]
h &= 1.60 \cdot 10^{-19} \cdot 4.156 \cdot 10^{-15}\,\mathrm{J\cdot s} \approx 6.65 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\cdot s} \\[6pt]
\frac{W_0}{e} &= 4.156 \cdot 10^{-15} \cdot 7.50 \cdot 10^{14}\,\mathrm{V} - 1.11\,\mathrm{V} \approx 2.01\,\mathrm{V}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
W_0 \approx 2.01\,\mathrm{eV} \\[6pt]
f_0 &= \frac{2.007}{4.156 \cdot 10^{-15}}\,\mathrm{Hz} \approx 4.83 \cdot 10^{14}\,\mathrm{Hz} ,
\qquad
\lambda_0 = \frac{c}{f_0} \approx 621\,\mathrm{nm}
\end{aligned}
$$

**b)** Red laser.

$$
E = \frac{h c}{\lambda} = \frac{6.63 \cdot 10^{-34} \cdot 3.00 \cdot 10^{8}}{6.50 \cdot 10^{-7} \cdot 1.60 \cdot 10^{-19}}\,\mathrm{eV} \approx 1.91\,\mathrm{eV} < W_0 = 2.01\,\mathrm{eV}
$$

**c)** Violet laser.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{N_f}{\Delta t} &= \frac{P \lambda}{h c} = \frac{2.0 \cdot 10^{-3} \cdot 4.05 \cdot 10^{-7}}{6.63 \cdot 10^{-34} \cdot 3.00 \cdot 10^{8}}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}} \approx 4.07 \cdot 10^{15}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}} \\[6pt]
I_s &= 0.080 \cdot \frac{N_f}{\Delta t} \cdot e = 0.080 \cdot 4.07 \cdot 10^{15} \cdot 1.60 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{A} \approx 5.2 \cdot 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{A} = 52\,\mu\mathrm{A} \\[6pt]
\frac{h c}{\lambda} &= \frac{6.63 \cdot 10^{-34} \cdot 3.00 \cdot 10^{8}}{4.05 \cdot 10^{-7} \cdot 1.60 \cdot 10^{-19}}\,\mathrm{eV} \approx 3.07\,\mathrm{eV} \\[6pt]
K_{\max} &= 3.07\,\mathrm{eV} - 2.01\,\mathrm{eV} \approx 1.06\,\mathrm{eV}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
V_0 \approx 1.06\,\mathrm{V} \\[6pt]
v_{\max} &= \sqrt{\frac{2 K_{\max}}{m_e}} = \sqrt{\frac{2 \cdot 1.06 \cdot 1.60 \cdot 10^{-19}}{9.11 \cdot 10^{-31}}}\,\mathrm{m/s} \approx 6.1 \cdot 10^{5}\,\mathrm{m/s}
\end{aligned}
$$

**d)** Doubled power.

$$
I_s' = 2 I_s \approx 1.0 \cdot 10^{2}\,\mu\mathrm{A} ,
\qquad
V_0' = V_0 \approx 1.06\,\mathrm{V}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

**a)** Each frequency is $c$ divided by the wavelength in meters. The frequencies are not equally spaced, so to test for a straight line, calculate the slopes between consecutive points. The values of $\dfrac{0.23}{0.545 \cdot 10^{14}}$, $\dfrac{0.27}{0.667 \cdot 10^{14}}$, and $\dfrac{0.35}{0.833 \cdot 10^{14}}$ are about $4.2$, $4.0$, and $4.2 \cdot 10^{-15}\,\mathrm{V\cdot s}$, equal within the precision of the measurements. The points therefore lie on a straight line, as predicted by the result on **stopping potential as a function of frequency**, $V_0 = \dfrac{h}{e} f - \dfrac{W_0}{e}$.

Using the two endpoints gives a slope of about $4.16 \cdot 10^{-15}\,\mathrm{V\cdot s}$. Multiplying by $e$ gives $h \approx 6.65 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\cdot s}$, which differs from the accepted value by about $0.3\%$. The point at $400\,\mathrm{nm}$ gives $\dfrac{W_0}{e} \approx 2.01\,\mathrm{V}$, so the work function is $2.01\,\mathrm{eV}$. The threshold frequency is the point where the line crosses the frequency axis, $f_0 = \dfrac{W_0 / e}{h / e} \approx 4.83 \cdot 10^{14}\,\mathrm{Hz}$, and the threshold wavelength is $\dfrac{c}{f_0} \approx 621\,\mathrm{nm}$.

**b)** The red laser wavelength, $650\,\mathrm{nm}$, exceeds the threshold wavelength, and its photons have energy $1.91\,\mathrm{eV}$, less than the work function. Each electron absorbs only one photon, so none receives enough energy to escape. The red laser therefore **does not produce** the photoelectric effect, regardless of its power.

**c)** The number of photons arriving per second is the power divided by the energy of one photon, $\dfrac{P \lambda}{hc} \approx 4.07 \cdot 10^{15}$. A quantum efficiency of $8.0\%$ means that only $8$ out of every $100$ photons eject an electron, so the number of electrons emitted per second is $0.080 \cdot 4.07 \cdot 10^{15}$. At saturation all of them reach the anode, each carrying charge of magnitude $e$. The saturation current is therefore about $52\,\mu\mathrm{A}$.

By **Einstein’s equation**, the maximum kinetic energy equals the photon energy, $3.07\,\mathrm{eV}$, minus the work function found in part a), giving $1.06\,\mathrm{eV}$. Since $eV_0 = K_{\max}$, the stopping potential is $1.06\,\mathrm{V}$. The maximum speed follows from $K_{\max} = \dfrac{1}{2} m_e v_{\max}^2$, with the energy in joules, and is about $6.1 \cdot 10^{5}\,\mathrm{m/s}$. This speed is $0.2\%$ of $c$, so the classical kinetic energy formula is justified.

**d)** Doubling the power at fixed wavelength doubles the number of photons per second, while the energy of each photon stays the same. With the same quantum efficiency this doubles the number of electrons emitted, and the saturation current becomes about $1.0 \cdot 10^{2}\,\mu\mathrm{A}$. The maximum kinetic energy depends only on $hf$ and $W_0$, so the stopping potential remains $1.06\,\mathrm{V}$.

In classical wave theory the intensity of a wave is proportional to the square of its electric field amplitude. Doubling the power over the same area increases the field amplitude by a factor of $\sqrt{2}$, and with it the force $e E$ on the electrons. Classical wave theory therefore predicts faster electrons and a higher stopping potential. The measured stopping potential, however, is **unchanged**. Photons explain this result because the power determines the number of photons arriving per second while the frequency determines each photon’s energy.

</div>
