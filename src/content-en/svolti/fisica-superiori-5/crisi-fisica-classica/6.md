## Exercise 1

Calculate the momentum of an orange-light photon with $\lambda_1 = 600\,\mathrm{nm}$, the momentum of an X-ray photon with $\lambda_2 = 0.100\,\mathrm{nm}$, and their ratio. Determine the speed of an electron with the same momentum as the X-ray photon, checking that classical mechanics is applicable. Use $h = 6.63 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\cdot s}$, $m_e = 9.11 \cdot 10^{-31}\,\mathrm{kg}$, and $c = 3.00 \cdot 10^{8}\,\mathrm{m/s}$.

$$
p = \frac{h}{\lambda} ,
\qquad
\frac{p_2}{p_1} = \frac{\lambda_1}{\lambda_2} ,
\qquad
v = \frac{p_2}{m_e}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
p_1 &= \frac{6.63 \cdot 10^{-34}}{6.00 \cdot 10^{-7}}\,\mathrm{kg\cdot m/s} \approx 1.11 \cdot 10^{-27}\,\mathrm{kg\cdot m/s} \\[6pt]
p_2 &= \frac{6.63 \cdot 10^{-34}}{1.00 \cdot 10^{-10}}\,\mathrm{kg\cdot m/s} = 6.63 \cdot 10^{-24}\,\mathrm{kg\cdot m/s} \\[6pt]
\frac{p_2}{p_1} &= \frac{600\,\mathrm{nm}}{0.100\,\mathrm{nm}} = 6.00 \cdot 10^{3} \\[6pt]
v &= \frac{6.63 \cdot 10^{-24}}{9.11 \cdot 10^{-31}}\,\mathrm{m/s} \approx 7.28 \cdot 10^{6}\,\mathrm{m/s} \approx 0.024\, c
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

By the relation for **photon momentum**, $p = \dfrac{h}{\lambda}$, so momentum is inversely proportional to wavelength. The X-ray photon has a wavelength smaller by a factor of $6000$ and therefore a momentum larger by a factor of $6000$.

In classical mechanics, an electron with the same momentum has speed $v = \dfrac{p}{m_e}$, or about $7.3 \cdot 10^{6}\,\mathrm{m/s}$. This is $2.4\%$ of $c$, so $v \ll c$. The Lorentz factor differs from $1$ by less than three parts in ten thousand, and the classical formula $p = m_e v$ is justified. An X-ray photon therefore has momentum comparable to that of a fast electron. In a collision with an X-ray photon, an electron can thus acquire a measurable speed, as in the Compton effect.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A helium–neon laser emits a beam of power $P = 2.0\,\mathrm{mW}$ and wavelength $\lambda = 633\,\mathrm{nm}$ incident normally on a surface. Calculate the number of photons arriving per second, the momentum of one photon, and the force exerted by the beam if the surface absorbs every photon. Then calculate the force if the surface is a mirror that reflects every photon. Use $h = 6.63 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\cdot s}$ and $c = 3.00 \cdot 10^{8}\,\mathrm{m/s}$.

::grafico{assi=0 uguale=1 linee="3,-1.2 3,1.2" poligoni="3,-1.2 3.3,-1.2 3.3,1.2 3,1.2" vettori="0,0.5,2.9,0.5,p;2.9,-0.5,0,-0.5,−p" etichette="1.45,0.85,incident photon;1.45,-0.15,reflected photon" xmin=-0.5 xmax=4 ymin=-1.5 ymax=1.5 legenda=0 titolo="A photon reflected by the mirror changes its momentum from $\vec{p}$ to $-\vec{p}$, transferring momentum $2p$ to the mirror"}

$$
\frac{N}{\Delta t} = \frac{P \lambda}{h c} ,
\qquad
p = \frac{h}{\lambda} ,
\qquad
F_{\text{abs}} = \frac{N}{\Delta t}\, p = \frac{P}{c} ,
\qquad
F_{\text{refl}} = \frac{N}{\Delta t}\, 2p = \frac{2P}{c}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{N}{\Delta t} &= \frac{2.0 \cdot 10^{-3} \cdot 6.33 \cdot 10^{-7}}{6.63 \cdot 10^{-34} \cdot 3.00 \cdot 10^{8}}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}} \approx 6.4 \cdot 10^{15}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}} \\[6pt]
p &= \frac{6.63 \cdot 10^{-34}}{6.33 \cdot 10^{-7}}\,\mathrm{kg\cdot m/s} \approx 1.05 \cdot 10^{-27}\,\mathrm{kg\cdot m/s} \\[6pt]
F_{\text{abs}} &= \frac{2.0 \cdot 10^{-3}}{3.00 \cdot 10^{8}}\,\mathrm{N} \approx 6.7 \cdot 10^{-12}\,\mathrm{N} \\[6pt]
F_{\text{refl}} &= \frac{2 \cdot 2.0 \cdot 10^{-3}}{3.00 \cdot 10^{8}}\,\mathrm{N} \approx 1.3 \cdot 10^{-11}\,\mathrm{N}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The number of photons per second is the power divided by the energy of one photon, $\dfrac{hc}{\lambda}$, and equals about $6.4 \cdot 10^{15}$. Each photon has momentum $\dfrac{h}{\lambda}$.

A surface that absorbs a photon receives its entire momentum $p$. By the impulse–momentum theorem, the average force equals the momentum transferred per second: the number of photons per second times $p$. In the product $\dfrac{P\lambda}{hc} \cdot \dfrac{h}{\lambda}$, the factors $h$ and $\lambda$ cancel, so the force equals $\dfrac{P}{c}$ and is independent of wavelength.

The mirror reflects the photon back with the opposite momentum. The magnitude of the change in photon momentum is therefore $2p$. By conservation of momentum, the mirror receives an equal and opposite momentum change. The force **doubles**, reaching about $1.3 \cdot 10^{-11}\,\mathrm{N}$. Such a small force requires a very sensitive torsion balance to measure.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A sodium atom of mass $m = 3.82 \cdot 10^{-26}\,\mathrm{kg}$ moves at speed $v = 600\,\mathrm{m/s}$ and encounters a laser beam of wavelength $\lambda = 589\,\mathrm{nm}$ traveling in the opposite direction. Each time the atom absorbs a photon from the beam, its momentum decreases by the magnitude of the photon’s momentum. Calculate the change in the atom’s speed per photon absorbed and the number of photons it must absorb to stop. Use $h = 6.63 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\cdot s}$.

$$
\Delta p = \frac{h}{\lambda} ,
\qquad
\Delta v = \frac{\Delta p}{m} = \frac{h}{m \lambda} ,
\qquad
N = \frac{v}{\Delta v}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta p &= \frac{6.63 \cdot 10^{-34}}{5.89 \cdot 10^{-7}}\,\mathrm{kg\cdot m/s} \approx 1.13 \cdot 10^{-27}\,\mathrm{kg\cdot m/s} \\[6pt]
\Delta v &= \frac{1.126 \cdot 10^{-27}}{3.82 \cdot 10^{-26}}\,\mathrm{m/s} \approx 2.95 \cdot 10^{-2}\,\mathrm{m/s} \\[6pt]
N &= \frac{600}{2.95 \cdot 10^{-2}} \approx 2.0 \cdot 10^{4}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

During absorption the atom and photon form an isolated system, so total momentum is conserved. The photon travels opposite to the atom, and its momentum $\dfrac{h}{\lambda}$ therefore points opposite to the atom’s momentum. After absorption the photon is gone and the atom has momentum $mv - \dfrac{h}{\lambda}$, so its speed decreases by $\dfrac{h}{m \lambda}$, about $3\,\mathrm{cm/s}$.

Each photon reduces the speed by the same amount, so the number of photons needed to stop the atom is its initial speed divided by $\Delta v$, about $2.0 \cdot 10^{4}$. The atom subsequently emits photons in random directions, so on average the resulting recoil momenta cancel. This is the principle behind **laser cooling** of atoms.

</div>
