## Exercise 1

In a photocell experiment, the stopping potential is measured as $V_1 = 0.28\,\mathrm{V}$ at a light frequency of $f_1 = 6.00 \cdot 10^{14}\,\mathrm{Hz}$ and $V_2 = 1.11\,\mathrm{V}$ at $f_2 = 8.00 \cdot 10^{14}\,\mathrm{Hz}$. Use these measurements to determine Planck’s constant, the cathode work function, and the threshold frequency. Use $e = 1.60 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{C}$ and $c = 3.00 \cdot 10^{8}\,\mathrm{m/s}$.

$$
V_0 = \frac{h}{e}\, f - \frac{W_0}{e}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\frac{h}{e} = \frac{V_2 - V_1}{f_2 - f_1} ,
\qquad
\frac{W_0}{e} = \frac{h}{e}\, f_1 - V_1 ,
\qquad
f_0 = \frac{W_0}{h}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{h}{e} &= \frac{1.11 - 0.28}{2.00 \cdot 10^{14}}\,\mathrm{V\cdot s} = 4.15 \cdot 10^{-15}\,\mathrm{V\cdot s} \\[6pt]
h &= 1.60 \cdot 10^{-19} \cdot 4.15 \cdot 10^{-15}\,\mathrm{J\cdot s} \approx 6.64 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\cdot s} \\[6pt]
\frac{W_0}{e} &= 4.15 \cdot 10^{-15} \cdot 6.00 \cdot 10^{14}\,\mathrm{V} - 0.28\,\mathrm{V} = 2.49\,\mathrm{V} - 0.28\,\mathrm{V} = 2.21\,\mathrm{V}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
W_0 \approx 2.21\,\mathrm{eV} \\[6pt]
f_0 &= \frac{2.21\,\mathrm{V}}{4.15 \cdot 10^{-15}\,\mathrm{V\cdot s}} \approx 5.33 \cdot 10^{14}\,\mathrm{Hz}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The stopping potential is a linear function of frequency with slope $\dfrac{h}{e}$, as established in the result on **stopping potential as a function of frequency**. Two points determine the line, and its slope is the change in $V_0$ divided by the change in $f$, or $4.15 \cdot 10^{-15}\,\mathrm{V\cdot s}$. Multiplying by $e$ gives $h \approx 6.64 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\cdot s}$, which differs from the accepted value $6.63 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\cdot s}$ by about $0.2\%$, within the measurement uncertainty.

The work function can be found from either point. The equation of the line $V_0 = \dfrac{h}{e} f - \dfrac{W_0}{e}$ gives $\dfrac{W_0}{e} = \dfrac{h}{e} f_1 - V_1 = 2.21\,\mathrm{V}$, so $W_0 = 2.21\,\mathrm{eV}$. The second point gives the same value because $4.15 \cdot 10^{-15} \cdot 8.00 \cdot 10^{14} - 1.11 = 2.21$.

The threshold frequency is the point where the line crosses the frequency axis, that is, the frequency for which $V_0 = 0$. The ratio $\dfrac{W_0}{h}$ equals $\dfrac{W_0 / e}{h / e}$, so it can be calculated with the two quantities in volts and volts per hertz (equivalently, volt-seconds), respectively, without converting back to joules.

</div>

## Exercise 2

The table gives the stopping potential measured for a metal cathode at four different frequencies.

| $f$ ($10^{14}\,\mathrm{Hz}$) | $6.00$ | $7.00$ | $8.00$ | $9.00$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $V_0$ ($\mathrm{V}$) | $0.39$ | $0.80$ | $1.22$ | $1.63$ |

Verify that the points lie on a straight line. Use the line to determine Planck’s constant, the threshold frequency, the work function, and the threshold wavelength. Use $e = 1.60 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{C}$ and $c = 3.00 \cdot 10^{8}\,\mathrm{m/s}$.

::grafico{f="(x>=5.06?0.4133*x-2.090:NaN)" segmenti="5.06,0,0,-2.09" punti="6,0.39,pieno;7,0.80,pieno;8,1.22,pieno;9,1.63,pieno;5.06,0,vuoto,f₀" xmin=-0.5 xmax=10.5 ymin=-2.5 ymax=2.5 nomi="f,V₀" legenda=0 titolo="Stopping potential $V_0$ in volts versus frequency $f$ in units of $10^{14}\,\mathrm{Hz}$. The four points lie on a straight line that crosses the frequency axis at $f_0$. Its extension crosses the $V_0$-axis at $-\dfrac{W_0}{e}$"}

$$
\frac{h}{e} = \frac{V_0(9.00) - V_0(6.00)}{(9.00 - 6.00) \cdot 10^{14}\,\mathrm{Hz}} = \frac{1.63 - 0.39}{3.00 \cdot 10^{14}}\,\mathrm{V\cdot s} \approx 4.13 \cdot 10^{-15}\,\mathrm{V\cdot s}
$$
$$
h = 1.60 \cdot 10^{-19} \cdot 4.13 \cdot 10^{-15}\,\mathrm{J\cdot s} \approx 6.61 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\cdot s}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{W_0}{e} &= 4.133 \cdot 10^{-15} \cdot 6.00 \cdot 10^{14}\,\mathrm{V} - 0.39\,\mathrm{V} \approx 2.09\,\mathrm{V}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
W_0 \approx 2.09\,\mathrm{eV} \\[6pt]
f_0 &= \frac{2.09}{4.133 \cdot 10^{-15}}\,\mathrm{Hz} \approx 5.06 \cdot 10^{14}\,\mathrm{Hz} \\[6pt]
\lambda_0 &= \frac{3.00 \cdot 10^{8}}{5.06 \cdot 10^{14}}\,\mathrm{m} \approx 5.93 \cdot 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{m} = 593\,\mathrm{nm}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The frequencies increase in equal steps of $1.00 \cdot 10^{14}\,\mathrm{Hz}$, and the stopping potentials increase by $0.41$, $0.42$, and $0.41\,\mathrm{V}$. These increments are equal within the precision of the measurements, so the points **lie on a straight line**, as predicted by the relation $V_0 = \dfrac{h}{e} f - \dfrac{W_0}{e}$.

Calculate the slope using the two endpoints. They are farthest apart, making the result least sensitive to errors in individual measurements. Multiplying the slope by $e$ gives $h \approx 6.61 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\cdot s}$, which differs from the accepted value by about $0.3\%$.

The first pair of measurements gives $\dfrac{W_0}{e} \approx 2.09\,\mathrm{V}$, so $W_0 \approx 2.09\,\mathrm{eV}$, a value close to that for cesium. The threshold frequency is the ratio $\dfrac{W_0 / e}{h / e}$, about $5.06 \cdot 10^{14}\,\mathrm{Hz}$, and the threshold wavelength is $\dfrac{c}{f_0} \approx 593\,\mathrm{nm}$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Two photocells, one with a sodium cathode ($W_0 = 2.30\,\mathrm{eV}$) and the other with a zinc cathode ($W_0 = 4.30\,\mathrm{eV}$), are illuminated by ultraviolet radiation of frequency $f = 1.20 \cdot 10^{15}\,\mathrm{Hz}$. Calculate their stopping potentials and threshold frequencies. Explain why the lines for the two metals are parallel on a graph of $V_0$ versus $f$ and determine their vertical separation. Use $h = 6.63 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\cdot s}$ and $e = 1.60 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{C}$.

$$
V_0 = \frac{h f}{e} - \frac{W_0}{e} ,
\qquad
f_0 = \frac{W_0}{h}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{h f}{e} &= \frac{6.63 \cdot 10^{-34} \cdot 1.20 \cdot 10^{15}}{1.60 \cdot 10^{-19}}\,\mathrm{V} \approx 4.97\,\mathrm{V} \\[6pt]
V_{0,\text{Na}} &= 4.97\,\mathrm{V} - 2.30\,\mathrm{V} \approx 2.67\,\mathrm{V} ,
\qquad
V_{0,\text{Zn}} = 4.97\,\mathrm{V} - 4.30\,\mathrm{V} \approx 0.67\,\mathrm{V} \\[6pt]
f_{0,\text{Na}} &= \frac{2.30 \cdot 1.60 \cdot 10^{-19}}{6.63 \cdot 10^{-34}}\,\mathrm{Hz} \approx 5.55 \cdot 10^{14}\,\mathrm{Hz} ,
\qquad
f_{0,\text{Zn}} = \frac{4.30 \cdot 1.60 \cdot 10^{-19}}{6.63 \cdot 10^{-34}}\,\mathrm{Hz} \approx 1.04 \cdot 10^{15}\,\mathrm{Hz}
\end{aligned}
$$
$$
V_{0,\text{Na}} - V_{0,\text{Zn}} = \frac{W_{0,\text{Zn}} - W_{0,\text{Na}}}{e} = 2.00\,\mathrm{V}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The frequency $1.20 \cdot 10^{15}\,\mathrm{Hz}$ is above the threshold frequencies of both metals, so both photocells emit electrons. The photon energy in electronvolts is $4.97\,\mathrm{eV}$. By **Einstein’s equation**, the stopping potential in volts equals this energy minus the work function expressed in electronvolts. Sodium has the lower work function, so it gives the higher stopping potential.

In the relation $V_0 = \dfrac{h}{e} f - \dfrac{W_0}{e}$, the slope $\dfrac{h}{e}$ contains only universal constants and is independent of the metal, so the lines for the two metals are **parallel**. Only the intercept $-\dfrac{W_0}{e}$ changes. At any given frequency the difference in stopping potentials therefore equals the difference in work functions divided by $e$, or $2.00\,\mathrm{V}$, as the calculated values confirm: $2.67 - 0.67 = 2.00$.

</div>
