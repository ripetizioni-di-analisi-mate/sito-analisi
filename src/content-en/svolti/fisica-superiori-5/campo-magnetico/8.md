## Exercise 1

A rigid rectangular coil of $10$ turns hangs vertically from a spring scale. Its horizontal lower side, of length $4.0\,\mathrm{cm}$, lies in a region with a uniform horizontal magnetic field perpendicular to the side and directed into the page. The upper side is outside the field. When the coil carries a current $I = 2.0\,\mathrm{A}$, the scale reading increases by $0.048\,\mathrm{N}$.

- Determine the field magnitude and the direction of the current along the lower side.
- Explain what the scale reads if the current is reversed.
- The scale can detect changes of $1.0\,\mathrm{mN}$. Calculate the smallest current that produces a measurable change in the reading.

::grafico{assi=0 uguale=1 poligoni="-2.2,-2.6 2.2,-2.6 2.2,-0.6 -2.2,-0.6" linee="-1,-1.6 1,-1.6 1,1.2 -1,1.2 -1,-1.6;0,1.2 0,2.2" vettori="0.7,-1.3,-0.7,-1.3,I;0,-1.6,0,-2.5,F" etichette="-1.8,-1.1,⊗;-1.8,-2.2,⊗;-0.6,-1.1,⊗;0.4,-1.1,⊗;1.6,-1.1,⊗;1.6,-2.2,⊗;0.15,2.3,spring scale" xmin=-2.4 xmax=2.4 ymin=-2.8 ymax=2.6 titolo="Current balance. The lower side of the coil is in a field directed into the page ($\otimes$). With the current flowing to the left, it experiences a downward force $\vec{F}$"}

$$
F = N I \ell B
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
B = \frac{F}{N I \ell} ,
\qquad
I_{\min} = \frac{F_{\min}}{N \ell B}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
B &= \frac{0.048}{10 \cdot 2.0 \cdot 0.040}\,\mathrm{T} = \frac{0.048}{0.80}\,\mathrm{T} = 0.060\,\mathrm{T} \\[6pt]
I_{\min} &= \frac{1.0 \cdot 10^{-3}}{10 \cdot 0.040 \cdot 0.060}\,\mathrm{A} = \frac{1.0 \cdot 10^{-3}}{2.4 \cdot 10^{-2}}\,\mathrm{A} \approx 4.2 \cdot 10^{-2}\,\mathrm{A}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Consider the magnetic forces on the sides of the coil. The upper side lies outside the field and experiences no magnetic force. The portions of the vertical sides inside the field carry currents in opposite directions. By the law for the **force on a straight wire**, they experience opposite horizontal forces that cancel. This leaves the force on the lower side, which is perpendicular to the field. Each of the $10$ turns contributes $I \ell B$, so the total force is $N I \ell B$ and is vertical. The increased scale reading shows that the force points downward. Solving $N I \ell B = 0.048\,\mathrm{N}$ gives $B = 0.060\,\mathrm{T}$.

Apply the **right-hand rule** with the fingers pointing into the page and the palm facing down. The thumb points to the left, so the current in the lower side flows to the left.

Reversing the current also reverses the force on the lower side, which now points upward with the same magnitude. The spring scale then reads the weight of the coil **reduced** by $0.048\,\mathrm{N}$.

The change in the reading equals the magnetic force $N I \ell B$, which is proportional to the current. The smallest measurable change is $1.0 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{N}$, so the minimum current is $I_{\min} = \dfrac{F_{\min}}{N \ell B} \approx 42\,\mathrm{mA}$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A horizontal conducting rod of length $\ell = 15\,\mathrm{cm}$ and mass $m = 20\,\mathrm{g}$ is suspended from two identical, lightweight vertical conducting wires. The wires are attached to a horizontal axis parallel to the rod and can rotate about that axis. The system is in a uniform upward vertical magnetic field of unknown magnitude $B$. When the rod carries a current $I$, it moves sideways and reaches equilibrium with the wires inclined at an angle $\alpha$ to the vertical. The following values are measured in the laboratory.

| $I$ ($\mathrm{A}$) | $1.0$ | $2.0$ | $3.0$ | $4.0$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $\alpha$ | $10.9^\circ$ | $20.7^\circ$ | $30.0^\circ$ | $37.3^\circ$ |

1. Explain why the magnetic force on the rod is horizontal and show that $\tan\alpha = \dfrac{I \ell B}{m g}$ at equilibrium.
2. Plot $\tan\alpha$ as a function of $I$ and use the graph to determine the magnetic field magnitude.
3. Calculate the tension in each wire when the current is $3.0\,\mathrm{A}$.
4. Calculate the current required for the wires to make an angle of $45^\circ$ with the vertical.

Use $g = 9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$.

::grafico{assi=0 uguale=1 linee="-0.6,0 0.6,0;0,0 0.99,-1.73" segmenti="0,0,0,-2" vettori="0.99,-1.73,0.99,-3.23,mg;0.99,-1.73,1.85,-1.73,F;0.99,-1.73,0.13,-0.23,T;-1.6,-3,-1.6,-1.8,B" punti="0,0,pieno;0.99,-1.73,pieno" etichette="0.12,-0.75,α;1.1,-1.5,⊗" xmin=-2 xmax=2.4 ymin=-3.5 ymax=0.4 titolo="View along the rod ($\otimes$, current into the page). At equilibrium, the weight $m\vec{g}$, the horizontal magnetic force $\vec{F}$, and the total tension $\vec{T}$ in the wires sum to zero"}

**Part 1**

$$
\vec{F} = I\,\vec{\ell} \times \vec{B} ,
\qquad
\vec{\ell} \perp \vec{B}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
F = I \ell B
$$
$$
\begin{cases}
T \sin\alpha = I \ell B \\[4pt]
T \cos\alpha = m g
\end{cases}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\tan\alpha = \frac{I \ell B}{m g}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The rod is horizontal and the field is vertical, so they are perpendicular. By the law for the **force on a straight wire**, the magnetic force has magnitude $I \ell B$ and is perpendicular to both the rod and the field. A vector perpendicular to the vertical field is horizontal, and a vector perpendicular to the rod lies in a plane perpendicular to it. Thus the force is *horizontal* and displaces the rod sideways, rotating the wires about the axis. In the diagram, the current flows into the page. By the **right-hand rule**, with the thumb pointing into the page and the fingers up, the palm faces right, in the direction of the displacement.

The suspension wires also carry current, in opposite directions in the two wires. Each wire lies in a plane perpendicular to the rod. The force on it, perpendicular to the wire and the field, is therefore parallel to the rod. The two forces are opposite and parallel to the rotation axis, so they do not change the inclination of the wires.

The forces acting on the rod are its weight, the magnetic force, and the tensions in the two wires. Their combined tension $\vec{T}$ points along the wires. At equilibrium the vector sum of the forces is zero. The horizontal component $T \sin\alpha$ balances the magnetic force, and the vertical component $T \cos\alpha$ balances the weight. Both conditions hold simultaneously. Dividing the corresponding sides of the first equation by those of the second gives $\tan\alpha = \dfrac{I \ell B}{m g}$.

</div>

**Part 2**

| $I$ ($\mathrm{A}$) | $1.0$ | $2.0$ | $3.0$ | $4.0$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $\tan\alpha$ | $0.193$ | $0.378$ | $0.577$ | $0.762$ |

::grafico{f="0.191*x" punti="1,0.193,pieno;2,0.378,pieno;3,0.577,pieno;4,0.762,pieno" xmin=-1 xmax=5 ymin=-0.1 ymax=1 nomi="I,tan α" legenda="\tan\alpha = 0.191\,I" titolo="Tangent of the wires' angle as a function of current in amperes. The experimental points lie close to a straight line through the origin with slope $0.191\,\mathrm{A^{-1}}$"}

$$
\tan\alpha = \frac{\ell B}{m g}\,I
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\text{slope} = \frac{\ell B}{m g}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
B = \frac{m g}{\ell} \cdot \text{slope}
$$
$$
\text{slope} = \frac{0.764 - 0}{4.0\,\mathrm{A} - 0} \approx 0.191\,\mathrm{A^{-1}}
$$
$$
B = \frac{0.020 \cdot 9.81}{0.15} \cdot 0.191\,\mathrm{T} \approx 0.25\,\mathrm{T}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Use a calculator to find the tangent of each measured angle. By the relation derived in part 1, for a fixed mass, length, and field, $\tan\alpha$ is directly proportional to $I$ with proportionality constant $\dfrac{\ell B}{m g}$. The graph of $\tan\alpha$ against $I$ should therefore be a straight line through the origin. The experimental points do indeed lie close to such a line.

The line through the origin that best fits the points has a slope of about $0.191\,\mathrm{A^{-1}}$. For example, it passes through the point with horizontal coordinate $4.0\,\mathrm{A}$ and vertical coordinate $0.764$. The tangent is dimensionless, so the slope is measured in $\mathrm{A^{-1}}$. Since the slope equals $\dfrac{\ell B}{m g}$, multiplying it by $\dfrac{m g}{\ell}$ gives $B \approx 0.25\,\mathrm{T}$. The units are consistent because $\dfrac{\mathrm{N}}{\mathrm{m}} \cdot \dfrac{1}{\mathrm{A}} = \mathrm{T}$ by the definition of the tesla.

</div>

**Part 3**

$$
T = \sqrt{(m g)^2 + (I \ell B)^2} ,
\qquad
T_1 = \frac{T}{2}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
m g &= 0.020 \cdot 9.81\,\mathrm{N} \approx 0.196\,\mathrm{N} ,
\qquad
I \ell B = 3.0 \cdot 0.15 \cdot 0.25\,\mathrm{N} \approx 0.113\,\mathrm{N} \\[6pt]
T &= \sqrt{0.196^2 + 0.113^2}\,\mathrm{N} \approx 0.226\,\mathrm{N} ,
\qquad
T_1 \approx 0.11\,\mathrm{N}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

At equilibrium the total tension in the wires balances the sum of the weight and the magnetic force. These two vectors are perpendicular, one vertical and the other horizontal. The Pythagorean theorem therefore gives the magnitude of their sum as approximately $0.226\,\mathrm{N}$. The two wires are identical and arranged symmetrically about the center of the rod, so each carries half the total force, or about $0.11\,\mathrm{N}$. With the field found in part 2, the predicted angle at $I = 3.0\,\mathrm{A}$ is $\arctan \dfrac{0.113}{0.196} \approx 29.8^\circ$, in agreement with the measured value of $30.0^\circ$.

</div>

**Part 4**

$$
\tan 45^\circ = 1
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
I \ell B = m g
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
I = \frac{m g}{\ell B}
$$
$$
I = \frac{0.196}{0.15 \cdot 0.25}\,\mathrm{A} = \frac{0.196}{0.0375}\,\mathrm{A} \approx 5.2\,\mathrm{A}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

At an angle of $45^\circ$, the tangent is $1$, so the relation from part 1 requires the magnetic force to equal the weight. Solving $I \ell B = m g$ gives $I \approx 5.2\,\mathrm{A}$. The result agrees with the graph in part 2 because the line of slope $0.191\,\mathrm{A^{-1}}$ reaches $1$ at $I = \dfrac{1}{0.191}\,\mathrm{A} \approx 5.2\,\mathrm{A}$.

</div>
