## Exercise 1

A copper wire of cross-sectional area $S = 1.0\,\mathrm{mm^2}$ carries a current $I = 4.0\,\mathrm{A}$. A segment of length $\ell = 10\,\mathrm{cm}$ is in a uniform magnetic field $B = 0.50\,\mathrm{T}$ perpendicular to the wire. Copper contains $n = 8.5 \cdot 10^{28}$ conduction electrons per cubic meter.

- Calculate the force on the wire segment.
- Calculate the number of conduction electrons in the segment and the average force on each electron.
- Calculate the drift speed of the electrons and verify that the Lorentz force on one electron agrees with the previous result.

Use $e = 1.60 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{C}$.

$$
F = I \ell B ,
\qquad
N = n S \ell ,
\qquad
I = \frac{N e}{\Delta t} = \frac{n S \ell\, e}{\dfrac{\ell}{v_d}} = n e S v_d
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
v_d = \frac{I}{n e S}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
F &= 4.0 \cdot 0.10 \cdot 0.50\,\mathrm{N} = 0.20\,\mathrm{N} \\[6pt]
N &= 8.5 \cdot 10^{28} \cdot 1.0 \cdot 10^{-6} \cdot 0.10 = 8.5 \cdot 10^{21} \\[6pt]
\frac{F}{N} &= \frac{0.20\,\mathrm{N}}{8.5 \cdot 10^{21}} \approx 2.4 \cdot 10^{-23}\,\mathrm{N}
\end{aligned}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
v_d &= \frac{4.0}{8.5 \cdot 10^{28} \cdot 1.60 \cdot 10^{-19} \cdot 1.0 \cdot 10^{-6}}\,\mathrm{m/s} \approx 2.9 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m/s} \\[6pt]
e v_d B &= 1.60 \cdot 10^{-19} \cdot 2.94 \cdot 10^{-4} \cdot 0.50\,\mathrm{N} \approx 2.4 \cdot 10^{-23}\,\mathrm{N}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The segment is straight and perpendicular to the uniform field, so the law for the **force on a straight wire** gives $I \ell B = 0.20\,\mathrm{N}$.

The volume of the segment is $S \ell$, with the cross-sectional area expressed in square meters, $1.0\,\mathrm{mm^2} = 1.0 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\mathrm{m^2}$. The number of electrons is the number density $n$ multiplied by the volume, or $8.5 \cdot 10^{21}$. The force on the wire is the sum of the forces on the electrons, so the average force on each electron is the ratio $\dfrac{F}{N} \approx 2.4 \cdot 10^{-23}\,\mathrm{N}$.

For the drift speed, use the reasoning in the derivation of the **Lorentz force**. The electrons in the segment pass through the cross section at its far end in a time $\Delta t = \dfrac{\ell}{v_d}$, so the current is the charge $N e$ divided by this time, giving $I = n e S v_d$. Solving for $v_d$ gives about $2.9 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m/s}$, less than one millimeter per second.

The electrons move perpendicular to the field, so each experiences a Lorentz force $e v_d B \approx 2.4 \cdot 10^{-23}\,\mathrm{N}$. This value **agrees** with the average force calculated from the force on the wire, as predicted by the relation $I \ell = N e v_d$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A beam of electrons, all moving to the right with velocity $v = 1.0 \cdot 10^7\,\mathrm{m/s}$, carries a current $I = 2.0\,\mathrm{mA}$. The beam travels a distance $\ell = 4.0\,\mathrm{cm}$ through a region containing a uniform magnetic field $B = 5.0\,\mathrm{mT}$ directed into the page. Calculate the number of electrons present in the region at any instant, the total force on the electrons in the region, and the force on a single electron. Determine the direction of the force. Use $e = 1.60 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{C}$.

::grafico{assi=0 uguale=1 linee="-3,0 3,0" poligoni="-1,-1.4 1,-1.4 1,1.4 -1,1.4" vettori="-2.6,0.35,-1.4,0.35,v;0,0,0,-1.1,F;-1.4,-0.4,-2.6,-0.4,I" punti="0,0,pieno" etichette="-0.7,0.9,⊗;0.4,0.9,⊗;-0.7,-0.9,⊗;0.4,-0.9,⊗;-0.25,0.25,e⁻;-0.15,1.6,ℓ" xmin=-3 xmax=3 ymin=-1.8 ymax=1.9 titolo="Electron beam passing a distance $\ell$ through a region with a field directed into the page ($\otimes$). The current points opposite to the velocity, and the force on the electrons points downward"}

$$
I \ell = N e v
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
N = \frac{I \ell}{e v} ,
\qquad
F_{\text{tot}} = I \ell B ,
\qquad
F_1 = e v B
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
N &= \frac{2.0 \cdot 10^{-3} \cdot 0.040}{1.60 \cdot 10^{-19} \cdot 1.0 \cdot 10^7} = 5.0 \cdot 10^7 \\[6pt]
F_{\text{tot}} &= 2.0 \cdot 10^{-3} \cdot 0.040 \cdot 5.0 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{N} = 4.0 \cdot 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{N} \\[6pt]
F_1 &= 1.60 \cdot 10^{-19} \cdot 1.0 \cdot 10^7 \cdot 5.0 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{N} = 8.0 \cdot 10^{-15}\,\mathrm{N}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The beam behaves like a wire carrying a current $I$. The derivation of the **Lorentz force** gave the relation $I \ell = N q v$ between the current, the length of a segment, and the number of charges in that segment. Using $q = e$ for the charge magnitude gives $N = \dfrac{I \ell}{e v} = 5.0 \cdot 10^7$ electrons.

Since the beam is perpendicular to the field, the law for the **force on a straight wire** gives a total force of $4.0 \cdot 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{N}$. The Lorentz force on a single electron is $8.0 \cdot 10^{-15}\,\mathrm{N}$. Multiplying by the number of electrons recovers the total force, $5.0 \cdot 10^7 \cdot 8.0 \cdot 10^{-15}\,\mathrm{N} = 4.0 \cdot 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{N}$.

The electrons have negative charge and move to the right, so the beam current points to the left. Apply the right-hand rule to the current with the thumb pointing left and the fingers into the page. The palm faces down. The Lorentz force gives the same direction: with $\vec{v}$ pointing right, the rule gives $\vec{v} \times \vec{B}$ pointing upward, and the negative charge reverses the direction. The force on the electrons is therefore **directed downward**.

</div>

## Exercise 3

An electron initially at rest is accelerated through a potential difference $\Delta V = 200\,\mathrm{V}$ and enters a region with a uniform magnetic field $B = 5.0\,\mathrm{mT}$ perpendicular to its velocity. Calculate the electron's speed on entering the field and the Lorentz force it experiences. Explain what its kinetic energy and speed will be when it leaves the field region if only the magnetic force acts on it. Use $e = 1.60 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{C}$, $m_e = 9.11 \cdot 10^{-31}\,\mathrm{kg}$, and $c = 3.00 \cdot 10^{8}\,\mathrm{m/s}$.

$$
e\,\Delta V = \frac{1}{2} m_e v^2
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
v = \sqrt{\frac{2 e\,\Delta V}{m_e}} ,
\qquad
F = e v B
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
K &= e\,\Delta V = 1.60 \cdot 10^{-19} \cdot 200\,\mathrm{J} = 3.2 \cdot 10^{-17}\,\mathrm{J} \\[6pt]
v &= \sqrt{\frac{2 \cdot 3.2 \cdot 10^{-17}}{9.11 \cdot 10^{-31}}}\,\mathrm{m/s} \approx 8.4 \cdot 10^6\,\mathrm{m/s} \\[6pt]
F &= 1.60 \cdot 10^{-19} \cdot 8.38 \cdot 10^6 \cdot 5.0 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{N} \approx 6.7 \cdot 10^{-15}\,\mathrm{N}
\end{aligned}
$$
$$
\frac{v}{c} = \frac{8.4 \cdot 10^6}{3.00 \cdot 10^8} \approx 0.028
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

During acceleration, the electric field does work $e\,\Delta V$ on the electron. The electron starts from rest, so by the work–energy theorem its final kinetic energy is $K = e\,\Delta V = 3.2 \cdot 10^{-17}\,\mathrm{J}$, or $200\,\mathrm{eV}$. Solving $K = \dfrac{1}{2} m_e v^2$ gives $v \approx 8.4 \cdot 10^6\,\mathrm{m/s}$. This speed is $2.8\%$ of the speed of light, so $v \ll c$ and the classical expression for kinetic energy is appropriate.

In the magnetic field, the velocity is perpendicular to $\vec{B}$, so the **Lorentz force** on the electron is $e v B \approx 6.7 \cdot 10^{-15}\,\mathrm{N}$, perpendicular to its velocity.

The Lorentz force is always perpendicular to the velocity, so by the theorem on **zero work by the Lorentz force**, it does no work. If only the magnetic force acts, the kinetic energy does not change. The electron therefore leaves the field with **the same kinetic energy**, $200\,\mathrm{eV}$, and the same speed, $8.4 \cdot 10^6\,\mathrm{m/s}$. Only the direction of motion changes.

</div>
