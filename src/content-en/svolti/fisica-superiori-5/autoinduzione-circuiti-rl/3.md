## Exercise 1

An ideal source of emf $\mathcal{E} = 12\,\mathrm{V}$, a resistor $R = 4.0\,\mathrm{\Omega}$, and an ideal inductor $L = 12\,\mathrm{mH}$ are connected in series with a switch, which closes at time $t = 0$. Calculate the time constant, the steady-state current, and the rate at which the current rises immediately after the switch closes. Then determine the current and the voltages across the resistor and the inductor at time $t_1 = 6.0\,\mathrm{ms}$.

$$
\tau = \frac{L}{R} , \qquad
I = \frac{\mathcal{E}}{R} , \qquad
\left( \frac{di}{dt} \right)_{t = 0} = \frac{\mathcal{E}}{L} , \qquad
i(t) = I \left( 1 - e^{-\frac{t}{\tau}} \right) , \qquad
V_R = R\,i , \qquad
V_L = \mathcal{E} - V_R
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
\tau &= \frac{12 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{H}}{4.0\,\mathrm{\Omega}} = 3.0 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{s} , \qquad
I = \frac{12\,\mathrm{V}}{4.0\,\mathrm{\Omega}} = 3.0\,\mathrm{A} \\[6pt]
\left( \frac{di}{dt} \right)_{t = 0} &= \frac{12\,\mathrm{V}}{12 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{H}} = 1.0 \cdot 10^3\,\mathrm{A/s} \\[6pt]
i(t_1) &= 3.0 \cdot \left( 1 - e^{-\frac{6.0}{3.0}} \right)\,\mathrm{A} = 3.0 \cdot \left( 1 - e^{-2} \right)\,\mathrm{A} = 2.6\,\mathrm{A} \\[6pt]
V_R &= 4.0 \cdot 2.59\,\mathrm{V} = 10.4\,\mathrm{V} , \qquad
V_L = 12\,\mathrm{V} - 10.4\,\mathrm{V} = 1.6\,\mathrm{V}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

In this RL circuit the switch closes when the current is zero, so the expression for the **current when an RL circuit is switched on** applies. The time constant is $\dfrac{L}{R} = 3.0\,\mathrm{ms}$ and the current approaches its steady-state value $\dfrac{\mathcal{E}}{R} = 3.0\,\mathrm{A}$.

Just after the switch closes, the current is still zero, so the voltage $R\,i$ across the resistor is also zero. The circuit equation then gives the full source emf across the inductor, that is, $L\,\dfrac{di}{dt} = \mathcal{E}$, and the current rises at the rate $\dfrac{\mathcal{E}}{L} = 1000\,\mathrm{A/s}$.

Time $t_1 = 6.0\,\mathrm{ms}$ corresponds to two time constants, so the exponent is $-2$ and the current is $3.0 \cdot (1 - 0.135)\,\mathrm{A} = 2.6\,\mathrm{A}$. Ohm's law gives the voltage across the resistor. By the circuit equation, the two voltages add up to $\mathcal{E}$ at every instant, leaving $1.6\,\mathrm{V}$ across the inductor. The same value follows from $V_L = \mathcal{E}\,e^{-\frac{t}{\tau}} = 12 \cdot e^{-2}\,\mathrm{V} = 1.6\,\mathrm{V}$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

An RL circuit has $L = 0.10\,\mathrm{H}$ and $R = 2.0\,\mathrm{\Omega}$, and the switch closes at time $t = 0$. Calculate how long it takes for the current to reach $90\,\%$ of its steady-state value and how long it takes for the voltage across the resistor to equal the voltage across the inductor.

$$
\tau = \frac{L}{R} , \qquad
1 - e^{-\frac{t_1}{\tau}} = 0.90
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
e^{-\frac{t_1}{\tau}} = 0.10
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
t_1 = \tau \ln 10
$$
$$
V_R = V_L
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\mathcal{E} \left( 1 - e^{-\frac{t_2}{\tau}} \right) = \mathcal{E}\,e^{-\frac{t_2}{\tau}}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
e^{-\frac{t_2}{\tau}} = \frac{1}{2}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
t_2 = \tau \ln 2
$$
$$
\tau = \frac{0.10\,\mathrm{H}}{2.0\,\mathrm{\Omega}} = 0.050\,\mathrm{s} , \qquad
t_1 = 0.050 \cdot 2.303\,\mathrm{s} = 0.12\,\mathrm{s} , \qquad
t_2 = 0.050 \cdot 0.693\,\mathrm{s} = 0.035\,\mathrm{s}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

From the expression for the **current when an RL circuit is switched on**, the ratio of the current to its steady-state value is $1 - e^{-\frac{t}{\tau}}$. Set this ratio equal to $0.90$. The exponential factor must therefore equal $0.10$. Taking natural logarithms yields $-\dfrac{t_1}{\tau} = \ln 0.10 = -\ln 10$, so $t_1 = \tau \ln 10$, about $2.3$ time constants.

The voltage across the resistor is $R\,i = \mathcal{E} \left( 1 - e^{-\frac{t}{\tau}} \right)$, and the voltage across the inductor is $L\,\dfrac{di}{dt} = \mathcal{E}\,e^{-\frac{t}{\tau}}$. Equating the two expressions and dividing by $\mathcal{E}$ gives $1 = 2\,e^{-\frac{t_2}{\tau}}$, so the exponential factor equals $\dfrac{1}{2}$ and $t_2 = \tau \ln 2$. At that instant each voltage equals half the source emf. Neither result depends on $\mathcal{E}$ because the emf cancels.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A real coil has inductance $L = 0.50\,\mathrm{H}$ and resistance $R_b = 5.0\,\mathrm{\Omega}$. It is connected in series with a resistor $R_1 = 15\,\mathrm{\Omega}$ and an ideal source of emf $\mathcal{E} = 10\,\mathrm{V}$ when the switch closes at time $t = 0$. Calculate the time constant and the steady-state current. Determine the reading of a voltmeter connected across the coil at $t = \tau$ and in steady state.

$$
R = R_1 + R_b , \qquad
\tau = \frac{L}{R} , \qquad
I = \frac{\mathcal{E}}{R} , \qquad
V_{\text{coil}} = \mathcal{E} - R_1\,i
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
R &= 15\,\mathrm{\Omega} + 5.0\,\mathrm{\Omega} = 20\,\mathrm{\Omega} , \qquad
\tau = \frac{0.50\,\mathrm{H}}{20\,\mathrm{\Omega}} = 0.025\,\mathrm{s} , \qquad
I = \frac{10\,\mathrm{V}}{20\,\mathrm{\Omega}} = 0.50\,\mathrm{A} \\[6pt]
i(\tau) &= 0.50 \cdot \left( 1 - e^{-1} \right)\,\mathrm{A} = 0.316\,\mathrm{A} \\[6pt]
V_{\text{coil}}(\tau) &= 10\,\mathrm{V} - 15 \cdot 0.316\,\mathrm{V} = 5.3\,\mathrm{V} \\[6pt]
V_{\text{coil}}(\infty) &= 10\,\mathrm{V} - 15 \cdot 0.50\,\mathrm{V} = 2.5\,\mathrm{V}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

A real coil is equivalent to an ideal **inductor** in series with its own resistance. The total resistance of this RL circuit is therefore the sum $R_1 + R_b = 20\,\mathrm{\Omega}$. This sum determines both the time constant and the steady-state current.

The voltmeter across the coil measures the voltage across the ideal inductor plus the voltage across the resistance $R_b$. By the circuit equation, the source emf equals the sum of the voltage across the resistor $R_1$ and the voltage across the coil. The coil voltage is therefore found by subtracting $R_1\,i$ from $\mathcal{E}$. At $\tau$ the current is $63\,\%$ of its steady-state value, and the meter reads $5.3\,\mathrm{V}$.

In steady state the current is constant and the self-induced emf is zero, so the coil behaves like its resistance $R_b$ alone. The voltmeter then reads $R_b I = 5.0 \cdot 0.50\,\mathrm{V} = 2.5\,\mathrm{V}$, in agreement with the calculation. The steady-state voltage across an ideal inductor would be zero. The value $2.5\,\mathrm{V}$ can therefore be used to measure the coil resistance.

</div>
