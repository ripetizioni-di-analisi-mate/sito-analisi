## Exercise 1

An inductor of inductance $L = 0.40\,\mathrm{H}$ carries a current $I_1 = 2.5\,\mathrm{A}$. Calculate the stored energy. Then determine the current needed for the energy to reach $U_2 = 5.0\,\mathrm{J}$, and explain by what factor the energy changes when the current doubles.

$$
U = \frac{1}{2} L I^2 , \qquad
I_2 = \sqrt{\frac{2 U_2}{L}}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
U_1 &= \frac{1}{2} \cdot 0.40 \cdot 2.5^2\,\mathrm{J} = 1.25\,\mathrm{J} \approx 1.3\,\mathrm{J} \\[6pt]
I_2 &= \sqrt{\frac{2 \cdot 5.0}{0.40}}\,\mathrm{A} = \sqrt{25}\,\mathrm{A} = 5.0\,\mathrm{A}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

From the expression for the **energy stored in an inductor**, the energy is $\dfrac{1}{2} L I^2$. Substituting the data gives $1.25\,\mathrm{J}$, or $1.3\,\mathrm{J}$ to two significant figures.

To find the current, multiply both sides of $U_2 = \dfrac{1}{2} L I_2^2$ by $2$ and divide by $L$ to isolate $I_2^2 = \dfrac{2 U_2}{L} = 25\,\mathrm{A^2}$. Then take the square root. The required current is $5.0\,\mathrm{A}$.

The current $I_2$ is twice $I_1$, and the energy $U_2 = 5.0\,\mathrm{J}$ is four times $U_1$. The energy is proportional to the square of the current, so doubling the current makes the energy **four times** as large.

</div>

## Exercise 2

An RL circuit has $\mathcal{E} = 10\,\mathrm{V}$, $R = 5.0\,\mathrm{\Omega}$, and $L = 0.50\,\mathrm{H}$. The switch closes at time $t = 0$. Calculate the steady-state energy stored in the inductor. Determine when the stored energy reaches half its steady-state value. At that instant, determine the power supplied by the source, the power dissipated by the resistor, and the rate of increase of the energy stored in the inductor.

$$
I = \frac{\mathcal{E}}{R} , \qquad
U_{\infty} = \frac{1}{2} L I^2 , \qquad
\frac{1}{2} L i_1^2 = \frac{1}{2} U_{\infty}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
i_1 = \frac{I}{\sqrt{2}}
$$
$$
1 - e^{-\frac{t_1}{\tau}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
t_1 = -\tau \ln \left( 1 - \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \right) , \qquad
P_{\text{src}} = \mathcal{E}\,i_1 , \qquad
P_R = R\,i_1^2 , \qquad
\frac{dU}{dt} = P_{\text{src}} - P_R
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
I &= \frac{10\,\mathrm{V}}{5.0\,\mathrm{\Omega}} = 2.0\,\mathrm{A} , \qquad
U_{\infty} = \frac{1}{2} \cdot 0.50 \cdot 2.0^2\,\mathrm{J} = 1.0\,\mathrm{J} \\[6pt]
i_1 &= \frac{2.0}{\sqrt{2}}\,\mathrm{A} = 1.41\,\mathrm{A} , \qquad
\tau = \frac{0.50\,\mathrm{H}}{5.0\,\mathrm{\Omega}} = 0.10\,\mathrm{s} \\[6pt]
t_1 &= -0.10 \cdot \ln (1 - 0.707)\,\mathrm{s} = 0.10 \cdot 1.23\,\mathrm{s} = 0.12\,\mathrm{s} \\[6pt]
P_{\text{src}} &= 10 \cdot 1.41\,\mathrm{W} = 14\,\mathrm{W} , \qquad
P_R = 5.0 \cdot 1.41^2\,\mathrm{W} = 10\,\mathrm{W} , \qquad
\frac{dU}{dt} = 14.1\,\mathrm{W} - 10.0\,\mathrm{W} = 4.1\,\mathrm{W}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

In steady state the current is $\dfrac{\mathcal{E}}{R} = 2.0\,\mathrm{A}$, and the expression for the **energy stored in an inductor** gives $1.0\,\mathrm{J}$. The energy is proportional to the square of the current, so it reaches half its steady-state value when the current equals the steady-state current divided by $\sqrt{2}$, or $1.41\,\mathrm{A}$.

In the expression for the **current when an RL circuit is switched on**, set $1 - e^{-\frac{t_1}{\tau}} = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}} = 0.707$. The exponential factor then equals $0.293$. Taking logarithms gives $\dfrac{t_1}{\tau} = -\ln 0.293 = 1.23$. The energy therefore reaches half its final value after $0.12\,\mathrm{s}$, or about $1.2$ time constants.

At that instant the source supplies power $\mathcal{E}\,i_1 = 14\,\mathrm{W}$ and the resistor dissipates $R\,i_1^2 = 10\,\mathrm{W}$. By the **energy balance in an RL circuit**, the power supplied equals the power dissipated plus the rate of increase of energy in the inductor. The difference, $4.1\,\mathrm{W}$, is therefore the rate at which the inductor stores energy. The same value follows from $L\,i_1\,\dfrac{di}{dt}$, where $\dfrac{di}{dt} = \dfrac{\mathcal{E}}{L}\,e^{-\frac{t_1}{\tau}} = 20 \cdot 0.293\,\mathrm{A/s} = 5.86\,\mathrm{A/s}$. Indeed, $0.50 \cdot 1.41 \cdot 5.86\,\mathrm{W} = 4.1\,\mathrm{W}$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

An air-core solenoid of length $\ell = 50\,\mathrm{cm}$ with $N = 2000$ turns of radius $r = 2.5\,\mathrm{cm}$ carries a current $I = 3.0\,\mathrm{A}$. It is long compared with its radius ($\mu_0 = 4\pi \cdot 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{T\cdot m/A}$). Calculate the internal magnetic field and the magnetic energy density. Calculate the energy in the solenoid in two ways, using the energy density and using the inductance.

$$
B = \mu_0\,\frac{N}{\ell}\,I , \qquad
u_B = \frac{B^2}{2\mu_0} , \qquad
U = u_B \cdot \pi r^2 \ell , \qquad
L = \frac{\mu_0 N^2 \pi r^2}{\ell} , \qquad
U = \frac{1}{2} L I^2
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
B &= 4\pi \cdot 10^{-7} \cdot \frac{2000}{0.50} \cdot 3.0\,\mathrm{T} = 1.51 \cdot 10^{-2}\,\mathrm{T} \\[6pt]
u_B &= \frac{\left( 1.51 \cdot 10^{-2} \right)^2}{2 \cdot 4\pi \cdot 10^{-7}}\,\mathrm{J/m^3} = 90\,\mathrm{J/m^3} \\[6pt]
V &= \pi \cdot 0.025^2 \cdot 0.50\,\mathrm{m^3} = 9.82 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m^3} , \qquad
U = 90.5 \cdot 9.82 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{J} = 8.9 \cdot 10^{-2}\,\mathrm{J} \\[6pt]
L &= \frac{4\pi \cdot 10^{-7} \cdot 2000^2 \cdot \pi \cdot 0.025^2}{0.50}\,\mathrm{H} = 1.97 \cdot 10^{-2}\,\mathrm{H} , \qquad
U = \frac{1}{2} \cdot 1.97 \cdot 10^{-2} \cdot 3.0^2\,\mathrm{J} = 8.9 \cdot 10^{-2}\,\mathrm{J}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The solenoid is long compared with its radius, so the internal field is uniform and equals $\mu_0\,\dfrac{N}{\ell}\,I \approx 15\,\mathrm{mT}$, and the field outside is negligible. From the expression for the **energy density of a magnetic field**, the energy per unit volume is $\dfrac{B^2}{2\mu_0} \approx 90\,\mathrm{J/m^3}$. It has the same value at every point inside because the field is uniform.

The total energy is the density times the internal volume, which is the volume of a cylinder of radius $r$ and height $\ell$. The result is approximately $89\,\mathrm{mJ}$.

In the second method, first calculate the **inductance of the solenoid**, approximately $20\,\mathrm{mH}$, and then the energy $\dfrac{1}{2} L I^2$. The result is the same, as expected, because the formula for energy density was derived from the energy of a solenoid. A check of the units confirms the first calculation: $\dfrac{\mathrm{T^2}}{\mathrm{T\cdot m/A}} \cdot \mathrm{m^3} = \mathrm{T \cdot A \cdot m^2}$, and since $1\,\mathrm{T} = 1\,\mathrm{N/(A \cdot m)}$, we obtain $\mathrm{N \cdot m} = \mathrm{J}$.

</div>
