## Exercise 1

A coil carrying a current $i_1 = 1.5\,\mathrm{A}$ has a self-flux linkage $\Phi_1 = 4.5 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{Wb}$. Calculate the inductance of the coil. The current then increases uniformly to $i_2 = 4.5\,\mathrm{A}$ in a time interval $\Delta t = 20\,\mathrm{ms}$. Calculate the self-induced emf during the increase and the final self-flux linkage.

$$
L = \frac{\Phi_1}{i_1} , \qquad
\mathcal{E}_L = -L\,\frac{\Delta i}{\Delta t} , \qquad
\Phi_2 = L\,i_2
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
L &= \frac{4.5 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{Wb}}{1.5\,\mathrm{A}} = 3.0 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{H} \\[6pt]
\frac{\Delta i}{\Delta t} &= \frac{4.5\,\mathrm{A} - 1.5\,\mathrm{A}}{0.020\,\mathrm{s}} = 150\,\mathrm{A/s} \\[6pt]
\mathcal{E}_L &= -3.0 \cdot 10^{-3} \cdot 150\,\mathrm{V} = -0.45\,\mathrm{V} \\[6pt]
\Phi_2 &= 3.0 \cdot 10^{-3} \cdot 4.5\,\mathrm{Wb} = 1.35 \cdot 10^{-2}\,\mathrm{Wb}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The **inductance** is the ratio of the self-flux linkage to the current that produces it. Dividing $\Phi_1$ by $i_1$ therefore gives $L = 3.0\,\mathrm{mH}$. The unit is the henry, since one weber divided by one ampere is one henry.

The current increases uniformly, so its derivative is constant and equals the change $\Delta i = 3.0\,\mathrm{A}$ divided by the time interval $\Delta t = 0.020\,\mathrm{s}$. To find the **self-induced emf**, multiply this rate by $L$ and reverse the sign. The result is $\mathcal{E}_L = -0.45\,\mathrm{V}$. The minus sign indicates that the emf acts in the direction opposite to the current, as Lenz's law predicts for an increasing current.

The inductance depends only on the shape of the coil, so it remains constant when the current changes. The final flux linkage is therefore $L$ times the new current. Like the current, it has tripled from its initial value.

</div>

## Exercise 2

In a coil of inductance $L = 0.25\,\mathrm{H}$, the current, measured in amperes with time in seconds, follows $i(t) = 0.50\,t^2$ for $0 \leq t \leq 4.0\,\mathrm{s}$. Determine the self-induced emf as a function of time and its value at time $t_1 = 3.0\,\mathrm{s}$. Calculate the time at which the magnitude of the self-induced emf is $1.0\,\mathrm{V}$ and the average self-induced emf between $0$ and $4.0\,\mathrm{s}$.

$$
\mathcal{E}_L(t) = -L\,\frac{di}{dt} , \qquad
\frac{di}{dt} = 1.0\,t
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{E}_L(t) &= -0.25 \cdot 1.0\,t = -0.25\,t \quad (\mathrm{V}) \\[6pt]
\mathcal{E}_L(3.0\,\mathrm{s}) &= -0.25 \cdot 3.0\,\mathrm{V} = -0.75\,\mathrm{V} \\[6pt]
0.25\,t_2 = 1.0 \quad &\Longrightarrow \quad t_2 = 4.0\,\mathrm{s} \\[6pt]
\mathcal{E}_{L,m} &= -L\,\frac{i(4.0) - i(0)}{4.0} = -0.25 \cdot \frac{8.0 - 0}{4.0}\,\mathrm{V} = -0.50\,\mathrm{V}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The current does not increase uniformly, so the instantaneous self-induced emf must be calculated from the derivative of the current. The derivative of $0.50\,t^2$ is $2 \cdot 0.50\,t = 1.0\,t$, in amperes per second. The **self-induced emf** is obtained by multiplying by $-L$, giving $\mathcal{E}_L(t) = -0.25\,t$ in volts.

At $3.0\,\mathrm{s}$ the emf is $-0.75\,\mathrm{V}$. Its magnitude $0.25\,t$ increases with time because the current increases more and more rapidly. The magnitude reaches $1.0\,\mathrm{V}$ when $0.25\,t_2 = 1.0$, at the end of the interval, when $t_2 = 4.0\,\mathrm{s}$.

To find the average emf, divide the change in current from $0$ to $i(4.0) = 0.50 \cdot 16\,\mathrm{A} = 8.0\,\mathrm{A}$ by the duration of the interval and multiply by the negative of the inductance. The result, $-0.50\,\mathrm{V}$, is the average of its initial value $0$ and its final value $-1.0\,\mathrm{V}$ because the emf is a linear function of time. After the initial instant, the emf is negative because the current is increasing.

</div>

## Exercise 3

The current in a coil of inductance $L = 40\,\mathrm{mH}$ varies as shown in the graph. It increases uniformly from $0$ to $3.0\,\mathrm{A}$ during the first $2.0\,\mathrm{ms}$, remains constant until $4.0\,\mathrm{ms}$, and then decreases uniformly to $0$ at time $10\,\mathrm{ms}$. Determine the self-induced emf over each interval, plot it, and explain its sign using Lenz's law.

::grafico{f="max(0,min(1.5*x,3,3-0.5*(x-4)))+0*sqrt(x)" segmenti="2,0,2,3;4,0,4,3" xmin=-2 xmax=12 ymin=-1 ymax=4 nomi="t,i" legenda=0 titolo="Coil current in amperes, with time in milliseconds"}

$$
\mathcal{E}_L = -L\,\frac{\Delta i}{\Delta t}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\Delta i_1}{\Delta t_1} &= \frac{3.0\,\mathrm{A}}{2.0 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{s}} = 1.5 \cdot 10^3\,\mathrm{A/s} \\[6pt]
\mathcal{E}_1 &= -0.040 \cdot 1.5 \cdot 10^3\,\mathrm{V} = -60\,\mathrm{V}
\end{aligned}
\qquad (0 < t < 2.0\,\mathrm{ms})
$$
$$
\frac{\Delta i_2}{\Delta t_2} = 0 , \qquad \mathcal{E}_2 = 0
\qquad (2.0\,\mathrm{ms} < t < 4.0\,\mathrm{ms})
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\Delta i_3}{\Delta t_3} &= \frac{-3.0\,\mathrm{A}}{6.0 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{s}} = -500\,\mathrm{A/s} \\[6pt]
\mathcal{E}_3 &= -0.040 \cdot (-500)\,\mathrm{V} = 20\,\mathrm{V}
\end{aligned}
\qquad (4.0\,\mathrm{ms} < t < 10\,\mathrm{ms})
$$

::grafico{f="(x>0&&x<2)?-60:((x>4&&x<10)?20:0)" segmenti="2,-60,2,0;4,0,4,20;10,0,10,20" xmin=-2 xmax=12 ymin=-70 ymax=30 nomi="t,ℰ" legenda=0 titolo="Self-induced emf in volts, with time in milliseconds. It is $-60\,\mathrm{V}$ while the current rises, $0$ while the current is constant, and $20\,\mathrm{V}$ while it falls"}

<div class="spiegazione">

The current graph is a straight line segment on each interval, so the current changes uniformly and its derivative equals the slope of the segment. On the first interval the current increases by $3.0\,\mathrm{A}$ in $2.0\,\mathrm{ms}$. On the last it decreases by $3.0\,\mathrm{A}$ in $6.0\,\mathrm{ms}$, so the change is negative. To obtain the **self-induced emf**, multiply each slope by $-L = -0.040\,\mathrm{H}$.

The emf graph consists of three horizontal segments because the derivative of the current is constant on each interval. On the first interval the current increases. By **Lenz's law**, the self-induced emf acts in the direction opposite to the current, so it is negative. On the third interval the current decreases. The emf acts in the same direction as the current and slows its decay, so it is positive. Its magnitude is three times as large on the first interval because the current changes three times as rapidly there.

</div>
