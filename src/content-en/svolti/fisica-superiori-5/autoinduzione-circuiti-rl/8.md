## Exercise 1

An air-core solenoid of length $\ell = 40\,\mathrm{cm}$ with $N = 1200$ turns of radius $r = 2.0\,\mathrm{cm}$ and negligible resistance is connected in series with a resistor $R = 6.0\,\mathrm{\Omega}$ and an ideal source of emf $\mathcal{E} = 12\,\mathrm{V}$. The switch closes at time $t = 0$. The solenoid is long compared with its radius ($\mu_0 = 4\pi \cdot 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{T\cdot m/A}$).
1. Calculate the inductance of the solenoid and the time constant of the circuit.
2. Calculate how long it takes for the current to reach $99\,\%$ of its steady-state value.
3. Calculate the steady-state magnetic energy, first using the inductance and then using the magnetic field energy density.

$$
L = \frac{\mu_0 N^2 \pi r^2}{\ell} , \qquad
\tau = \frac{L}{R} , \qquad
1 - e^{-\frac{t_1}{\tau}} = 0.99
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
t_1 = \tau \ln 100
$$
$$
I = \frac{\mathcal{E}}{R} , \qquad
U = \frac{1}{2} L I^2 , \qquad
B = \mu_0\,\frac{N}{\ell}\,I , \qquad
U = \frac{B^2}{2\mu_0} \cdot \pi r^2 \ell
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
L &= \frac{4\pi \cdot 10^{-7} \cdot 1200^2 \cdot \pi \cdot 0.020^2}{0.40}\,\mathrm{H} = 5.69 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{H} \\[6pt]
\tau &= \frac{5.69 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{H}}{6.0\,\mathrm{\Omega}} = 9.5 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{s} \\[6pt]
t_1 &= 9.48 \cdot 10^{-4} \cdot 4.61\,\mathrm{s} = 4.4 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{s} \\[6pt]
I &= \frac{12\,\mathrm{V}}{6.0\,\mathrm{\Omega}} = 2.0\,\mathrm{A} , \qquad
U = \frac{1}{2} \cdot 5.69 \cdot 10^{-3} \cdot 2.0^2\,\mathrm{J} = 1.1 \cdot 10^{-2}\,\mathrm{J} \\[6pt]
B &= 4\pi \cdot 10^{-7} \cdot \frac{1200}{0.40} \cdot 2.0\,\mathrm{T} = 7.54 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{T} \\[6pt]
U &= \frac{\left( 7.54 \cdot 10^{-3} \right)^2}{2 \cdot 4\pi \cdot 10^{-7}} \cdot \pi \cdot 0.020^2 \cdot 0.40\,\mathrm{J} = 22.6 \cdot 5.03 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{J} = 1.1 \cdot 10^{-2}\,\mathrm{J}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The solenoid is long compared with its radius, so its inductance follows from the formula for the **inductance of a solenoid**, with $S = \pi r^2$. The solenoid resistance is negligible, so the only resistance in the circuit is that of the resistor. The time constant is $\dfrac{L}{R}$, just under a millisecond.

From the expression for the **current when an RL circuit is switched on**, the ratio of the current to its steady-state value is $1 - e^{-\frac{t}{\tau}}$. Setting this equal to $0.99$ gives $e^{-\frac{t_1}{\tau}} = 0.01$, and taking logarithms gives $t_1 = \tau \ln 100 \approx 4.6\,\tau$. After about $4.4\,\mathrm{ms}$, the circuit can therefore be considered to have reached steady state.

In steady state the current is $\dfrac{\mathcal{E}}{R} = 2.0\,\mathrm{A}$, and the **energy stored in the inductor** is approximately $11\,\mathrm{mJ}$. The second method gives a uniform field of about $7.5\,\mathrm{mT}$ inside the solenoid and a **magnetic field energy density** of $22.6\,\mathrm{J/m^3}$. Multiplying by the internal volume $\pi r^2 \ell = 5.03 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m^3}$ gives the same energy because the field outside the solenoid is negligible.

</div>

## Exercise 2

To measure the inductance of a coil that can be treated as ideal, a student connects it in series with a resistor $R = 30\,\mathrm{\Omega}$, a switch, and an ideal source of emf $\mathcal{E} = 9.0\,\mathrm{V}$. The student closes the switch at $t = 0$ and records the current using a sensor. The graph of the data shows that the current approaches a steady-state value and that at $t = 4.0\,\mathrm{ms}$ the current is $190\,\mathrm{mA}$.

::grafico{f="300*(1-exp(-x/4))+0*sqrt(x)" asintoti="y=300" segmenti="4,0,4,189.6;0,189.6,4,189.6" punti="4,189.6,pieno" xmin=-4 xmax=32 ymin=-40 ymax=360 nomi="t,i" legenda=0 titolo="Recorded current in milliamperes, with time in milliseconds. At $4.0\,\mathrm{ms}$ the current is $190\,\mathrm{mA}$. The dashed line indicates the steady-state current"}

1. Explain why the current does not reach steady state immediately. Calculate the steady-state current, the time constant, and the inductance of the coil.
2. Calculate the voltage across the coil at $t = 4.0\,\mathrm{ms}$ and explain the direction of the self-induced emf.
3. For the first $4.0\,\mathrm{ms}$, calculate the work done by the source, the energy stored in the coil, and the energy dissipated in the resistor.
4. Once the circuit reaches steady state, a changeover switch disconnects the source and resistor and connects the coil across a protective resistor $R' = 1200\,\mathrm{\Omega}$. Calculate the maximum voltage across $R'$, the new time constant, and the time it takes for the current to fall to $1.0\,\mathrm{mA}$.

**Question 1**

$$
I = \frac{\mathcal{E}}{R} , \qquad
I \left( 1 - e^{-\frac{t_1}{\tau}} \right) = i_1
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
e^{-\frac{t_1}{\tau}} = \frac{I - i_1}{I}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\tau = \frac{t_1}{\ln \dfrac{I}{I - i_1}} , \qquad
L = R\,\tau
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
I &= \frac{9.0\,\mathrm{V}}{30\,\mathrm{\Omega}} = 0.30\,\mathrm{A} = 300\,\mathrm{mA} \\[6pt]
\tau &= \frac{4.0\,\mathrm{ms}}{\ln \dfrac{300}{110}} = \frac{4.0\,\mathrm{ms}}{1.003} = 4.0\,\mathrm{ms} \\[6pt]
L &= 30 \cdot 4.0 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{H} = 0.12\,\mathrm{H}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

When the current starts to rise, the self-induced emf $-L\,\dfrac{di}{dt}$ develops in the coil and, by Lenz's law, opposes the increase. The current therefore rises gradually according to the expression for the **current when an RL circuit is switched on**. In steady state the current is constant, the self-induced emf is zero, and the current is $\dfrac{\mathcal{E}}{R} = 300\,\mathrm{mA}$.

At $t_1 = 4.0\,\mathrm{ms}$ the current must equal $i_1 = 190\,\mathrm{mA}$. Dividing by $I$ gives $1 - e^{-\frac{t_1}{\tau}} = \dfrac{190}{300}$ and hence $e^{-\frac{t_1}{\tau}} = \dfrac{110}{300}$. Taking logarithms gives $\dfrac{t_1}{\tau} = \ln \dfrac{300}{110} = 1.003$, so $\tau = 4.0\,\mathrm{ms}$. The measurement was therefore taken one time constant after switch-on. Indeed, $190\,\mathrm{mA}$ is $63\,\%$ of the steady-state value. The time constant is $\dfrac{L}{R}$, giving $L = R\,\tau = 0.12\,\mathrm{H}$.

</div>

**Question 2**

$$
V_L = \mathcal{E} - R\,i_1 , \qquad
\mathcal{E}_L = -V_L
$$
$$
V_L = 9.0\,\mathrm{V} - 30 \cdot 0.19\,\mathrm{V} = 3.3\,\mathrm{V} , \qquad
\mathcal{E}_L = -3.3\,\mathrm{V}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

By the RL circuit equation, the source emf equals the sum of the voltages across the resistor and coil. At $4.0\,\mathrm{ms}$ the voltage across the resistor is $30 \cdot 0.19\,\mathrm{V} = 5.7\,\mathrm{V}$, so the voltage across the coil is $3.3\,\mathrm{V}$. The same value follows from $V_L = \mathcal{E}\,e^{-\frac{t}{\tau}} = 9.0 \cdot e^{-1}\,\mathrm{V} = 3.3\,\mathrm{V}$.

The self-induced emf is $-L\,\dfrac{di}{dt}$, and the current is rising at this instant. The emf is therefore negative and acts in the direction opposite to the current, as **Lenz's law** predicts for an increasing current. Its magnitude decreases with time as the current rises more slowly.

</div>

**Question 3**

$$
W_{\text{src}} = \int_0^{\tau} \mathcal{E}\,i\,dt = \mathcal{E} I \int_0^{\tau} \left( 1 - e^{-\frac{t}{\tau}} \right) dt = \mathcal{E} I \left[ t + \tau\,e^{-\frac{t}{\tau}} \right]_0^{\tau} = \mathcal{E} I\,\tau\,e^{-1}
$$
$$
U(\tau) = \frac{1}{2} L\,i(\tau)^2 , \qquad
W_J = W_{\text{src}} - U(\tau)
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
W_{\text{src}} &= 9.0 \cdot 0.30 \cdot 4.0 \cdot 10^{-3} \cdot 0.368\,\mathrm{J} = 3.97 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{J} \\[6pt]
i(\tau) &= 0.30 \cdot 0.632\,\mathrm{A} = 0.190\,\mathrm{A} , \qquad
U(\tau) = \frac{1}{2} \cdot 0.12 \cdot 0.190^2\,\mathrm{J} = 2.16 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{J} \\[6pt]
W_J &= 3.97 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{J} - 2.16 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{J} = 1.8 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{J}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The source supplies power $\mathcal{E}\,i$, and the current varies with time. The work done by the source, denoted by $W$ because $L$ denotes inductance, is therefore the integral of the power from $0$ to $\tau$. An antiderivative of $1 - e^{-\frac{t}{\tau}}$ is $t + \tau\,e^{-\frac{t}{\tau}}$ because the derivative of $\tau\,e^{-\frac{t}{\tau}}$ is $-e^{-\frac{t}{\tau}}$. Evaluating the antiderivative between $0$ and $\tau$ gives $\tau + \tau\,e^{-1} - \tau = \tau\,e^{-1}$, and the work is approximately $4.0\,\mathrm{mJ}$.

At $\tau$ the current is $0.190\,\mathrm{A}$, and the expression for the **energy stored in an inductor** gives $2.2\,\mathrm{mJ}$ for the coil. By the **energy balance in an RL circuit**, the work done by the source is divided between the energy stored in the coil and the heat produced in the resistor. The resistor has therefore dissipated the difference, about $1.8\,\mathrm{mJ}$. The same value follows by integrating $R\,i^2$ from $0$ to $\tau$.

</div>

**Question 4**

$$
V_{\max} = R' I , \qquad
\tau' = \frac{L}{R'} , \qquad
I\,e^{-\frac{t_2}{\tau'}} = i_2
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
t_2 = \tau' \ln \frac{I}{i_2}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
V_{\max} &= 1200 \cdot 0.30\,\mathrm{V} = 360\,\mathrm{V} \\[6pt]
\tau' &= \frac{0.12\,\mathrm{H}}{1200\,\mathrm{\Omega}} = 1.0 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{s} \\[6pt]
t_2 &= 1.0 \cdot 10^{-4} \cdot \ln \frac{300}{1.0}\,\mathrm{s} = 1.0 \cdot 10^{-4} \cdot 5.70\,\mathrm{s} = 5.7 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{s}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

At the instant the changeover switch moves, the current in the coil is still $0.30\,\mathrm{A}$ because the self-induced emf prevents a jump in current. This current now flows through the resistor $R'$, producing a voltage $R' I = 360\,\mathrm{V}$ across it, forty times the source emf. The current then decreases, so $360\,\mathrm{V}$ is the maximum voltage. This is the **switch-off voltage spike**.

After the changeover, the coil forms a loop with only the resistor $R'$. The expression for the **current when an RL circuit is switched off** therefore applies, with the new time constant $\dfrac{L}{R'} = 0.10\,\mathrm{ms}$, one-fortieth of the time constant at switch-on. Setting the current equal to $1.0\,\mathrm{mA}$ and taking logarithms gives $t_2 = \tau' \ln 300 \approx 0.57\,\mathrm{ms}$. During this interval, almost all the energy stored in the coil at steady state, $\dfrac{1}{2} \cdot 0.12 \cdot 0.30^2\,\mathrm{J} = 5.4\,\mathrm{mJ}$, is converted to heat in the protective resistor.

</div>
