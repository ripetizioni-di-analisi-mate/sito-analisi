## Exercise 1

A copper strip of width $w = 2.5\,\mathrm{cm}$ and thickness $s = 0.50\,\mathrm{mm}$ carries a current $I = 15\,\mathrm{A}$ in a uniform magnetic field $B = 1.2\,\mathrm{T}$ perpendicular to the strip. Copper has $n = 8.5 \cdot 10^{28}$ conduction electrons per cubic meter. Calculate the electron drift speed, the Hall field, and the Hall voltage. ($e = 1.60 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{C}$)

$$
v_d = \frac{I}{n e\, w s} ,
\qquad
E_H = v_d B ,
\qquad
\Delta V_H = E_H\, w = \frac{I B}{n e\, s}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
v_d &= \frac{15}{8.5 \cdot 10^{28} \cdot 1.60 \cdot 10^{-19} \cdot 2.5 \cdot 10^{-2} \cdot 5.0 \cdot 10^{-4}}\,\mathrm{m/s} \approx 8.8 \cdot 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{m/s} \\[6pt]
E_H &= 8.82 \cdot 10^{-5} \cdot 1.2\,\mathrm{V/m} \approx 1.1 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{V/m} \\[6pt]
\Delta V_H &= \frac{15 \cdot 1.2}{8.5 \cdot 10^{28} \cdot 1.60 \cdot 10^{-19} \cdot 5.0 \cdot 10^{-4}}\,\mathrm{V} \approx 2.6 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\mathrm{V}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The current flows through the cross section perpendicular to the length of the strip, which has area $w s$. The equation $I = n e\, v_d\, w s$ gives the drift speed, less than a tenth of a millimeter per second. The conduction electrons drift so slowly because there are so many of them.

At equilibrium in the **Hall effect**, the force of the Hall field on the electrons has the same magnitude as the magnetic Lorentz force, so $eE_H = ev_dB$ and $E_H = v_d B$. The Hall field is uniform across the width of the strip, so the voltage between the edges is $E_H\, w$, about $2.6\,\mu\mathrm{V}$. The formula $\dfrac{IB}{nes}$ gives the same value as a check. The voltage is very small because the electron density in copper is very high.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A Hall probe consists of a semiconductor strip of thickness $s = 0.10\,\mathrm{mm}$ carrying a current $I = 5.0\,\mathrm{mA}$. To calibrate it, the probe is placed in a known field $B_1 = 0.20\,\mathrm{T}$ perpendicular to the strip, and a Hall voltage $\Delta V_1 = 3.0\,\mathrm{mV}$ is measured.

1. Calculate the charge-carrier density in the semiconductor, assuming a charge magnitude of $e$ per carrier.
2. With the same current, the probe reads $\Delta V_2 = 7.5\,\mathrm{mV}$ in an unknown field. Calculate the field strength.

($e = 1.60 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{C}$)

$$
\Delta V_H = \frac{I B}{n e\, s}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
n = \frac{I B_1}{e\, s\, \Delta V_1}
$$
$$
n = \frac{5.0 \cdot 10^{-3} \cdot 0.20}{1.60 \cdot 10^{-19} \cdot 1.0 \cdot 10^{-4} \cdot 3.0 \cdot 10^{-3}}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}} \approx 2.1 \cdot 10^{22}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}
$$
$$
\frac{\Delta V_2}{\Delta V_1} = \frac{B_2}{B_1}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
B_2 = B_1\, \frac{\Delta V_2}{\Delta V_1} = 0.20 \cdot \frac{7.5}{3.0}\,\mathrm{T} = 0.50\,\mathrm{T}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

In the **Hall effect** formula, the current, field, strip thickness, and voltage are known, so the carrier density can be found. It is approximately $2.1 \cdot 10^{22}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}$. This density is lower than in copper by a factor of about four million, which is why the Hall voltage is a few millivolts rather than a few microvolts.

The current, strip thickness, and carrier density in the probe are unchanged, so the Hall voltage is directly proportional to the field strength. The ratio of the voltages equals the ratio of the fields, and the unknown field is $0.50\,\mathrm{T}$. A calibrated probe can therefore measure the field from a single voltage reading.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A semiconductor strip of width $w = 5.0\,\mathrm{mm}$ and thickness $s = 0.25\,\mathrm{mm}$ carries a current $I = 20\,\mathrm{mA}$ to the right. A magnetic field $B = 0.40\,\mathrm{T}$ points into the page, perpendicular to the strip. The measured Hall voltage between the top and bottom edges is $\Delta V_H = 16\,\mathrm{mV}$, and the top edge is at *lower* potential.

1. Determine the sign of the charge carriers, assuming a single type of carrier with charge $\pm e$.
2. Calculate the carrier density, the drift speed, and the Hall field.

($e = 1.60 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{C}$)

::grafico{assi=0 uguale=1 poligoni="0,-1.1 6.4,-1.1 6.4,1.1 0,1.1" linee="-1.4,0 0,0;6.4,0 7.6,0" vettori="-1.3,0.25,-0.2,0.25,I;3,0,1.8,0,v;3,0,3,0.8,F" punti="3,0,pieno" etichette="3.2,-0.4,e⁻;0.6,0.75,−;1.6,0.75,−;2.6,0.75,−;3.6,0.75,−;4.6,0.75,−;5.6,0.75,−;0.6,-0.95,+;1.6,-0.95,+;2.6,-0.95,+;3.6,-0.95,+;4.6,-0.95,+;5.6,-0.95,+;0.33,1.43,⊗;0.33,-1.77,⊗;1.83,1.43,⊗;1.83,-1.77,⊗;3.33,1.43,⊗;3.33,-1.77,⊗;4.83,1.43,⊗;4.83,-1.77,⊗;6.6,-0.6,w" xmin=-1.6 xmax=7.8 ymin=-2.1 ymax=2.1 titolo="For negative carriers, the electrons move to the left. The Lorentz force drives them toward the top edge, which becomes negatively charged and is at lower potential"}

$$
\text{for } q > 0 , \quad \vec{v}_d \text{ to the right} ,
\quad
\vec{v}_d \times \vec{B} \text{ upward} ,
\quad
\vec{F} = q\,\vec{v}_d \times \vec{B} \text{ upward}
$$
$$
\text{for } q < 0 , \quad \vec{v}_d \text{ to the left} ,
\quad
\vec{v}_d \times \vec{B} \text{ downward} ,
\quad
\vec{F} = q\,\vec{v}_d \times \vec{B} \text{ upward}
$$
$$
n = \frac{I B}{e\, s\, \Delta V_H} = \frac{2.0 \cdot 10^{-2} \cdot 0.40}{1.60 \cdot 10^{-19} \cdot 2.5 \cdot 10^{-4} \cdot 1.6 \cdot 10^{-2}}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}} = 1.25 \cdot 10^{22}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}
$$
$$
v_d = \frac{I}{n e\, w s} = \frac{2.0 \cdot 10^{-2}}{1.25 \cdot 10^{22} \cdot 1.60 \cdot 10^{-19} \cdot 5.0 \cdot 10^{-3} \cdot 2.5 \cdot 10^{-4}}\,\mathrm{m/s} = 8.0\,\mathrm{m/s}
$$
$$
E_H = \frac{\Delta V_H}{w} = \frac{1.6 \cdot 10^{-2}}{5.0 \cdot 10^{-3}}\,\mathrm{V/m} = 3.2\,\mathrm{V/m}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

To determine the sign, compare the two possibilities. With positive carriers the drift velocity points to the right, in the direction of the current. By the **right-hand rule**, with the thumb pointing to the right and the fingers into the page, the palm faces upward. A positive charge therefore experiences an upward force. With negative carriers the velocity points left, the product $\vec{v}_d \times \vec{B}$ points downward, and the negative charge reverses the force, which again points upward. In both cases the carriers accumulate along the top edge, as predicted in the discussion of the **sign of the carriers**.

If the carriers were positive, the top edge would be positively charged and at higher potential. The top edge is at lower potential, so negative charge accumulates there and the carriers are **negative**. This is an $n$-type semiconductor.

The carrier density follows from the **Hall effect** formula. The drift speed, $8.0\,\mathrm{m/s}$, is much greater than in metals because there are far fewer carriers, and they must move faster to carry the same current. The Hall field is uniform across the width, so its magnitude is $\dfrac{\Delta V_H}{w}$. As a check, $v_d B = 8.0 \cdot 0.40\,\mathrm{V/m} = 3.2\,\mathrm{V/m}$, the same value.

</div>
