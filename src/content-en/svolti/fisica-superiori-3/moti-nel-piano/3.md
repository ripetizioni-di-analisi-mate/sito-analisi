## Exercise 1

A projectile has $v_{0x}=6\,\mathrm{m/s}$ and $v_{0y}=10\,\mathrm{m/s}$. With $g=10\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$, how far does it rise above launch? Neglect air resistance.

::grafico{curve="6*t,10*t-5*t^2,0,2" punti="6,5,pieno" etichette="6.17,5.25,A" xmin=0 xmax=13.4 ymin=0 ymax=6.4 nomi="x (m),Δy (m)" titolo="Maximum height occurs above horizontal coordinate 6 m"}

$$
\begin{aligned}
H&=\frac{v_{0y}^2}{2g}=\frac{10^2}{2\cdot10}\\[4pt]
&= 5\,\mathrm{m}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

At the top the vertical component vanishes, while the horizontal component remains six. The time-free equation applies along $y$ and uses only the initial vertical velocity. The vertex is reached after one second and six horizontal metres.

The same rise follows from the graph of $v_y$ against time. From launch to apex it is a line segment from $10\,\mathrm{m/s}$ to zero over one second. The triangle under that segment has area $5\,\mathrm m$. The horizontal distance in the sketch locates the apex, while its height depends on the vertical motion.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A projectile rises at most $20\,\mathrm m$ above launch. With $g=10\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$, what was its initial vertical velocity component with upward positive? Neglect air resistance.

$$
\begin{aligned}
v_{0y}&=\sqrt{2gH}=\sqrt{2\cdot10\cdot20}\\[4pt]
&= 20\,\mathrm{m/s}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Set vertical velocity to zero at maximum height. The equation $0=v_{0y}^2-2gH$ has two algebraic roots, but initial rise requires the positive one. The recovered component drops by ten metres per second each second and vanishes after two seconds.

The time to the apex also checks the rise using the height equation. In two seconds the initial-velocity term contributes $40\,\mathrm m$ and gravity subtracts $20\,\mathrm m$. This gives the required twenty metres above the launch point. The same rise is compatible with different horizontal components.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Two frames show the same ball at a height of $4.75\,\mathrm m$ at times $0.4\,\mathrm s$ and $1.4\,\mathrm s$ during an oblique launch. Neglect air resistance and use $g=10\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$. Find the maximum height and the vertical velocity component in each frame.

$$
\begin{aligned}
t_A&=\frac{0.4+1.4}{2}\,\mathrm s=0.9\,\mathrm s,\\[6pt]
v_y(0.4\,\mathrm s)&=[10(0.9-0.4)]\,\mathrm{m/s}=5\,\mathrm{m/s},\\[4pt]
v_y(1.4\,\mathrm s)&=[10(0.9-1.4)]\,\mathrm{m/s}=-5\,\mathrm{m/s},\\[4pt]
y_{\max}&=\left[4.75+\frac12\cdot10\cdot0.5^2\right]\,\mathrm m
=6\,\mathrm m.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The height equation is a downward-opening parabola. Two times with equal height are equally far from the vertex, so the apex occurs at their midpoint. The vertical velocity is zero there. Half a second earlier it is five metres per second upwards, and half a second later it has the same magnitude downwards. Between the first frame and the apex, the vertical component falls from $5\,\mathrm{m/s}$ to zero. The gain of $1.25\,\mathrm m$ also equals the area of the corresponding triangle in a velocity–time plot.

</div>
