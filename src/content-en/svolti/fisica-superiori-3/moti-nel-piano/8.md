## Exercise 1

A point on radius $4\,\mathrm m$ has speed $4\,\mathrm{m/s}$ and $a_t=3\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$. Find total acceleration magnitude.

::grafico{vettori="0,0,-4,0;0,0,0,3;0,0,-4,3" segmenti="-4,0,-4,3;-4,3,0,3" etichette="-2.41,0.17,a_c;0.14,1.44,a_t;-2.58,1.39,a" xmin=-4.9 xmax=1.1 ymin=-0.75 ymax=3.75 uguale=1 nomi="aₓ (m/s²),aᵧ (m/s²)" titolo="The resultant is the diagonal of the parallelogram."}

$$
\begin{aligned}
a&=\sqrt{\left(\frac{4^2}{4}\right)^2+3^2}\\[4pt]
&= 5\,\mathrm{m/s^2}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The centripetal part has magnitude four and the tangential part three. Their directions are perpendicular, so do not add the magnitudes directly. The diagonal of their parallelogram is five and represents the vector sum.

In local axes with the horizontal axis pointing outwards and the positive tangent upwards, the figure shows components $(-4;3)\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$. The vector makes an angle of about $37^\circ$ to the inward radial direction, towards the positive tangent. Comparing component magnitudes explains why the resultant lies closer to the radial direction.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Total acceleration magnitude is $13\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$ and the radial component has magnitude $5\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$. If the point slows down, what is its tangential component signed along velocity?

$$
\begin{aligned}
a_t&=-\sqrt{13^2-5^2}\\[4pt]
&= -12\,\mathrm{m/s^2}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Perpendicularity lets us subtract the radial component's square from total acceleration squared. The root has magnitude twelve. Slowing down forces the negative sign relative to the direction along velocity.

A positive tangential acceleration of magnitude twelve would give the same resultant magnitude, since only the direction parallel to the tangent changes. In this case the speed decreases at about $12\,\mathrm{m/s}$ per second over an interval short enough to treat the tangential component as constant.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A point moves around a circle of radius $4\,\mathrm m$. During the first $2\,\mathrm s$ its speed is $v(t)=2+2t$ in SI units. Find the magnitude of acceleration at $t=1\,\mathrm s$ and determine when the tangential and centripetal components have the same magnitude in this interval.

$$
\begin{aligned}
a_t&=2\,\mathrm{m/s^2},\qquad
a_c(t)=\frac{(2+2t)^2}{4}\,\mathrm{m/s^2},\\[6pt]
a(1\,\mathrm s)&=\sqrt{2^2+4^2}\,\mathrm{m/s^2}=2\sqrt5\,\mathrm{m/s^2},\\[6pt]
a_c(t)=a_t&\ \Longleftrightarrow\ (1+t)^2=2,\\[4pt]
t&=(\sqrt2-1)\,\mathrm s\simeq0.41\,\mathrm s.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The slope of the speed–time graph is the tangential component. Here it is constant and positive. The centripetal component depends on the square of the instantaneous speed, so at one second it is four metres per second squared. Apply Pythagoras to the resultant because the components are perpendicular. Their magnitudes become equal before one second, when the speed reaches $2\sqrt2\,\mathrm{m/s}$. The second mathematical root, $t=(-1-\sqrt2)\,\mathrm s$, lies outside the stated interval. The tangential component is larger before equality and the centripetal component is larger afterwards.

</div>
