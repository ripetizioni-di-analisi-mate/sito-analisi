## Exercise 1

A flywheel of inertia $2\,\mathrm{kg\cdot m^2}$ rotates at $6\,\mathrm{rad/s}$. Find its kinetic energy.

$$
\begin{aligned}
K&=\frac12I\omega^2\\[6pt]
&=\frac12\cdot2\cdot6^2\\[6pt]
&=36\,\mathrm J.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

In fixed-axis rotation each part of the flywheel has a speed equal to its distance from the axis times the magnitude of $\omega$. The kinetic energy calculation squares those speeds. The masses and distances are already accounted for by the given moment of inertia.

Squaring the angular velocity gives thirty-six per second squared because the radian is dimensionless. Multiplying by half the moment of inertia gives kilogram metres squared per second squared, or joules. For rotation at the same angular speed in the opposite direction, the square would have the same value and the flywheel would store the same amount of energy.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A rotor of inertia $0.50\,\mathrm{kg\cdot m^2}$ changes from $4$ to $8\,\mathrm{rad/s}$. Find the work done by net torque.

$$
\begin{aligned}
K_i&=\frac12\cdot0.50\cdot4^2=4\,\mathrm J\\[6pt]
K_f&=\frac12\cdot0.50\cdot8^2=16\,\mathrm J\\[6pt]
W_{\mathrm{tot}}&=K_f-K_i=16-4=12\,\mathrm J.
\end{aligned}
$$

::grafico{f=".25*x^2" punti="4,4,pieno;8,16,pieno" xmin=0 xmax=9 ymin=0 ymax=21 nomi="ω (rad/s),K (J)" titolo="Net work is the difference between the two ordinates"}

<div class="spiegazione">

The work done by the net torque on a fixed-axis rotor equals the change in its kinetic energy. Calculate two separate energies and subtract the initial one. At constant inertia, doubling the magnitude of the angular velocity gives four times the initial kinetic energy.

On the graph both points lie on the same parabola because the moment of inertia is unchanged. The work of twelve joules is the difference between their vertical coordinates. A motor might have transferred more than that amount to the rotor if there were resistive torques. The calculated value describes the net work of the torques that acted.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A flywheel with $I=3\,\mathrm{kg\cdot m^2}$ starts from rest. A constant net torque of $+6\,\mathrm{N\cdot m}$ acts on it while it turns through $4\,\mathrm{rad}$. Find its final signed angular velocity.

$$
\begin{aligned}
W&=M\Delta\theta=6\cdot4=24\,\mathrm J\\[6pt]
\frac12I\omega_f^2&=W\\[6pt]
|\omega_f|&=\sqrt{\frac{2\cdot24}{3}}=4\,\mathrm{rad/s}\\[6pt]
\omega_f&=+4\,\mathrm{rad/s}.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The positive torque applied to an initially stationary flywheel starts rotation in the same direction. Over the next four radians the torque remains constant. Its work is the product of the torque and the angular displacement, and is positive. Since the initial kinetic energy is zero, the final kinetic energy equals the work.

The energy equation contains the square of $\omega_f$, so it determines the magnitude. The sign is positive because the torque starts the motion and keeps its direction. As a check, the acceleration is constant at two radians per second squared. The product $2\alpha\Delta\theta$ gives sixteen radians squared per second squared, matching the squared value obtained from energy.

</div>
