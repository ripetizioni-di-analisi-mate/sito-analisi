## Exercise 1

A disc starts from rest at $\alpha=4\,\mathrm{rad/s^2}$. How far does it turn in its first $3\,\mathrm s$?

$$
\begin{aligned}
\omega_f&=0+4\cdot3=12\,\mathrm{rad/s}\\[6pt]
\Delta\theta&=\frac{\omega_0+\omega_f}{2}\,t\\[6pt]
&=\frac{0+12}{2}\cdot3=18\,\mathrm{rad}.
\end{aligned}
$$

::grafico{linee="0,0 3,12" area="4*x;0;0;3" xmin=0 xmax=3.5 ymin=0 ymax=15 nomi="t (s),ω (rad/s)" titolo="The triangular area gives the angle swept"}

<div class="spiegazione">

The angular velocity increases linearly from zero because the acceleration is constant. Each of the three seconds contributes to the angle according to the average velocity during that second. The average over the full interval is the midpoint of the two limiting velocities, so the angle is six radians per second multiplied by three seconds.

The diagram shows the same operation as a triangular area. The three-second base and the twelve-radians-per-second height yield radians when multiplied. The factor of one half accounts for the gradual increase in velocity compared with a rectangle of constant final height. The whole area lies on the positive side.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A disc starts from rest at $\alpha=2\,\mathrm{rad/s^2}$. How far does it turn from $t=2\,\mathrm s$ to $t=4\,\mathrm s$?

$$
\begin{aligned}
\theta(2)-\theta(0)&=\frac{2}{2}\cdot2^2=4\,\mathrm{rad}\\[6pt]
\theta(4)-\theta(0)&=\frac{2}{2}\cdot4^2=16\,\mathrm{rad}\\[6pt]
\theta(4)-\theta(2)&=16-4=12\,\mathrm{rad}.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The disc starts from rest at $t=0$. To isolate the requested interval, calculate two angles from the same starting time and subtract the angle accumulated up to two seconds. Substituting a duration of two seconds directly into the equation for a start from rest would calculate the angle in the first interval.

A second check uses the velocities at the ends of the actual interval. At two seconds the wheel is already turning at four radians per second and at four seconds it reaches eight. The mean height of the trapezium is therefore six radians per second. Multiplying that height by the two-second duration recovers the signed angle of twelve radians.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A blade starts at rest and turns through $18\,\mathrm{rad}$ in $3\,\mathrm s$ at constant angular acceleration. Find that acceleration.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta\theta&=\frac12\alpha t^2\\[6pt]
\alpha&=\frac{2\Delta\theta}{t^2}\\[6pt]
&=\frac{2\cdot18}{3^2}=4\,\mathrm{rad/s^2}.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Acceleration is the only unknown because the angle, the time and the zero initial angular velocity are given. To isolate it, divide twice the angle by the square of the time. The positive result agrees with a displacement in the chosen direction from rest.

As a check, this acceleration gives a final velocity of twelve radians per second and a mean of six. Multiplying the mean by three seconds returns eighteen radians. If the initial velocity were unknown, the angle alone would leave the acceleration undetermined. An initial velocity of three radians per second and an acceleration of two radians per second squared would give the same angle.

</div>
