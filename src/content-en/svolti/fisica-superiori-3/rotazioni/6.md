## Exercise 1

A rotor changes inertia from $3$ to $1.5\,\mathrm{kg\cdot m^2}$ with no external torque. It starts at $2\,\mathrm{rad/s}$. Find final angular velocity.

::grafico{f="6/x" punti="3,2,pieno;1.5,4,pieno" xmin=1 xmax=4 ymin=0 ymax=7 nomi="I,ω" titolo="The change follows a constant-angular-momentum curve"}

$$
\omega_f=\frac{I_i\omega_i}{I_f},\qquad
\begin{aligned}\omega_f&=3 \cdot 2 \cdot \frac{1}{3 \cdot \frac{1}{2}}\\[4pt]&=4\,\mathrm{rad/s}.\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Angular momentum is set by the initial product. Halving the inertia doubles the speed needed to preserve that product.

Check $3\cdot2=1.5\cdot4$, in $\mathrm{kg\cdot m^2/s}$. Twice the angular velocity exactly offsets half the inertia. Energy can change when mass is redistributed because the zero external torque balance constrains angular momentum.

</div>

## Exercise 2

An isolated system starts at $I_i=4\,\mathrm{kg\cdot m^2}$ and $\omega_i=3\,\mathrm{rad/s}$. It reaches $8\,\mathrm{rad/s}$. Find the final inertia.

$$
I_f=\frac{I_i\omega_i}{\omega_f},\qquad
\begin{aligned}I_f&=4 \cdot 3 \cdot \frac{1}{8}\\[4pt]&=1.5\,\mathrm{kg\cdot m^2}.\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Unknown inertia multiplied by final angular velocity has to give the same angular momentum. Divide the initial product by the new velocity.

Initial angular momentum along the axis is $4\cdot3=12\,\mathrm{kg\cdot m^2/s}$. Division by eight radians per second gives $1.5\,\mathrm{kg\cdot m^2}$. Inertia falls: if it had stayed at four, the prescribed final speed would require additional angular momentum.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A skater has $L=6\,\mathrm{kg\cdot m^2/s}$. They reduce inertia from $3$ to $1.5\,\mathrm{kg\cdot m^2}$ with no external torque. By how much does energy increase?

$$
\Delta K=\frac{L^2}{2}\left(\frac1{I_f}-\frac1{I_i}\right),\qquad
\begin{aligned}\Delta K&=\frac{6^{2} \cdot \left(- \frac{1}{3} + 1 \cdot \frac{1}{3 \cdot \frac{1}{2}}\right)}{2}\\[4pt]&=6\,\mathrm J.\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

At fixed angular momentum, energy is inversely proportional to inertia. Muscular work supplies the increase.

At $L=6\,\mathrm{kg\cdot m^2/s}$ the two energies are $\dfrac{36}{2\cdot3}=6\,\mathrm J$ and $\dfrac{36}{2\cdot1.5}=12\,\mathrm J$. The positive difference can come from internal work done in reducing inertia. Conservation of angular momentum does not force conservation of kinetic energy alone.

</div>
