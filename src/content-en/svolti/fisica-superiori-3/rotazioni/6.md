## Exercise 1

A rotor changes inertia from $3$ to $1.5\,\mathrm{kg\cdot m^2}$ with no external torque. It starts at $2\,\mathrm{rad/s}$. Find final angular velocity.

$$
\begin{aligned}
L_i&=I_i\omega_i=3\cdot2=6\,\mathrm{kg\cdot m^2/s}\\[6pt]
L_f&=L_i\\[6pt]
\omega_f&=\frac{L_i}{I_f}=\frac{6}{1.5}=4\,\mathrm{rad/s}.
\end{aligned}
$$

::grafico{f="6/x" punti="3,2,pieno;1.5,4,pieno" xmin=1 xmax=4 ymin=0 ymax=7 nomi="I (kg·m²),ω (rad/s)" titolo="The change follows a constant-angular-momentum curve"}

<div class="spiegazione">

The external torque along the axis is zero while the masses shift, so the axial component of angular momentum keeps its value. The initial values fix that value. The moment of inertia has halved by the end, and the angular velocity comes from dividing the conserved angular momentum by the final inertia.

On the graph every point on the curve has the same product of horizontal and vertical coordinates. When the inertia is three, the angular velocity is two radians per second. At an inertia of one and a half, four radians per second are needed to keep the product. The sign remains positive because the angular momentum has that sign and the moment of inertia is positive.

</div>

## Exercise 2

An isolated system starts at $I_i=4\,\mathrm{kg\cdot m^2}$ and $\omega_i=3\,\mathrm{rad/s}$. It reaches $8\,\mathrm{rad/s}$. Find the final inertia.

$$
\begin{aligned}
L&=I_i\omega_i=4\cdot3=12\,\mathrm{kg\cdot m^2/s}\\[6pt]
I_f&=\frac{L}{\omega_f}=\frac{12}{8}\\[6pt]
&=1.5\,\mathrm{kg\cdot m^2}.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Isolation lets us conserve angular momentum along the rotation axis. Before the change, the product of inertia and angular velocity is twelve in angular momentum units. The product must have the same value at the end. Since the new velocity is known, solve for the inertia as the other factor.

The ratio of final to initial angular velocity is greater than one. The final inertia should be less than four, consistent with the result. If the two velocities had opposite signs, conservation would demand a negative inertia, which is impossible for a physical mass distribution. Checking the sign therefore also checks the initial assumption.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A skater has $L=6\,\mathrm{kg\cdot m^2/s}$. They reduce inertia from $3$ to $1.5\,\mathrm{kg\cdot m^2}$ with no external torque. By how much does energy increase?

$$
\begin{aligned}
K_i&=\frac{L^2}{2I_i}=\frac{6^2}{2\cdot3}=6\,\mathrm J\\[6pt]
K_f&=\frac{L^2}{2I_f}=\frac{6^2}{2\cdot1.5}=12\,\mathrm J\\[6pt]
\Delta K&=K_f-K_i=6\,\mathrm J.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The angular momentum is given and stays the same because there is no external torque along the axis. We can therefore remove $\omega$ from the energy equation by substituting $\omega=\dfrac LI$. This leaves an inverse relationship between kinetic energy and inertia. Halving the inertia makes the final energy twice the initial energy.

The question asks for the gain in energy, which is the difference between the two values. Even without external angular impulse, the skater can convert internal energy into kinetic energy while pulling their arms in. Muscular work is consistent with the six joules gained. The energy and angular momentum balances describe distinct properties of the same change.

</div>
