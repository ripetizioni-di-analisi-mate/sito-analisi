## Exercise 1

Two masses attract with $24\,\mathrm N$ in the stated model. What is the force if their centre separation doubles?

::grafico{f="24/x^2" punti="1,24,pieno,A;2,6,pieno,B" xmin=.8 xmax=3 ymin=0 ymax=30 nomi="r/r_1,F (N)" titolo="Doubling separation reduces force magnitude to one quarter"}

$$
F_2=F_1(r_1/r_2)^2,\qquad
\begin{aligned}F_2&=\frac{24}{2^{2}}\\[4pt]&=6\,\mathrm N.\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The denominator alone changes at fixed masses. Squared separation is four times its initial value.

Use centre separation for point masses or nonoverlapping spherically symmetric bodies. As a check, multiplying the $6\,\mathrm N$ force at twice the separation by the square of that ratio gives the initial $24\,\mathrm N$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Two masses attract with $18\,\mathrm N$. One mass triples and their centre separation doubles. Find the new force.

$$
F_2=F_1\frac{m'_1}{m_1}\left(\frac{r_1}{r_2}\right)^2,\qquad
\begin{aligned}F_2&=18 \cdot 3 \cdot \frac{1}{4}\\[4pt]&=13.5\,\mathrm N.\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Tripling the numerator increases force, while doubling distance quadruples the denominator. Combine both changes.

First triple the mass at fixed separation, which would give $54\,\mathrm N$. Then double separation, dividing that value by four. The resulting $13.5\,\mathrm N$ is less than the initial force because the second change has the greater effect.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Point masses of $150$ and $200\,\mathrm{kg}$ are $1\,\mathrm m$ apart. With $G=6.67\cdot10^{-11}\,\mathrm{N\cdot m^2/kg^2}$ find their attraction.

$$
F=\frac{Gm_1m_2}{r^2},\qquad
\begin{aligned}F&=667 \cdot \frac{1}{100} \cdot \frac{1}{100000000000} \cdot 150 \cdot 200\\[4pt]&=2.001\cdot10^{-6}\,\mathrm N.\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The squared separation is one in SI units. The mass product times $G$ gives a very small force at laboratory scales.

The mass product is $3\cdot10^4\,\mathrm{kg^2}$. Multiply by the constant and divide by the square metre to cancel all units except newtons. The result is a few micronewtons, consistent with the small gravitational constant.

</div>
