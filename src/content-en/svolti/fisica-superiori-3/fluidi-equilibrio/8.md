## Exercise 1

In a water manometer, a pressure difference of $2000\,\mathrm{Pa}$ produces a level difference. With $g=10\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$ find this level difference.

::grafico{assi=0 linee="0,.7 0,0 1,0 1,.7;.2,.7 .2,.2 .8,.2 .8,.7;0,.3 .2,.3;.8,.5 1,.5;.2,.3 .8,.3" vettori=".1,.65,.1,.34,p_L;.9,.65,.9,.52,p_R" xmin=-.3 xmax=1.4 ymin=-.1 ymax=.88 titolo="The levels differ by twenty centimetres"}

$$
\Delta h=\frac{\Delta p}{\rho g},\qquad
\begin{aligned}\Delta h&=\frac{2000}{10 \cdot 1000}\\[4pt]&=0.2\,\mathrm m.\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The equivalent column must supply the measured pressure difference. Divide by ten thousand pascals per metre.

The ratio uses the difference between pressures at the two interfaces. For water at $g=10\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$, a metre of level difference corresponds to $10000\,\mathrm{Pa}$. The stated $2000\,\mathrm{Pa}$ is one fifth of that, so the height difference is one fifth of a metre.

</div>

## Exercise 2

In a U-tube with oil of density $800\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}$, the left arm is $0.15\,\mathrm m$ higher. With $g=10\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$ find $p_\mathrm{right}-p_\mathrm{left}$.

$$
\Delta p=\rho g\Delta h,\qquad
\begin{aligned}\Delta p&=800 \cdot 10 \cdot 15 \cdot \frac{1}{100}\\[4pt]&=1200\,\mathrm{Pa}.\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The right arm is lower, so its applied pressure is higher. The positive change is that from the high level to the low level in the same liquid.

Choose a level below both surfaces: pressure at that level in one connected liquid must agree in the two arms. The left-to-reference drop is greater, so pressure applied at the left is lower. Right minus left is positive and equals $800\cdot10\cdot0.15=1200\,\mathrm{Pa}$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Two manometer liquids measure the same pressure difference. Their densities are $1000$ and $1250\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}$. Find the second-to-first level difference ratio.

$$
h_2/h_1=\rho_1/\rho_2,\qquad
\begin{aligned}h_2/h_1&=\frac{1000}{1250}\\[4pt]&=0.8.\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Equal gravity and pressure difference give equal density times level difference. The denser liquid requires a smaller level difference.

Equating the pressure differences gives $\rho_1g\Delta h_1=\rho_2g\Delta h_2$. Cancelling $g$ leaves the inverse density ratio. The denser liquid needs less height because each metre of column carries greater weight per unit area.

</div>
