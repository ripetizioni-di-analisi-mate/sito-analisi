## Exercise 1

In a manometer filled with water of density $1000\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}$, a pressure difference of $2000\,\mathrm{Pa}$ produces a level difference. With $g=10\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$ find this level difference.

::grafico{assi=0 linee="0,.7 0,0 1,0 1,.7;.2,.7 .2,.2 .8,.2 .8,.7;0,.3 .2,.3;.8,.5 1,.5" segmenti=".2,.3,.8,.3" vettori=".1,.65,.1,.34,p_L;.9,.65,.9,.52,p_R" xmin=-.3 xmax=1.4 ymin=-.1 ymax=.88 titolo="The levels differ by twenty centimetres"}

$$
\Delta h=\dfrac{\Delta p}{\rho g}=\dfrac{2000}{1000\cdot10}\,\mathrm{m}=0.20\,\mathrm{m}.
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The equivalent column must supply the measured pressure difference. Divide by ten thousand pascals per metre.

The ratio uses the difference between pressures at the two interfaces. For water at $g=10\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$, a metre of level difference corresponds to $10000\,\mathrm{Pa}$. The stated $2000\,\mathrm{Pa}$ is one fifth of that, so the height difference is one fifth of a metre.

Compare the pressures along the horizontal reference line through the lower level. The two arms have the same pressure there. The branch with the higher surface connects to the lower applied pressure.

</div>

## Exercise 2

In the left arm of a U-tube, oil of density $800\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}$ forms a column $0.30\,\mathrm m$ high above the water. The absolute pressure on the oil surface is $98\,\mathrm{kPa}$. The right arm contains only water of density $1000\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}$ and is open to air at $100\,\mathrm{kPa}$. With $g=10\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$, find the signed difference in height between the two free surfaces, left minus right.

$$
\begin{aligned}
p_I&=98\,\mathrm{kPa}+800\cdot10\cdot0.30\,\mathrm{Pa}=100.4\,\mathrm{kPa}\\[4pt]
z_D-z_I&=\dfrac{p_I-p_{\rm atm}}{\rho_a g}
=\dfrac{400}{1000\cdot10}\,\mathrm m=0.04\,\mathrm m\\[4pt]
z_S-z_D&=(z_S-z_I)-(z_D-z_I)=(0.30-0.04)\,\mathrm m=+0.26\,\mathrm m.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Descend from the oil surface to interface I and add the column's weight per unit area. At that same height in the right arm, the connected water must be at the pressure of the interface. Since it exceeds atmospheric pressure by $400\,\mathrm{Pa}$, the right free surface lies just above I. Subtracting that rise from the oil column height gives the visible height difference. Here the higher surface also has the lower gas pressure, while the contributions of both liquids determine the actual height difference. Applying the single-liquid manometer relation would use the wrong column.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Two manometer liquids measure the same pressure difference. Their densities are $1000$ and $1250\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}$. Find the second-to-first level difference ratio.

$$
\dfrac{h_2}{h_1}=\dfrac{\rho_1}{\rho_2}=\dfrac{1000}{1250}=0.80.
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Equal gravity and pressure difference give equal density times level difference. The denser liquid requires a smaller level difference.

Equating the pressure differences gives $\rho_1g\Delta h_1=\rho_2g\Delta h_2$. Cancelling $g$ leaves the inverse density ratio. The denser liquid needs less height because each metre of column carries greater weight per unit area.

The ratio is dimensionless. If the first manometer showed a height difference of $0.30\,\mathrm m$, the second would show $0.24\,\mathrm m$. With the same $\Delta p$ the denser column is shorter.

</div>
