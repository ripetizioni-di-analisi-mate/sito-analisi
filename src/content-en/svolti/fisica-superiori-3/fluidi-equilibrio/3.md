## Exercise 1

The surface pressure is $100\,\mathrm{kPa}$. Find absolute pressure $3\,\mathrm m$ below water with $g=10\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$.

::grafico{f="100+10*x" punti="0,100,pieno;3,130,pieno" xmin=0 xmax=4 ymin=90 ymax=145 nomi="h (m),p (kPa)" titolo="The intercept is absolute pressure at the surface"}

$$
p=p_0+\rho gh,\qquad
\begin{aligned}p&=1000 \cdot 10 \cdot 3 + 100000\\[4pt]&=1.3\cdot10^{5}\,\mathrm{Pa}.\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Add thirty thousand pascals from the water column to the absolute surface value. Absolute zero pressure is vacuum.

At water density $1000\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}$, three metres add $30\,\mathrm{kPa}$. The initial $100\,\mathrm{kPa}$ already exists at the surface and is added. The result is $130\,\mathrm{kPa}$ absolute. Thirty kilopascals alone would be the increase relative to surface pressure.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A closed tank has gas above the water at $180\,\mathrm{kPa}$ absolute. Its water is $2\,\mathrm m$ deep with $g=10\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$. Find the absolute bottom pressure.

$$
p=p_0+\rho gh,\qquad
\begin{aligned}p&=1000 \cdot 10 \cdot 2 \cdot \frac{1}{1000} + 180\\[4pt]&=200\,\mathrm{kPa}.\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The water layer adds twenty kilopascals to the gas pressure. A closed vessel uses the specified gas pressure rather than outside atmospheric pressure.

Water is at rest and receives the gas pressure at its top boundary. With density about $1000\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}$, two metres of water add $20000\,\mathrm{Pa}$ or $20\,\mathrm{kPa}$. The $180\,\mathrm{kPa}$ gas pressure is already absolute and is the pressure at zero depth for this calculation, without adding outside air pressure.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A sensor reads $125\,\mathrm{kPa}$ absolute $2\,\mathrm m$ below water. Find surface pressure with $g=10\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$.

$$
p_0=p-\rho gh,\qquad
\begin{aligned}p_0&=125 - 1000 \cdot 10 \cdot 2 \cdot \frac{1}{1000}\\[4pt]&=105\,\mathrm{kPa}.\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Rising by two metres subtracts the column weight per area. A sensor near the surface would therefore read a lower value.

Moving from the sensor up to the surface removes the water-column contribution, here $1000\cdot10\cdot2=20000\,\mathrm{Pa}$. The sensor reads $125\,\mathrm{kPa}$ and the surface pressure must therefore be lower. Restoring the hydrostatic twenty kilopascals to the calculated $105$ reproduces the reading.

</div>
