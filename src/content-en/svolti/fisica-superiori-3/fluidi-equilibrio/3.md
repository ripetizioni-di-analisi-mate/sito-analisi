## Exercise 1

The surface pressure is $100\,\mathrm{kPa}$. Find absolute pressure $3\,\mathrm m$ below the surface of water of density $1000\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}$ with $g=10\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$.

::grafico{f="100+10*x" punti="0,100,pieno;3,130,pieno" xmin=0 xmax=4 ymin=90 ymax=140 nomi="h (m),p (kPa)" titolo="The intercept is absolute pressure at the surface"}

$$
p=p_0+\rho gh=(100000+1000\cdot10\cdot3)\,\mathrm{Pa}=130000\,\mathrm{Pa}.
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Add thirty thousand pascals from the water column to the absolute surface value. Absolute zero pressure is vacuum.

At water density $1000\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}$, three metres add $30\,\mathrm{kPa}$. The initial $100\,\mathrm{kPa}$ already exists at the surface and is added. The result is $130\,\mathrm{kPa}$ absolute. Thirty kilopascals alone would be the increase relative to surface pressure.

On the graph the initial point is at $100\,\mathrm{kPa}$ and water gives a slope of $10\,\mathrm{kPa}$ per metre. Find the final ordinate by adding the increase to that intercept.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A closed tank has gas above the water at $180\,\mathrm{kPa}$ absolute. The water has density $1000\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}$ and is $2\,\mathrm m$ deep with $g=10\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$. Find the absolute bottom pressure.

$$
p=p_0+\rho gh=180\,\mathrm{kPa}+20000\,\mathrm{Pa}=200\,\mathrm{kPa}.
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The water layer adds twenty kilopascals to the gas pressure. A closed vessel uses the specified gas pressure rather than outside atmospheric pressure.

Water is at rest and receives the gas pressure at its top boundary. With density $1000\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}$, two metres of water add $20000\,\mathrm{Pa}$ or $20\,\mathrm{kPa}$. The $180\,\mathrm{kPa}$ gas pressure is already absolute and is the pressure at zero depth for this calculation, without adding outside air pressure.

A sensor referenced to outside air at $100\,\mathrm{kPa}$ would read $100\,\mathrm{kPa}$ at the bottom. The same physical point can have a different reading when the pressure reference changes.

</div>

## Exercise 3

In a closed tank, a sensor at depth $2.0\,\mathrm m$ below water of density $1000\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}$ reads $125\,\mathrm{kPa}$ absolute. A second sensor measures the gas pressure at the water surface against the local atmosphere of $98\,\mathrm{kPa}$. With $g=10\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$, find the absolute gas pressure and the second sensor reading.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\rho gh&=1000\cdot10\cdot2.0\,\mathrm{Pa}=20\,\mathrm{kPa}\\[4pt]
p_0&=p-\rho gh=(125-20)\,\mathrm{kPa}=105\,\mathrm{kPa}\\[4pt]
p_{\rm rel}&=p_0-p_{\rm atm}=(105-98)\,\mathrm{kPa}=7\,\mathrm{kPa}.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The two subtractions serve different purposes. The first moves from the absolute sensor in the water up to the free surface and removes the column's hydrostatic contribution. The resulting value is also the pressure of the gas touching the water. The second subtraction changes the reference from vacuum to the local atmosphere. The gauge reading is positive, consistent with gas at $105\,\mathrm{kPa}$ being slightly above outside pressure. As a check, a gauge at the depth of the first sensor would read $27\,\mathrm{kPa}$, and a two-metre ascent would reduce that reading by $20\,\mathrm{kPa}$.

</div>
