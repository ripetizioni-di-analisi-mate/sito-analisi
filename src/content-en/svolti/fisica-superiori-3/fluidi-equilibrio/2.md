## Exercise 1

You descend $2\,\mathrm m$ in water with $g=10\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$ and $\rho=1000\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}$. Find the pressure change.

$$
\Delta p=\rho gh,\qquad
\begin{aligned}\Delta p&=1000 \cdot 10 \cdot 2\\[4pt]&=20000\,\mathrm{Pa}.\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Each metre of added water gives ten thousand pascals of weight per area. Two metres give twenty thousand pascals.

Above a horizontal section of $1\,\mathrm{m^2}$, two metres of water add $2000\,\mathrm{kg}$ of fluid. Its weight is $20000\,\mathrm N$, or $20000\,\mathrm{Pa}$ per unit area. Choosing another area would scale weight and area by the same factor. At uniform density and gravity only the vertical drop remains.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Oil of density $800\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}$ and depth $0.5\,\mathrm m$ lies over one metre of water. With $g=10\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$ find the gauge pressure at the bottom.

::grafico{linee="0,0 .5,4 1.5,14" punti="0,0,pieno;.5,4,pieno;1.5,14,pieno" xmin=0 xmax=1.8 ymin=0 ymax=16 nomi="h (m),Δp (kPa)" titolo="Pressure relative to the surface increases faster within water"}

$$
\Delta p=\rho_1gh_1+\rho_2gh_2,\qquad
\begin{aligned}\Delta p&=800 \cdot 10 \cdot \frac{1}{2} + 1000 \cdot 10\\[4pt]&=14000\,\mathrm{Pa}.\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The two columns have different weights per area. Add four thousand from the oil to ten thousand from the water, relative to the surface pressure.

Oil contributes $800\cdot10\cdot0.5=4000\,\mathrm{Pa}$. At the interface this rise is the starting datum for water, which adds $10000\,\mathrm{Pa}$. Pressure is continuous through the flat interface, but its slope with depth changes because the layer densities differ.

</div>

## Exercise 3

In a homogeneous liquid pressure increases by $24\,\mathrm{kPa}$ per $3\,\mathrm m$. With $g=10\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$ find its density.

$$
\rho=\frac{\Delta p}{gh},\qquad
\begin{aligned}\rho&=\frac{24000}{3 \cdot 10}\\[4pt]&=800\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}.\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Divide the observed increase by gravity and depth. The resulting density sets the slope of the hydrostatic graph.

Pressure rises by $24000/3=8000\,\mathrm{Pa/m}$. That slope is weight per unit volume, $\rho g$. Dividing by ten metres per second squared gives density $800\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}$, below water's. As a check, three metres of water would give thirty thousand pascals here, more than the measured rise.

</div>
