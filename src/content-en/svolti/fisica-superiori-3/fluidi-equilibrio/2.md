## Exercise 1

You descend $2\,\mathrm m$ in water with $g=10\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$ and $\rho=1000\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}$. Find the pressure change.

$$
\Delta p=\rho gh=1000\cdot10\cdot2\,\mathrm{Pa}=20000\,\mathrm{Pa}.
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Each metre of added water gives ten thousand pascals of weight per area. Two metres give twenty thousand pascals.

Above a horizontal section of $1\,\mathrm{m^2}$, two metres of water add $2000\,\mathrm{kg}$ of fluid. Its weight is $20000\,\mathrm N$, or $20000\,\mathrm{Pa}$ per unit area. Choosing another area would scale weight and area by the same factor. At uniform density and gravity only the vertical drop remains.

The result is an increase along the descent. Travelling back through the same two metres reverses the sign of that change and restores the starting pressure.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Oil of density $800\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}$ and depth $0.5\,\mathrm m$ lies over one metre of water of density $1000\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}$ in an open vessel. With $g=10\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$ find the gauge pressure at the bottom.

::grafico{linee="0,0 .5,4 1.5,14" punti="0,0,pieno;.5,4,pieno;1.5,14,pieno" xmin=0 xmax=1.8 ymin=0 ymax=16 nomi="h (m),Δp (kPa)" titolo="Pressure relative to the surface increases faster within water"}

$$
\Delta p=\rho_1gh_1+\rho_2gh_2=(800\cdot10\cdot0.5+1000\cdot10\cdot1)\,\mathrm{Pa}=14000\,\mathrm{Pa}.
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The two columns have different weights per area. Add four thousand from the oil to ten thousand from the water, relative to the surface pressure.

The oil contributes $800\cdot10\cdot0.5=4000\,\mathrm{Pa}$. At the interface this rise is the starting datum for water, which adds $10000\,\mathrm{Pa}$. Pressure is continuous through the flat interface, but its slope with depth changes because the layer densities differ.

To recover the surface reading from the bottom, subtract $10000\,\mathrm{Pa}$ and then $4000\,\mathrm{Pa}$. The excess pressure returns to zero relative to the starting reference.

</div>

## Exercise 3

In a stationary tank, two immiscible liquids meet $0.60\,\mathrm m$ below the surface. A sensor at the interface reads $4.0\,\mathrm{kPa}$ gauge pressure, and another at depth $1.35\,\mathrm m$ reads $11.5\,\mathrm{kPa}$ against the same reference. With $g=10\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$ and negligible surface tension, find the lower layer's density.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta h&=(1.35-0.60)\,\mathrm m=0.75\,\mathrm m\\[4pt]
\Delta p&=(11.5-4.0)\,\mathrm{kPa}=7500\,\mathrm{Pa}\\[4pt]
\rho_2&=\dfrac{\Delta p}{g\Delta h}=\dfrac{7500}{10\cdot0.75}\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}
=1000\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The density of interest sets the slope below the interface. Before dividing, subtract both the readings and the depths. The deeper sensor includes the contribution from the fluid above the interface and the same pressure reference. Using the last reading and total depth alone would give a mean slope through both layers. The density is consistent with water, which would add $10\,\mathrm{kPa}$ per metre. Across $0.75\,\mathrm m$ it adds $7.5\,\mathrm{kPa}$, which restores the lower reading when added to $4.0\,\mathrm{kPa}$.

</div>
