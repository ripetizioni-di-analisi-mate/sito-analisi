## Exercise 1

Water in an ideal horizontal pipe speeds up from $2$ to $4\,\mathrm{m/s}$. Find the pressure drop. Use $\rho=1000\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}$.

$$
\begin{aligned}p_1-p_2&=\tfrac12\rho(v_2^2-v_1^2)\\&=\tfrac12(1000)(4^2-2^2)\,\mathrm{Pa}=6000\,\mathrm{Pa}.\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Compare two points on the same streamline. The pipe is horizontal, so the height terms agree. The kinetic term goes from $2\,\mathrm{kPa}$ to $8\,\mathrm{kPa}$ when speed doubles because it depends on the square. In the ideal model, pressure gives up exactly those six kilopascals. The requested drop is $p_1-p_2$, positive through the contraction. If two taps measured a larger drop with the same geometry and the stated speeds, it would be worth checking for dissipation between the points.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Water of density $1000\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}$ flows steadily through an ideal horizontal Venturi. The volume flow rate is $0.024\,\mathrm{m^3/s}$ and the inlet area is $0.012\,\mathrm{m^2}$. Pressure falls from $125\,\mathrm{kPa}$ at the inlet to $119\,\mathrm{kPa}$ at the throat. Find the throat area.

::grafico{assi=0 linee="-4,1.4 -1,1.4 1,1 4,1;-4,-1.4 -1,-1.4 1,-1 4,-1" vettori="-3.3,0,-1.9,0,v_1;1,0,3.8,0,v_2" xmin=-4.6 xmax=4.9 ymin=-2.2 ymax=2.3 titolo="Ideal flow rate is conserved through the throat"}

$$
\begin{aligned}v_1&=\frac{0.024}{0.012}\,\mathrm{m/s}=2\,\mathrm{m/s},\\ v_2&=\sqrt{2^2+\frac{2\cdot6000}{1000}}\,\mathrm{m/s}=4\,\mathrm{m/s},\\ A_2&=\frac{0.024}{4}\,\mathrm{m^2}=0.006\,\mathrm{m^2}.\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The flow measurement gives the inlet speed through the known area. Then compare two points on the same streamline. The height term agrees for the horizontal pipe. The pressure drop of $6000\,\mathrm{Pa}$ therefore goes into an increase in the kinetic term. Take the positive square root to find the throat speed. Finally, the flow rate at the throat equals the inlet flow rate, so dividing by the new speed gives the area. Twice the speed requires half the area, as the numbers show. Swapping the pressures would give a negative squared speed and expose the sign error.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Water of density $1000\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}$ flows steadily through a horizontal pipe with no pumps or branches. The velocity is uniform across each of the two sections. The measured values are $p_1=130\,\mathrm{kPa}$ and $v_1=2\,\mathrm{m/s}$ upstream, and $p_2=117\,\mathrm{kPa}$ and $v_2=6\,\mathrm{m/s}$ downstream. Check whether the data follow ideal Bernoulli and whether dissipation alone could explain the discrepancy.

$$
\begin{aligned}(p_1-p_2)_{\mathrm{id}}&=\tfrac12\rho(6^2-2^2)\,\mathrm{m^2/s^2}=16\,\mathrm{kPa},\\ (p_1-p_2)_{\mathrm{mis}}&=(130-117)\,\mathrm{kPa}=13\,\mathrm{kPa},\\ \Delta\left(p+\tfrac12\rho v^2\right)&=(16-13)\,\mathrm{kPa}=3\,\mathrm{kPa}.\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

At equal heights, the increase in the kinetic term requires an equal drop in the pressure term. The kinetic increase is sixteen kilopascals, while the measured pressure drop is thirteen. Some of the predicted drop is therefore missing. The given data would leave $3\,\mathrm{kPa}$ more mechanical energy per unit volume downstream. Dissipation causes a negative balance, requiring a greater pressure drop than the ideal one at the stated speeds. The data therefore fail the ideal balance, and dissipation alone cannot reconcile them. Recheck the pressure readings, the speed readings and the height reference.

</div>
