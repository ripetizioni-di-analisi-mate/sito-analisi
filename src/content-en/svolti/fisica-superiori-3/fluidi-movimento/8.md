## Exercise 1

A sphere of radius $0.50\,\mathrm{mm}$ and density $1200\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}$ falls far from the walls in a stationary Newtonian liquid. The liquid has density $1000\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}$ and viscosity $0.20\,\mathrm{Pa\cdot s}$. Use $g=10\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$. Estimate its terminal speed using Stokes drag and then check that the Reynolds number is very small.

$$
\begin{aligned}6\pi\eta rv_\ell&=(\rho_{\mathrm s}-\rho)\frac{4\pi r^3}{3}g,\\ v_\ell&=\frac{2(\rho_{\mathrm s}-\rho)gr^2}{9\eta}\\&\simeq5.56\cdot10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m/s}=0.556\,\mathrm{mm/s},\\ \mathrm{Re}&=\frac{\rho v_\ell(2r)}{\eta}\simeq0.00278.\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

At terminal speed, weight is balanced by buoyancy plus drag. The quantity drag must balance is weight minus buoyancy, which equals the sphere's volume times the density difference and gravity. Equate that quantity to Stokes drag and isolate the speed. Convert the radius to metres first, including in the diameter used for Reynolds number. The speed is much smaller than a metre per second. The resulting Reynolds number is below a hundredth, consistent with the requirement that it be much smaller than one. The sphere is denser than the fluid, so its terminal motion is downward.

</div>

## Exercise 2

In Stokes flow the same sphere doubles its relative speed and moves into a liquid of triple viscosity. Find the drag force ratio.

$$
\frac{F_2}{F_1}=\frac{\eta_2}{\eta_1}\frac{v_2}{v_1}=3\cdot2=6.
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Compare the drag magnitudes for the same sphere. The Stokes expression contains the product of viscosity, radius and relative speed. Radius and the common coefficient cancel from the ratio. The factor of three in viscosity and the factor of two in speed act together, so multiply them. Drag still opposes relative velocity in each liquid. If the liquid densities also agreed, the second Reynolds number would be two thirds of the first because viscosity is in the denominator of Reynolds number.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A sphere rises at terminal speed in a stationary fluid. The upward buoyancy is $0.010\,\mathrm N$ and the drag magnitude is $0.006\,\mathrm N$. Find the sphere's weight and state the direction of the drag.

::grafico{assi=0 uguale=1 curve=".48*cos(t),.48*sin(t),0,6.283185" vettori="0,0,0,2,F_A;-.36,0,-.36,-.8,P;.36,0,.36,-1.2,F_v" xmin=-1.4 xmax=1.4 ymin=-1.65 ymax=2.35 titolo="During ascent at terminal speed, weight and drag point downward."}

$$
\begin{aligned}F_A-P-F_v&=0,\\ P&=(0.010-0.006)\,\mathrm N=0.004\,\mathrm N.\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The sphere rises and the distant fluid is stationary, so drag points downward. Weight also acts downward. Taking upward as positive, buoyancy must equal the sum of the two downward magnitudes. Subtracting drag from buoyancy gives the weight. Buoyancy is greater than weight, as expected for a sphere that rises on its own. If speed dropped, drag would decrease and the net force would point upward again. Terminal speed is the speed that allows the three forces to balance.

</div>
