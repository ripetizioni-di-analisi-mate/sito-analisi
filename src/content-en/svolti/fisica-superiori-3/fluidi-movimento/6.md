## Exercise 1

Ideal water has equal speeds at two sections, with the second $2\,\mathrm m$ higher. Use $g=10\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$ to find $p_2-p_1$.

$$
p_2-p_1=-\rho g(y_2-y_1),\qquad
\begin{aligned}p_2-p_1&=\left(-1000\right) \cdot 10 \cdot 2\\[4pt]&=-20000\,\mathrm{Pa}.\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Kinetic terms agree. The higher pressure level is lower by the gravitational energy change per volume.

Check the sign by taking the limit of equal speeds close to zero, which recovers the hydrostatic law. The higher section, $2$, therefore has $20\,\mathrm{kPa}$ less pressure than section $1$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Ideal water rises $1\,\mathrm m$ and slows from $6$ to $2\,\mathrm{m/s}$. With $g=10\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$ find $p_2-p_1$.

::grafico{assi=0 linee="0,0 2,0 4,1 6,1" vettori=".2,0,1.6,0,v_1;4.6,1,5.07,1,v_2" punti="1,0,pieno,1;5,1,pieno,2" xmin=-1 xmax=7 ymin=-1 ymax=2.2 titolo="Point 2 is one metre higher. Horizontal distance is schematic"}

$$
p_2-p_1=\frac12\rho(v_1^2-v_2^2)-\rho g\Delta y,\qquad
\begin{aligned}p_2-p_1&=- 10 \cdot 1000 + \frac{1000 \cdot \left(- 2^{2} + 6^{2}\right)}{2}\\[4pt]&=6000\,\mathrm{Pa}.\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Slowing adds sixteen thousand pascals, and rising subtracts ten thousand. The net change remains positive.

The decrease in the kinetic term would exactly compensate for a rise of $16000/(\rho g)=1.6\,\mathrm m$. The given rise is smaller, so $p_2$ exceeds $p_1$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Ideal water enters at $p_1=160\,\mathrm{kPa}$ and $2\,\mathrm{m/s}$, then rises $1\,\mathrm m$ and reaches $4\,\mathrm{m/s}$. Find $p_2$ in kilopascals with $g=10\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$.

$$
p_2=p_1+\frac12\rho(v_1^2-v_2^2)-\rho g\Delta y,\qquad
\begin{aligned}p_2&=\frac{- 10 \cdot 1000 + \frac{1000 \cdot \left(- 4^{2} + 2^{2}\right)}{2}}{1000} + 160\\[4pt]&=144\,\mathrm{kPa}.\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Acceleration and ascent each subtract a pressure contribution. Convert the terms from pascals before adding them to $p_1$.

The kinetic term rises by $6\,\mathrm{kPa}$ and the gravitational term by $10\,\mathrm{kPa}$. Subtracting both from $160\,\mathrm{kPa}$ gives $144\,\mathrm{kPa}$.

</div>
