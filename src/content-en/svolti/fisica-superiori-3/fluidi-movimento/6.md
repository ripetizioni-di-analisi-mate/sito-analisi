## Exercise 1

Ideal water has equal speeds at two sections, with the second $2\,\mathrm m$ higher. Use $g=10\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$ to find $p_2-p_1$. Use $\rho=1000\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}$.

$$
p_2-p_1=-\rho g(y_2-y_1)=-1000\cdot10\cdot2\,\mathrm{Pa}=-20000\,\mathrm{Pa}.
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Choose upward as the positive vertical direction. The height change from point 1 to point 2 is therefore positive. In Bernoulli's equation the kinetic terms agree, so pressure compensates only for the gravitational change. The negative result means the second point's pressure is $20\,\mathrm{kPa}$ lower. It matches the hydrostatic result for the same height change even though the water is moving. Compare either two absolute pressures or two gauge pressures with the same reference. The calculated difference is the same in both cases.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Water flows steadily along a streamline in an ideal pipe with no pumps. Upstream gauge pressure is $35\,\mathrm{kPa}$ and speed is $3\,\mathrm{m/s}$. Downstream the pipe is $0.80\,\mathrm m$ lower and speed is $5\,\mathrm{m/s}$. Use $\rho=1000\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}$, $g=10\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$ and a reference atmospheric pressure of $101\,\mathrm{kPa}$. Find downstream gauge and absolute pressures.

::grafico{assi=0 linee="0,.8 2,.8 4,0 6,0" vettori=".45,1.03,1.35,1.03,v_1;4.44,.23,5.94,.23,v_2" punti="1.1,.8,pieno;5.15,0,pieno" segmenti="1.45,.8,6.55,.8;5.91,0,6.55,0;6.43,0,6.43,.8" etichette=".86,.54,1;5.06,-.28,2;6.55,.46,h" xmin=-.5 xmax=7.21 ymin=-.6 ymax=1.65 titolo="The downward height change has magnitude 0.80 m. Horizontal distances are schematic."}

$$
\begin{aligned}\tfrac12\rho(v_1^2-v_2^2)&=-8\,\mathrm{kPa},\\ -\rho g\Delta y&=+8\,\mathrm{kPa},\\p_{2,\mathrm{rel}}&=(35-8+8)\,\mathrm{kPa}=35\,\mathrm{kPa},\\p_{2,\mathrm{abs}}&=(35+101)\,\mathrm{kPa}=136\,\mathrm{kPa}.\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Use one pressure convention on both sides of Bernoulli's equation. Gauge pressures are suitable when both points use the same atmospheric reference. Speeding up the water increases kinetic energy per unit volume and tends to lower pressure. The descent provides exactly the opposite contribution. The net change is zero, so the gauge would still read thirty-five kilopascals. Finally add the atmospheric reference of $101\,\mathrm{kPa}$ to obtain absolute pressure. As a check, start from the absolute upstream value. The two contributions remain equal and opposite with that reference.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Ideal water enters at $p_1=160\,\mathrm{kPa}$ and $2\,\mathrm{m/s}$, then rises $1\,\mathrm m$ and reaches $4\,\mathrm{m/s}$. Find $p_2$ in kilopascals with $g=10\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$. Use $\rho=1000\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}$.

$$
\begin{aligned}\Delta p_{\mathrm{cin}}&=\tfrac12(1000)(4^2-2^2)\,\mathrm{Pa}=6\,\mathrm{kPa},\\p_2&=(160-6-10)\,\mathrm{kPa}=144\,\mathrm{kPa}.\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The balance compares two effects that both lower downstream pressure. The kinetic term rises by $6\,\mathrm{kPa}$ as speed increases. The height term rises by $10\,\mathrm{kPa}$ as the water climbs. Convert both terms to the unit of the initial reading and subtract them. To check the sign, imagine a section where only height increases. Even that change on its own would reduce pressure. Here speeding up increases the drop further. If the water slowed while rising, two opposing contributions would have to be compared.

</div>
