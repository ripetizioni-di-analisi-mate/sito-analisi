## Exercise 1

A $4\,\mathrm{kg}$ cart passes a sensor at $18\,\mathrm{km/h}$. Find its kinetic energy in the laboratory frame.

::grafico{f="2*x^2" punti="5,50,pieno" xmin=0 xmax=6 ymin=0 ymax=76 nomi="v (m/s),K (J)" titolo="Kinetic energy of the cart versus speed"}

$$
K=\frac12 mv^2,\qquad
\begin{aligned}K&=\frac{4 \cdot \left(18 \cdot 1000 \cdot \frac{1}{3600}\right)^{2}}{2}\\[4pt]&=50\,\mathrm J.\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Convert speed before squaring it. Eighteen kilometres in one hour is eighteen thousand metres in thirty-six hundred seconds, giving five metres per second.

The resulting units are kilogram metres squared per second squared. This combination equals a joule.

The curve shows a fifty-joule ordinate at five metres per second. Using eighteen without conversion would treat the speed as $18\,\mathrm{m/s}$ and overestimate energy by a factor of $3.6^2$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A $3\,\mathrm{kg}$ body has velocity components $-4$ and $3\,\mathrm{m/s}$. Find its kinetic energy.

$$
K=\frac12 m(v_x^2+v_y^2),\qquad
\begin{aligned}K&=\frac{3 \cdot \left(3^{2} + 4^{2}\right)}{2}\\[4pt]&=37.5\,\mathrm J.\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The axes are perpendicular, so add the squares of the components. The squared speed is twenty-five, and the sign of the first component does not affect the energy.

The speed is five metres per second. Adding the two components directly would incorrectly treat them as lying on the same axis.

The same result follows from adding $24\,\mathrm J$ from the horizontal component and $13.5\,\mathrm J$ from the vertical one. This equals using speed because on perpendicular axes $v^2=v_x^2+v_y^2$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A cyclist and bicycle have total mass $80\,\mathrm{kg}$ and travel at $9\,\mathrm{m/s}$. An observer moves in the same direction at $4\,\mathrm{m/s}$. Find the kinetic energy in the observer's frame.

$$
K=\frac12 m(v-u)^2,\qquad
\begin{aligned}K&=\frac{80 \cdot \left(9 - 4\right)^{2}}{2}\\[4pt]&=1000\,\mathrm J.\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The observer measures the difference of the ground-relative velocities because both move in the same direction. Use this difference, five metres per second, in the definition of kinetic energy.

In the road frame the speed is nine metres per second, so the value is different. Kinetic energy always refers to the specified frame.

Check by returning to the original frame, adding the observer's $4\,\mathrm{m/s}$ to the relative speed $5\,\mathrm{m/s}$. This recovers $9\,\mathrm{m/s}$. If the observer travelled alongside the cyclist at the same speed, the cyclist's energy in that frame would be zero.

</div>
