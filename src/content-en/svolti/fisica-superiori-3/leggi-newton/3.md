## Exercise 1

An $8\,\mathrm{kg}$ mass is in a lab where $g=9.8\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$. Find the magnitude of its weight.

$$
\begin{aligned}
P&=mg=8\cdot9.8\\[4pt]
&\simeq 78.4\,\mathrm{N}
\end{aligned}
$$

::grafico{f="9.8*x" punti="8,78.4,pieno,A" xmin=0 xmax=10.8 ymin=0 ymax=108 nomi="m (kg),P (N)" titolo="Point A links 8 kg to a weight of 78.4 N. The line passes through the origin because g is constant."}

<div class="spiegazione">

Mass measures inertia while weight measures gravitational interaction. Kilograms times metres per second squared gives newtons. Since $g$ is close to ten, the result is expected to be near eighty newtons, with the computed value slightly below it.

The graph shows how weight changes for different masses at the same location. The point coordinates are mass and its weight. The slope is measured in newtons per kilogram, equivalent to metres per second squared.

</div>

## Exercise 2

In a laboratory on another celestial body, a horizontal resultant of $18\,\mathrm N$ gives a sample an acceleration of $3\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$. When the sample hangs at rest, a spring scale reads $9.6\,\mathrm N$. Find the mass and the local acceleration due to gravity.

$$
\begin{aligned}
m&=\frac{18}{3}\,\mathrm{kg}=6\,\mathrm{kg},\\
g&=\frac{P}{m}=\frac{9.6}{6}\,\mathrm{m/s^2}=1.6\,\mathrm{m/s^2}.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The first measurement determines inertial mass from the force-to-acceleration ratio. In the second measurement, rest means the scale’s tension balances weight. Dividing that reading by the mass already found gives the local field. Dividing newtons by kilograms gives metres per second squared.

The local value is about a sixth of Earth’s. A spring scale on that body therefore gives roughly one sixth of the Earth reading for the same sample, while the first experiment would give the same mass.

</div>

## Exercise 3

An object weighs $60\,\mathrm N$ where $g=10\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$. What does it weigh where $g=2\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$?

$$
\begin{aligned}
P_2&=P_1\frac{g_2}{g_1}=60\frac2{10}\\[4pt]
&= 12\,\mathrm{N}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The object still has six kilograms of mass. Weight at the second location is that same mass times two metres per second squared. The second field is one fifth of the first, so weight changes by the same factor.

Compare the second field directly to the first: the ratio is $2/10$, which is also the ratio of the weights. The reading in newtons depends on location. Dividing each weight by its local field gives the same mass.

</div>
