## Exercise 1

An $8\,\mathrm{kg}$ mass is in a lab where $g=9.8\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$. Find the magnitude of its weight.

$$
\begin{aligned}
P&=mg=8\cdot9.8\\[4pt]
&\simeq 78.4\,\mathrm{N}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Mass measures inertia while weight measures gravitational interaction. Kilograms times metres per second squared gives newtons. Since $g$ is close to ten, the result is expected to be near eighty newtons, with the computed value slightly below it.

</div>

## Exercise 2

An object's weight is $120\,\mathrm N$ where $g=8\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$. What is its mass?

$$
\begin{aligned}
m&=\frac Pg=\frac{120}{8}\\[4pt]
&= 15\,\mathrm{kg}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Each kilogram weighs eight newtons in that location. Divide the full weight by that weight per kilogram. Taking the same mass to a different value of gravity would change its weight while leaving its inertia unchanged.

</div>

## Exercise 3

An object weighs $60\,\mathrm N$ where $g=10\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$. What does it weigh where $g=2\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$?

$$
\begin{aligned}
P_2&=P_1\frac{g_2}{g_1}=60\frac2{10}\\[4pt]
&= 12\,\mathrm{N}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The object still has six kilograms of mass. Weight at the second location is that same mass times two metres per second squared. The second field is one fifth of the first, so weight changes by the same factor.

</div>
