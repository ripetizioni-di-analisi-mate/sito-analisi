## Exercise 1

A force of $20\,\mathrm N$ acts to the right and one of $8\,\mathrm N$ to the left on a $6\,\mathrm{kg}$ body. What is its rightward acceleration?

$$
\begin{aligned}
a&=\frac{20-8}{6}\\[4pt]
&= 2\,\mathrm{m/s^2}
\end{aligned}
$$

::grafico{assi=0 uguale=1 vettori="0,0,5,0,F_1;0,0,-2,0,F_2" xmin=-3 xmax=6 ymin=-1 ymax=1 nomi="x,y" titolo="Arrow lengths 5 and 2 match the ratio 20 to 8"}

<div class="spiegazione">

The two forces lie along one line but point oppositely. With right positive, their signed sum is twelve newtons. Normal force and weight, if balanced vertically, do not enter the horizontal sum. Dividing twelve by six gives two metres per second squared.

One diagram unit of arrow length represents $4\,\mathrm N$. The signed sum leaves three units to the right. If the body were moving left, this same acceleration would make it slow down at that instant.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A $4\,\mathrm{kg}$ block rests on a fixed slope of $30^\circ$. A string along the slope can pull uphill with adjustable tension $T$. Use $g=10\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$ and $\mu_{\mathrm s}=\dfrac1{2\sqrt3}$. For what range of tensions can the block stay at rest, and how does the direction of friction change?

$$
\begin{aligned}
N&=mg\cos30^\circ=20\sqrt3\,\mathrm N, \qquad \mu_{\mathrm s}N=10\,\mathrm N,\\
T+F_{\mathrm s}-mg\sin30^\circ&=0,\qquad F_{\mathrm s}=20\,\mathrm N-T,\\
|20\,\mathrm N-T|&\leq10\,\mathrm N,\qquad 10\,\mathrm N\leq T\leq30\,\mathrm N.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Take uphill as positive. Tension is parallel to the slope, so it leaves the normal force unchanged. At rest, tension and friction together have to balance the downhill component of weight. The required friction magnitude must stay within the static limit.

For $T<20\,\mathrm N$ friction points uphill. At $20\,\mathrm N$ it is zero. For $T>20\,\mathrm N$ up to the upper limit, it points downhill. Both endpoints require the same magnitude of $10\,\mathrm N$, with opposite signs. Outside the range the block moves in the direction of the resultant that static friction cannot balance.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A $5\,\mathrm{kg}$ crate slides right at $3\,\mathrm{m/s}$ on a horizontal surface. The push is removed, leaving friction as the only horizontal force. Use $\mu_{\mathrm d}=0.20$ and $g=10\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$. Find the time and distance to stop, and describe the motion afterwards.

$$
\begin{aligned}
a_x&=-\mu_{\mathrm d}g=-2\,\mathrm{m/s^2},\\
t_{\mathrm s}&=\frac{3}{2}\,\mathrm s=1.5\,\mathrm s,\qquad
\Delta x=\frac{3+0}{2}\cdot1.5\,\mathrm m=2.25\,\mathrm m.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

While the crate slides right, kinetic friction points left. The normal force is $mg$, so dividing the horizontal force by the mass gives $-\mu_{\mathrm d}g$. While acceleration is constant, average velocity is the mean of its initial and final values. Multiplying by the stopping time gives the distance travelled.

The law for sliding friction applies up to rest. Once stopped, there is no horizontal force tending to restart the crate. It stays at rest with zero static friction. Extending the velocity line beyond that point would predict leftward sliding with no force to start it.

</div>
