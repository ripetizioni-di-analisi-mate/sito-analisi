## Exercise 1

In an ideal Atwood machine, $2$ and $3\,\mathrm{kg}$ masses hang on either side. At $g=10\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$, what is the acceleration magnitude?

::grafico{assi=0 curve="cos(t),2+sin(t),0,3.141592654" segmenti="-1,2,-1,0;1,2,1,-0.5" punti="-1,0,pieno;1,-0.5,pieno" vettori="-1,0,-1,0.7,T;1,-0.5,1,0.2,T" etichette="-1.35,-0.25,2;1.35,-0.7,3" xmin=-2 xmax=2 ymin=-1 ymax=3.4 titolo="The two branches exert equal tensions on the masses"}

$$
\begin{aligned}
a&=g\frac{m_2-m_1}{m_1+m_2}=10\frac{3-2}{3+2}\\[4pt]
&= 2\,\mathrm{m/s^2}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The three-kilogram mass falls and the two-kilogram mass rises. Taking each direction of motion as positive, the weight difference provides ten newtons to accelerate both. Dividing by five kilograms gives two. This is below $g$, as expected when one mass resists the other's fall.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Masses of $2$ and $3\,\mathrm{kg}$ hang on an ideal pulley at $g=10\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$. What is the tension?

$$
\begin{aligned}
a&=10\frac{3-2}{3+2}=2\,\mathrm{m/s^2}\\[4pt]T&=2(10+2)\\[4pt]
&= 24\,\mathrm{N}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The lighter mass rises, so tension must exceed its twenty-newton weight. Acceleration of two on two kilograms requires four more newtons. The descending mass equation also gives $30-3\cdot2=24\,\mathrm N$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

One side of an ideal Atwood machine holds $2\,\mathrm{kg}$. The other descends at $2\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$ with $g=10\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$. What is the descending mass?

$$
\begin{aligned}
m_2&=m_1\frac{g+a}{g-a}=2\frac{10+2}{10-2}\\[4pt]
&= 3\,\mathrm{kg}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The lighter side rises, so tension is $2(10+2)$. It is the same on the descending side, where $T=m_2(10-2)$. Equating them leaves one unknown. Three kilograms is more than the two on the rising side, as required by the observed direction.

</div>
