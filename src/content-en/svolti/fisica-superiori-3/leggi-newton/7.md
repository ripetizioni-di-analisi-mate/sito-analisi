## Exercise 1

In an ideal Atwood machine, $2$ and $3\,\mathrm{kg}$ masses hang on either side, initially at rest. At $g=10\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$, what is the acceleration magnitude?

$$
\begin{aligned}
a&=g\frac{m_2-m_1}{m_1+m_2}=10\frac{3-2}{3+2}\\[4pt]
&= 2\,\mathrm{m/s^2}
\end{aligned}
$$

::grafico{assi=0 uguale=1 curve="cos(t),2+sin(t),0,3.141592654" segmenti="-1,2,-1,0;1,2,1,-0.5" punti="-1,0,pieno;1,-0.5,pieno" vettori="-1,0,-1,0.7,T;1,-0.5,1,0.2,T;-1,0,-1,-0.583333,P₁;1,-0.5,1,-1.375,P₂" etichette="-1.72,-0.25,2 kg;1.23,-0.7,3 kg" xmin=-2.2 xmax=2.2 ymin=-1.75 ymax=3.4 titolo="The two branches exert equal tensions on the masses"}

<div class="spiegazione">

The three-kilogram mass falls and the two-kilogram mass rises. Taking each direction of motion as positive, the weight difference provides ten newtons to accelerate both. Dividing by five kilograms gives two. This is below $g$, as expected when one mass resists the other's fall.

If both diagrams used upwards as positive, their signed accelerations would have opposite signs. The formula already uses one shared magnitude. A downward displacement on one branch lengthens that branch and shortens the other by the same distance.

</div>

## Exercise 2

An ideal Atwood machine carries masses of $2$ and $3\,\mathrm{kg}$. Initially the heavier mass is rising at $1.2\,\mathrm{m/s}$. Use $g=10\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$. How long until it stops before reversing, and what is the tension while it rises? The string stays taut.

$$
\begin{aligned}
a&=\frac{(3-2)\cdot10}{3+2}\,\mathrm{m/s^2}=2\,\mathrm{m/s^2},\\
t_{\mathrm s}&=\frac{0-(-1.2)}{2}\,\mathrm s=0.60\,\mathrm s,\\
T&=3(10-2)\,\mathrm N=24\,\mathrm N.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Take downwards as positive for the heavier mass. The difference in weights gives it positive acceleration, while its initial velocity is negative. It slows as it rises until the velocity reaches zero. The acceleration stays positive at that instant, and the mass starts descending.

Find tension from the second law for the heavier mass, with weight downwards and tension upwards. Check using the lighter mass, where $T=2(10+2)\,\mathrm N=24\,\mathrm N$. Reversing velocity does not change the forces.

</div>

## Exercise 3

One side of an ideal Atwood machine holds $2\,\mathrm{kg}$. The other descends at $2\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$ with $g=10\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$. What is the descending mass?

$$
\begin{aligned}
m_2&=m_1\frac{g+a}{g-a}=2\frac{10+2}{10-2}\\[4pt]
&= 3\,\mathrm{kg}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The lighter side rises, so tension is $2(10+2)$. It is the same on the descending side, where $T=m_2(10-2)$. Equating them leaves one unknown. Three kilograms is more than the two on the rising side, as required by the observed direction.

After finding the mass, substitute both masses into the expression for $a$ to check the observation. The denominator $g-a$ is positive. If the measured acceleration reached $g$, no finite positive mass on the descending side would satisfy these assumptions.

</div>
