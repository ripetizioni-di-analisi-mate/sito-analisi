## Exercise 1

An ideal vertical spring of $100\,\mathrm{N/m}$ holds a stationary $0.40\,\mathrm{kg}$ mass. With $g=10\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$, what is its extension?

::grafico{f="100*x" punti="0.04,4,pieno" xmin=0 xmax=0.065 ymin=0 ymax=7 nomi="x,F" titolo="Equilibrium is the point where restoring force measures 4 N"}

$$
\begin{aligned}
x&=\frac{mg}{k}=\frac{0.40\cdot10}{100}\\[4pt]
&\simeq 0.04\,\mathrm{m}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The mass is in equilibrium, so the upward restoring force equals its four-newton weight. The constant is one hundred newtons per metre of extension. Their ratio is four hundredths of a metre, or four centimetres. The final unit is length because force is divided by force per metre.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A vertical $250\,\mathrm{N/m}$ spring extends by $6\,\mathrm{cm}$ with a stationary mass. At $g=10\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$, what is the mass?

$$
\begin{aligned}
m&=\frac{kx}g=\frac{250\cdot0.06}{10}\\[4pt]
&\simeq 1.5\,\mathrm{kg}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

First find the fifteen-newton elastic force. In equilibrium its magnitude equals weight, so divide by ten newtons per kilogram. Swapping grams for kilograms or centimetres for metres would distort the relation between the two quantities.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A vertical $200\,\mathrm{N/m}$ spring already stretched by a mass receives another $0.30\,\mathrm{kg}$. Between equilibria, how much does extension grow at $g=10\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$?

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta x&=\frac{\Delta m\,g}{k}=\frac{0.30\cdot10}{200}\\[4pt]
&\simeq 0.015\,\mathrm{m}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The weight of the original mass occurs in both equilibria. Subtracting the two equations cancels it, leaving just three newtons of added weight. Extension grows by one and a half centimetres. This applies if the new state stays within the elastic range.

</div>
