## Exercise 1

A single convex spherical interface has radius $0.20\,\mathrm{mm}$ and uniform surface tension $0.025\,\mathrm{N/m}$. Neglect gravity and dynamic stresses. Calculate internal minus external pressure and state what would change for a flat interface.

$$
r=2.0\cdot10^{-4}\,\mathrm m,\qquad
\Delta p=\dfrac{2\gamma}{r}
=\dfrac{2\cdot0.025}{2.0\cdot10^{-4}}\,\mathrm{Pa}=250\,\mathrm{Pa}.
$$
$$
R_1,R_2\to\infty:\qquad \Delta p=0.
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The factor of two comes from the two principal curvatures of the **same** spherical surface, not two interfaces. Internal pressure is higher to balance the interface's tendency to reduce area. Units are $\dfrac{\mathrm{N\,m^{-1}}}{\mathrm m}=\mathrm{Pa}$.

In the flat limit curvature vanishes, so the local capillary jump vanishes. This does not eliminate fluid pressure or a possible hydrostatic gradient. Atmospheric pressure is not added because the requested quantity is a difference between inside and outside.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Two single spherical interfaces have radii $r$ and $\dfrac{r}{2}$. The first requires $300\,\mathrm{Pa}$ excess pressure. Calculate the second when surface tensions are equal; then determine the tension ratio that would make the two excess pressures equal.

$$
\dfrac{\Delta p_2}{\Delta p_1}
=\dfrac{\gamma_2}{\gamma_1}\dfrac{r_1}{r_2}.
$$
$$
\gamma_2=\gamma_1:\quad \Delta p_2=2\cdot300=600\,\mathrm{Pa}.
$$
$$
\Delta p_2=\Delta p_1:\quad
\dfrac{\gamma_2}{\gamma_1}=\dfrac{r_2}{r_1}=\dfrac12.
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Before inserting numbers, divide the two Laplace equations: the shared geometric factor cancels. At equal tension the smaller sphere requires greater excess pressure. This compares specified equilibria rather than fully predicting the dynamics of connected bubbles.

If the smaller surface's tension halves, the radius effect is compensated. The second calculation explains why the constant-tension comparison cannot automatically be applied to surfactant-coated surfaces whose tension may depend on area.

</div>

## Exercise 3

With $\gamma=0.030\,\mathrm{N/m}$ and radius $1.0\,\mathrm{mm}$, compare three static models: a spherical drop with one interface, a soap bubble with a thin film and two interfaces of nearly equal radius, and a single very long cylindrical interface. Neglect gravity and end effects.

![One or two curvatures are not the same as one or two interfaces.](/figure/fisica-semestre-filtro/fluidi/laplace-en.svg)

$$
\Delta p_{\mathrm{sph}}=\dfrac{2\gamma}{r}=60\,\mathrm{Pa},\qquad
\Delta p_{\mathrm{soap}}\simeq\dfrac{4\gamma}{r}=120\,\mathrm{Pa},\qquad
\Delta p_{\mathrm{cyl}}=\dfrac{\gamma}{r}=30\,\mathrm{Pa}.
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The sphere has two non-zero curvatures; the cylinder has only one because the curvature radius along its axis is infinite. A soap bubble instead adds pressure jumps across two spherical surfaces. The difference compared is between internal and external gas.

At equal tension and radius, the order is therefore cylinder, sphere, soap bubble. A gas bubble immersed in liquid would have one interface and follow the spherical case, not the soap-film case. The cylindrical formula concerns the lateral surface far from the ends.

</div>
