## Exercise 1

A U-tube open at both ends contains water of density $1000\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}$. Immiscible oil of density $850\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}$ is poured into one arm, forming a column $12.0\,\mathrm{cm}$ high. At equilibrium, neglecting capillarity, find the water height in the other arm above the interface level and the difference between the free-surface heights.

![An oil column 12.0 cm high in equilibrium with 10.2 cm of water.](/figure/fisica-semestre-filtro/fluidi/manometer-en.svg)

$$
p_A=p_B,\qquad p_A=p_0+\rho_o gh_o,\qquad p_B=p_0+\rho_a gh_a.
$$
$$
h_a=\dfrac{\rho_o}{\rho_a}h_o
=\dfrac{850}{1000}\cdot12.0\,\mathrm{cm}=10.2\,\mathrm{cm}.
$$
$$
\Delta=h_o-h_a=12.0\,\mathrm{cm}-10.2\,\mathrm{cm}=1.8\,\mathrm{cm}.
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Compare two points at the interface height, both in the connected water: one immediately below the oil and the other in the opposite arm. Do not compare pressures at a height where one arm contains oil and the other water. The same $p_0$ acts at both free surfaces.

Equating pressures cancels $p_0$ and $g$. Density times height must be the same in the two arms; the less dense liquid requires a taller column. The oil surface is therefore **higher** than the water surface. The result does not require knowing how far the levels moved from their initial positions.

</div>

## Exercise 2

In a barometer, the mercury column, with density $13.6\cdot10^3\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}$, stands $755\,\mathrm{mm}$ above the reservoir. Use $g=9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$ and neglect pressure above the column. Calculate atmospheric pressure and the height of an ideal water barometer with density $1000\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}$ at the same pressure, also neglecting vapour pressure in this case.

$$
p_0=\rho_{\mathrm{Hg}}gh
=13.6\cdot10^3\cdot9.81\cdot0.755\,\mathrm{Pa}
=100729.08\,\mathrm{Pa}\simeq1.01\cdot10^5\,\mathrm{Pa}.
$$
$$
h_a=\dfrac{p_0}{\rho_a g}
=\dfrac{\rho_{\mathrm{Hg}}}{\rho_a}h
=13.6\cdot0.755\,\mathrm m=10.268\,\mathrm m\simeq10.3\,\mathrm m.
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

At the reservoir surface height, mercury inside the tube is at atmospheric pressure. Moving up to the column top lowers pressure by $\rho_{\mathrm{Hg}}gh$ to the assumed negligible value. The vertical difference must be converted into metres.

For the water column, intermediate rounding can be avoided: equating density times height eliminates $g$ and pressure. Less dense water requires a much taller column. A real water barometer would also require vapour pressure and experimental difficulties to be considered; this comparison isolates the density effect.

</div>
