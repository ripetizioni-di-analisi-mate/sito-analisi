## Exercise 1

An ideal hydraulic lift has piston diameters $d_1=2.0\,\mathrm{cm}$ and $d_2=30\,\mathrm{cm}$. The large piston supports a car of mass $1200\,\mathrm{kg}$. Neglect friction, piston masses and hydrostatic differences between them; external atmospheric pressure is common. With $g=9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$ find the force on the small piston, the equivalent total stroke to raise the car by $10\,\mathrm{cm}$ and the work. The volume may be supplied through many pump strokes.

$$
\dfrac{A_2}{A_1}=\left(\dfrac{30}{2.0}\right)^2=225,
\qquad F_1=\dfrac{mg}{225}=\dfrac{1200\cdot9.81}{225}\,\mathrm N
\simeq52\,\mathrm N.
$$
$$
A_1s_1=A_2s_2,\qquad s_1=225\cdot0.10\,\mathrm m=22.5\,\mathrm m.
$$
$$
W_1=W_2=mgs_2=1200\cdot9.81\cdot0.10\,\mathrm J
=1177.2\,\mathrm J\simeq1.2\cdot10^3\,\mathrm J.
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Pascal's principle equates pressure increments, so the force ratio equals the **area** ratio, not the diameter ratio. The small piston requires much less force than the car's weight.

Volume conservation nevertheless requires a much longer total stroke. The value $22.5\,\mathrm m$ represents total volume divided by the small area, possibly accumulated through many pump strokes; it need not describe a single cylinder of that length. Equal works require the stated idealisations, including negligible hydrostatic contributions. Using the unrounded force verifies $F_1s_1=mgs_2$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

An ideal hydraulic pump has piston area $4.0\,\mathrm{cm^2}$ and useful stroke $5.0\,\mathrm{cm}$; the lifting piston has area $200\,\mathrm{cm^2}$. It must provide a rise of $15\,\mathrm{cm}$ for a load of mass $800\,\mathrm{kg}$. Ideal valves prevent backflow. Neglect friction, piston weight and hydrostatic differences; assume an incompressible fluid and $g=9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$. Determine force, number of complete pump strokes and total work.

$$
F_1=\dfrac{A_1}{A_2}mg
=\dfrac{4.0}{200}\cdot800\cdot9.81\,\mathrm N
=156.96\,\mathrm N\simeq1.6\cdot10^2\,\mathrm N.
$$
$$
V_1=A_1s=4.0\cdot5.0\,\mathrm{cm^3}=20\,\mathrm{cm^3},
\quad V_2=A_2h=200\cdot15\,\mathrm{cm^3}=3000\,\mathrm{cm^3}.
$$
$$
N=\dfrac{V_2}{V_1}=150,\qquad
W=NF_1s=mgh=800\cdot9.81\cdot0.15\,\mathrm J
=1177.2\,\mathrm J\simeq1.2\cdot10^3\,\mathrm J.
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Areas may remain in square centimetres in the force ratio because units cancel. The volume ratio may likewise be calculated in cubic centimetres; work instead requires metres when force is in newtons.

Each stroke transfers a definite volume, and the valves retain it in the lifting cylinder during the pump's return. The required number is already an integer; if it were not, and only complete strokes were allowed, it would have to be rounded upwards. Work equals the load's potential-energy gain, whereas a dissipative device would require more input work.

</div>
