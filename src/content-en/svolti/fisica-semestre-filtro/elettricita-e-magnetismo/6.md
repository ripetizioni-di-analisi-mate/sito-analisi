## Exercise 1

A purely resistive heater draws $P = 1.2\,\mathrm{kW}$ from a constant DC voltage $\Delta V = 230\,\mathrm{V}$. Calculate current, its resistance at operating temperature, and the energy consumed in $15$ minutes, in kilowatt-hours and joules.

$$
I = \dfrac{P}{\Delta V} ,
\qquad
R = \dfrac{\Delta V^2}{P} ,
\qquad
W = P\, \Delta t
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
I &= \dfrac{1200}{230}\,\mathrm{A} \approx 5.2\,\mathrm{A} ,
\qquad
R = \dfrac{230^2}{1200}\,\Omega \approx 44\,\Omega \\[6pt]
W &= 1.2\,\mathrm{kW} \cdot 0.25\,\mathrm{h} = 0.30\,\mathrm{kWh} = 0.30 \cdot 3.6 \cdot 10^{6}\,\mathrm{J} \approx 1.1 \cdot 10^{6}\,\mathrm{J}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Absorbed power is voltage times current, so current is $\dfrac{P}{\Delta V}$. The model specifies a purely resistive load, and **Joule heating** gives $P=\dfrac{(\Delta V)^2}{R}$. Solve for resistance; Ohm’s law, $R=\dfrac{\Delta V}{I}$, gives the same value. Since power is constant, energy consumed, or work $W$ done by the electric field on charges, is power times time. Expressing $15\,\mathrm{min}=0.25\,\mathrm h$ directly gives kilowatt-hours, with $1\,\mathrm{kWh}=3.6\cdot10^6\,\mathrm J$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Two heating elements are rated $60\,\mathrm{W}$ and $100\,\mathrm{W}$ at $230\,\mathrm{V}$. They are connected in series across $230\,\mathrm{V}$. Assuming their resistances remain unchanged, calculate each element’s dissipated power and identify which converts electrical energy to heat faster.

$$
R_k = \dfrac{\Delta V_n^2}{P_{n,k}} ,
\qquad
I = \dfrac{\Delta V}{R_1 + R_2} ,
\qquad
P_k = R_k I^2
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
R_1 &= \dfrac{230^2}{60}\,\Omega \approx 882\,\Omega ,
\qquad
R_2 = \dfrac{230^2}{100}\,\Omega = 529\,\Omega \\[6pt]
I &= \dfrac{230}{882 + 529}\,\mathrm{A} \approx 0.163\,\mathrm{A} \\[6pt]
P_1 &= 882 \cdot 0.163^2\,\mathrm{W} \approx 23\,\mathrm{W} ,
\qquad
P_2 = 529 \cdot 0.163^2\,\mathrm{W} \approx 14\,\mathrm{W}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Rated powers refer to each element connected alone across $230\,\mathrm V$. With specified voltage, **Joule power** is $P=\dfrac{(\Delta V)^2}{R}$, so the lower-rated element has higher resistance. In series, the two elements share a current determined by $R_1+R_2$.

At common current, power is $RI^2$, so the higher resistance dissipates more: the nominal $60\,\mathrm W$ element absorbs about $23\,\mathrm W$ and produces heat faster than the nominal $100\,\mathrm W$ element, which absorbs about $14\,\mathrm W$. Rated power is not actual power in this new connection. The total, $37.5\,\mathrm W$, equals $\Delta V I$. Real resistance can change with temperature, so actual powers may differ from the constant-resistance model. Electrical power alone would not generally determine the brightness of lamps, which also depends on thermal and spectral response.

</div>
