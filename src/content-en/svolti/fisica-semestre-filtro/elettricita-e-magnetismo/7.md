## Exercise 1

A parallel-plate capacitor has square plates of side $\ell = 20\,\mathrm{cm}$ separated by $d = 1.0\,\mathrm{mm}$ in vacuum. A source maintains $\Delta V = 50\,\mathrm{V}$. Calculate capacitance, plate charges, surface charge density and the electric field between plates. Use $\varepsilon_0=8.85\cdot10^{-12}\,\mathrm{F/m}$ and neglect fringing.

$$
C = \dfrac{\varepsilon_0 A}{d} , \qquad A = \ell^2 , \qquad Q = C\,\Delta V , \qquad \sigma = \dfrac{Q}{A} , \qquad E = \dfrac{\Delta V}{d}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
C &= \dfrac{8.85 \cdot 10^{-12} \cdot 0.040}{1.0 \cdot 10^{-3}}\,\mathrm{F} \approx 3.5 \cdot 10^{-10}\,\mathrm{F} = 350\,\mathrm{pF} \\[6pt]
Q &= 3.54 \cdot 10^{-10} \cdot 50\,\mathrm{C} \approx 1.8 \cdot 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{C} = 18\,\mathrm{nC} \\[6pt]
\sigma &= \dfrac{1.77 \cdot 10^{-8}}{0.040}\,\mathrm{C/m^2} \approx 4.4 \cdot 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{C/m^2} \\[6pt]
E &= \dfrac{50}{1.0 \cdot 10^{-3}}\,\mathrm{V/m} = 5.0 \cdot 10^{4}\,\mathrm{V/m}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Plate separation is much smaller than side length, so fringing is neglected and the **parallel-plate capacitor formula** applies with $A=0.20^2\,\mathrm{m^2}=0.040\,\mathrm{m^2}$. The source fixes voltage, so charge follows from capacitance, $Q=C\Delta V$. Surface density is charge divided by area, assuming uniform charge on the facing surfaces.

The field between plates is uniform, giving voltage divided by separation. As a check, the ideal-plate field gives $E=\dfrac{\sigma}{\varepsilon_0}=\dfrac{4.425\cdot10^{-7}}{8.85\cdot10^{-12}}\,\mathrm{V/m}=5.0\cdot10^4\,\mathrm{V/m}$, the same value. Retain guard digits in intermediate steps and round only final results.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A vacuum parallel-plate capacitor is to have $C = 1.0\,\mu\mathrm{F}$ with plate separation $d = 0.10\,\mathrm{mm}$. Find the plate area and the side length for square plates. Also find charge and field when it is connected to $12\,\mathrm{V}$. Use $\varepsilon_0=8.85\cdot10^{-12}\,\mathrm{F/m}$ and the ideal model without fringing.

$$
C = \dfrac{\varepsilon_0 A}{d}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
A = \dfrac{C\,d}{\varepsilon_0} , \qquad \ell = \sqrt{A}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
A &= \dfrac{1.0 \cdot 10^{-6} \cdot 1.0 \cdot 10^{-4}}{8.85 \cdot 10^{-12}}\,\mathrm{m^2} \approx 11\,\mathrm{m^2} \\[6pt]
\ell &= \sqrt{11.3}\,\mathrm{m} \approx 3.4\,\mathrm{m} \\[6pt]
Q &= C\,\Delta V = 1.0 \cdot 10^{-6} \cdot 12\,\mathrm{C} = 12\,\mu\mathrm{C} \\[6pt]
E &= \dfrac{\Delta V}{d} = \dfrac{12}{1.0 \cdot 10^{-4}}\,\mathrm{V/m} = 1.2 \cdot 10^{5}\,\mathrm{V/m}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Rearrange the **parallel-plate capacitor formula** for area because capacitance and separation are given. About $11\,\mathrm{m^2}$ is required: two square sheets over three metres wide separated by one tenth of a millimetre. This shows that a microfarad is large for a vacuum capacitor; practical capacitors can use thin rolled strips separated by a dielectric, which multiplies capacitance by $\varepsilon_r$.

Charge and field follow from the capacitance definition and field uniformity. Separation is very small relative to side length, consistent with the planar approximation away from edges. This idealization cannot guarantee a real device’s insulation: roughness, edge geometry and medium properties affect local field and possible discharge. A single average field value cannot establish a guarantee of operation.

</div>
