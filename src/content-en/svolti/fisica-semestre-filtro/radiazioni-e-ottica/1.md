## Exercise 1

Monochromatic light has vacuum wavelength $600\,\mathrm{nm}$ and enters a stationary transparent material with $n=1.50$. Calculate its frequency, speed and wavelength in the material, then compare photon energies. Use $c=3.00\cdot10^8\,\mathrm{m/s}$ and $h=6.63\cdot10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\,s}$.


$$
\nu=\dfrac{3.00\cdot10^8}{600\cdot10^{-9}}\,\mathrm{Hz}=5.00\cdot10^{14}\,\mathrm{Hz},
\qquad v=\dfrac c n=2.00\cdot10^8\,\mathrm{m/s}.
$$
$$
\lambda=\dfrac v\nu=400\,\mathrm{nm},\qquad
E_\gamma=h\nu\simeq3.32\cdot10^{-19}\,\mathrm J.
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Start with the vacuum wavelength, converting nanometres to metres. The ratio $\dfrac{c}{\lambda_0}$ gives the source frequency. At a stationary interface the oscillations retain the same frequency: the index change modifies speed and the spatial separation between crests, not their temporal rhythm. Therefore use $\dfrac{v}{\nu}$ for the new wavelength.

The energy of each transmitted photon remains $h\nu$. Using $\dfrac{hc}{400\,\mathrm{nm}}$ would confuse wavelength in the material with vacuum wavelength and create a fictitious $50\%$ increase. A direct check is that both $\lambda_0\nu$ and $n\lambda\nu$ must return $c$. Possible reflection at the interface reduces the transmitted photon count, without changing this individual energy balance.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A sinusoidal plane wave in vacuum has electric amplitude $E_0=120\,\mathrm{V/m}$. Find its magnetic amplitude and average intensity. How does intensity change if $E_0$ is halved? Use $c=3.00\cdot10^8\,\mathrm{m/s}$ and $\varepsilon_0=8.85\cdot10^{-12}\,\mathrm{F/m}$.


$$
B_0=\dfrac{E_0}{c}=4.00\cdot10^{-7}\,\mathrm T,
\qquad I=\dfrac12\varepsilon_0cE_0^2\simeq19.1\,\mathrm{W/m^2}.
$$
$$
\dfrac{I'}I=\left(\dfrac{E'_0}{E_0}\right)^2=\dfrac14,
\qquad I'\simeq4.78\,\mathrm{W/m^2}.
$$

![In-phase field components plotted separately.](/figure/fisica-semestre-filtro/radiazioni-e-ottica/em-wave-en.svg)

<div class="spiegazione">

The field-amplitude relation is linear, whereas intensity depends quadratically on amplitude. Use peak amplitude in the stated formula: the factor $\dfrac{1}{2}$ comes from time-averaging the square of the sinusoid. Substituting an rms amplitude already divided by $\sqrt2$ would count the same averaging twice.

The graph shows in-phase fields along perpendicular directions: one component reaches its maximum together with the other. Halving the electric field also halves the magnetic field, but intensity becomes one quarter. The result in teslas is numerically much smaller than the electric-field value because their ratio is $c$, with its units; comparing their bare numerical values does not compare energies.

</div>
