## Exercise 1

A sample initially contains $8000$ nuclei of a radionuclide with half-life $4.00\,\mathrm h$. No nuclei are added or removed and there are no precursors. Calculate the mean surviving number after $6.00\,\mathrm h$ and the probability that a nucleus initially present has decayed by that time.


$$
\dfrac t{T_{1/2}}=1.50,\qquad N(t)=8000\cdot2^{-1.50}\simeq2828,
$$
$$
P_{\mathrm{dec}}=1-2^{-1.50}\simeq0.6464.
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The elapsed time is one and a half half-lives: do not round it to one or two half-lives. Evaluate the fractional power directly. After four hours the mean would be $4000$, and after eight hours $2000$; the six-hour result must lie between them, but it is not their arithmetic mean $3000$.

Decay probability is the complement of survival, about $64.6\%$. Mean decays can also be obtained as $8000-N(t)$, approximately $5172$. These values are statistical expectations: an actual sample has an integer number of survivors and may differ from the mean. Using $\lambda_d=\dfrac{\ln2}{4.00\,\mathrm h}$ and $e^{-\lambda_dt}$ provides an alternative check with consistent time units.

</div>

## Exercise 2

One hundred independent nuclei have half-life $100\,\mathrm s$. What is the mean surviving population after $100\,\mathrm s$? For two nuclei selected initially, what are the probabilities that both survive and that at least one survives? Does a nucleus already surviving for $300\,\mathrm s$ have a different probability of surviving the next $100\,\mathrm s$?


$$
\langle N(100\,\mathrm s)\rangle=50,\qquad
P_{\mathrm{both}}=\left(\dfrac12\right)^2=\dfrac14,\qquad
P_{\ge1}=1-\left(\dfrac12\right)^2=\dfrac34.
$$
$$
P(\tau>400\,\mathrm s\mid\tau>300\,\mathrm s)
=\dfrac{2^{-4}}{2^{-3}}=\dfrac12.
$$

![Mean curve and two discrete realizations of independent decays.](/figure/fisica-semestre-filtro/radiazioni-e-ottica/decay-statistics-en.svg)

<div class="spiegazione">

Each nucleus has probability $\dfrac{1}{2}$ of surviving one half-life; adding one hundred such probabilities gives fifty expected survivors. Independence allows multiplication when both selected nuclei must survive. For “at least one”, use the complement of the event in which neither survives.

The last question already conditions on survival through three half-lives. A conditional probability is required, not the initial probability of reaching four half-lives, which would be $\dfrac{1}{16}$. The ratio is again $\dfrac{1}{2}$: the exponential model assigns no individual deadline. The discrete realizations in the graph illustrate why fifty is an expectation rather than a requirement for every sample.

</div>
