## Exercise 1

Follow the transformation of ${}^{238}_{92}\mathrm U$ through one alpha decay followed by two beta-minus decays. Write the three daughter nuclei, include the beta particles, and compare the final nucleus with the initial one. Use $\mathrm{Th}$ for $Z=90$ and $\mathrm{Pa}$ for $Z=91$.


$$
{}^{238}_{92}\mathrm U\to{}^{234}_{90}\mathrm{Th}+{}^4_2\mathrm{He},
$$
$$
{}^{234}_{90}\mathrm{Th}\to{}^{234}_{91}\mathrm{Pa}+e^-+\bar\nu_e,
\qquad
{}^{234}_{91}\mathrm{Pa}\to{}^{234}_{92}\mathrm U+e^-+\bar\nu_e.
$$

![Uranium–thorium–protactinium–uranium chain with emitted particles.](/figure/fisica-semestre-filtro/radiazioni-e-ottica/nuclear-decays-en.svg)

<div class="spiegazione">

The alpha particle removes two protons and two neutrons, so subtract four from $A$ and two from $Z$. In beta-minus decay, a nuclear neutron becomes a proton: total nucleon number stays fixed and $Z$ increases by one. The emitted electron and antineutrino are transformation products, not nucleons to subtract from $A$.

The final nucleus is uranium again because $Z$ returns to $92$, but it is a different isotope: $A$ is $234$ rather than $238$. The overall nuclear-charge check is $92=92+2-1-1$; the nucleon check is $238=234+4$. The equations track nuclide balances without attempting to detail every possible excited state or accompanying gamma emission.

</div>

## Exercise 2

An ideal $2.00\,\mathrm{MBq}$ source emits exactly one gamma photon per decay: in $80.0\%$ of cases its energy is $100\,\mathrm{keV}$ and in $20.0\%$ it is $200\,\mathrm{keV}$. Calculate mean photon energy and emitted gamma power. Use $1\,\mathrm{eV}=1.602\cdot10^{-19}\,\mathrm J$ and neglect other radiative channels. Does a gamma transition change $A$ or $Z$?


$$
\langle E_\gamma\rangle=0.80(100)+0.20(200)=120\,\mathrm{keV},
$$
$$
P_\gamma=\mathscr A\langle E_\gamma\rangle
=(2.00\cdot10^6)(120\cdot10^3)(1.602\cdot10^{-19})\,\mathrm W
\simeq3.84\cdot10^{-8}\,\mathrm W.
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The two energies are not added for every decay: they are alternatives with different probabilities. Use a weighted mean, which must lie between $100$ and $200\,\mathrm{keV}$ and closer to the more frequent value. Photon rate equals activity here only because exactly one photon per decay is assumed.

To obtain watts, convert kiloelectronvolts first to electronvolts and then to joules. The result is power emitted by the source, not power absorbed by a distant target. A gamma transition leaves $A$ and $Z$ unchanged while reducing nuclear internal energy. The value $120\,\mathrm{keV}$ is a beam average: neither specified channel emits a photon with that energy.

</div>
