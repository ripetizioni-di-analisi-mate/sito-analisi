## Exercise 1

Two independent filters have transmittances $\mathcal T_1=0.600$ and $\mathcal T_2=0.250$ at the same wavelength. A collimated $80.0\,\mathrm{mW}$ beam passes through both. Determine final power and total absorbance. Does filter order change the result in a model without multiple reflections or added radiation?


$$
\mathcal T_{12}=\mathcal T_1\mathcal T_2=0.150,\qquad
P_{12}=80.0\cdot0.150\,\mathrm{mW}=12.0\,\mathrm{mW}.
$$
$$
\mathcal A_{12}=-\log_{10}(0.150)
=-\log_{10}(0.600)-\log_{10}(0.250)\simeq0.824.
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The first filter transmits $48.0\,\mathrm{mW}$; the second passes one quarter of it, leaving $12.0\,\mathrm{mW}$. Do not add transmitted percentages because the second filter acts on an already reduced power. Reversing their order gives $20.0\,\mathrm{mW}$ first and then $60\%$ of that power: the result is identical.

The product is commutative in the stated model. In a real apparatus, interactions between filters, unmodelled spectral changes or collection of scattered radiation may require a broader model. Absorbances add because the logarithm converts the product into a sum. As a check, total transmittance must be smaller than either individual transmittance for these passive filters.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A beam has two components: $80\%$ of its power has coefficient $\mu_s=3.00\,\mathrm{cm^{-1}}$ and $20\%$ has $\mu_h=0.500\,\mathrm{cm^{-1}}$. Calculate total transmittances after $1.00$ and $2.00\,\mathrm{cm}$. Does the second centimetre reduce power in the same proportion as the first? What fraction of transmitted power belongs to the less attenuated component after the first centimetre?


$$
\mathcal T(x)=0.80e^{-\mu_sx}+0.20e^{-\mu_hx},
\quad \mathcal T(1\,\mathrm{cm})\simeq0.161,
\quad \mathcal T(2\,\mathrm{cm})\simeq0.0756.
$$
$$
\dfrac{\mathcal T(2\,\mathrm{cm})}{\mathcal T(1\,\mathrm{cm})}\simeq0.469,
\qquad
\dfrac{0.20e^{-0.500}}{\mathcal T(1\,\mathrm{cm})}\simeq0.753.
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Apply the exponential separately to each component and then add powers, rather than adding coefficients inside one exponential. After the first centimetre, about $16.1\%$ of initial power survives; the second centimetre transmits about $46.9\%$ of the power reaching it. These fractions are unequal.

The less attenuated component rises from an initial $20\%$ to about $75.3\%$ of transmitted power. The material selects the beam components, so the next layer encounters a different composition. Therefore $\mathcal T(2\,\mathrm{cm})$ differs from $\mathcal T(1\,\mathrm{cm})^2$. In X-ray beams this selection can correspond to beam hardening; here the coefficients are supplied for a two-component model, without assuming a universal energy–attenuation relation.

</div>
