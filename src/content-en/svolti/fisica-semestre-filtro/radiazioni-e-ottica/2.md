## Exercise 1

A monochromatic source emits $P=2.00\,\mathrm{mW}$ at $500\,\mathrm{nm}$ in vacuum for $10.0\,\mathrm s$. Calculate photon energy, emission rate and total photon number. Use $h=6.63\cdot10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\,s}$ and $c=3.00\cdot10^8\,\mathrm{m/s}$.


$$
E_\gamma=\dfrac{hc}{\lambda_0}=3.978\cdot10^{-19}\,\mathrm J
\simeq3.98\cdot10^{-19}\,\mathrm J.
$$
$$
\dot N_\gamma=\dfrac{2.00\cdot10^{-3}}{3.978\cdot10^{-19}}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}
\simeq5.03\cdot10^{15}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}},\qquad
N_\gamma\simeq5.03\cdot10^{16}.
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Power is energy per time: first convert milliwatts to watts, then divide the energy emitted each second by the energy of one photon. The intermediate result is a rate, not a count already covering ten seconds. Multiplying by duration gives the total count.

Keep extra intermediate digits in the energy to avoid premature rounding. As an independent check, total energy is $P\Delta t=0.0200\,\mathrm J$; dividing that energy directly by $E_\gamma$ must give the same count. The enormous count does not mean that individual photons carry large energies: it follows from their tiny energies in joules. An actual recorded count would also require detector efficiency, which is irrelevant to the emitted count here.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Two monochromatic beams have equal power: one at $400\,\mathrm{nm}$ and the other at $800\,\mathrm{nm}$ in vacuum. Compare photon energies and photon-number rates. For a target with ionization threshold $3.00\,\mathrm{eV}$, which beam can exceed the threshold in one absorption event? Use $hc\simeq1240\,\mathrm{eV\,nm}$.


$$
E_{400}=3.10\,\mathrm{eV},\qquad E_{800}=1.55\,\mathrm{eV},\qquad
\dfrac{\dot N_{400}}{\dot N_{800}}=\dfrac{E_{800}}{E_{400}}=\dfrac12.
$$
$$
\lambda_{0,\max}=\dfrac{1240}{3.00}\,\mathrm{nm}\simeq413\,\mathrm{nm}.
$$

![Photon-energy hyperbola and ionization threshold.](/figure/fisica-semestre-filtro/radiazioni-e-ottica/photon-energy-en.svg)

<div class="spiegazione">

The shorter-wavelength beam carries more energy per photon. At fixed power it must therefore emit fewer photons per second: the relation reverses because $\dot N=\dfrac P{E_\gamma}$. The curve shows both energies and the threshold intersection.

Only the $400\,\mathrm{nm}$ photon has enough energy in the single-photon model. This does not mean that every photon in that beam ionizes the target: a suitable interaction must occur, and absorption probability is unspecified. Increasing power at $800\,\mathrm{nm}$ increases the number of available events but does not turn each $1.55\,\mathrm{eV}$ photon into a $3.10\,\mathrm{eV}$ photon. Multiphoton processes are excluded here.

</div>
