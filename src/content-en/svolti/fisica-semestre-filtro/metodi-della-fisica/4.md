## Exercise 1

Two perpendicular forces act on a particle: $\vec{F}_1$ of magnitude $30\,\mathrm{N}$ along positive $x$ and $\vec{F}_2$ of magnitude $40\,\mathrm{N}$ along positive $y$. Find the resultant’s magnitude and direction.

$$
\vec{R} = \vec{F}_1 + \vec{F}_2 = (30\,\hat{\imath} + 40\,\hat{\jmath})\,\mathrm{N}
$$
$$
R = \sqrt{F_1^2 + F_2^2} = \sqrt{30^2 + 40^2}\,\mathrm{N} = \sqrt{2500}\,\mathrm{N} = 50\,\mathrm{N}
$$
$$
\theta = \arctan\dfrac{F_2}{F_1} = \arctan\dfrac{40}{30} \approx 53.1^\circ
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Forces applied to the same particle add as vectors. Because the given forces already lie on the axes, resultant components are directly $30\,\mathrm{N}$ and $40\,\mathrm{N}$. Their positive signs put the resultant in the first quadrant.

Perpendicular components form a right triangle, whose hypotenuse is the resultant magnitude. This gives fifty newtons, rather than the seventy obtained by adding magnitudes. The tangent of its angle to positive $x$ is $\dfrac{40}{30}$, giving approximately $53.1^\circ$.

As a check, the magnitude lies between the difference and sum of the individual magnitudes, ten and seventy newtons. Its direction lies between the two forces and closer to the larger vertical force. Those geometric checks can reveal a swapped sine/cosine or a reversed component.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Three horizontal cords pull a ring modelled as a particle: $\vec{F}_1$ of $50\,\mathrm{N}$ at $30^\circ$, $\vec{F}_2$ of $40\,\mathrm{N}$ at $135^\circ$, and $\vec{F}_3$ of $30\,\mathrm{N}$ at $270^\circ$, with angles anticlockwise from $x$. Find the resultant and the additional force $\vec{F}_{\text{eq}}$ needed for equilibrium.

$$
\vec{R} = \vec{F}_1 + \vec{F}_2 + \vec{F}_3,
\qquad
\vec{R} + \vec{F}_{\text{eq}} = \vec{0}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\vec{F}_{\text{eq}} = -\vec{R}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
R_x &= (50\cos 30^\circ + 40\cos 135^\circ + 30\cos 270^\circ)\,\mathrm{N} \\[6pt]
&= (43.30 - 28.28 + 0)\,\mathrm{N} = 15.02\,\mathrm{N}
\end{aligned}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
R_y &= (50\sin 30^\circ + 40\sin 135^\circ + 30\sin 270^\circ)\,\mathrm{N} \\[6pt]
&= (25.00 + 28.28 - 30.00)\,\mathrm{N} = 23.28\,\mathrm{N}
\end{aligned}
$$
$$
R = \sqrt{15.02^2 + 23.28^2}\,\mathrm{N} \approx 27.7\,\mathrm{N},
\qquad
\theta_R = \arctan\dfrac{23.28}{15.02} \approx 57.2^\circ
$$
$$
\vec{F}_{\text{eq}} = (-15.0\,\hat{\imath} - 23.3\,\hat{\jmath})\,\mathrm{N},
\qquad
F_{\text{eq}} = 27.7\,\mathrm{N},
\qquad
\theta_{\text{eq}} = 57.2^\circ + 180^\circ = 237.2^\circ
$$

![Three forces and resultant on common axes, in newtons.](/figure/fisica-semestre-filtro/metodi-della-fisica/forces-en.svg)

<div class="spiegazione">

Resolve every force using the same axes and angle convention. The force at $270^\circ$ contributes zero horizontally and minus thirty newtons vertically. The force at $135^\circ$ has negative horizontal and positive vertical components; changing both signs would put it in the wrong quadrant.

Add each axis separately before finding the resultant magnitude. Both resultant components are positive, giving a first-quadrant direction of about $57.2^\circ$ and magnitude $27.7\,\mathrm{N}$.

Equilibrium requires the sum including the additional force to vanish. The equilibrant is therefore the opposite resultant: equal magnitude, both components reversed and angle increased by $180^\circ$. Checking the final four-force sum component by component is more reliable than merely checking equal magnitudes.

</div>
