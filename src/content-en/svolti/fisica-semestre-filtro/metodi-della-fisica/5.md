## Exercise 1

A crate moves $8.00\,\mathrm{m}$ horizontally while a constant $60.0\,\mathrm{N}$ force pulls along a cord at $30^\circ$ above horizontal. Find this force’s work and the work it would do if horizontal.

$$
L = \vec{F} \cdot \vec{s} = F s \cos\theta
$$
$$
L = 60.0\,\mathrm{N} \cdot 8.00\,\mathrm{m} \cdot \cos 30^\circ = 480\,\mathrm{J} \cdot \dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2} \approx 416\,\mathrm{J}
$$
$$
L_{\text{horizontal}} = F s = 60.0\,\mathrm{N} \cdot 8.00\,\mathrm{m} = 480\,\mathrm{J}
$$

![Force at thirty degrees and horizontal displacement; arrow lengths are schematic.](/figure/fisica-semestre-filtro/metodi-della-fisica/work-en.svg)

<div class="spiegazione">

With a constant force, work is the dot product of force and displacement. Their angle is $30^\circ$ because displacement is horizontal. Only the force component parallel to displacement contributes: $F\cos30^\circ$ is approximately $52.0\,\mathrm{N}$.

The vertical component contributes zero work over this horizontal displacement, even though it is a real nonzero force component. Multiplying the horizontal component by distance gives about $416\,\mathrm{J}$, rounded to three significant figures under the usual rule for these data.

For the horizontal force, the angle is zero and work is $480\,\mathrm{J}$. This is the largest possible positive work for that force magnitude and displacement because cosine cannot exceed one. The question concerns the stated pulling force, not total work by every force on the crate.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A particle undergoes displacement $\vec{s}=(4.0\,\hat{\imath}+1.0\,\hat{\jmath}+2.0\,\hat{k})\,\mathrm{m}$ under constant force $\vec{F}=(3.0\,\hat{\imath}-2.0\,\hat{\jmath}+1.0\,\hat{k})\,\mathrm{N}$. Find the force’s work and the angle between force and displacement.

$$
L = \vec{F} \cdot \vec{s} = F_x s_x + F_y s_y + F_z s_z,
\qquad
\cos\theta = \dfrac{\vec{F} \cdot \vec{s}}{F s}
$$
$$
L = (3.0 \cdot 4.0 + (-2.0) \cdot 1.0 + 1.0 \cdot 2.0)\,\mathrm{J} = (12 - 2.0 + 2.0)\,\mathrm{J} = 12\,\mathrm{J}
$$
$$
F = \sqrt{9.0 + 4.0 + 1.0}\,\mathrm{N} = \sqrt{14}\,\mathrm{N} \approx 3.74\,\mathrm{N},
\qquad
s = \sqrt{16 + 1.0 + 4.0}\,\mathrm{m} = \sqrt{21}\,\mathrm{m} \approx 4.58\,\mathrm{m}
$$
$$
\cos\theta = \dfrac{12\,\mathrm{J}}{\sqrt{14}\,\mathrm{N} \cdot \sqrt{21}\,\mathrm{m}} = \dfrac{12}{\sqrt{294}} \approx 0.700,
\qquad
\theta \approx 45.6^\circ
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

With component data, sum products of corresponding components. The result has unit newton metre, written joule for work. The $y$ term is negative because force and displacement components there have opposite signs; the $z$ term is positive and cancels it numerically.

The remaining total is positive, so the angle between the complete vectors must be acute. Dividing the dot product by the product of magnitudes gives its cosine. The ratio is dimensionless because $\mathrm{J}=\mathrm{N\,m}$, and it lies between minus one and one as required.

Use unrounded square roots before applying the inverse cosine to obtain approximately $45.6^\circ$. A negative component of force does not by itself make total work negative: all corresponding products contribute to the scalar sum.

</div>
