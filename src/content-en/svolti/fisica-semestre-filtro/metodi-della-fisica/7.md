## Exercise 1

Two identical water samples each have mass $0.25\,\mathrm{kg}$, volume $0.25\,\mathrm{L}$ and temperature $20\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$. They are combined with no work, heat exchange or volume change. Find final mass, volume, temperature and density.

$$
m_f=0.50\,\mathrm{kg},\qquad V_f=0.50\,\mathrm{L}=5.0\cdot10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m^3}.
$$
$$
\rho_f=\dfrac{0.50\,\mathrm{kg}}{5.0\cdot10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m^3}}=1000\,\mathrm{kg/m^3},\qquad T_f=20\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}.
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Mass and volume add under the stated conditions. Temperature is not an amount of energy: adding twenty and twenty degrees would not describe the physical outcome. The portions are in the same state and combining them introduces no process changing temperature.

For density use total mass divided by total volume. Both double, so their ratio remains the original one. Converting litres to cubic metres is needed only to express the result in the coherent SI unit.

The data represent a rounded water model. If samples had different temperatures or underwent a transformation, the label “intensive” would not predict final temperature by itself: an energy balance would be needed. Here sample symmetry and process assumptions determine the result.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A homogeneous specimen has mass $80\,\mathrm{g}$ and volume $40\,\mathrm{cm^3}$. It is divided into four equal-volume parts without a state change. Find each part’s mass and density. Would a heterogeneous specimen with the same totals allow the same deduction?

$$
V_p=\dfrac{40\,\mathrm{cm^3}}4=10\,\mathrm{cm^3},\qquad m_p=\dfrac{80\,\mathrm{g}}4=20\,\mathrm{g}.
$$
$$
\rho_p=\dfrac{20\,\mathrm{g}}{10\,\mathrm{cm^3}}=2.0\,\mathrm{g/cm^3}=2000\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}.
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Homogeneity gives equal density in all parts. Therefore division into four equal volumes also divides mass into four equal shares. Density is not divided by four: it is a ratio of two quantities reduced in the same way.

As a check, adding the four masses and volumes reconstructs the original data. Adding the four densities, however, does not give the overall specimen’s density. The intensive property is compared at the same state, not added.

For a heterogeneous specimen, only overall average density would be known. Four equal-volume parts could contain different materials and have different masses. Information on the distribution of composition would be required: totals do not determine individual masses.

</div>
