## Exercise 1

A uniform steady beam transfers $0.060\,\mathrm J$ in $3.0\,\mathrm s$ through a perpendicular area of $4.0\,\mathrm{cm^2}$. Calculate power and intensity. How much energy crosses a $1.0\,\mathrm{cm^2}$ portion of the same section in $5.0\,\mathrm s$?

$$
P=\dfrac{0.060\,\mathrm J}{3.0\,\mathrm s}=0.020\,\mathrm W,\qquad
S=4.0\cdot10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m^2}.
$$
$$
I=50\,\mathrm{W/m^2},\qquad
E'=IS'\Delta t'=50(10^{-4})(5.0)\,\mathrm J=0.025\,\mathrm J.
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Divide energy by time to obtain power, then power by area to obtain intensity. These answer different questions: how much flows per second, and how much of that flow crosses each square metre. Area conversion uses $1\,\mathrm{cm^2}=10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m^2}$ because the conversion factor must also be squared.

Uniformity lets a portion of the section have the same intensity as the whole beam. Power collected by the portion is one quarter of the total, $0.0050\,\mathrm W$; multiplying by five seconds gives the requested energy. The beam’s entire power must not be assigned to the smaller portion.

The dimensional check is $\mathrm{W/m^2}\cdot\mathrm{m^2}\cdot\mathrm s=\mathrm J$. A result below the initial energy is consistent despite the longer interval: area fell by a factor of four, whereas time increased only by $\dfrac{5}{3}$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

An ideal string has $\mu_\ell=0.020\,\mathrm{kg/m}$ and wave speed $10\,\mathrm{m/s}$. The source produces a sinusoid with $f=5.0\,\mathrm{Hz}$ and $A=1.0\,\mathrm{cm}$. Calculate tension and mean power. Then double frequency and halve amplitude while retaining the same string tension and linear density: how does power change?

$$
F_T=\mu_\ell v^2=2.0\,\mathrm N,\qquad\omega=10\pi\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}.
$$
$$
P_{\mathrm m}=\dfrac12(0.020)(10)(10\pi)^2(0.010)^2\,\mathrm W
\simeq9.87\cdot10^{-3}\,\mathrm W.
$$
$$
\dfrac{P_2}{P_1}=\left(\dfrac{f_2}{f_1}\right)^2\left(\dfrac{A_2}{A_1}\right)^2=2^2\left(\dfrac12\right)^2=1.
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

String wave speed depends on tension and linear density. Inverting the relation gives tension without introducing an unnecessary total mass. Power requires amplitude in metres and frequency converted to angular frequency: substituting $f$ directly for $\omega$ would lose a factor of $(2\pi)^2$.

The formula gives watts, not watts per square metre. The dimensions of $\mu_\ell v\omega^2A^2$ are $\mathrm{kg\,m^2/s^3}$, a power. Time averaging is already included in the factor $\dfrac{1}{2}$.

In the second situation, frequency doubles and amplitude halves: maximum local speed $A\omega$ stays equal. In the power expression, the two squared factors cancel exactly. Applying only “halved amplitude means quarter power” would be incomplete because frequency changes too. Disturbance speed remains $10\,\mathrm{m/s}$; the new wavelength halves, consistent with $v=f\lambda$.

</div>
