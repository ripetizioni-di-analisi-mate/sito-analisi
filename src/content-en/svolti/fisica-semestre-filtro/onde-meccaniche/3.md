## Exercise 1

A wave travels at $300\,\mathrm{m/s}$ and a sensor records $45$ complete cycles in $0.30\,\mathrm s$. Determine frequency, period and wavelength. How many wavelengths fit in an $8.0\,\mathrm m$ segment?

$$
f=\dfrac{45}{0.30\,\mathrm s}=150\,\mathrm{Hz},\qquad
T=\dfrac1f\simeq6.67\,\mathrm{ms}.
$$
$$
\lambda=\dfrac vf=2.0\,\mathrm m,\qquad
N=\dfrac{8.0\,\mathrm m}{2.0\,\mathrm m}=4.
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The sensor is stationary, so recorded cycles form a time count. Dividing cycle count by duration gives frequency; reversing the ratio gives the time per cycle. Cycle count is dimensionless, so frequency has inverse-time dimensions.

Moving from time to space requires disturbance speed. During $T$, the wave travels $vT$, one wavelength. The ratio $\dfrac{v}{f}$ gives metres because inverse seconds in speed cancel those in frequency. The segment contains four spatial periods regardless of its starting phase.

Four wavelengths must not be confused with four visible crests: counting both endpoints can give five crests if the segment begins and ends at a crest. An independent check is crossing time, $\dfrac{8.0}{300}\,\mathrm s\simeq0.0267\,\mathrm s$, equal to four periods.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Ultrasound at $5.0\,\mathrm{MHz}$ crosses a stationary interface from a medium with $v_1=1500\,\mathrm{m/s}$ into one with $v_2=1800\,\mathrm{m/s}$. Find both wavelengths and the one-way time through $3.0\,\mathrm{cm}$ of the second medium. Assume normal propagation and homogeneous media.

$$
f_2=f_1=5.0\cdot10^6\,\mathrm{Hz},\quad
\lambda_1=0.30\,\mathrm{mm},\quad\lambda_2=0.36\,\mathrm{mm}.
$$
$$
t=\dfrac{0.030\,\mathrm m}{1800\,\mathrm{m/s}}\simeq16.7\,\mu\mathrm s.
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The stationary interface must respond at the incident disturbance’s rhythm. Transmitted frequency therefore remains the source frequency: it is not multiplied by the speed ratio. Greater speed in the second medium means greater distance travelled during each cycle, hence a longer wavelength.

The wavelength ratio is $\dfrac{1800}{1500}=1.20$, checking both results without repeating conversions. Time of flight instead uses only the speed and length of the requested segment, expressed in metres. Frequency does not enter this division because wave speed is already given in the model.

This is one-way time: neither double the path nor halve the distance. An echo over the same segment would require approximately $33.3\,\mu\mathrm s$. The frequency conclusion assumes a stationary interface; a moving surface would create a different problem related to Doppler.

</div>
