## Exercise 1

A stationary source emits $680\,\mathrm{Hz}$ in a stationary medium with $v=340\,\mathrm{m/s}$. An observer moves along the source–observer line at $20\,\mathrm{m/s}$, first approaching and then receding. Calculate both frequencies. Does wavelength in the medium change between observations?

$$
\lambda=\dfrac{340}{680}\,\mathrm m=0.50\,\mathrm m.
$$
$$
f_{\mathrm{approach}}=\dfrac{340+20}{0.50}\,\mathrm{Hz}=720\,\mathrm{Hz},\qquad
f_{\mathrm{recede}}=\dfrac{340-20}{0.50}\,\mathrm{Hz}=640\,\mathrm{Hz}.
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The source stays stationary, so crest spacing in the medium is determined by sound speed and emitted frequency. Moving the receiver does not alter the wave already present in the medium. The same wavelength therefore enters both calculations.

Approaching, the receiver encounters fronts at relative speed $v+u$; receding, at $v-u$. Dividing these speeds by front spacing gives the number of arrivals per second. Before calculating, one therefore predicts a frequency above $680\,\mathrm{Hz}$ in the first case and below it in the second.

The shifts are symmetric here, $\pm40\,\mathrm{Hz}$, because the source is stationary and the formula is linear in observer velocity. For a moving source, velocity instead appears in the denominator and exact symmetry no longer holds. The limit $u=0$ restores emitted frequency; $u<v$ respects the requested model’s domain.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A pulse normally crosses a $1.5\,\mathrm{cm}$ layer at $1500\,\mathrm{m/s}$, then a layer of unknown thickness at $1600\,\mathrm{m/s}$. The echo from the bottom of the second layer returns after $60\,\mu\mathrm s$, already corrected for instrument delays. Calculate the unknown thickness and total distance from the transducer.

$$
\Delta t=2\left(\dfrac{d_1}{v_1}+\dfrac{d_2}{v_2}\right),\qquad
\dfrac{d_1}{v_1}=\dfrac{0.015}{1500}\,\mathrm s=10\,\mu\mathrm s.
$$
$$
\dfrac{d_2}{v_2}=\dfrac{60}{2}\,\mu\mathrm s-10\,\mu\mathrm s=20\,\mu\mathrm s.
$$
$$
d_2=(1600\,\mathrm{m/s})(20\cdot10^{-6}\,\mathrm s)=0.032\,\mathrm m,
\qquad d_1+d_2=4.7\,\mathrm{cm}.
$$

![Outward and return paths through two layers at 1500 and 1600 metres per second; the second thickness is unknown.](/figure/fisica-semestre-filtro/onde-meccaniche/echo-layers-en.svg)

<div class="spiegazione">

Return time includes two crossings of each layer. Segment times are added, not speeds, because each segment has its own distance and the same disturbance crosses them in succession. Normal incidence makes path lengths equal to thicknesses, avoiding a refraction geometry correction.

Halving total time gives thirty microseconds one way. Ten are spent in the first layer, leaving twenty in the second. Multiplying the remaining time by the second medium’s speed gives unknown thickness; adding the first thickness gives distance from the transducer.

The check reconstructs the echo: $2(10+20)=60\,\mu\mathrm s$. An arithmetic mean of the two speeds is unjustified because travel times are unequal. The measured time must also belong to the bottom echo identified in the question; reflection from the intermediate interface would have a different path and time.

</div>
