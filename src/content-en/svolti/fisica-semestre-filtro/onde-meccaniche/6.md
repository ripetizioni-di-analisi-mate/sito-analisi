## Exercise 1

Two coherent emitters have initial phase difference $\pi$. The second path to a point is $0.60\,\mathrm m$ longer; wavelength is $0.40\,\mathrm m$. Assume total phase difference $\Delta\varphi=\pi-k\Delta\ell$. Determine interference type and total amplitude if both local contributions have amplitude $2.0\,\mathrm{mm}$.

$$
k\Delta\ell=\dfrac{2\pi}{0.40\,\mathrm m}(0.60\,\mathrm m)=3\pi,
\qquad\Delta\varphi=\pi-3\pi=-2\pi.
$$
$$
\cos\Delta\varphi=1,\qquad A_R=4.0\,\mathrm{mm}.
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The path difference is a half-integer multiple of wavelength: by itself it would introduce antiphase. However, the emitters already start in antiphase. Initial phase and accumulated path phase must be combined using the stated convention for the second contribution.

The result $-2\pi$ is equivalent to zero for a periodic function: the contributions arrive in phase. Interference is therefore constructive and local amplitudes add. Automatically applying “half-integer path means minimum” would ignore the assumption of initially in-phase sources, which does not hold here.

As a check, increasing path difference by another wavelength changes phase by $-2\pi$ and leaves interference type unchanged. Doubled amplitude depends on equal contributions at the point, not merely equal nominal emitters: different distances or attenuation could make their local contributions unequal.

</div>

## Exercise 2

On a string, $y_1=A\cos(kx-\omega t)$ and $y_2=A\cos(kx+\omega t)$ overlap, with $\lambda=0.80\,\mathrm m$. Obtain the total disturbance, locate the first two nodes at $x>0$, and decide whether a crest of the complete pattern advances at speed $\dfrac{\omega}{k}$.

$$
y=2A\cos(kx)\cos(\omega t),\qquad
k=\dfrac{2\pi}{\lambda}.
$$
$$
\cos(kx)=0\Longrightarrow x_n=\dfrac{(2n+1)\lambda}{4},\quad n=0,1,\ldots
$$
$$
x_0=0.20\,\mathrm m,\qquad x_1=0.60\,\mathrm m,\qquad x_1-x_0=0.40\,\mathrm m.
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The identity for the sum of two cosines separates a spatial factor from a temporal factor. Wherever $\cos(kx)=0$, displacement vanishes at every instant: these are permanent nodes, unlike instantaneous zeros travelling with a single progressive wave.

The first positive solution occurs at one quarter-wavelength; successive nodes are half a wavelength apart. Node spacing therefore must not be read directly as $\lambda$. Local amplitude varies with position while the nodes remain fixed.

The complete pattern is a standing wave: it changes sign and magnitude without translating as a rigid shape. The two components individually travel at speed $\dfrac{\omega}{k}$, but assigning that speed to their resultant maximum would be inappropriate. In the ideal model, opposite mean powers cancel even though the oscillations contain energy. This shows why local energy does not necessarily imply a net mean flux in the same direction.

</div>
