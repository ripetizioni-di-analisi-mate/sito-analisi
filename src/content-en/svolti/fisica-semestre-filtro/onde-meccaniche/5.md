## Exercise 1

Two coherent harmonic waves produce contributions at one point with amplitudes $3.0$ and $4.0\,\mathrm{mm}$ and phase difference $\dfrac{\pi}{2}$. Calculate the resultant amplitude. For travelling contributions in the same direction and medium, compare resultant intensity with the sum of separate intensities.

$$
A_R^2=(3.0\,\mathrm{mm})^2+(4.0\,\mathrm{mm})^2+2(3.0\,\mathrm{mm})(4.0\,\mathrm{mm})\cos\dfrac\pi2=25\,\mathrm{mm^2}.
$$
$$
A_R=5.0\,\mathrm{mm},\qquad I_R=C A_R^2=C(A_1^2+A_2^2)=I_1+I_2.
$$

![Three- and four-millimetre phasors in quadrature and resultant amplitude versus phase difference.](/figure/fisica-semestre-filtro/onde-meccaniche/interference-worked-en.svg)

<div class="spiegazione">

Adding amplitudes would give $7\,\mathrm{mm}$ only for in-phase contributions. Here maxima do not occur simultaneously: when one contribution reaches its maximum, the other crosses zero. The interference term contains the cosine of the phase difference and vanishes at $\dfrac{\pi}{2}$.

Thus the squares add before taking a square root: the result forms the $3$–$4$–$5$ triangle of quadrature components. It is not an average of the two amplitudes. The positive coefficient $C$ relating intensity to squared amplitude is shared because frequency, medium and propagation direction are fixed.

Here intensity equals the sum of separate intensities even though the waves are coherent. Equality depends on the particular phase difference and does not establish incoherent sources. As a check, the result lies between the amplitude difference and sum, $1$ and $7\,\mathrm{mm}$, the extremes available when phase varies.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Two same-frequency contributions have positive constant but unknown amplitudes. Varying phase alone changes total amplitude from a minimum of $1.0\,\mathrm{mm}$ to a maximum of $7.0\,\mathrm{mm}$. Determine both amplitudes and explain whether the stronger source can be identified.

$$
A_1+A_2=7.0\,\mathrm{mm},\qquad |A_1-A_2|=1.0\,\mathrm{mm}.
$$
$$
A_{\mathrm{large}}=\dfrac{7.0+1.0}{2}\,\mathrm{mm}=4.0\,\mathrm{mm},\qquad
A_{\mathrm{small}}=\dfrac{7.0-1.0}{2}\,\mathrm{mm}=3.0\,\mathrm{mm}.
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The maximum corresponds to matching phase, when displacements reinforce. The minimum corresponds to antiphase, when they subtract. A nonzero minimum shows that amplitudes differ. These measurements are amplitudes, not intensities, so no initial square roots of the data are needed.

Temporarily labelling the larger amplitude $A_1$ removes the absolute value and produces a sum-and-difference system. Adding equations isolates twice the larger amplitude; subtracting isolates twice the smaller one. Both are positive and therefore consistent with the model.

Substitution checks the extremes: $4+3=7$ and $4-3=1$. However, the measurement does not identify the physical label of the stronger source, because exchanging sources leaves the maximum and minimum unchanged. At least one contribution would need measuring separately. The result determines an amplitude pair, not a unique amplitude-to-source assignment.

</div>
