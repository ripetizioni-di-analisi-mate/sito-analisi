## Exercise 1

Heat $Q=2000\,\mathrm J$ passes spontaneously from a reservoir at $400\,\mathrm K$ to one at $250\,\mathrm K$. Both maintain their temperatures; the connection stores no energy, and the combined system exchanges no energy with other systems. Calculate the entropy changes of each reservoir and of the combined system.

$$
\Delta S_h=-\dfrac{Q}{T_h}=-\dfrac{2000}{400}=-5\,\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}},
\qquad \Delta S_c=\dfrac{Q}{T_c}=\dfrac{2000}{250}=8\,\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}}.
$$
$$
\Delta S_{\mathrm{tot}}=\Delta S_h+\Delta S_c=3\,\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}}>0.
$$

![Entropy changes minus 5, plus 8 and total plus 3 joules per kelvin](/figure/fisica-semestre-filtro/termodinamica/entropia-en.svg)

<div class="spiegazione">

Heat has the same magnitude at both ends because the connection stores none, but it is transferred at different temperatures. The hot source loses entropy; the cold source gains more than was lost. For a constant-temperature reservoir, entropy change is signed received heat divided by absolute temperature.

The combined energy remains unchanged while entropy increases: these statements are compatible because they concern different quantities. The positive increase indicates irreversibility of transfer across a finite temperature difference. Opposite flow between the same reservoirs, without other effects, would give a negative total and would not occur spontaneously. A refrigerator can reverse the transfer only by also introducing work and further energy transfers.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Two kilograms of liquid water are heated from $20.0$ to $80.0\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$, without evaporation, using constant $c=4186\,\mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}K^{-1}}$. Find the required heat in the calorimetric model and the water's entropy change. Details of the actual heating are unknown: explain what can still be calculated.

$$
T_i=293.15\,\mathrm K,\quad T_f=353.15\,\mathrm K,
\quad Q=mc(T_f-T_i)=502320\,\mathrm J\simeq502\,\mathrm{kJ}.
$$
$$
\Delta S=\int_{T_i}^{T_f}\dfrac{mc\,dT}{T}
=mc\ln\dfrac{T_f}{T_i}
=8372\ln\dfrac{353.15}{293.15}
\simeq1.56\cdot10^3\,\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}}.
$$
$$
\dfrac{Q}{T_f}<\Delta S<\dfrac{Q}{T_i}.
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Entropy is a state function: equilibrated endpoints and the calorimetric model suffice to determine the water's change. Calculate it using an imagined auxiliary reversible heating process with successively warmer reservoirs, each infinitesimally close to the water's temperature. Along that path, elementary heat is $mc\,dT$; dividing by variable temperature leads to the logarithm.

Dividing total heat by final temperature alone would underestimate the result; using initial temperature alone would overestimate it. These two estimates provide a check without extra data. Total entropy produced in the real process cannot instead be found without knowing how the source supplied energy. Using a reversible path for calculation does not justify calling the actual heating reversible.

</div>
