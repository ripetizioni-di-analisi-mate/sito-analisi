## Exercise 1

Two moles of monatomic ideal helium are heated from $300$ to $450\,\mathrm K$ in a rigid, sealed vessel. Only volume work is allowed. With $R=8.314\,\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}K^{-1}}$, calculate $W$, $\Delta U$, $Q$ and the final-to-initial pressure ratio. Take $W>0$ for work done by the gas.

$$
W=0,\qquad C_V=\dfrac32R,
$$
$$
\Delta U=nC_V\Delta T=2\cdot\dfrac32\cdot8.314\cdot150
=3741.3\,\mathrm J\simeq3.74\,\mathrm{kJ}.
$$
$$
Q=\Delta U+W=3.74\,\mathrm{kJ},\qquad
\dfrac{p_f}{p_i}=\dfrac{T_f}{T_i}=1.50.
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The vessel does not change volume, so the gas does not move its boundary and volume work is zero. Restricting the type of work matters: a stirrer could supply work even in a rigid vessel. Here all energy absorbed as heat increases the gas's internal energy.

$C_V$ is a molar heat capacity, so the factor $n$ is required. The three translational degrees of freedom contribute for a classical monatomic gas. With the stated convention, the first law is $\Delta U=Q-W$, confirming $Q=\Delta U>0$. The pressure ratio independently checks the state change: heating at fixed volume raises pressure even though no work is produced.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Half a mole of diatomic ideal gas expands from $10.0$ to $15.0\,\mathrm L$ at constant absolute pressure $100\,\mathrm{kPa}$. The system is closed, the process mechanically reversible, and only volume work occurs. Assume active rotations and negligible vibrations, hence constant $C_V=\dfrac52R$, with $R=8.314\,\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}K^{-1}}$. Find the temperatures, work done, internal energy change and heat.

$$
T_i=\dfrac{10^5\cdot0.0100}{0.500\cdot8.314}
\simeq240.6\,\mathrm K,\qquad
T_f=\dfrac{10^5\cdot0.0150}{0.500\cdot8.314}
\simeq360.8\,\mathrm K.
$$
$$
W=p(V_f-V_i)=10^5\cdot0.0050=500\,\mathrm J.
$$
$$
\Delta U=\dfrac52nR\Delta T=\dfrac52p\Delta V=1250\,\mathrm J,
\qquad Q=\Delta U+W=1750\,\mathrm J.
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

At constant pressure, a closed gas can increase its volume only by increasing temperature. Work is positive because the gas expands against external pressure, which equals gas pressure in the mechanically reversible limit. To find $\Delta U$, directly use $nR\Delta T=p\Delta V$, avoiding errors from rounded temperatures.

The absorbed energy divides into increased internal energy and work, so $Q$ exceeds $\Delta U$. An independent check uses $C_p=C_V+R=\dfrac72R$, giving $Q=nC_p\Delta T=1750\,\mathrm J$. The diatomic value applies over the specified range; it should not be assumed valid when vibrations become important.

</div>
