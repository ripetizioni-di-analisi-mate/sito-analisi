## Exercise 1

Bring $0.200\,\mathrm{kg}$ of ice from $-30\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$ to liquid water at $40\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$, at atmospheric pressure. Neglect evaporation and the vessel's heat capacity. Use constant $c_g=2090$, $c_a=4186$, in $\mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}K^{-1}}$, and $\ell_f=333000\,\mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}}$. How much heat is needed, and which part is used in melting?

$$
Q_1=mc_g(0-(-30))=12540\,\mathrm J,
$$
$$
Q_2=m\ell_f=66600\,\mathrm J,\qquad
Q_3=mc_a(40-0)=33488\,\mathrm J.
$$
$$
Q=Q_1+Q_2+Q_3=112628\,\mathrm J\simeq113\,\mathrm{kJ},
\qquad \dfrac{Q_2}{Q}=0.5913\ldots\simeq59.1\%.
$$

![Temperature versus absorbed heat through the three heating stages](/figure/fisica-semestre-filtro/termodinamica/riscaldamento-en.svg)

<div class="spiegazione">

The substance passes through three stages, each governed by a different law. Ice first warms, then melts at constant temperature, and liquid water finally warms. Applying one $mc\Delta T$ term from minus thirty to forty degrees would use an inappropriate specific heat capacity over at least one stage and entirely omit latent heat.

The graph's plateau spans a heat input of $66.6\,\mathrm{kJ}$: temperature remains at zero while the liquid fraction grows. This is the largest contribution despite producing no temperature rise. The two changes of regime occur at cumulative inputs of $12.54$ and $79.14\,\mathrm{kJ}$; comparing them with the total checks that no stage has been counted twice.

</div>

## Exercise 2

In an adiabatic calorimeter of negligible heat capacity, add $0.0500\,\mathrm{kg}$ of ice at $0\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$ to $0.400\,\mathrm{kg}$ of water at $30.0\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$. At atmospheric pressure, use $c_a=4186\,\mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}K^{-1}}$ and $\ell_f=333000\,\mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}}$, with no evaporation. Establish whether all the ice melts and find the final state.

$$
Q_{\max}=0.400\cdot4186\cdot30.0=50232\,\mathrm J,
\qquad Q_f=0.0500\cdot333000=16650\,\mathrm J.
$$
$$
Q_{\max}>Q_f,\qquad
0.400\cdot4186(30.0-t_f)=16650+0.0500\cdot4186t_f.
$$
$$
t_f=\dfrac{50232-16650}{(0.400+0.0500)\cdot4186}
=17.8277\ldots\,{}^\circ\mathrm C\simeq17.8\,{}^\circ\mathrm C.
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Before writing an equation that assumes entirely liquid water, check whether the warm water can melt the whole ice mass. The maximum heat available before reaching zero degrees exceeds the melting requirement, making that assumption consistent. After melting, the water originating from ice must also warm, which is why its heat capacity belongs in the total denominator.

The final state contains $0.450\,\mathrm{kg}$ of liquid water at a positive temperature below the warm water's initial temperature. If the first comparison had shown insufficient energy, the system would remain at zero degrees with residual ice; a negative result obtained from the previous formula could not be interpreted as ordinary liquid water.

</div>
