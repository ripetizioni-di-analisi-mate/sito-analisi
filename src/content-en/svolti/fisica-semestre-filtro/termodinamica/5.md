## Exercise 1

A closed ideal gas of $0.200\,\mathrm{mol}$ expands reversibly and isothermally at $300\,\mathrm K$ from $5.00$ to $15.0\,\mathrm L$. Only volume work occurs. Using $R=8.314\,\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}K^{-1}}$, find the endpoint pressures, work done and heat absorbed.

$$
p_i=\dfrac{nRT}{V_i}=99768\,\mathrm{Pa},\qquad
p_f=\dfrac{nRT}{V_f}=33256\,\mathrm{Pa}.
$$
$$
W=\int_{V_i}^{V_f}\dfrac{nRT}{V}\,dV
=nRT\ln\dfrac{V_f}{V_i}
=498.84\ln3\,\mathrm J\simeq548\,\mathrm J.
$$
$$
\Delta U=0,\qquad Q=W\simeq548\,\mathrm J.
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Pressure changes during expansion, so neither endpoint pressure can be multiplied by the whole volume change. Mechanical reversibility allows gas pressure, expressed through its equation of state, to be used in the integral. Integrating inverse volume produces the logarithm of a dimensionless ratio.

For an ideal gas, constant temperature means constant internal energy. Work leaving the gas must therefore be balanced by incoming heat, supplied reversibly by a reservoir at the same temperature in the limit of an infinitesimal difference. Isothermal does not mean adiabatic: both energy transfers here are nonzero. Tripling volume reduces pressure to one third, consistent with the calculated states.

</div>

## Exercise 2

An ideal gas with constant $\gamma=1.40$ is compressed reversibly in an adiabatic cylinder from $V_i=1.00\,\mathrm L$ to $V_f=0.125\,\mathrm L$. Initially $p_i=100\,\mathrm{kPa}$ and $T_i=300\,\mathrm K$. The system is closed and does only volume work. Find $T_f$, $p_f$, $W$ and $\Delta U$, assuming the constant-$\gamma$ model holds throughout the interval.

$$
T_f=T_i\left(\dfrac{V_i}{V_f}\right)^{\gamma-1}
=300\cdot8^{0.40}\,\mathrm K\simeq689\,\mathrm K,
$$
$$
p_f=p_i\left(\dfrac{V_i}{V_f}\right)^\gamma
=10^5\cdot8^{1.40}\,\mathrm{Pa}\simeq1.84\cdot10^6\,\mathrm{Pa}.
$$
$$
W=\dfrac{p_iV_i-p_fV_f}{\gamma-1}
=-324.349\ldots\,\mathrm J\simeq-324\,\mathrm J,
\qquad Q=0,\qquad \Delta U=-W\simeq324\,\mathrm J.
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The constant-$TV^{\gamma-1}$ and constant-$pV^\gamma$ relations require both zero heat transfer and reversibility, together with the ideal, constant-capacity model. The word “adiabatic” alone would not suffice. Compression involves work done on the gas: with this chapter's convention, gas work is negative and internal energy increases.

Work can be checked without integrating again. The equation of state gives $nR=\dfrac{p_iV_i}{T_i}$, and the heat-capacity relation gives $C_V=\dfrac{R}{\gamma-1}$. Thus $\Delta U=\dfrac{p_iV_i}{T_i(\gamma-1)}(T_f-T_i)$ returns the same positive value. Retaining intermediate digits in the powers prevents rounded pressure and temperature values from affecting this comparison.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Consider again $0.200\,\mathrm{mol}$ of ideal gas at $300\,\mathrm K$, initially in $5.00\,\mathrm L$. It now occupies part of a rigid, adiabatic, closed vessel of total volume $15.0\,\mathrm L$; the rest is evacuated. Remove the partition and determine $Q$, $W$, $\Delta U$, final temperature and $\Delta S$. Use $R=8.314\,\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}K^{-1}}$ and compare with the reversible expansion in the first exercise.

$$
Q=0,\qquad W=0,\qquad \Delta U=Q-W=0,\qquad T_f=T_i=300\,\mathrm K.
$$
$$
\Delta S=nC_V\ln\dfrac{T_f}{T_i}+nR\ln\dfrac{V_f}{V_i}
=0.200\cdot8.314\ln3\,\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}}
\simeq1.83\,\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}}.
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The gas does not push against an external pressure, and the vessel's rigid boundary does not move, so no work leaves. Adiabatic walls prevent heat transfer. The first law fixes internal energy, and the ideal model then determines final temperature. During the process the gas does not follow a sequence of uniform equilibrium states: it should not be drawn as a reversible isotherm whose area is evaluated.

The endpoints are nevertheless the same as in the first exercise, so the gas entropy change is equal. It is calculated using an auxiliary reversible path, not by dividing the zero actual heat by temperature. Here the increase is entirely entropy production within the isolated system. In the reversible process, the reservoir instead loses exactly the entropy gained by the gas.

</div>
