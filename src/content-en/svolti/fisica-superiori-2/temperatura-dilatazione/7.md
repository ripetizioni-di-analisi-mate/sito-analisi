## Exercise 1

An isotropic solid occupies $150\,\mathrm{cm^3}$. With $\alpha=1.5\cdot10^{-5}\,\mathrm{K^{-1}}$ and $\Delta T=40\,\mathrm K$, what is the increase in volume?

::grafico{f="0.00675*x" punti="40,0.27,pieno,A" segmenti="0,.27,40,.27;40,0,40,.27" xmin=0 xmax=50 ymin=0 ymax=0.39 etichette="1,.294,0.27 cm³" nomi="ΔT (K),ΔV (cm³)" titolo="Point A gives the volume increase for the stated heating."}

$$
\begin{aligned} \Delta V&\simeq3\alpha V_0\Delta T\\[6pt] &=3\cdot(1.5\cdot10^{-5})\cdot150\cdot40\,\mathrm{cm^3}=0.27\,\mathrm{cm^3}. \end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Because all three dimensions grow in the same proportion, the linear factor appears three times when calculating volume. To first order the fractional change is $0.0018$. Multiplying it by $150$ cubic centimetres gives $0.27$ cubic centimetres. The final volume would be about $150.27$ cubic centimetres.

As a check, compute the increase per kelvin. The product $3\alpha V_0$ is $0.00675\,\mathrm{cm^3/K}$. Multiplying by $40\,\mathrm K$ recovers the absolute increase. Counting only one direction would give just one third of the volume increase.

</div>

## Exercise 2

An isotropic block of initial volume $200\,\mathrm{cm^3}$ warms by $60\,\mathrm K$ and gains $0.72\,\mathrm{cm^3}$. Estimate $\alpha$.

$$
\begin{aligned} \beta&\simeq\frac{0.72}{200\cdot60}\,\mathrm{K^{-1}}=6.0\cdot10^{-5}\,\mathrm{K^{-1}}\\[6pt] \alpha&\simeq\frac{\beta}{3}=2.0\cdot10^{-5}\,\mathrm{K^{-1}}. \end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The measurement concerns volume. Divide the change by the initial volume and by the temperature increase to determine the volume coefficient first. The body is isotropic, so in the small-deformation model $\beta\simeq3\alpha$. Dividing by three gives the requested coefficient. Using the volume coefficient directly would predict a linear expansion three times as large.

With the calculated linear coefficient, one dimension changes by the fraction $2.0\cdot10^{-5}\cdot60=0.0012$. To first order the volume fraction is three times this, or $0.0036$. Multiplying that fraction by $200\,\mathrm{cm^3}$ recovers the measured $0.72\,\mathrm{cm^3}$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

In a geometric model each dimension of a cube of volume $125\,\mathrm{cm^3}$ increases by $0.2\%$. Compare the exact volume increase with the linear estimate.

$$
\begin{aligned} \Delta V_{\mathrm{geom}}&=125[(1.002)^3-1]\,\mathrm{cm^3}=0.751501\,\mathrm{cm^3}\\[6pt] \Delta V_{\mathrm{lin}}&=125\cdot3\cdot0.002\,\mathrm{cm^3}=0.75\,\mathrm{cm^3}. \end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The linear factor is $1.002$ because $0.2\%$ equals $0.002$ of the initial value. The volume, a product of three lengths, is multiplied by the cube of that factor. Expanding the cube, the linear term agrees with the second expression. The difference is $0.001501\,\mathrm{cm^3}$ and comes from the quadratic and cubic terms.

The difference of $0.001501\,\mathrm{cm^3}$ is about $0.20\%$ of the predicted linear change. This lets us compare the correction with the available resolution. An instrument with steps of $0.01\,\mathrm{cm^3}$ could report the same value for both results.

</div>
