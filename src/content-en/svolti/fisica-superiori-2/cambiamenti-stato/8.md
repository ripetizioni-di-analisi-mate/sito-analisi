## Exercise 1

A mass of steam of $0.02\,\mathrm{kg}$ condenses at $100\,\mathrm{^{\circ}C}$ at normal atmospheric pressure. Use $L_v=2.26\cdot10^{6}\,\mathrm{J/kg}$. What is the magnitude of the energy released?

::grafico{f="-2260*x" punti="0.02,-45.2,pieno,A" xmin=0 xmax=0.027 ymin=-64 ymax=0 nomi="m (kg),Q (kJ)" titolo="The magnitude of the vertical coordinate is the energy released by the steam."}

$$
\begin{aligned}
|Q| &= L m \\[6pt]
&= \left(2.26\cdot10^{6}\right) \left(0.02\right) \\[6pt]
&\simeq 45200\,\mathrm{J}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The steam of mass $0.02\,\mathrm{kg}$ condenses at $100\,\mathrm{^{\circ}C}$. The magnitude of its released energy is the mass times $L_v=2.26\cdot10^{6}\,\mathrm{J/kg}$. The change reverses boiling and has the opposite effect in the energy balance. A magnitude is positive because the question asks how much energy reaches the surroundings.

</div>

## Exercise 2

During condensation at $100\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$, the surroundings absorb $135.6\,\mathrm{kJ}$. Use $L_v=2260\,\mathrm{kJ/kg}$. Find the mass condensed.

$$
m=\frac{135.6}{2260}\,\mathrm{kg}=0.060\,\mathrm{kg}.
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The energy received by the surroundings has the same magnitude as that released by the steam. The sign is negative for the steam, but use its magnitude to obtain a positive mass. The result is sixty grams.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Condense $0.050\,\mathrm{kg}$ of steam at $100\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$, then cool the condensate to $80\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$. With $L_v=2260\,\mathrm{kJ/kg}$ and $c_a=4200\,\mathrm{J/(kg\,K)}$, find the total magnitude released.

$$
\begin{aligned}|Q_{\mathrm{cond}}|&=0.050\cdot2260\,\mathrm{kJ}=113\,\mathrm{kJ}\\|Q_{\mathrm{raff}}|&=0.050\cdot4200\cdot20\,\mathrm J=4.2\,\mathrm{kJ}\\|Q_{\mathrm{tot}}|&=117.2\,\mathrm{kJ}.\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The first stage changes phase, so use the latent heat of vaporisation with the opposite sign to heating. The second stage has liquid water and uses its specific heat capacity. Both release energy, so the magnitudes add.

</div>
