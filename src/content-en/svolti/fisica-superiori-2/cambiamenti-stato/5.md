## Exercise 1

Take $0.2\,\mathrm{kg}$ of ice from $-10\,\mathrm{^{\circ}C}$ to liquid water at $12\,\mathrm{^{\circ}C}$. Use $c_g=2100$, $c_a=4200\,\mathrm{J/(kg\,K)}$ and $L_f=334000\,\mathrm{J/kg}$ at normal atmospheric pressure. How much heat is needed?

::grafico{linee="0,-10 4.2,0 71,0 81.08,12" punti="4.2,0,pieno;71,0,pieno;81.08,12,pieno" xmin=0 xmax=87 ymin=-12 ymax=16 nomi="Q (kJ),T (°C)" titolo="The process ends at 12 °C and 81.08 kJ."}

$$
\begin{aligned}
Q &= m c_g(0-T_i)+mL_f+mc_a(T_f-0) \\[6pt]
&= (0.2\cdot2100\cdot10 + 0.2\cdot334000 + 0.2\cdot4200\cdot12)\,\mathrm{J} \\[6pt]
&= (4200+66800+10080)\,\mathrm{J} \\[6pt]
&= 81080\,\mathrm{J}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

There are three contributions for the same mass $0.2\,\mathrm{kg}$. The ice reaches zero from $-10\,\mathrm{^{\circ}C}$ using $c_g=2100$. Melting absorbs the mass times $L_f=334000\,\mathrm{J/kg}$. After the change, the water warms from zero to $12\,\mathrm{^{\circ}C}$ using $c_a=4200\,\mathrm{J/(kg\,K)}$. The three contributions are positive and their sum includes both temperature increases and the phase change.

Melting uses $66.8\,\mathrm{kJ}$ out of $81.08\,\mathrm{kJ}$ in total, or about $82\%$. This is the plateau on the graph. Temperature stays the same on this section while its energy interval is much wider than that of either warming stage.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Warm $0.30\,\mathrm{kg}$ of ice at $-5\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$ until it becomes water at $10\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$. Use $c_g=2100$, $c_a=4200\,\mathrm{J/(kg\,K)}$ and $L_f=334\,\mathrm{kJ/kg}$. Which stage takes the most energy?

$$
\begin{aligned}Q_1&=0.30\cdot2100\cdot5\,\mathrm J=3.15\,\mathrm{kJ}\\Q_2&=0.30\cdot334\,\mathrm{kJ}=100.2\,\mathrm{kJ}\\Q_3&=0.30\cdot4200\cdot10\,\mathrm J=12.6\,\mathrm{kJ}.\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The stage with the greater rise in temperature is not the most costly in energy. Melting occurs at fixed temperature and takes 100.2 kilojoules. Specific heat applies to the two sloped parts of the curve.

The two warming stages together use $15.75\,\mathrm{kJ}$. Even combined, they use much less than the $100.2\,\mathrm{kJ}$ spent on melting. At constant useful power, the plateau would therefore take longer than the two warming stages together.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A $0.25\,\mathrm{kg}$ ice sample initially at $-10\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$ receives $99.25\,\mathrm{kJ}$ without losses. With $c_g=2100$, $c_a=4200\,\mathrm{J/(kg\,K)}$ and $L_f=334\,\mathrm{kJ/kg}$, find its final state and temperature.

$$
\begin{aligned}Q_g&=0.25\cdot2100\cdot10\,\mathrm J=5.25\,\mathrm{kJ}\\ Q_f&=0.25\cdot334\,\mathrm{kJ}=83.5\,\mathrm{kJ}\\Q_{\mathrm a}&=(99.25-5.25-83.5)\,\mathrm{kJ}=10.5\,\mathrm{kJ}\\\Delta T_a&=\frac{10500}{0.25\cdot4200}\,\mathrm K=10\,\mathrm K.\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Reach the melting point first and compare the remaining energy with the energy required to melt the whole mass. The input exceeds both thresholds and leaves 10.5 kilojoules for the liquid phase. The water starts at zero, so its final temperature is 10 °C.

The horizontal coordinate at the end of melting is $5.25+83.5=88.75$ in kilojoules. The available energy is larger, so the final state is entirely liquid. To check, the resulting water has a heat capacity of $1050\,\mathrm{J/K}$ and a $10\,\mathrm K$ rise uses exactly the remaining $10500\,\mathrm J$.

</div>
