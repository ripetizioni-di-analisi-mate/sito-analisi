## Exercise 1

A mass of water of $0.2\,\mathrm{kg}$ warms by $12\,\mathrm K$ without changing phase. Use $c=4200\,\mathrm{J/(kg\,K)}$. How much heat does it absorb?

::grafico{f="0.84*x" punti="12,10.08,pieno,A" xmin=0 xmax=16 ymin=0 ymax=15 nomi="ΔT (K),Q (kJ)" titolo="A rise of 12 K corresponds to 10.08 kJ."}

$$
\begin{aligned}
Q &= \Delta T c m \\[6pt]
&= \left(12\right) \left(4200\right) \left(0.2\right) \\[6pt]
&\simeq 10080\,\mathrm{J}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The water stays liquid, so use its specific heat capacity in $Q=mc\Delta T$. Multiply $c=4200\,\mathrm{J/(kg\,K)}$ by $0.2\,\mathrm{kg}$ to obtain the heat capacity of the sample. Thus every kelvin of warming requires $840\,\mathrm J$. The interval is $12\,\mathrm K$ and the total is $840\cdot12\,\mathrm J$. The positive sign indicates that the water received energy.

The result is $10.08\,\mathrm{kJ}$. In the same model, doubling the mass while holding the temperature rise fixed doubles both the heat capacity and the heat that must be transferred.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A $0.50\,\mathrm{kg}$ piece of copper with $c=400\,\mathrm{J/(kg\,K)}$ goes from $85$ to $25\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$. Find the signed heat and its magnitude.

$$
\begin{aligned}\Delta T&=(25-85)\,\mathrm K=-60\,\mathrm K\\ Q&=0.50\cdot400\cdot(-60)\,\mathrm J=-12000\,\mathrm J\\ |Q|&=12\,\mathrm{kJ}.\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The final reading is below the initial reading. In the convention for the sample, heat is negative because the copper releases energy. The surroundings absorb its magnitude if they receive all the energy released. The copper remains solid while cooling.

The copper has a heat capacity of $200\,\mathrm{J/K}$. As a check, bringing the same piece back from $25$ to $85\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$ under the same conditions would require $+12000\,\mathrm J$. The heat magnitude is the same in either direction because the capacity and temperature interval magnitude are unchanged.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Separately warm $0.40\,\mathrm{kg}$ of water and $0.60\,\mathrm{kg}$ of copper by $15\,\mathrm K$. Use $c_a=4200$ and $c_r=400\,\mathrm{J/(kg\,K)}$. Which absorbs more energy, and by how much?

$$
\begin{aligned}Q_a&=0.40\cdot4200\cdot15\,\mathrm J=25200\,\mathrm J\\ Q_r&=0.60\cdot400\cdot15\,\mathrm J=3600\,\mathrm J\\ Q_a-Q_r&=21600\,\mathrm J.\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The temperature change is common, so the comparison depends on the two heat capacities. The mass of water is smaller, but its specific heat capacity is greater. Mass times specific heat is 1680 versus 240 joules per kelvin. The water absorbs 21.6 kJ more.

The ratio of heat capacities is $\dfrac{1680}{240}=7$. For the same temperature rise, the water absorbs seven times as much heat as the copper. The required difference is six times the $3600\,\mathrm J$ absorbed by the copper.

</div>
