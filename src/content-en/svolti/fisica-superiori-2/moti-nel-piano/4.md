## Exercise 1

A ball is launched horizontally and is still in flight after $1\,\mathrm{s}$. Ignoring air resistance, what is the magnitude of the vertical component of its velocity then? Use $g=10\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$.

::grafico{curve="t,-10*t,0,1" punti="0,0,pieno;1,-10,pieno" etichette="0.10,-0.87,I;1.023,-9.55,F" xmin=0 xmax=1.15 ymin=-11 ymax=2 nomi="t (s),vᵧ (m/s)" titolo="The vertical component goes from zero to −10 m/s in the first second."}

$$
\begin{aligned}
v_y &= 0-(10)\cdot 1=-10\,\mathrm{m/s} \\[6pt]
\lvert v_y\rvert &= 10\,\mathrm{m/s}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Evaluate the components at the same time $1\,\mathrm{s}$. The vertical equation needs no horizontal component. With upward positive, velocity along $y$ starts at zero and decreases by $10\,\mathrm{m/s}$ each second. The negative sign means the ball is falling. The question asks for magnitude, $10\,\mathrm{m/s}$. The total speed would also require the horizontal component.

The slope on the velocity–time graph is $-10\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$. The change is ten metres per second over the first second. Between launch and the final reading, the average vertical component is $-5\,\mathrm{m/s}$, consistent with a downward displacement of five metres.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A ball is launched horizontally and is still in flight after $2.5\,\mathrm{s}$. Ignoring air resistance, what is the magnitude of the vertical component of its velocity then? Use $g=10\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
v_y &= 0-(10)\cdot \frac{5}{2}=-25\,\mathrm{m/s} \\[6pt]
\lvert v_y\rvert &= 25\,\mathrm{m/s}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Evaluate the components at the same time $2.5\,\mathrm{s}$. The vertical equation needs no horizontal component. With upward positive, velocity along $y$ starts at zero and decreases by $10\,\mathrm{m/s}$ each second. The negative sign means the ball is falling. The question asks for magnitude, $25\,\mathrm{m/s}$. The total speed would also require the horizontal component.

The magnitude $25\,\mathrm{m/s}$ applies at an instant. Over the interval the component goes from zero to $-25\,\mathrm{m/s}$, so its average is $-12.5\,\mathrm{m/s}$. Multiplying by $2.5\,\mathrm s$ gives vertical displacement $-31.25\,\mathrm m$. This distinguishes a velocity reading from a drop in height.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A ball is launched horizontally from a height of $60\,\mathrm m$ at $6\,\mathrm{m/s}$. A light gate lies in the vertical plane $18\,\mathrm m$ from the launch vertical. With $g=9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$ and negligible air resistance, find the magnitude of the vertical velocity at that plane and its sign on a downward-positive axis. Write $d$ for the drop in height.

$$
\begin{aligned}
t&=\frac{18\,\mathrm m}{6\,\mathrm{m/s}}=3\,\mathrm s,\\[4pt]
d&=\frac12\cdot9.81\cdot3^2\,\mathrm m=44.145\,\mathrm m,\\[4pt]
v_y&=gt=29.43\,\mathrm{m/s}.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Use the horizontal equation for the time because the horizontal velocity is constant. The drop after three seconds is smaller than the initial sixty metres, so the ball reaches the light-gate plane while still in flight. Acceleration points in the chosen positive direction and the vertical component starts at zero. The component is therefore $+29.43\,\mathrm{m/s}$. An upward-positive axis would give a negative sign and the same magnitude. As a check, the average vertical velocity over three seconds is half the final component. Multiplying it by the duration recovers the drop in height.

</div>
