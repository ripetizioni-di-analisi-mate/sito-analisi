## Exercise 1

An object moves around a circle of radius $4\,\mathrm{m}$ at constant speed $6\,\mathrm{m/s}$. What is the magnitude of the centripetal acceleration?

::grafico{curve="4*cos(t),4*sin(t),0,6.2831853" vettori="4,0,4,2.7;4,0,1.4,0" punti="0,0,pieno;4,0,pieno" etichette="4.33,2.02,v;1.8,0.47,a;4.38,0.33,P" xmin=-5 xmax=6 ymin=-5 ymax=5 uguale=1 nomi="x (m),y (m)" titolo="At P velocity is tangent and acceleration points towards the centre. The arrows show the directions."}

$$
\begin{aligned}
a_c&=\frac{v^2}{r}=\frac{6^2}{4}\,\mathrm{m/s^2} \\[6pt]
&\simeq 9\,\mathrm{m/s^2}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

At constant speed the velocity vector still changes direction. Acceleration points towards the centre and has magnitude equal to speed squared divided by radius. The squared speed from the data is $36\,\mathrm{m^2/s^2}$. Dividing by $4\,\mathrm{m}$ gives $9\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$. Check that a length factor cancels and leaves metres per second squared.

In the diagram the acceleration arrow points from the body towards the centre. Velocity is tangent and therefore perpendicular to it. After one turn the velocity vector returns to its initial value. Instantaneous acceleration has magnitude $9\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$ even though the velocity change over the full turn is zero.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A point follows a curve of radius $4\,\mathrm m$ at $6\,\mathrm{m/s}$. In a second curve it is to travel at $9\,\mathrm{m/s}$ without exceeding the first curve's centripetal acceleration. What is the smallest radius of the second curve?

$$
\begin{aligned}
a_{c1}&=\frac{6^2}{4}\,\mathrm{m/s^2}=9\,\mathrm{m/s^2},\\[4pt]
a_{c2}&=\frac{(9\,\mathrm{m/s})^2}{r_2}\leq9\,\mathrm{m/s^2},\\[4pt]
r_2&\geq\frac{81\,\mathrm{m^2/s^2}}{9\,\mathrm{m/s^2}}=9\,\mathrm m.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Use the acceleration magnitude of the first curve as the limit. At the new speed, increasing the radius reduces the acceleration. The smallest allowed radius is therefore at the limit, where the two accelerations agree. Check the scale factor: the speed increases by the ratio $\dfrac96$, so its square increases by $\dfrac94$, and the minimum radius must grow by the same factor. Multiplying the original four metres by $\dfrac94$ gives nine metres. Eight metres would be insufficient because $81$ divided by $8$ exceeds the limit of nine.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A point rotates at distance $1.6\,\mathrm{m}$ from the centre at constant angular speed $3\,\mathrm{rad/s}$. Find the centripetal acceleration.

$$
\begin{aligned}
v_t&=\omega r=3\cdot1.6\,\mathrm{m/s}=4.8\,\mathrm{m/s} \\[6pt]
a_c&=\omega^2r=3^2\cdot 1.6\,\mathrm{m/s^2}=14.4\,\mathrm{m/s^2}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Given angular speed and radius, use $\omega^2r$ directly. As a check, multiply them to get the tangential speed of $4.8\,\mathrm{m/s}$ and substitute it into the other formula. This gives $\dfrac{4.8^2}{1.6}\,\mathrm{m/s^2}=14.4\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$. Velocity is tangent to the circle, while acceleration points towards the centre. At fixed angular speed, doubling the radius would double both magnitudes.

</div>
