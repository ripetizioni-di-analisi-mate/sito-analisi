## Exercise 1

A motor delivers $400\,\mathrm J$ of useful work at constant power over $5\,\mathrm s$. What is its useful power?

::grafico{linee="0,0 5,400" punti="5,400,pieno,A" segmenti="5,0,5,400;0,400,5,400" xmin=0 xmax=6.1 ymin=0 ymax=480 nomi="t (s),W (J)" titolo="The slope represents the work done in each second."}

$$
\mathcal P=\frac{400\,\mathrm J}{5\,\mathrm s}=80\,\mathrm W.
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Power is the work transferred per unit time. The graph shows the cumulative work, starting from zero. Since the power is constant, the line is straight and its slope gives the joules produced each second. The product $80\,\mathrm W\cdot5\,\mathrm s$ recovers $400\,\mathrm J$.

Halfway through, the motor has produced half the work, or $200\,\mathrm J$. This check uses constant power. The ratio of work to time would give the same average power even if the power varied.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A crane raises a $250\,\mathrm{kg}$ load vertically at a constant speed of $0.40\,\mathrm{m/s}$. With $g=10\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$, what power does the crane transfer to the load?

$$
\begin{aligned}
F&=mg=2500\,\mathrm N,\\[6pt]
\mathcal P&=2500\cdot0.40\,\mathrm W=1000\,\mathrm W.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The load moves without acceleration, so the upward force from the crane has the same magnitude as the weight. The load moves in the direction of the crane force. The useful power is therefore positive and equals the force times the speed.

As a check, in $5\,\mathrm s$ the load rises by $2\,\mathrm m$. Its gravitational potential energy increases by $5000\,\mathrm J$, so dividing by the time again gives $1000\,\mathrm W$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A device delivers $180\,\mathrm W$ for $3\,\mathrm s$, is switched off for $3\,\mathrm s$ and then delivers $60\,\mathrm W$ for $6\,\mathrm s$. What is its average useful power over the whole cycle?

$$
\begin{aligned}
W&=(180\cdot3+0\cdot3+60\cdot6)\,\mathrm J=900\,\mathrm J,\\[6pt]
\mathcal P_m&=\frac{900}{3+3+6}\,\mathrm W=75\,\mathrm W.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Each phase contributes the product of its power and duration. The pause leaves the cumulative work unchanged and counts towards the cycle length. Add the work from the two active phases and divide by the full $12\,\mathrm s$.

For comparison, considering only the $9\,\mathrm s$ of operation, the same energy would give $100\,\mathrm W$. The whole-cycle average is lower because it includes the time when the device transfers no work.

</div>
