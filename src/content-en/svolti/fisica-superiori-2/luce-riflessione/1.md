## Exercise 1

Light travels in a vacuum for $2\cdot10^{-6}\,\mathrm s$. Use $c=3\cdot10^{8}\,\mathrm{m/s}$. What distance does it cover?

$$
\begin{aligned}
s &= c t \\[6pt]
&= \left(3\cdot10^{8}\right) \left(2\cdot10^{-6}\right) \\[6pt]
&\simeq 600\,\mathrm{m}
\end{aligned}
$$

::grafico{f="300*x" punti="2,600,pieno,A" xmin=0 xmax=2.7 ymin=0 ymax=850 nomi="t (μs),d (m)" titolo="In 2 μs light travels 600 m in a vacuum."}

<div class="spiegazione">

The route is in a vacuum and the speed $c=3\cdot10^{8}\,\mathrm{m/s}$ remains constant. Every microsecond is one millionth of a second. Multiplying $2\cdot10^{-6}\,\mathrm s$ by the speed cancels seconds and gives metres. The factors $10^8$ and $10^{-6}$ give $10^2$, so a distance of several hundred metres is expected.

If the source emitted a second pulse one microsecond after the first, the second would leave when the first had already travelled three hundred metres. The time in the problem is twice that interval, consistent with a constant speed.

</div>

## Exercise 2

In an ideal experiment a flash returns to the source after $8\,\mathrm{\mu s}$ from a target in vacuum. Use $c=3.0\cdot10^8\,\mathrm{m/s}$. What is the target distance?

$$
\begin{aligned}s&=c\Delta t=3.0\cdot10^8\cdot8\cdot10^{-6}\,\mathrm m=2400\,\mathrm m\\d&=\frac{s}{2}=1200\,\mathrm m.\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The timer records both outward and return travel. Multiplying by speed gives the full path. Since the distance is the same in both directions, divide by two. A measurement of just the outward trip would take four microseconds.

The $1200\,\mathrm m$ distance locates the target relative to the detector. After the return, the pulse's total displacement is zero while its path length is $2400\,\mathrm m$. This check distinguishes the quantity measured by the timer from the location being found.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Two pulses in vacuum start together in opposite directions. After $3\,\mathrm{\mu s}$ from launch, how far apart are they if $c=3.0\cdot10^8\,\mathrm{m/s}$?

$$
\begin{aligned}d_1&=3.0\cdot10^8\cdot3\cdot10^{-6}\,\mathrm m=900\,\mathrm m\\s&=d_1+d_1=1800\,\mathrm m.\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

After the same time the displacement magnitudes match. The positions have opposite signs relative to the origin, so their separation is the sum of the magnitudes. Neither individual pulse travels 1800 metres.

With right positive, the positions after the interval are $+900\,\mathrm m$ and $-900\,\mathrm m$. Subtracting the second from the first recovers the positive separation. Reversing the axis would change both signs and leave the distance between the positions unchanged.

</div>
