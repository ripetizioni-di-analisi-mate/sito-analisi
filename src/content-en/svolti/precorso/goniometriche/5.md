## Exercise 1

Solve on $[0, 2\pi)$ the inequality
$$
\sin x > \frac{1}{2} .
$$

The associated equation $\sin x = \dfrac{1}{2}$ has on $[0, 2\pi)$ the solutions
$$
x = \frac{\pi}{6}, \qquad x = \frac{5\pi}{6} .
$$
The points of the unit circle with ordinate greater than $\dfrac{1}{2}$ form the upper arc between the two points found, therefore the solutions are
$$
\frac{\pi}{6} < x < \frac{5\pi}{6} .
$$

Solution: $\dfrac{\pi}{6} < x < \dfrac{5\pi}{6}$.

<div class="spiegazione">

One first solves the **associated equation**, which identifies on the circle the two points with ordinate exactly $\dfrac{1}{2}$: the angles $\dfrac{\pi}{6}$ and its supplement $\dfrac{5\pi}{6}$. These two points divide the circle into two arcs, and the inequality asks for the arc with ordinate greater than $\dfrac{1}{2}$, that is the one containing the **highest point**, of angle $\dfrac{\pi}{2}$. The endpoints are **excluded** because the inequality is strict. The general solution on all of $\mathbb{R}$ would be obtained by adding $2n\pi$ to both endpoints.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Solve on $[0, 2\pi)$ the inequality
$$
\cos x \leq \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} .
$$

The associated equation $\cos x = \dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$ has on $[0, 2\pi)$ the solutions
$$
x = \frac{\pi}{4}, \qquad x = \frac{7\pi}{4} .
$$
The points of the circle with abscissa less than or equal to $\dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$ form the arc containing the point of angle $\pi$, therefore the solutions are
$$
\frac{\pi}{4} \leq x \leq \frac{7\pi}{4} .
$$

Solution: $\dfrac{\pi}{4} \leq x \leq \dfrac{7\pi}{4}$.

<div class="spiegazione">

For the cosine one looks at the **abscissa** of the points of the circle. The associated equation identifies the two points with abscissa $\dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$, at the angles $\dfrac{\pi}{4}$ and $-\dfrac{\pi}{4}$; on $[0, 2\pi)$ the second is written $\dfrac{7\pi}{4}$. The required arc is the one to the left of the vertical line with abscissa $\dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$, that is the arc passing through the point of angle $\pi$, where the cosine equals $-1$. The endpoints are **included** because the inequality is **non-strict**.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Solve on $[0, 2\pi)$ the inequality
$$
2\sin x + \sqrt{3} < 0 .
$$

Isolate the sine:
$$
\sin x < -\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} .
$$
The associated equation $\sin x = -\dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$ has on $[0, 2\pi)$ the solutions
$$
x = \pi + \frac{\pi}{3} = \frac{4\pi}{3},
\qquad
x = 2\pi - \frac{\pi}{3} = \frac{5\pi}{3} .
$$
The points of the circle with ordinate less than $-\dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$ form the lower arc between the two points found, therefore the solutions are
$$
\frac{4\pi}{3} < x < \frac{5\pi}{3} .
$$

Solution: $\dfrac{4\pi}{3} < x < \dfrac{5\pi}{3}$.

<div class="spiegazione">

The inequality is *not* yet elementary, so one first **isolates the sine** by moving $\sqrt{3}$ to the right-hand side and dividing by $2$. The angles with sine $-\dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$ are found with **related angles** starting from $\sin\dfrac{\pi}{3} = \dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$: the sine is negative in the third and fourth quadrants, at the angles $\pi + \dfrac{\pi}{3}$ and $2\pi - \dfrac{\pi}{3}$. The inequality asks for ordinate less than $-\dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$, therefore the **lowest arc** of the circle, the one containing the point of angle $\dfrac{3\pi}{2}$, where the sine equals $-1$. The endpoints are excluded because the inequality is strict.

</div>
