## Exercise 1

Verify with the truth table that the proposition
$$
\bigl( (p \Rightarrow q) \land p \bigr) \Rightarrow q
$$
is true for every choice of the truth values of $p$ and $q$.

$$
\begin{array}{c|c|c|c|c}
p & q & p \Rightarrow q & (p \Rightarrow q) \land p & \bigl( (p \Rightarrow q) \land p \bigr) \Rightarrow q \\
\hline
\text{T} & \text{T} & \text{T} & \text{T} & \text{T} \\
\text{T} & \text{F} & \text{F} & \text{F} & \text{T} \\
\text{F} & \text{T} & \text{T} & \text{F} & \text{T} \\
\text{F} & \text{F} & \text{T} & \text{F} & \text{T}
\end{array}
$$

The last column contains only T, therefore the proposition is **always true**.

<div class="spiegazione">

There are two variables, therefore the combinations of truth values are $2 \cdot 2 = 4$ and the table has four rows. The columns are filled from the inside to the outside of the formula.

The column of $p \Rightarrow q$ follows the table of the **implication**, which is false only when the hypothesis is true and the conclusion is false; hence it has F in the second row and T in the others. The conjunction $(p \Rightarrow q) \land p$ is true when *both* parts are true, that is, only in the first row, because in the second the implication is false and in the last two $p$ is false.

The last column is the implication whose hypothesis is the column just computed and whose conclusion is $q$. In the first row hypothesis and conclusion are true, therefore the implication is true; in the other three rows the hypothesis is false, therefore the implication is true whatever $q$ is. A proposition that is true in every row of its table is called a **tautology**.

This tautology is the rule by which a theorem is used: if the implication $p \Rightarrow q$ is true and the hypothesis $p$ is satisfied, then the conclusion $q$ is true.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Determine with the truth table whether the propositions $p \Rightarrow q$ and $\lnot p \Rightarrow \lnot q$ are equivalent. If they are not, find an example with $p$: "$x > 2$" and $q$: "$x^2 > 4$" in which they have different truth values.

$$
\begin{array}{c|c|c|c|c|c}
p & q & p \Rightarrow q & \lnot p & \lnot q & \lnot p \Rightarrow \lnot q \\
\hline
\text{T} & \text{T} & \text{T} & \text{F} & \text{F} & \text{T} \\
\text{T} & \text{F} & \text{F} & \text{F} & \text{T} & \text{T} \\
\text{F} & \text{T} & \text{T} & \text{T} & \text{F} & \text{F} \\
\text{F} & \text{F} & \text{T} & \text{T} & \text{T} & \text{T}
\end{array}
$$

$$
x = -3: \qquad x > 2 \ \text{ false}, \qquad x^2 = 9 > 4 \ \text{ true}
$$

The two propositions **are not equivalent**.

<div class="spiegazione">

The column of $\lnot p \Rightarrow \lnot q$ is computed with the table of the implication, taking $\lnot p$ as hypothesis and $\lnot q$ as conclusion. The implication is false only when $\lnot p$ is true and $\lnot q$ is false, that is, when $p$ is false and $q$ is true; this happens in the third row.

The columns of $p \Rightarrow q$ and of $\lnot p \Rightarrow \lnot q$ differ in the second and in the third row, therefore the two propositions have different truth tables and **are not equivalent**.

The required example must lie in the third row, with $p$ false and $q$ true. We therefore need a number with $x \leq 2$ and $x^2 > 4$, and $x = -3$ has these properties, because $-3 \leq 2$ and $(-3)^2 = 9 > 4$. For $x = -3$ the implication "if $x > 2$ then $x^2 > 4$" is true, because the hypothesis is false, while "if $x \leq 2$ then $x^2 \leq 4$" is false, because the hypothesis is true and the conclusion is false.

The proposition $\lnot p \Rightarrow \lnot q$ is the contrapositive of the converse $q \Rightarrow p$, hence it has the same table as the converse.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Verify with the truth table the equivalence
$$
\lnot (p \Leftrightarrow q) \equiv (p \land \lnot q) \lor (\lnot p \land q) .
$$

$$
\begin{array}{c|c|c|c|c|c|c}
p & q & p \Leftrightarrow q & \lnot (p \Leftrightarrow q) & p \land \lnot q & \lnot p \land q & (p \land \lnot q) \lor (\lnot p \land q) \\
\hline
\text{T} & \text{T} & \text{T} & \text{F} & \text{F} & \text{F} & \text{F} \\
\text{T} & \text{F} & \text{F} & \text{T} & \text{T} & \text{F} & \text{T} \\
\text{F} & \text{T} & \text{F} & \text{T} & \text{F} & \text{T} & \text{T} \\
\text{F} & \text{F} & \text{T} & \text{F} & \text{F} & \text{F} & \text{F}
\end{array}
$$

::venn{zone="a,b" nomi="P,Q" universo=1 titolo="$\lnot (p \Leftrightarrow q)$ is true where exactly one of the two propositions holds: in the part of $P$ outside $Q$ and in the part of $Q$ outside $P$"}

The columns of $\lnot (p \Leftrightarrow q)$ and of the last proposition coincide, therefore the equivalence **holds**.

<div class="spiegazione">

The proposition $p \Leftrightarrow q$ is the conjunction of $p \Rightarrow q$ and of $q \Rightarrow p$. The first implication is false in the row with $p$ true and $q$ false, the second in the row with $p$ false and $q$ true; hence $p \Leftrightarrow q$ is true exactly when $p$ and $q$ have the *same* truth value, that is, in the first and in the last row. Its negation is true in the two middle rows.

The conjunction $p \land \lnot q$ is true only when $p$ is true and $q$ is false, that is, in the second row; the conjunction $\lnot p \land q$ is true only in the third row. Their disjunction is true when at least one of the two is true, therefore in the second and in the third row. The two columns to be compared coincide in all four rows, hence the propositions are **equivalent**.

The proposition $(p \land \lnot q) \lor (\lnot p \land q)$ is the **exclusive or**: it is true when exactly one of the two propositions is true. In the diagram it corresponds to the two colored regions, which exclude the common part $P \cap Q$.

</div>
