## Exercise 1

Prove that the sum of a rational number and an irrational number is irrational.

By contradiction, let $r$ be rational, $s$ irrational and $r + s = q$ with $q$ rational.

$$
r + s = q \quad\Longrightarrow\quad s = q - r
$$

$$
q = \frac{a}{b}, \quad r = \frac{c}{d}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
s = \frac{a}{b} - \frac{c}{d} = \frac{ad - bc}{bd}
$$

The number $s$ turns out to be rational, contradicting the hypothesis. Therefore $r + s$ is irrational.

<div class="spiegazione">

The conclusion states that the sum is irrational, therefore the proof by contradiction starts from its negation: the sum $r + s$ is a rational number $q$. Subtracting $r$ from both sides isolates $s = q - r$.

The numbers $q$ and $r$ are rational by assumption and by hypothesis, therefore they can be written as fractions $\dfrac{a}{b}$ and $\dfrac{c}{d}$ with $a$, $b$, $c$, $d$ integers and $b, d \neq 0$. The difference is computed with the common denominator $bd$: the numerator $ad - bc$ is an integer and the denominator $bd$ is a nonzero integer, hence $s$ is a quotient of integers, that is, a rational number. This contradicts the hypothesis that $s$ is irrational, therefore the assumption was false and the sum $r + s$ is irrational.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Prove that there is no positive rational number smaller than all the other positive rational numbers.

By contradiction, let $q$ be rational, $q > 0$, less than or equal to every positive rational.

$$
q > 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{q}{2} > 0
$$

$$
\frac{q}{2} < q \qquad \text{because} \qquad q - \frac{q}{2} = \frac{q}{2} > 0
$$

The number $\dfrac{q}{2}$ is rational, positive and less than $q$, contradicting the minimality of $q$. Therefore such a $q$ does not exist.

<div class="spiegazione">

The conclusion negates an existence, therefore by contradiction one assumes the existence: a positive rational $q$ less than or equal to every other positive rational. The strategy is to exhibit an even smaller positive rational.

The candidate is half of $q$. The number $\dfrac{q}{2}$ is rational, because it is the quotient of the rational $q$ by the integer $2$, and it is positive, because it is half of a positive number. Moreover the difference $q - \dfrac{q}{2}$ equals $\dfrac{q}{2}$, which is positive, therefore $\dfrac{q}{2} < q$. The number $\dfrac{q}{2}$ is thus a positive rational less than $q$, and this contradicts the assumption that $q$ was the minimum. The assumption is therefore false, hence the minimum positive rational does not exist.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Prove that $\sqrt{3}$ is irrational.

By contradiction, let $\sqrt{3} = \dfrac{m}{n}$ with $m$, $n$ positive integers with no common factors.

$$
\sqrt{3} = \frac{m}{n} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad 3 = \frac{m^2}{n^2} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad m^2 = 3n^2
$$

If $m$ has nonzero remainder in the division by $3$:
$$
(3k + 1)^2 = 3(3k^2 + 2k) + 1, \qquad (3k + 2)^2 = 3(3k^2 + 4k + 1) + 1
$$

Therefore $m = 3h$ with $h$ an integer:
$$
9h^2 = 3n^2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad n^2 = 3h^2
$$

$n$ is also a multiple of $3$: contradiction, because $m$ and $n$ would have the common factor $3$. Therefore $\sqrt{3}$ is irrational.

<div class="spiegazione">

The assumption for contradiction is that $\sqrt{3}$ is rational; the fraction can always be reduced to lowest terms, therefore $m$ and $n$ are chosen with no common factors. Squaring both sides and multiplying by $n^2$ gives $m^2 = 3n^2$, hence $m^2$ is a multiple of $3$.

From this one deduces that $m$ itself is a multiple of $3$. If $m$ had remainder $1$ or remainder $2$ in the division by $3$, that is, $m = 3k + 1$ or $m = 3k + 2$, expanding the square would show that $m^2$ has remainder $1$ in both cases, while $m^2$ is a multiple of $3$; therefore the remainder of $m$ is zero and $m = 3h$.

Substituting $m = 3h$ into the equality $m^2 = 3n^2$ gives $9h^2 = 3n^2$ and, dividing by $3$, $n^2 = 3h^2$. The same argument applied to $n$ shows that $n$ is also a multiple of $3$. Then $m$ and $n$ have the common factor $3$, whereas the fraction had been reduced to lowest terms: the assumption leads to a contradiction, hence $\sqrt{3}$ is irrational.

</div>
