## Exercise 1

Solve the equation
$$
4^{x} - 5 \cdot 2^{x} + 4 = 0 .
$$

Setting $t = 2^{x}$, with $t > 0$, since $4^{x} = (2^{x})^{2} = t^{2}$:
$$
t^{2} - 5t + 4 = 0 \iff t = 1 \ \text{or} \ t = 4
$$
$$
2^{x} = 1 = 2^{0} \iff x = 0, \qquad 2^{x} = 4 = 2^{2} \iff x = 2
$$

Solutions: $x = 0$ and $x = 2$.

<div class="spiegazione">

The equation contains $4^{x} = (2^{x})^{2}$ and $2^{x}$, so the **substitution** $t = 2^{x}$ transforms it into a second-degree equation in $t$, with the condition $t > 0$ because a power with a positive base is positive. The solutions $t = 1$ and $t = 4$ are both positive, therefore *both* are **acceptable**. Each one gives an equation with the same base: from $2^{x} = 2^{0}$ it follows that $x = 0$ and from $2^{x} = 2^{2}$ it follows that $x = 2$, by the **equality of the exponents**.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Solve the equation
$$
2^{2x} - 2^{x+1} - 8 = 0 .
$$

Since $2^{2x} = (2^{x})^{2}$ and $2^{x+1} = 2 \cdot 2^{x}$, setting $t = 2^{x}$, with $t > 0$:
$$
t^{2} - 2t - 8 = 0 \iff t = 4 \ \text{or} \ t = -2
$$
The solution $t = -2$ must be **discarded**, because $t > 0$.
$$
2^{x} = 4 = 2^{2} \iff x = 2
$$

Solution: $x = 2$.

<div class="spiegazione">

The term $2^{x+1}$ is rewritten as $2 \cdot 2^{x}$ with the **product rule** for powers, so the whole equation is expressed in terms of $2^{x}$ and the substitution $t = 2^{x}$ gives a second-degree equation. Of the two solutions, $t = -2$ is negative and therefore must be **discarded**, because $2^{x} > 0$ for *every* $x$. What remains is $2^{x} = 4$, which by the **equality of powers with the same base** gives $x = 2$. The check confirms the result: for $x = 2$ we obtain $16 - 8 - 8 = 0$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Solve the equation
$$
3^{x} + 3^{1-x} = 4 .
$$

Since $3^{1-x} = \dfrac{3}{3^{x}}$, setting $t = 3^{x}$, with $t > 0$:
$$
t + \frac{3}{t} = 4 \iff t^{2} - 4t + 3 = 0 \iff t = 1 \ \text{or} \ t = 3
$$
$$
3^{x} = 1 = 3^{0} \iff x = 0, \qquad 3^{x} = 3 \iff x = 1
$$

Solutions: $x = 0$ and $x = 1$.

<div class="spiegazione">

The term $3^{1-x}$ is rewritten as $\dfrac{3}{3^{x}}$ with the **quotient rule** for powers, so the substitution $t = 3^{x}$ gives the equation $t + \dfrac{3}{t} = 4$. Since $t > 0$, multiplying both sides by $t$ is legitimate and introduces *no* extraneous solutions; this gives the second-degree equation $t^{2} - 4t + 3 = 0$, with solutions $t = 1$ and $t = 3$, both positive and therefore **acceptable**. The two equations with the same base give $x = 0$ and $x = 1$; the check confirms them, because $1 + 3 = 4$ and $3 + 1 = 4$.

</div>
