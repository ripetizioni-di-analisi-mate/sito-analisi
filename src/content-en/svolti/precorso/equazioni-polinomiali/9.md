## Exercise 1

Solve the equation
$$
x^6 - 7x^3 - 8 = 0 .
$$

$$
t = x^3: \qquad t^2 - 7t - 8 = 0
$$

$$
\Delta = 49 + 32 = 81, \qquad t = \frac{7 \pm 9}{2}, \qquad t_1 = 8, \quad t_2 = -1
$$

$$
x^3 = 8 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad x = 2; \qquad x^3 = -1 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad x = -1
$$

$$
x^6 - 7x^3 - 8 = (x - 2)\left( x^2 + 2x + 4 \right)(x + 1)\left( x^2 - x + 1 \right)
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The equation contains only the degrees $6$, $3$ and $0$, and $x^6 = \left( x^3 \right)^2$, so it is a **trinomial** equation. With the **substitution** $t = x^3$ it becomes a quadratic equation in $t$, with discriminant $81$ and solutions $t_1 = \dfrac{7 + 9}{2} = 8$ and $t_2 = \dfrac{7 - 9}{2} = -1$.

To return to the variable $x$ we solve $x^3 = t$ for each value. A cube takes *every* real value, negative ones included, exactly once, so each equation has exactly one real solution, $x = \sqrt[3]{t}$. From $x^3 = 8$ it follows that $x = 2$, and from $x^3 = -1$ it follows that $x = -1$. In biquadratic equations, instead, a negative value of $t$ gives no solution, because $x^2$ is never negative.

The equation has degree $6$ and only **two real solutions**, $2$ and $-1$. Indeed, by the difference and the sum of cubes, $x^3 - 8 = (x - 2)\left( x^2 + 2x + 4 \right)$ and $x^3 + 1 = (x + 1)\left( x^2 - x + 1 \right)$; the two quadratic factors $x^2 + 2x + 4$ and $x^2 - x + 1$ have discriminants $-12$ and $-3$, both negative, therefore they do not vanish for any real $x$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Solve the equation
$$
\left( x^2 - 2x \right)^2 - 2\left( x^2 - 2x \right) - 3 = 0 .
$$

$$
t = x^2 - 2x: \qquad t^2 - 2t - 3 = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad (t - 3)(t + 1) = 0
$$

$$
t = 3: \qquad x^2 - 2x - 3 = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad (x - 3)(x + 1) = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad x = 3, \ x = -1
$$

$$
t = -1: \qquad x^2 - 2x + 1 = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad (x - 1)^2 = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad x = 1 \ \text{(double)}
$$

::grafico{f="(x^2-2*x)^2-2*(x^2-2*x)-3" xmin=-1.8 xmax=3.8 ymin=-5 ymax=6 punti="-1,0,pieno;1,0,pieno;3,0,pieno" titolo="$y = \left( x^2 - 2x \right)^2 - 2\left( x^2 - 2x \right) - 3$ crosses the $x$-axis at $-1$ and at $3$ and touches it at the double solution $1$"}

<div class="spiegazione">

The expression $x^2 - 2x$ appears twice, once squared and once to the first degree. The **substitution** $t = x^2 - 2x$ turns the equation, which expanded would be of the fourth degree, into the quadratic equation $t^2 - 2t - 3 = 0$. Two numbers with sum $2$ and product $-3$ are $3$ and $-1$, so the trinomial in $t$ factors as $(t - 3)(t + 1)$.

We return to the variable $x$ by setting $x^2 - 2x$ equal to each value of $t$. The equation $x^2 - 2x = 3$ becomes $x^2 - 2x - 3 = 0$, with roots $3$ and $-1$. The equation $x^2 - 2x = -1$ becomes $x^2 - 2x + 1 = 0$, which is the square of the binomial $x - 1$, so it has only the **double solution** $x = 1$.

The solutions are therefore $-1$, $1$ and $3$. The original polynomial has degree $4$ and is written $(x - 3)(x + 1)(x - 1)^2$, so the solutions counted with their multiplicity are four.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Solve the equation
$$
\left( x + \frac{1}{x} \right)^2 - 5\left( x + \frac{1}{x} \right) + 6 = 0 .
$$

$$
x \neq 0
$$

$$
t = x + \frac{1}{x}: \qquad t^2 - 5t + 6 = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad t = 2, \ t = 3
$$

$$
x + \frac{1}{x} = 2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad x^2 - 2x + 1 = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad x = 1
$$

$$
x + \frac{1}{x} = 3 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad x^2 - 3x + 1 = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad x = \frac{3 \pm \sqrt{5}}{2}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The unknown appears in the denominator, so the equation is **rational** and the **domain restriction** is $x \neq 0$. The expression $x + \dfrac{1}{x}$ appears squared and to the first degree, therefore the substitution $t = x + \dfrac{1}{x}$ gives the equation $t^2 - 5t + 6 = 0$, with solutions $2$ and $3$, which have sum $5$ and product $6$.

We return to the variable $x$. In the domain $x \neq 0$, so multiplying both sides of $x + \dfrac{1}{x} = 2$ by $x$ produces an equivalent equation, $x^2 + 1 = 2x$, that is, $(x - 1)^2 = 0$, with the solution $x = 1$. In the same way $x + \dfrac{1}{x} = 3$ becomes $x^2 - 3x + 1 = 0$, which has discriminant $9 - 4 = 5 > 0$ and solutions $\dfrac{3 \pm \sqrt{5}}{2}$.

The three values found are nonzero, so they satisfy the domain restriction. The solutions of the equation are therefore $1$, $\dfrac{3 - \sqrt{5}}{2}$ and $\dfrac{3 + \sqrt{5}}{2}$.

</div>
