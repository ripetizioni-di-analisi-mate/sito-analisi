## Exercise 1

Solve the equation
$$
x^3 - 4x = 0 .
$$

$$
x^3 - 4x = x\left( x^2 - 4 \right) = x\,(x - 2)(x + 2) = 0
$$

$$
x = 0, \qquad x = 2, \qquad x = -2
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

All terms contain the factor $x$, so the first step is **factoring out the common factor**. The factor $x^2 - 4$ is a **difference of squares**, therefore it factors as $(x - 2)(x + 2)$ and the left-hand side becomes a product of three first-degree factors.

By the **zero product property**, the product is zero when *at least one* factor is zero, so the solutions are $0$, $2$ and $-2$. The equation has degree $3$ and three solutions were found, therefore they are *all* of them.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Solve the equation
$$
x^3 + 3x^2 - 4 = 0 .
$$

Setting $P(x) = x^3 + 3x^2 - 4$, we have $P(1) = 1 + 3 - 4 = 0$.

$$
\begin{array}{c|ccc|c}
 & 1 & 3 & 0 & -4 \\
1 &  & 1 & 4 & 4 \\ \hline
 & 1 & 4 & 4 & 0
\end{array}
$$

$$
(x - 1)\left( x^2 + 4x + 4 \right) = (x - 1)(x + 2)^2 = 0
$$

$$
x = 1, \qquad x = -2 \ \text{(double)}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The polynomial has leading coefficient $1$, so any rational roots are integers and divide the constant term $-4$. The candidate $1$ is a root, since $P(1) = 0$, therefore by **Ruffini's theorem** $x - 1$ divides $P$. In Ruffini's scheme the term of degree $1$ is missing, so its coefficient is $0$.

The quotient $x^2 + 4x + 4$ contains the squares of $x$ and of $2$ and twice their product $4x$, so it is the **square of the binomial** $x + 2$. By the zero product property the solutions are $1$ and $-2$; the solution $-2$ is **double**, because the factor $x + 2$ appears squared.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Solve the biquadratic equation
$$
x^4 + 3x^2 - 4 = 0 .
$$

With the substitution $t = x^2$:

$$
t^2 + 3t - 4 = 0, \qquad \Delta = 9 + 16 = 25, \qquad t = \frac{-3 \pm 5}{2}
$$

$$
t_1 = 1, \qquad t_2 = -4
$$

$$
x^2 = 1 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad x = \pm 1; \qquad x^2 = -4 \ \text{ no real solutions}
$$

The solutions are $x = 1$ and $x = -1$.

<div class="spiegazione">

The equation contains only the degrees $4$, $2$ and $0$, so it is **biquadratic** and the **substitution** $t = x^2$ turns it into a second-degree equation in $t$. The discriminant equals $25$ and the solutions are $t_1 = \dfrac{-3 + 5}{2} = 1$ and $t_2 = \dfrac{-3 - 5}{2} = -4$.

One returns to the variable $x$ by solving $x^2 = t$ for each value. From $x^2 = 1$ the solutions $\pm 1$ follow. The equation $x^2 = -4$ has no real solutions, because a square is **never negative**, so the value $t_2 = -4$ produces no solutions and the original equation has *exactly* the two solutions $\pm 1$.

</div>
