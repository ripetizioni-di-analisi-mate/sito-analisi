## Exercise 1

Solve the equation
$$
\sqrt[3]{x^2 - 2} = -1 .
$$

The cube root requires no conditions, so both sides are cubed:
$$
x^2 - 2 = (-1)^3 = -1
$$
$$
x^2 = 1
$$
$$
x = -1 \ \vee \ x = 1
$$

The solutions are $x = -1$ and $x = 1$.

<div class="spiegazione">

The root has odd index, so it is defined for every radicand and can equal $-1$: the case of a negative right-hand side, which for the square root ends the exercise with no solutions, creates no obstacle here. Cubing preserves the equality in both directions, because the cube function is increasing on all of $\mathbb{R}$, and gives $x^2 = 1$. Checking by substitution confirms the result: for $x = \pm 1$ the radicand equals $1 - 2 = -1$ and $\sqrt[3]{-1} = -1$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Solve the inequality
$$
\sqrt[3]{5 - x} \geq 2 .
$$

Cubing preserves the direction:
$$
5 - x \geq 2^3 = 8
$$
$$
-x \geq 3
$$
$$
x \leq -3
$$

The solution is $x \leq -3$.

<div class="spiegazione">

For roots of odd index neither domain conditions nor cases on the sign of the two sides are needed: since the cube function is increasing, the inequality between the root and a constant is equivalent to the inequality between the radicand and the cube of the constant. In the last step both sides are multiplied by $-1$, so the direction of the inequality is reversed. Checking with a test value confirms the result: for $x = -3$ we have $\sqrt[3]{8} = 2 \geq 2$, while for $x = 0$ we have $\sqrt[3]{5} < 2$, since $5 < 8$, and indeed $0$ does not belong to the solution.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Solve the equation
$$
\sqrt{x} = \sqrt[3]{x} .
$$

Domain condition: $x \geq 0$, imposed by the square root. Where $x \geq 0$ both sides are nonnegative, so raising to the sixth power preserves the equality:
$$
\left( \sqrt{x} \right)^6 = \left( \sqrt[3]{x} \right)^6
$$
$$
x^3 = x^2
$$
$$
x^2 (x - 1) = 0
$$
$$
x = 0 \ \vee \ x = 1
$$

Both values satisfy the condition $x \geq 0$. The solutions are $x = 0$ and $x = 1$.

<div class="spiegazione">

The indices of the two roots are different, so both sides are raised to a power that is a multiple of both: the sixth, which is the least common multiple of $2$ and $3$. Raising to this power is legitimate because, on the domain $x \geq 0$, the square root is nonnegative by definition and the cube root of a nonnegative number is nonnegative. The sixth power of the square root is $x^3$ and that of the cube root is $x^2$; moving everything to one side and factoring out $x^2$ gives the roots $0$ and $1$. Checking by substitution confirms the result: for $x = 0$ both sides equal $0$, and for $x = 1$ both equal $1$.

</div>
