## Exercise 1

Determine the points $A$ and $B$ where the line $y = x + 1$ intersects the parabola $y = x^2 - 2x - 3$, and compute the area of the triangle $ABV$, where $V$ is the vertex of the parabola.

$$
\begin{cases}
y = x^2 - 2x - 3 \\
y = x + 1
\end{cases}
\qquad
x^2 - 2x - 3 = x + 1
$$
$$
x^2 - 3x - 4 = 0, \qquad x = \frac{3 \pm \sqrt{9 + 16}}{2} = \frac{3 \pm 5}{2}
$$
$$
A(-1, 0), \qquad B(4, 5)
$$
$$
x_V = -\frac{-2}{2} = 1, \qquad y_V = 1 - 2 - 3 = -4, \qquad V(1, -4)
$$
$$
\overline{AB} = \sqrt{(4 + 1)^2 + (5 - 0)^2} = \sqrt{50} = 5\sqrt{2}
$$
$$
x - y + 1 = 0, \qquad d(V, AB) = \frac{|1 - (-4) + 1|}{\sqrt{2}} = \frac{6}{\sqrt{2}} = 3\sqrt{2}
$$
$$
\text{Area} = \frac{1}{2} \cdot 5\sqrt{2} \cdot 3\sqrt{2} = \frac{1}{2} \cdot 30 = 15
$$

::grafico{f="x^2-2*x-3;x+1" legenda="y = x^2 - 2x - 3;y = x + 1" xmin=-3 xmax=6 ymin=-5 ymax=8 punti="-1,0,pieno,A;4,5,pieno,B;1,-4,pieno,V" segmenti="-1,0,1,-4;4,5,1,-4" titolo="The triangle $ABV$ has base $AB = 5\sqrt{2}$ on the line $y = x + 1$ and height $3\sqrt{2}$, the distance of $V(1, -4)$ from the line"}

<div class="spiegazione">

The points common to the line and the parabola have coordinates that satisfy the two equations together. The two expressions of $y$ must therefore coincide, and equating them gives a quadratic equation in $x$ with discriminant $25 > 0$; therefore the line intersects the parabola at two points. The solutions $x = -1$ and $x = 4$, substituted into the equation of the line, give the ordinates $0$ and $5$.

The vertex has abscissa $x_V = -\dfrac{b}{2a} = 1$, and substituting $x = 1$ into the equation of the parabola gives the ordinate $y_V = 1 - 2 - 3 = -4$.

The area of a triangle is half the product of a base and the corresponding height. We choose as base the side $AB$, whose length is computed with the formula for the distance between two points. The height relative to $AB$ is the distance of the opposite vertex $V$ from the line $AB$, which is the line $y = x + 1$; in general form this line is written $x - y + 1 = 0$, and the **point-line distance formula** gives $3\sqrt{2}$.

The product $5\sqrt{2} \cdot 3\sqrt{2}$ equals $15 \cdot 2 = 30$, because $\sqrt{2} \cdot \sqrt{2} = 2$.

The points of intersection are $A(-1, 0)$ and $B(4, 5)$, and the triangle $ABV$ has **area** $15$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

As the real parameter $q$ varies, determine how many points the line $y = 2x + q$ and the parabola $y = x^2$ have in common. Find the tangent line and the point of tangency.

$$
x^2 = 2x + q, \qquad x^2 - 2x - q = 0
$$
$$
\frac{\Delta}{4} = 1 + q
$$
$$
\frac{\Delta}{4} > 0 \iff q > -1, \qquad \frac{\Delta}{4} = 0 \iff q = -1, \qquad \frac{\Delta}{4} < 0 \iff q < -1
$$
$$
q = -1: \quad x^2 - 2x + 1 = 0, \quad (x - 1)^2 = 0, \quad x = 1, \quad y = 1
$$

::grafico{f="x^2;2*x-1" legenda="y = x^2;y = 2x - 1" xmin=-2 xmax=3.5 ymin=-2 ymax=6 punti="1,1,pieno,T" titolo="The line $y = 2x - 1$ is tangent to the parabola $y = x^2$ at the point $T(1, 1)$"}

<div class="spiegazione">

The abscissas of the common points are the solutions of the equation obtained by equating the two expressions of $y$. The number of common points therefore coincides with the number of real solutions of the equation $x^2 - 2x - q = 0$, which depends on the sign of the discriminant.

The coefficient of $x$ is even, therefore we use the reduced discriminant $\dfrac{\Delta}{4} = \left( \dfrac{b}{2} \right)^2 - ac = 1 - (-q) = 1 + q$. Consequently the line is **secant** at two points for $q > -1$, is **tangent** for $q = -1$ and has no points in common with the parabola for $q < -1$.

For a parabola, tangency is recognized by a zero discriminant. The criterion of the distance from the center holds only for the circle and cannot be applied here.

For $q = -1$ the equation is $x^2 - 2x + 1 = 0$, that is, the square $(x - 1)^2 = 0$, with the single solution $x = 1$ counted twice. The ordinate of the point of tangency is obtained from $y = x^2$, so $y = 1$.

The line $y = 2x - 1$ is **tangent** to the parabola $y = x^2$ at the point $T(1, 1)$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Determine the points common to the circle $x^2 + y^2 = 2$ and the parabola $y = x^2$.

$$
\begin{cases}
x^2 + y^2 = 2 \\
y = x^2
\end{cases}
\qquad
y + y^2 = 2
$$
$$
y^2 + y - 2 = 0, \qquad (y + 2)(y - 1) = 0, \qquad y = -2 \ \text{or} \ y = 1
$$
$$
y = -2: \quad x^2 = -2 \quad \text{no real solution}
$$
$$
y = 1: \quad x^2 = 1, \quad x = \pm 1
$$
$$
(-1, 1), \qquad (1, 1)
$$

::grafico{curve="sqrt(2)*cos(t),sqrt(2)*sin(t),0,6.29" f="x^2" legenda="y = x^2;x^2 + y^2 = 2" uguale=1 xmin=-3 xmax=3 ymin=-2 ymax=3 punti="-1,1,pieno;1,1,pieno" etichette="-1.9,1.3,(−1, 1);1.15,1.3,(1, 1)" titolo="The circle $x^2 + y^2 = 2$ and the parabola $y = x^2$ have in common the points $(-1, 1)$ and $(1, 1)$"}

<div class="spiegazione">

The common points satisfy the two equations together. The first equation contains $x^2$, and by the second $x^2 = y$, so we substitute $y$ for $x^2$. We obtain a quadratic equation in $y$ alone, simpler than the fourth-degree equation $x^2 + x^4 = 2$ that would result from substituting $y = x^2$.

The equation $y^2 + y - 2 = 0$ factors as $(y + 2)(y - 1) = 0$, because $-2$ and $1$ have sum $-1$ and product $-2$; by the zero product property the solutions are $y = -2$ and $y = 1$.

Each value of $y$ must be substituted back into the relation $y = x^2$ to find the abscissas. The value $y = -2$ leads to $x^2 = -2$, which has no real solutions because a square is greater than or equal to zero; hence $y = -2$ corresponds to no common point, even though it is a solution of the equation in $y$. The points of the parabola all have $y \geq 0$, while this constraint does not appear in the equation $y + y^2 = 2$, which therefore also admits a negative solution. The value $y = 1$ instead gives $x^2 = 1$, that is, $x = 1$ or $x = -1$.

The two curves have **two points** in common, $(-1, 1)$ and $(1, 1)$, symmetric with respect to the $y$-axis like the two curves.

</div>
