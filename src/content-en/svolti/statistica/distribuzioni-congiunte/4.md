## Exercise 1

Two readings $X$ and $Y$ satisfy $\operatorname{Var}(X)=9$, $\operatorname{Var}(Y)=16$, and $\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)=6$. Find the variances of $X+Y$ and $X-Y$.

$$
\operatorname{Var}(aX+bY)=a^2\operatorname{Var}(X)+b^2\operatorname{Var}(Y)+2ab\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{Var}(X+Y)&=1^2\cdot9+1^2\cdot16+2\cdot1\cdot1\cdot6\\[6pt]
&=9+16+2\cdot6=37,\\[6pt]
\operatorname{Var}(X-Y)&=1^2\cdot9+(-1)^2\cdot16+2\cdot1\cdot(-1)\cdot6\\[6pt]
&=9+16-2\cdot6=13,\\[6pt]
\operatorname{Corr}(X,Y)&=\frac{6}{\sqrt9\cdot\sqrt{16}}=\frac6{12}=\frac12.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The given variances are finite, so both readings have finite second moments and the formula for the **variance of a linear combination** applies. Use $a=b=1$ for the sum, and $a=1$ and $b=-1$ for the difference.

In both expressions the squared coefficients are the same, so the individual variance contributions have the same sum $25$. The product of the coefficients is positive for the sum and negative for the difference. The cross term has magnitude $12$ in both cases and changes sign. If the readings were uncorrelated, both variances would equal $25$.

Positive covariance means the readings tend to move in the same direction, making the difference less variable than the sum. The correlation $\dfrac12$ indicates a positive linear association of moderate strength. The sum thus has variance $37$ and the difference variance $13$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Two instruments estimate the same quantity with readings $X$ and $Y$ of equal mean. Suppose $\operatorname{Var}(X)=4$, $\operatorname{Var}(Y)=9$, and $\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)=1$. Find the weight $w\in[0,1]$ minimizing the variance of the estimate $wX+(1-w)Y$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
V(w)&=4w^2+9(1-w)^2+2w(1-w)\\[6pt]
&=4w^2+9-18w+9w^2+2w-2w^2\\[6pt]
&=11w^2-16w+9,\\[6pt]
V'(w)&=22w-16=0
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad w=\frac{16}{22},\\[6pt]
w_*&=\frac8{11},\\[6pt]
V(w_*)&=11\cdot\frac{64}{121}-16\cdot\frac8{11}+9\\[6pt]
&=\frac{64}{11}-\frac{128}{11}+\frac{99}{11}=\frac{35}{11}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The endpoint values are the variances of the individual instruments."}

<div class="spiegazione">

The coefficients sum to one and the means coincide, so every choice of $w$ preserves the common mean. The variance changes because both squared weights and their product change. Apply the variance formula for a linear combination with $a=w$ and $b=1-w$. The cross term is $2w(1-w)\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)$, which reduces to $2w(1-w)$ since the covariance is $1$.

Expanding $(1-w)^2=1-2w+w^2$ and collecting like powers gives the polynomial $11w^2-16w+9$. Its graph is a parabola with positive quadratic coefficient, so the point where the derivative vanishes is the minimum. The resulting weight lies in the permitted interval.

Give the three terms the common denominator $11$ to compute the minimum. The result $\dfrac{35}{11}$ is about $3.18$, below both individual instrument variances $V(1)=4$ and $V(0)=9$. The instrument with variance $4$ receives greater weight. The optimal weight is $w_*=\dfrac8{11}$ and the combined estimate has variance $\dfrac{35}{11}$.

</div>
