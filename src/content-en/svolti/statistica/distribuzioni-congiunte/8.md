## Exercise 1

Let $X$ and $Y$ be independent and uniform on $(0,1)$. Determine the joint density of $U=2X$ and $V=3Y$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
T(x,y)&=(2x,3y),\qquad
x=\frac u2,\qquad y=\frac v3,\\[6pt]
D T^{-1}(u,v)&=
\begin{pmatrix}
\frac12&0\\
0&\frac13
\end{pmatrix},\qquad
\det D T^{-1}(u,v)=\frac12\cdot\frac13-0\cdot0=\frac16,\\[6pt]
f_{X,Y}\left(\frac u2,\frac v3\right)&=1
\iff
\begin{cases}
0<\dfrac u2<1\\[6pt]
0<\dfrac v3<1
\end{cases}
\iff
\begin{cases}
0<u<2\\
0<v<3
\end{cases}\\[6pt]
f_{U,V}(u,v)&=1\cdot\frac16=\frac16
&& (0<u<2,\ 0<v<3),\\[6pt]
\int_0^2\int_0^3\frac16\,dv\,du&=\frac16\cdot6=1.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Independence and uniformity give density one on the original open square $D=(0,1)^2$. The transformation $T$ is a linear bijection with linear inverse. Both are of class $C^1$, so the change-of-variables theorem applies.

The transformation scales the first side by $2$ and the second by $3$, giving a rectangle of area $6$. The two system conditions describe it because the original density is positive only when both coordinates of $T^{-1}(u,v)$ lie in $(0,1)$.

The determinant of the inverse is $\dfrac16$ and converts rectangle area back to square area. The new density is constant on the rectangle and zero outside. Its integral is $\dfrac16\cdot6=1$.

Alternatively, $U$ is uniform on $(0,2)$ with density $\dfrac12$, and $V$ is uniform on $(0,3)$ with density $\dfrac13$. They are independent because one is a function of $X$ and the other a function of $Y$. Multiplying their densities gives $\dfrac16$. Thus $(U,V)$ is uniform on the rectangle $(0,2)\times(0,3)$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

The joint density of $(X,Y)$ is $2$ for $0<y<x<1$ and zero elsewhere. Set $U=X$ and $V=\dfrac YX$. Find the joint density and determine whether $U$ and $V$ are independent.

$$
\begin{aligned}
x&=u,\qquad y=uv,\\[6pt]
0<y<x<1
&\iff 0<uv<u<1
\iff
\begin{cases}
0<u<1\\
0<v<1
\end{cases}\\[6pt]
D T^{-1}(u,v)&=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\v&u\end{pmatrix},\qquad
\det D T^{-1}(u,v)=1\cdot u-0\cdot v=u,\\[6pt]
f_{U,V}(u,v)&=2\cdot|u|=2u &&(0<u<1,\ 0<v<1),\\[6pt]
f_U(u)&=\int_0^1 2u\,dv=2u,\\[6pt]
f_V(v)&=\int_0^1 2u\,du=\left[u^2\right]_0^1=1,\\[6pt]
f_U(u)\,f_V(v)&=2u\cdot1=f_{U,V}(u,v).
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Since $x$ is positive, dividing $0<y<x$ by $x$ gives $0<v<1$. The condition $0<x<1$ becomes $0<u<1$. The inverse maps the entire square to the triangle and has determinant $u$. The map $T(x,y)=(x,\dfrac yx)$ is a bijection between the open triangle and the open square. It and its inverse are of class $C^1$ because $x>0$, so the change-of-variables theorem applies.

The rays from the origin in the graph are curves of constant $V$. Each ray with $0<v<1$ crosses the triangle from $x=0$ to $x=1$, so the range of $u$ does not depend on $v$.

The resulting density varies only with $u$. The marginals vanish outside $(0,1)$ and their product equals the joint density everywhere in the plane. Thus **the two variables are independent**, with $U$ having density $2u$ and $V$ uniform on $(0,1)$.

</div>
