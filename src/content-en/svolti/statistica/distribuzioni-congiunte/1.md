## Exercise 1

Two indicators have joint masses $p_{00}=\dfrac15$, $p_{01}=\dfrac1{10}$, $p_{10}=\dfrac3{10}$, and $p_{11}=\dfrac25$. Find both marginal masses at $1$ and the probability that the indicators are equal.

::grafico{xmin=-0.55 xmax=1.8 ymin=-0.55 ymax=1.58 punti="0,0;0,1;1,0;1,1" segmenti="1,-0.06,1,1.1" etichette="0.1,0.22,0.20;0.08,1.16,0.10;1.1,0.22,0.30;1.1,1.16,0.40" titolo="The labels give the probability masses of the four pairs. The dashed vertical line picks out the two points with X = 1."}

$$
\begin{aligned}
p_{00}+p_{01}+p_{10}+p_{11}
&=\frac2{10}+\frac1{10}+\frac3{10}+\frac4{10}=1,\\[6pt]
p_X(1)&=p_{10}+p_{11}=\frac3{10}+\frac25=\frac7{10},\\[6pt]
p_X(0)&=p_{00}+p_{01}=\frac15+\frac1{10}=\frac3{10},\\[6pt]
p_Y(1)&=p_{01}+p_{11}=\frac1{10}+\frac25=\frac12,\\[6pt]
p_Y(0)&=p_{00}+p_{10}=\frac15+\frac3{10}=\frac12,\\[6pt]
P(X=Y)&=p_{00}+p_{11}=\frac15+\frac25=\frac35,\\[6pt]
P(X\neq Y)&=p_{01}+p_{10}=\frac1{10}+\frac3{10}=\frac25.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

With common denominator $10$, the four masses have numerators $2$, $1$, $3$, and $4$. They are nonnegative and sum to one, so they define a joint distribution.

The first coordinate equals $1$ in cells $(1,0)$ and $(1,1)$. Summing their masses gives the second-row total. For the second coordinate use the second column, since $Y=1$ can occur with either value of $X$. The marginal masses at $0$ follow similarly from the first row and first column. Since $\dfrac3{10}+\dfrac7{10}=1$ and $\dfrac12+\dfrac12=1$, both marginals are probability distributions.

Equality selects the diagonal cells $(0,0)$ and $(1,1)$. The complementary event $X\neq Y$ consists of the two off-diagonal cells. Its probability $\dfrac25$ plus $\dfrac35$ equals one, confirming the result.

Thus the marginal masses at $1$ are $\dfrac7{10}$ for $X$ and $\dfrac12$ for $Y$. The indicators are **equal** with probability $\dfrac35$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A pair has density $c$ on the triangle $0<x<y<2$ and zero elsewhere. Find $c$ and the two marginal densities.

$$
\begin{aligned}
1&=\int_0^2\int_x^2 c\,dy\,dx
=c\int_0^2(2-x)\,dx\\[6pt]
&=c\left[2x-\frac{x^2}{2}\right]_0^2=c\,(4-2)=2c,\\[6pt]
c&=\frac12,\\[6pt]
f_X(x)&=\int_x^2\frac12\,dy=\frac{2-x}{2}
&& (0<x<2),\\[6pt]
f_Y(y)&=\int_0^y\frac12\,dx=\frac y2
&& (0<y<2),\\[6pt]
\int_0^2\frac{2-x}{2}\,dx&=\frac12\cdot2=1,\qquad
\int_0^2\frac y2\,dy=\left[\frac{y^2}{4}\right]_0^2=1.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

For fixed $x$ the vertical section extends from the diagonal $y=x$ to $y=2$. Its length is $2-x$, so the double integral of $c$ is $2c$ and must equal one. Geometry gives the same result. The density is constant, so its integral is $c$ times the triangle's area. The two legs have length $2$, giving area $2$.

For the marginal of $X$, stop after the vertical integral. For the marginal of $Y$, fix $y$ and integrate over $0<x<y$. Both densities vanish outside $(0,2)$ and integrate to one, as the last two calculations show.

The marginal density of $X$ decreases from $1$ to $0$ because the vertical sections shorten as $x$ approaches $2$. The marginal density of $Y$ increases from $0$ to $1$ because the horizontal sections lengthen as $y$ increases. Thus $c=\dfrac12$, $f_X(x)=\dfrac{2-x}{2}$, and $f_Y(y)=\dfrac y2$ on the interval $(0,2)$.

</div>
