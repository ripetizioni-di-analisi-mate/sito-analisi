## Exercise 1

For a parameter $\theta$, an estimator $T$ has expectation $\theta-2$ and variance $3$. An estimator $U$ has expectation $\theta$ and variance $8$. Calculate the mean squared errors and identify the more accurate estimator by this criterion.

$$
\begin{aligned}
b_\theta(T)&=E_\theta[T]-\theta=(\theta-2)-\theta=-2,\\[6pt]
b_\theta(U)&=E_\theta[U]-\theta=\theta-\theta=0,
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
MSE_\theta(T)&=\operatorname{Var}_\theta(T)+b_\theta(T)^2\\[6pt]
&=3+(-2)^2=7,\\[6pt]
MSE_\theta(U)&=\operatorname{Var}_\theta(U)+b_\theta(U)^2\\[6pt]
&=8+0^2=8.
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
MSE_\theta(T)=7<8=MSE_\theta(U).
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Bias is the estimator's expectation minus the parameter. The negative bias of $T$ places its expectation two units below the parameter, while $U$ is unbiased. By the **mean squared error decomposition**, each estimator's mean squared error is its variance plus its squared bias.

The mean squared error of $T$ includes the squared difference, four, added to variance $3$. For $U$ the bias term is zero, leaving only variance $8$. The lower variance of $T$ offsets its bias contribution, giving mean squared error seven versus eight. By this criterion $T$ is **more accurate** than $U$ for every $\theta$, since neither error depends on the parameter. Choosing on unbiasedness alone would favor $U$, a different criterion.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A measurement $X$ has mean $\theta$ and variance $4$. Compare $X$ and $\dfrac X2$ as estimators of $\theta$. For which $\theta$ does the second have strictly smaller mean squared error?

$$
MSE_\theta(X)=\operatorname{Var}(X)+0^2=4.
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
E_\theta\!\left[\frac X2\right]&=\frac{\theta}{2},\qquad
b_\theta\!\left(\frac X2\right)=\frac{\theta}{2}-\theta=-\frac{\theta}{2},\\[6pt]
\operatorname{Var}\!\left(\frac X2\right)&=\frac14\operatorname{Var}(X)=1,
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
MSE_\theta\!\left(\frac X2\right)
&=\frac14\operatorname{Var}(X)+\left(-\frac{\theta}{2}\right)^2\\[6pt]
&=1+\frac{\theta^2}{4}.
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
1+\frac{\theta^2}{4}<4
\iff \theta^2<12
\iff -2\sqrt3<\theta<2\sqrt3.
$$

::grafico{f="4;1+x^2/4" xmin=-5.2 xmax=5.2 ymin=-0.5 ymax=8.3 nomi="θ,R" punti="-3.4641,4;3.4641,4;0,1" segmenti="-3.4641,0,-3.4641,4;3.4641,0,3.4641,4" legenda="MSE_\theta(X);MSE_\theta\left(\dfrac X2\right)" titolo="The mean squared error of $\dfrac X2$ is below the line $R=4$ for $-2\sqrt3<\theta<2\sqrt3$. Outside this interval, the squared bias $-\dfrac{\theta}{2}$ exceeds the reduction in variance."}

<div class="spiegazione">

The measurement $X$ is unbiased, so its mean squared error equals variance $4$ for every $\theta$. Dividing the measurement by two halves its expectation and quarters its variance because the variance of $cX$ is $c^2\operatorname{Var}(X)$. The former introduces bias $-\dfrac{\theta}{2}$ whose magnitude increases with the distance of $\theta$ from zero. The latter reduces variance from $4$ to $1$.

Adding variance and squared bias gives $1+\dfrac{\theta^2}{4}$. Comparing with $4$, subtracting $1$ and multiplying by $4$ yields $\theta^2<12$, whose solutions satisfy $|\theta|<2\sqrt3\approx3.46$. The variance advantage therefore dominates only inside this interval. At its endpoints the mean squared errors agree, and beyond them $X$ has smaller error.

</div>
