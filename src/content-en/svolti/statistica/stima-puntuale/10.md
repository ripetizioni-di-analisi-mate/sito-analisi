## Exercise 1

Independent observations come from a population with mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2=9$. Compare $X_1$ and $\bar X$ as estimators of $\mu$ for $n=9$ and determine which estimator sequence is consistent.

$$
\begin{aligned}
E[X_1]&=\mu,\qquad E[\bar X]=\mu,\\[6pt]
MSE_\mu(X_1)&=\operatorname{Var}(X_1)=9,\\[6pt]
MSE_\mu(\bar X)&=\operatorname{Var}(\bar X)=\frac{\sigma^2}{n}=\frac9n,\qquad MSE_\mu(\bar X)\big|_{n=9}=\frac99=1,
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\lim_{n\to\infty}MSE_\mu(\bar X)&=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac9n=0,\\[6pt]
P_\mu(|\bar X-\mu|>\varepsilon)&\le\frac{9}{n\varepsilon^2}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0.
\end{aligned}
$$

::grafico{f="9" xmin=-0.6 xmax=12.4 ymin=-0.7 ymax=10.9 nomi="n,R" punti="1,9;2,4.5;3,3;4,2.25;5,1.8;6,1.5;7,1.285714;8,1.125;9,1;10,0.9;11,0.818182;12,0.75" segmenti="9,0,9,1" legenda="MSE_\mu(X_1)=9" titolo="The points give the mean squared error of $\bar X$ for integer values of $n$. At $n=9$ it equals $1$, while the mean squared error of $X_1$ remains $9$."}

<div class="spiegazione">

Both estimators are unbiased, so it suffices to compare variances. Independence gives the variance of the sample mean $\dfrac{\sigma^2}{n}$, decreasing as $n$ increases. For $n=9$ the mean squared error of the mean is $1$, one-ninth that of $X_1$.

The mean squared error of $\bar X$ tends to zero, implying consistency by the theorem on **mean squared error and consistency**. Markov's inequality bounds the probability of error greater than $\varepsilon$ by $\dfrac{9}{n\varepsilon^2}$, tending to zero. The rule using only $X_1$ has the same distribution for every $n$. Positive variance gives it a positive probability of exceeding some fixed error threshold. That probability does not depend on $n$ and cannot tend to zero, so the sequence $X_1$ is **unbiased but inconsistent**.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Two independent instruments measure the same quantity $\theta$. The readings $X_1,X_2$ have expectation $\theta$ and variances $1$ and $4$. Find the weight $w$ minimizing the variance of $T_w=wX_1+(1-w)X_2$ and calculate the minimum variance.

$$
\begin{aligned}
E[T_w]&=w\theta+(1-w)\theta=\theta,\\[6pt]
\operatorname{Var}(T_w)&=w^2\operatorname{Var}(X_1)+(1-w)^2\operatorname{Var}(X_2)\\[6pt]
&=w^2+4(1-w)^2\\[6pt]
&=5w^2-8w+4\\[6pt]
&=5\left(w-\frac45\right)^2+\frac45.
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
w=\frac45,\qquad
\operatorname{Var}(T_{\frac45})=\frac{16}{25}+4\cdot\frac{1}{25}=\frac{20}{25}=\frac45.
$$

$$
\operatorname{Var}(T_{\frac12})=\frac14+4\cdot\frac14=\frac54,\qquad
\operatorname{Var}(T_1)=\operatorname{Var}(X_1)=1.
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The weights sum to one, so $T_w$ has expectation $\theta$ for every $w$ and all estimators in this family are unbiased. Comparing **efficiency** therefore amounts to comparing variances. Independence removes the covariance term and each weight enters squared.

Expanding gives the polynomial $5w^2-8w+4$. Completing the square shows that the variable term vanishes at $w$ equal to $w=\dfrac45$, giving minimum variance $\dfrac45$. The more precise reading gets four-fifths of the weight because its variance is smaller. The optimal weights are proportional to the inverse variances $1$ and $\dfrac14$, in a four-to-one ratio. The simple mean with $w=\dfrac12$ has variance $\dfrac54$, greater even than the variance of $X_1$ alone, $1$.

</div>
