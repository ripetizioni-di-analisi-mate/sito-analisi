## Exercise 1

In a sample of five independent observations from $U(0,\theta)$ the observed maximum is $10$. Find the maximum likelihood estimate, the bias of the sample maximum, and the bias-corrected estimate.

$$
\widehat\theta_{\mathrm{ML}}=m=10.
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
P_\theta(M\le m)&=\left(\frac{m}{\theta}\right)^5,\qquad
f_M(m)=\frac{5m^4}{\theta^5},\quad 0<m<\theta,\\[6pt]
E[M]&=\frac{5}{\theta^5}\int_0^\theta m^5\,\mathrm dm
=\frac{5}{\theta^5}\cdot\frac{\theta^6}{6}=\frac56\theta,
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
b_\theta(M)&=\frac56\theta-\theta=-\frac{\theta}{6},\\[6pt]
E\!\left[\frac65M\right]&=\frac65\cdot\frac56\theta=\theta,\\[6pt]
\widehat\theta_{\mathrm{corr}}&=\frac65\cdot10=12.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The observed maximum is the smallest support endpoint compatible with all the data. The likelihood $\theta^{-5}$ decreases from that point onward, giving maximum likelihood estimate $10$.

Use the distribution function to find the expectation of $M$. The event $\{M\le m\}$ means that all five observations are at most $m$, giving probability $\left(\dfrac m\theta\right)^5$ by independence. Differentiation gives density $\dfrac{5m^4}{\theta^5}$, and integrating $m$ times the density yields expectation $\dfrac56\theta$. Thus the maximum underestimates the parameter on average, with bias $-\dfrac{\theta}{6}$. Multiplying by six-fifths restores expectation $\theta$, as in the **bias correction for the uniform maximum**, and gives twelve for this sample.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A sample of four independent observations is drawn from $U(0,\theta)$. Compare the unbiased estimator $\dfrac54M$ with the method-of-moments estimator $2\bar X$ using mean squared error.

$$
\begin{aligned}
f_M(m)&=\frac{4m^3}{\theta^4},\quad 0<m<\theta,\\[6pt]
E[M]&=\frac{4}{\theta^4}\int_0^\theta m^4\,\mathrm dm=\frac45\theta,\\[6pt]
E[M^2]&=\frac{4}{\theta^4}\int_0^\theta m^5\,\mathrm dm
=\frac23\theta^2,
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{Var}(M)&=\frac23\theta^2-\frac{16}{25}\theta^2=\frac{2}{75}\theta^2,\\[6pt]
\operatorname{Var}\!\left(\frac54 M\right)
&=\frac{25}{16}\cdot\frac{2}{75}\theta^2
=\frac{\theta^2}{24},
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{Var}(X)&=\frac{\theta^2}{12},\qquad
\operatorname{Var}(\bar X)=\frac{\theta^2}{12\cdot4}=\frac{\theta^2}{48},\\[6pt]
\operatorname{Var}(2\bar X)&=4\cdot\frac{\theta^2}{48}
=\frac{\theta^2}{12}.
\end{aligned}
$$

For every $\theta>0$, the mean squared error of $\dfrac54M$ is half that of $2\bar X$. At $\theta=6$ their values are $\dfrac32$ and $3$."}

<div class="spiegazione">

To find the second moment of the maximum, multiply its density by $m^2$ and integrate over the support. To find the variance, subtract the squared expectation $\dfrac{16}{25}\theta^2$ and then multiply by the squared correction factor $\dfrac{25}{16}$.

The estimator $2\bar X$ is also unbiased because $E[X]=\dfrac{\theta}{2}$. A uniform distribution on $[0,\theta]$ has variance $\dfrac{\theta^2}{12}$. The mean of four independent observations has one-quarter this variance and the factor $2$ multiplies it by $4$. Both estimators are unbiased, so the **mean squared error decomposition** makes their errors equal their variances. The ratio of $\dfrac{\theta^2}{12}$ to $\dfrac{\theta^2}{24}$ is $2$, so the corrected maximum gives smaller mean squared error for all $\theta>0$.

</div>
