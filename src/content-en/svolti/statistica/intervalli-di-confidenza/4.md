## Exercise 1

In $16$ independent Bernoulli trials there are zero successes. Calculate the Wilson interval with two-sided normal critical value $z=2$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\widehat p&=\frac{0}{16}=0,\\[6pt]
n(\widehat p-p)^2&\leq z^2p(1-p),\\[6pt]
16(0-p)^2&\leq4p(1-p),\\[6pt]
16p^2&\leq4p-4p^2,\\[6pt]
20p^2-4p&\leq0,\\[6pt]
4p(5p-1)&\leq0,\\[6pt]
I&=\left[0,\frac15\right].
\end{aligned}
$$

::grafico{xmin=-0.025 xmax=0.34 ymin=-0.27 ymax=1.08 nomi="p,y" f="20*x^2-4*x" legenda="20p^2-4p" area="0;20*x^2-4*x;0;0.2" punti="0,0;0.2,0" etichette="0.208,-0.12,0.20" titolo="The shaded region gives the candidate proportions for which the polynomial is at most zero. Its roots are 0 and 0.20."}

<div class="spiegazione">

The **Wilson interval** is obtained by inverting the score inequality. A candidate $p$ belongs to it if the distance between $\widehat p$ and $p$ is at most $z$ times the standard error calculated using that same $p$, namely $\sqrt{\dfrac{p(1-p)}{n}}$. Squaring and multiplying by $n$ gives the second line of the calculation.

With $n=16$, $\widehat p=0$, and $z^2=4$, the left side is $16p^2$ and the right side $4p-4p^2$. Bringing all terms to the left gives $20p^2-4p\leq0$.

The observed proportion is zero, so the constant term vanishes. Factor out $4p$ without needing the quadratic formula. The roots are $0$ and $\dfrac15$. The coefficient of $p^2$ is positive, so the product is nonpositive between the roots, as the shaded region shows.

The Wilson interval is $\left[0,\dfrac15\right]$. For the same data the Wald interval would be $[0,0]$ because its estimated variance $\widehat p(1-\widehat p)$ is zero. The Wilson interval includes positive $p$ values **compatible** with zero observed successes in sixteen trials.

</div>

## Exercise 2

In $25$ independent Bernoulli trials there are $9$ successes. Find the center and endpoints of the $95\,\%$ Wilson interval using $z=1.96$. Round the endpoints to four decimal places.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\widehat p&=\frac{9}{25}=0.36,\qquad z^2=1.96^2=3.8416,\\[6pt]
c&=\frac{n\widehat p+\frac{z^2}{2}}{n+z^2}
=\frac{9+1.9208}{25+3.8416}\\[6pt]
&=\frac{10.9208}{28.8416}\approx0.37865,\\[6pt]
n\widehat p(1-\widehat p)+\frac{z^2}{4}&=25\cdot0.36\cdot0.64+0.9604\\[6pt]
&=5.76+0.9604=6.7204,\\[6pt]
h&=\frac{z\sqrt{6.7204}}{n+z^2}
=\frac{1.96\cdot2.59237}{28.8416}\\[6pt]
&\approx\frac{5.08105}{28.8416}\approx0.17617,\\[6pt]
I&\approx[0.37865-0.17617,\,0.37865+0.17617]\\[6pt]
&\approx[0.2025,0.5548].
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

For the center, multiply numerator and denominator in the Wilson formula by $n$. The numerator then includes the observed success count $n\widehat p=9$. Adding $\dfrac{z^2}{2}=1.9208$ to that count shifts the center $0.37865$ toward $\dfrac12$ relative to $0.36$.

Rewrite the half-width by multiplying by $n$ as well. The terms under the square root are $n\widehat p(1-\widehat p)=5.76$ and $\dfrac{z^2}{4}=0.9604$, which sum to $6.7204$. The half-width uses the same denominator $28.8416$ as the center.

Use the unrounded center and margin to calculate the endpoints, rounding only at the end. The $95\,\%$ Wilson interval runs from approximately $0.2025$ to $0.5548$.

For the same data the Wald interval is centered at $0.36$ and runs from approximately $0.1718$ to $0.5482$. The Wilson interval thus has a **center different** from the observed proportion, and its endpoints delimit candidate population proportions.

</div>
