## Exercise 1

A sample of $64$ independent measurements from a normal population has mean $32\,\mathrm{g}$. The population standard deviation is $8\,\mathrm{g}$. Calculate the two-sided $95\,\%$ interval using $z_{0.975}=1.96$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
1-\alpha&=0.95,\qquad \frac{\alpha}{2}=0.025,\\[6pt]
q&=z_{0.975}=1.96,\\[6pt]
e&=\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}=\frac{8}{\sqrt{64}}=\frac{8}{8}=1\,\mathrm{g},\\[6pt]
h&=q\,e=1.96\cdot1=1.96\,\mathrm{g},\\[6pt]
I&=[\bar x-h,\,\bar x+h]\\[6pt]
 &=[32-1.96,\,32+1.96]\,\mathrm{g}\\[6pt]
 &=[30.04,33.96]\,\mathrm{g}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Each endpoint is 1.96 g from the center."}

<div class="spiegazione">

Since the measurements are independent and the population normal, the sample mean $\bar X$ is normal with mean $\mu$ and variance $\dfrac{\sigma^2}{n}$. The population standard deviation is also known, satisfying the assumptions of the **normal confidence interval**.

The standard deviation of the mean of $64$ measurements equals that of one measurement divided by $\sqrt{64}=8$, giving standard error $1\,\mathrm{g}$.

The confidence level is $0.95$, so $\alpha=0.05$. The excluded $5\,\%$ is split into two tails of area $0.025$. The reference quantile thus has order $0.975$ and equals $1.96$. Multiplying the quantile by the standard error gives half-width $1.96\,\mathrm{g}$.

Subtract the half-width from the observed mean $32\,\mathrm{g}$ and add it to the mean. The quantile is dimensionless, so the endpoints have the units of the mean. The $95\,\%$ interval for the population mean runs from $30.04$ to $33.96$ grams and is **centered** on the observed mean, as shown in the figure.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A normal sample of $36$ independent weighings gave the interval $[14,20]\,\mathrm{kg}$ for the mean. The procedure used a two-sided critical value of $2$ and the known standard deviation. Recover the observed mean and the population standard deviation.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\bar x&=\frac{14+20}{2}=\frac{34}{2}=17\,\mathrm{kg},\\[6pt]
h&=\frac{20-14}{2}=\frac{6}{2}=3\,\mathrm{kg},\\[6pt]
h=q\,e\ \Longrightarrow\ e&=\frac{h}{q}=\frac{3}{2}\,\mathrm{kg},\\[6pt]
e=\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}\ \Longrightarrow\ \sigma&=e\sqrt{n}\\[6pt]
&=\frac{3}{2}\cdot\sqrt{36}=\frac{3}{2}\cdot6=9\,\mathrm{kg}.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The normal interval has the form $[\bar x-h,\,\bar x+h]$ and is symmetric about the observed mean. Thus the arithmetic mean of its endpoints, $17\,\mathrm{kg}$, recovers the center even without the individual observations.

The full width is $20-14=6\,\mathrm{kg}$ and is twice the half-width, giving half-width $3\,\mathrm{kg}$.

Since the half-width is the quantile times the standard error, divide $3\,\mathrm{kg}$ by the quantile $2$ to obtain standard error $e=\dfrac{3}{2}\,\mathrm{kg}$.

The standard error of the mean is $\dfrac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}$ with $n=36$. Thus the standard deviation of one weighing is $\sqrt{36}=6$ times that of the mean. Multiplying $\dfrac{3}{2}\,\mathrm{kg}$ by $6$ gives a population standard deviation of **nine kilograms**, while the observed mean is seventeen kilograms.

</div>
