## Exercise 1

A sample of $11$ independent normal measurements has unbiased sample variance $s^2=6\,\mathrm{cm}^2$. Calculate the $90\,\%$ interval for $\sigma^2$ given $\chi^2_{10,0.05}=3.940$ and $\chi^2_{10,0.95}=18.307$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\nu&=11-1=10,\\[6pt]
D&=(n-1)s^2=10\cdot6=60\,\mathrm{cm}^2,\\[6pt]
a&=\chi^2_{10,0.05}=3.940,\qquad b=\chi^2_{10,0.95}=18.307,\\[6pt]
I&=\left[\frac{D}{b},\frac{D}{a}\right]
=\left[\frac{60}{18.307},\frac{60}{3.940}\right]\,\mathrm{cm}^2\\[6pt]
 &\approx[3.277,15.228]\,\mathrm{cm}^2.
\end{aligned}
$$

The shaded area between $3.940$ and $18.307$ is $0.90$, and each tail has area $0.05$."}

<div class="spiegazione">

The measurements are independent and normal, so the **chi-square interval** applies. The pivot $\dfrac{(n-1)S^2}{\sigma^2}$ follows a chi-square distribution with $n-1=10$ degrees of freedom.

The sum of squared deviations is $D=(n-1)s^2$, ten times the unbiased sample variance, giving $60\,\mathrm{cm}^2$. The observed pivot is $D$ divided by the population variance.

At level $90\,\%$, each tail excludes $5\,\%$. Use quantiles of orders $0.05$ and $0.95$. The pivot falls between $3.940$ and $18.307$ with probability $0.90$, as shown in the figure.

The unknown variance is in the denominator of the pivot, so $\dfrac{D}{\sigma^2}\leq18.307$ is equivalent to $\sigma^2\geq\dfrac{D}{18.307}$. The larger quantile gives the **lower limit** and the smaller quantile gives the upper limit.

The quantiles are dimensionless, so both endpoints are in square centimeters. The $90\,\%$ interval for the population variance runs from approximately $3.277$ to $15.228\,\mathrm{cm}^2$. Its midpoint is about $9.25\,\mathrm{cm}^2$ and differs from $s^2=6\,\mathrm{cm}^2$ because the chi-square density is asymmetric.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A sample of $9$ independent normally distributed part measurements has sample standard deviation $s=3\,\mathrm{mm}$. Find the $95\,\%$ interval for the population standard deviation. Use $\chi^2_{8,0.025}=2.180$ and $\chi^2_{8,0.975}=17.535$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
s^2&=3^2=9\,\mathrm{mm}^2,\qquad \nu=9-1=8,\\[6pt]
D&=(n-1)s^2=8\cdot9=72\,\mathrm{mm}^2,\\[6pt]
I_{\sigma^2}&=\left[\frac{72}{17.535},\frac{72}{2.180}\right]\,\mathrm{mm}^2\\[6pt]
&\approx[4.106,33.028]\,\mathrm{mm}^2,\\[6pt]
I_{\sigma}&=\left[\sqrt{\frac{72}{17.535}},\sqrt{\frac{72}{2.180}}\right]\,\mathrm{mm}\\[6pt]
&\approx[\sqrt{4.106},\sqrt{33.028}]\,\mathrm{mm}\\[6pt]
&\approx[2.03,5.75]\,\mathrm{mm}.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The chi-square pivot concerns population variance, so square the observed standard deviation before constructing the interval for $\sigma^2$. This gives $s^2=9\,\mathrm{mm}^2$. Nine parts leave eight degrees of freedom, so $D=8\cdot9=72\,\mathrm{mm}^2$.

At $95\,\%$ each tail has area $0.025$, so use the chi-square quantiles of orders $0.025$ and $0.975$ with eight degrees of freedom. Taking reciprocals reverses their order. Thus $17.535$ appears in the lower endpoint and $2.180$ in the upper endpoint. The variance interval runs from approximately $4.106$ to $33.028\,\mathrm{mm}^2$.

The square root increases on positive numbers and preserves the order of the endpoints. The event for the variance is equivalent to the event for the standard deviation and has the same probability. The $95\,\%$ interval for the population **standard deviation** therefore runs from approximately $2.03$ to $5.75\,\mathrm{mm}$.

</div>
