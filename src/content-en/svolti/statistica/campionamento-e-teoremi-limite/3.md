## Exercise 1

An i.i.d. sample from $N(40,9)$ has size $9$ and gives $s^2=13.5$. Calculate the observed value of $Q=\dfrac{(n-1)S^2}{\sigma^2}$ and state the distribution of $Q$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
n-1&=9-1=8,\qquad \sigma^2=9,\\[6pt]
Q&=\frac{(9-1)S^2}{9}=\frac{8S^2}{9}\sim\chi^2_8,\\[6pt]
q_{\mathrm{obs}}&=\frac{8\cdot13.5}{9}=\frac{108}{9}=12,\\[6pt]
E[Q]&=8.
\end{aligned}
$$

::grafico{f="x^3*exp(-x/2)/96" xmin=0 xmax=22 ymin=-0.012 ymax=0.13 nomi="q,f" legenda="\chi^2_8" segmenti="12,0,12,0.0446175" punti="12,0.0446175" titolo="The segment marks the observed value of 12 on the axis of a chi-square statistic with 8 degrees of freedom."}

<div class="spiegazione">

The normal parameter $9$ is the population variance, while sample size $9$ determines the degrees of freedom $n-1=8$. The sample variance $S^2$ uses divisor $n-1$, so the factor $8$ recovers the sum of squared deviations $\sum_{i=1}^9(X_i-\bar X_9)^2$. Dividing by $9$ expresses this sum relative to the population variance.

The observations are independent and normal. The theorem on the **t statistic and sample variance** therefore gives $Q$ a chi-square distribution with $8$ degrees of freedom. Substitution of $s^2=13.5$ gives $\dfrac{108}{9}$, so the observed sample yields the **value $q_{\mathrm{obs}}=12$**. A chi-square variable with $8$ degrees of freedom has expectation $8$. The value $12$ exceeds its expectation because the observed variance $13.5$ exceeds the population variance $9$, and the ratio $\dfrac{13.5}{9}=1.5$ equals $\dfrac{12}{8}$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

For nine i.i.d. normal measurements with mean $\mu$ and unknown variance, calculate the probability of $|\bar X_9-\mu|>1.86\dfrac S3$. The distribution function $F_8$ of $t_8$ satisfies $F_8(1.86)\approx0.95$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
T&=\frac{\bar X_9-\mu}{\dfrac S{\sqrt9}}=\frac{\bar X_9-\mu}{\dfrac S3}\sim t_8,\\[6pt]
P\left(|\bar X_9-\mu|>1.86\frac S3\right)
&=P(|T|>1.86)\\[6pt]
&=P(T>1.86)+P(T<-1.86),\\[6pt]
P(T>1.86)&=1-F_8(1.86)\approx0.05,\\[6pt]
P(T<-1.86)&=F_8(-1.86)=1-F_8(1.86)\approx0.05,\\[6pt]
P(|T|>1.86)&\approx0.05+0.05=0.10.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The estimated standard error of the mean is $\dfrac{S}{\sqrt9}=\dfrac S3$. This quantity is random because it is calculated from the same sample. Divide both sides by $\dfrac S3$, which is positive with probability one. The inequality retains its direction and the event becomes $|T|>1.86$. Under normality, $T$ has distribution $t_8$ since the degrees of freedom are $n-1=8$.

The event $|T|>1.86$ is the union of the disjoint tails $T>1.86$ and $T<-1.86$. The right-tail probability is approximately $1-0.95=0.05$. The distribution $t_8$ is symmetric about zero, so the left tail has the same probability. The required probability is **approximately $0.10$**. If $\sigma$ were known we would use the normal quantile $z_{0.95}\approx1.645$, smaller than $1.86$ because $t_8$ has heavier tails than the standard normal.

</div>
