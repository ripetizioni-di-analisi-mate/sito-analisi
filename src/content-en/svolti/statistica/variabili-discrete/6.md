## Exercise 1

Independent trials are repeated with success probability $\dfrac25$. Calculate the probability that the first success occurs on the third trial.

$$
p=\frac25,\qquad q=1-\frac25=\frac35
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(T=3)&=P(\text{two failures followed by one success})\\[6pt]
&=q^{2}p=\left(\frac35\right)^2\frac25\\[6pt]
&=\frac{9}{25}\cdot\frac25=\frac{18}{125}.
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
P(T\le3)=1-q^3=1-\frac{27}{125}=\frac{98}{125}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The trial number $T$ of the first success is geometric because trials are independent with common success probability $\dfrac25$. The required event fixes the order of the outcomes: the first two trials must fail and the third must succeed. Each trial fails with probability $\dfrac35$ and the trials are independent, so their joint probability is the product of the three factors. This agrees with the formula $P(T=k)=q^{k-1}p$ for $k=3$.

A success in either of the first two trials would give a value of $T$ different from $3$. Thus event $T=3$ is a proper subset of event $T\le3$, which also includes $T=1$ and $T=2$ and has probability $1-q^3=\dfrac{98}{125}$.

On average, the first success occurs on trial $E[T]=\dfrac1p=\dfrac52$, so $3$ is just above the mean.

The probability that the first success occurs on exactly the third trial is $\dfrac{18}{125}=0.144$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Independent trials have success probability $\dfrac13$. The first four have failed. Given these outcomes, calculate the probability that the first success occurs by the sixth trial.

$$
P(T>m)=q^m=\left(\frac23\right)^m
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(T\le6\mid T>4)
&=1-P(T>6\mid T>4)\\[6pt]
&=1-\frac{P(T>6)}{P(T>4)}\\[6pt]
&=1-\frac{\left(\frac23\right)^6}{\left(\frac23\right)^4}\\[6pt]
&=1-\left(\frac23\right)^2=1-\frac49=\frac59.
\end{aligned}
$$

::grafico{assi=0 xmin=-0.9 xmax=6.2 ymin=0.2 ymax=3.85 legenda=0 linee="0.65,2.35 2.65,3.13;0.65,2.35 2.65,1.4;2.65,1.4 4.55,2.02;2.65,1.4 4.55,0.72" punti="0.65,2.35,pieno;2.65,3.13,pieno;2.65,1.4,pieno;4.55,2.02,pieno;4.55,0.72,pieno" etichette="0.05,2.75,T > 4;1.65,3.02,R;1.6,1.6,F;3.55,1.92,R;3.55,0.85,F;3.08,3.13,T = 5;5.08,2.02,T = 6;5.08,0.72,T > 6" titolo="The tree starts after the first four failures. $$
P(T=5\mid T>4)+P(T=6\mid T>4)=\frac13+\frac23\cdot\frac13=\frac39+\frac29=\frac59
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Condition $T>4$ is already known. Among outcomes compatible with it, the only way to have no success by the end of the sixth trial is for both the fifth and sixth trials to fail. Independence leaves each failure probability unchanged. First calculate the conditional probability of the complementary event $T>6$.

Since $\{T>6\}$ is contained in $\{T>4\}$, the intersection has probability $P(T>6)$. The definition of conditional probability gives the ratio of the two tail probabilities. Their common factor for the first four trials cancels, leaving the complement of two additional failures. The result $\left(\dfrac23\right)^2=P(T>2)$ is an instance of the **memoryless property** with $m=4$ and $k=2$.

The tree gives the same result: success by the sixth trial means a first success on the fifth with probability $\dfrac13$ or on the sixth with probability $\dfrac23\cdot\dfrac13$. The required probability is $\dfrac59$.

</div>
