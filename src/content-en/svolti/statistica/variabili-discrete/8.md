## Exercise 1

In the same interval, two independent counters give Poisson counts with parameters $1$ and $2$. Calculate the probability that their total is $1$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
X+Y&\sim\operatorname{Pois}(1+2)=\operatorname{Pois}(3)\\[6pt]
P(X+Y=1)&=e^{-3}\frac{3^1}{1!}=3e^{-3}\approx0.149.
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(X=1,Y=0)+P(X=0,Y=1)&=e^{-1}\cdot e^{-2}+e^{-1}\cdot 2e^{-2}\\[6pt]
&=e^{-3}+2e^{-3}=3e^{-3}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The total is the sum of two independent Poisson counts. By the **theorem on sums of independent counts**, the parameter of the total is the sum of the two parameters, so $X+Y$ is Poisson with parameter $3$. Evaluate its mass at $1$, where the factor $\dfrac{3^1}{1!}$ equals $3$.

Event $X+Y=1$ is the union of the two disjoint cases in which one counter records one event and the other records zero. The cases can also be calculated directly. By independence, each case has probability equal to the product of the two marginal masses. The case $X=1$, $Y=0$ has probability $e^{-1}\cdot e^{-2}$, while $X=0$, $Y=1$ has probability $e^{-1}\cdot2e^{-2}$. The products sum to $3e^{-3}$, the result given by the theorem.

The probability that the two counters record a total of exactly $1$ is therefore $3e^{-3}\approx0.149$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Two separate groups contain two and three independent trials, respectively, all with success probability $\dfrac12$. Calculate the probability of at least four successes in total.

$$
X\sim\operatorname{Bin}\left(2,\frac12\right),\qquad Y\sim\operatorname{Bin}\left(3,\frac12\right)
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
X+Y&\sim\operatorname{Bin}\left(5,\frac12\right)\\[6pt]
P(X+Y\ge4)&=P(X+Y=4)+P(X+Y=5)\\[6pt]
&=\binom54\left(\frac12\right)^5
+\binom55\left(\frac12\right)^5\\[6pt]
&=\frac5{32}+\frac1{32}=\frac6{32}=\frac3{16}.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The group counts can be combined because all trials are independent with the same success probability. The total therefore counts five trials and, by the theorem on sums, has distribution $\operatorname{Bin}\left(5,\dfrac12\right)$. If the two groups had different success probabilities, the sum would not be binomial and we would need to add the probabilities of the individual cases.

The counts meeting the threshold are four and five. With success probability $\dfrac12$ all sequences are equally likely, but different numbers of sequences give the two counts. Each sequence of five outcomes has probability $\left(\dfrac12\right)^5=\dfrac1{32}$. There are $\binom54=5$ sequences with four successes, since the position of the only failure determines the sequence. Just one sequence has five successes. Add the masses because the two counts are mutually exclusive.

The probability of at least four successes in total is therefore $\dfrac3{16}\approx0.19$.

</div>
