## Exercise 1

Let $f_X(x)=c(3-x)$ for $1<x<3$, with $f_X(x)=0$ elsewhere. Determine $c$ and calculate $P(2<X<3)$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
1 &= \int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} f_X(x)\,dx = c\int_1^3 (3-x)\,dx \\[6pt]
  &= c\left[3x-\frac{x^2}{2}\right]_1^3 \\[6pt]
  &= c\left[\left(9-\frac92\right)-\left(3-\frac12\right)\right] \\[6pt]
  &= c\left(\frac92-\frac52\right) = 2c.
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
c=\frac12.
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(2<X<3)&=\frac12\int_2^3(3-x)\,dx
=\frac12\left[3x-\frac{x^2}{2}\right]_2^3\\[6pt]
&=\frac12\left[\left(9-\frac92\right)-\left(6-2\right)\right]\\[6pt]
&=\frac12\cdot\frac12=\frac14.
\end{aligned}
$$

::grafico{linee="0.25,0 1,0;1,1 3,0;3,0 3.65,0" area="(3-x)/2;0;2;3" segmenti="1,0,1,1;2,0,2,0.5" xmin=-0.24 xmax=3.72 ymin=-0.1 ymax=1.2 nomi="x,f_X" legenda=0 titolo="The shaded triangle has base $1$ and height $\dfrac12$, so its area is $\dfrac14$."}

<div class="spiegazione">

A density must be nonnegative and have total area one. The function is zero outside $(1,3)$, so its integral over the real line reduces to the integral from $1$ to $3$. An antiderivative of $3-x$ is $3x-\dfrac{x^2}{2}$. At $3$ it equals $\dfrac92$, and at $1$ it equals $\dfrac52$, so the integral is $2$ and the normalization condition becomes $2c=1$.

Since $3-x$ is positive between $1$ and $3$, the positive value $c=\dfrac12$ also satisfies the sign condition. By the definition of a **density**, integrate only over the final segment, from $2$ to $3$, to find the required probability. The antiderivative at $2$ is $4$, so the difference between the endpoint values is $\dfrac12$ and the probability is $\dfrac12\cdot\dfrac12=\dfrac14$.

Over this segment the graph bounds a triangle with base $1$ and height $f_X(2)=\dfrac12$, so the area formula confirms the value $\dfrac14$. The final segment covers half the length of the support but only a quarter of the probability because the density decreases along the interval.

</div>

## Exercise 2

The function $f_X$ equals $c$ on $(0,1)$, $cx$ on $(1,2)$ and zero elsewhere. Determine $c$ and calculate $P(\dfrac12<X<\dfrac32)$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
1 &= \int_0^1 c\,dx+\int_1^2 cx\,dx\\[6pt]
  &= c\,[x]_0^1+c\left[\frac{x^2}{2}\right]_1^2\\[6pt]
  &= c+c\left(2-\frac12\right)=c+\frac32c=\frac52c.
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
c=\frac25.
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
P\!\left(\frac12<X<\frac32\right)
  &=\int_{\frac12}^{1}\frac25\,dx+\int_1^{\frac32}\frac25x\,dx\\[6pt]
  &=\frac25\cdot\frac12+
    \frac25\left[\frac{x^2}{2}\right]_1^{\frac32}\\[6pt]
  &=\frac15+\frac25\left(\frac98-\frac12\right)\\[6pt]
  &=\frac15+\frac25\cdot\frac58=\frac15+\frac14=\frac9{20}.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

To normalize the function, add two integrals because its expression changes at $x=1$. The constant segment has length $1$ and contributes $c$. On the increasing segment, an antiderivative of $x$ is $\dfrac{x^2}{2}$. It equals $2$ at $x=2$ and $\dfrac12$ at $x=1$, so the contribution is $\dfrac32c$. The condition $\dfrac52c=1$ gives $c=\dfrac25$.

The resulting value is positive, so both pieces are nonnegative and $f_X$ is a **density**. The requested interval also crosses $1$, so split the probability into two integrals. The first contribution comes from the constant piece, with width $\dfrac12$ and height $\dfrac25$, giving $\dfrac15$. The second comes from the increasing piece. The antiderivative $\dfrac{x^2}{2}$ equals $\dfrac98$ at $\dfrac32$ and $\dfrac12$ at $1$. Multiplying the difference $\dfrac58$ by $\dfrac25$ gives $\dfrac14$.

The events on the two segments are disjoint apart from a single endpoint of probability zero. Add the results to obtain the required probability $\dfrac{9}{20}$.

</div>
