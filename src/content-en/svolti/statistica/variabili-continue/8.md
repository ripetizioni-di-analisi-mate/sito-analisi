## Exercise 1

Let $X$ have a gamma distribution with shape $6$ and rate $3$. Calculate $E[X]$, $\operatorname{Var}(X)$ and $E[X^2]$.

$$
k=6,\qquad \lambda=3,\qquad f_X(x)=\frac{3^6}{\Gamma(6)}x^5e^{-3x}=\frac{729}{120}x^5e^{-3x}\quad(x>0).
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
E[X]&=\frac{k}{\lambda}=\frac63=2,\\[6pt]
\operatorname{Var}(X)&=\frac{k}{\lambda^2}=\frac{6}{3^2}=\frac69=\frac23,\\[6pt]
E[X^2]&=\operatorname{Var}(X)+E[X]^2
       =\frac23+2^2=\frac23+\frac{12}{3}=\frac{14}{3}.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The distribution is parameterized by the shape $k=6$ and rate $\lambda=3$. By the **gamma distribution** formulas, the mean is the shape divided by the rate, and the variance is the shape divided by the squared rate. This gives $E[X]=2$ and $\operatorname{Var}(X)=\dfrac23$.

Solve the relation $\operatorname{Var}(X)=E[X^2]-E[X]^2$ for $E[X^2]$ to find the second moment. Add the squared mean $4=\dfrac{12}{3}$ to the variance, giving $\dfrac{14}{3}$. Since the variance is positive, $E[X^2]$ exceeds the squared mean.

The moment identity avoids integrating the density again. As a check, use the formula $E[X^2]=\dfrac{k(k+1)}{\lambda^2}$ obtained from the relation $\Gamma(k+1)=k\Gamma(k)$. With $k=6$ and $\lambda=3$ this gives $\dfrac{6\cdot7}{9}=\dfrac{42}{9}=\dfrac{14}{3}$, the same value.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Let $Z_1,\ldots,Z_5$ be independent standard normal variables. Set $S=\sum_{i=1}^5 Z_i^2$ and $Y=\dfrac S2+3$. Find the distribution of $S$ and the mean and variance of $Y$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
S&\sim\chi^2_5,\qquad E[S]=\nu=5,\qquad\operatorname{Var}(S)=2\nu=10,\\[6pt]
E[Y]&=E\!\left[\frac12S+3\right]=\frac12E[S]+3=\frac52+3=\frac{11}{2},\\[6pt]
\operatorname{Var}(Y)&=\left(\frac12\right)^2\operatorname{Var}(S)
=\frac14\cdot10=\frac52.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

By the definition of the **chi-square distribution**, the sum of the squares of $\nu$ independent standard normal variables has distribution $\chi^2_\nu$. The five terms meet this condition, so $S\sim\chi^2_5$ with five degrees of freedom.

The distribution $\chi^2_5$ is gamma with shape $\dfrac52$ and rate $\dfrac12$, so its mean is $\dfrac{\frac52}{\frac12}=5$ and its variance is $\dfrac{\frac52}{\frac14}=10$. The mean of $S$ therefore equals the degrees of freedom, and its variance is twice that number.

To go from $S$ to $Y$, halve each observation and add $3$, that is, $Y=aS+b$ with $a=\dfrac12$ and $b=3$. By linearity of expectation, both operations change the mean, giving $\dfrac{11}{2}$. The shift leaves deviations from the mean unchanged. The factor $\dfrac12$ halves the deviations, dividing their mean square by four. The variance of $Y$ is therefore $\dfrac{10}{4}=\dfrac52$.

</div>
