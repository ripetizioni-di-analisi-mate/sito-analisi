## Exercise 1

Of $80$ local shipments, $68$ arrive on time. Of another $40$ shipments, $24$ arrive on time. A shipment is chosen at random. Calculate the probability that it is late given that it is local, and the probability that it is on time given that it is nonlocal.

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(P\mid L)&=\frac{68}{80}=\frac{17}{20},\\[6pt]
P(P^c\mid L)&=1-\frac{17}{20}=\frac{3}{20}=\frac{12}{80},\\[6pt]
P(P\mid L^c)&=\frac{24}{40}=\frac{3}{5},\\[6pt]
P(P^c\mid L^c)&=1-\frac{3}{5}=\frac{2}{5}.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Let $P$ be the event of on-time arrival and $L$ that of a local shipment. The $80+40=120$ shipments are equally likely, so a probability conditional on a group is a ratio of counts within that group.

The first question conditions on the local group. The probability of on-time arrival among local shipments is $\dfrac{68}{80}=\dfrac{17}{20}$. By the **conditional complement rule**, the probability of a delay among local shipments is $1-\dfrac{17}{20}=\dfrac{3}{20}$. Indeed, there are $80-68=12$ late local shipments.

For the second question the reference group changes to the $40$ nonlocal shipments. Of these, $24$ arrive on time, giving probability $\dfrac{24}{40}=\dfrac35$. The two answers concern different reference groups, so their sum differs from one. The complement of the first probability describes on-time local shipments and equals $\dfrac{17}{20}$, while the complement of the second describes late nonlocal shipments and equals $\dfrac25$.

The probability of delay for a local shipment is therefore **$\dfrac{3}{20}$**, and the probability of on-time arrival for a nonlocal shipment is **$\dfrac{3}{5}$**.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Events $A$ and $B$ satisfy $P(A)=0.6$, $P(B)=0.5$ and $P(A\cup B)=0.8$. Calculate $P(A^c\mid B^c)$ and $P(A\mid B^c)$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(A^c\cap B^c)&=1-P(A\cup B)=1-0.8=0.2,\\[6pt]
P(B^c)&=1-0.5=0.5,\\[6pt]
P(A^c\mid B^c)&=\frac{P(A^c\cap B^c)}{P(B^c)}=\frac{0.2}{0.5}=0.4,\\[6pt]
P(A\mid B^c)&=1-0.4=0.6.
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(A\cap B)&=P(A)+P(B)-P(A\cup B)\\[6pt]
&=0.6+0.5-0.8=0.3,\\[6pt]
P(A\cap B^c)&=P(A)-P(A\cap B)=0.6-0.3=0.3,\\[6pt]
P(A\mid B^c)&=\frac{0.3}{0.5}=0.6.
\end{aligned}
$$

::venn{nomi="A,B" ambiente="Ω" zone="a,u" elementi="a:30%;ab:30%;b:20%;u:20%" titolo="The four regions are labeled with their percentages. The shaded region is $B^c$, with probability $0.5$. Within it, $A^c\cap B^c$ has probability $0.2$ and $A\cap B^c$ has probability $0.3$."}

<div class="spiegazione">

Condition $B^c$ has probability $0.5>0$, so both conditional probabilities are defined. The intersection $A^c\cap B^c$ contains the outcomes outside both events. By **De Morgan's law**, this set is the complement of $A\cup B$ and has probability $0.2$. To express its probability within $B^c$, divide by $P(B^c)=0.5$. The second question keeps condition $B^c$ and changes the event of interest from $A^c$ to $A$, so use the **conditional complement rule** and subtract from one.

A second calculation reconstructs the probabilities of all four regions. By the union formula $P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B)-P(A\cap B)$, the intersection has probability $0.3$. The part of $A$ outside $B$ therefore has probability $0.6-0.3=0.3$, whose ratio to $P(B^c)$ confirms $0.6$. The two parts of $B^c$ have probabilities $0.3$ and $0.2$, summing to $0.5$ as required.

In this case $P(A\mid B^c)=P(A)$, which also shows that $A$ and $B^c$ are independent. The theorem on independence of complements then implies that $A$ and $B$ are independent as well, and indeed $P(A)P(B)=0.6\cdot0.5=0.3=P(A\cap B)$.

The results are therefore **$P(A^c\mid B^c)=0.4$** and **$P(A\mid B^c)=0.6$**.

</div>
