## Exercise 1

The probability that an order requires manual review is $0.25$. Among orders reviewed manually, $12\%$ require a correction. Calculate the probability that a randomly chosen order requires both manual review and a correction.

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(M)&=0.25=\frac{1}{4},\qquad P(C\mid M)=0.12=\frac{3}{25},\\[6pt]
P(M\cap C)&=P(M)P(C\mid M)\\[6pt]
&=\frac{1}{4}\cdot\frac{3}{25}=\frac{3}{100}=0.03.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Let $M$ be the event “manual review” and $C$ the event “correction.” The proportion $0.25$ refers to all orders, so it is $P(M)$. The rate $0.12$ refers only to orders reviewed manually, so it is the conditional probability $P(C\mid M)$.

Since $P(M)>0$, the **multiplication rule** applies. The probability of the intersection is the proportion of orders in the review group times the correction rate within that group. As fractions the factors are $\dfrac14$ and $\dfrac{3}{25}$, whose product is $\dfrac{3}{100}$. Thus $3\%$ of all orders meet both conditions. Out of $100$ orders, we would expect $25$ to undergo manual review, and $12\%$ of those $25$ orders amounts to $3$ orders.

The data do not determine $P(C)$ for all orders because the correction rate for orders without manual review is missing.

The probability that an order requires both manual review and a correction is therefore **$0.03$**.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A job application passes an initial screening with probability $\dfrac{3}{5}$. Among applications that pass, $\dfrac{2}{3}$ proceed to an interview. Among those that pass screening and proceed to an interview, $\dfrac{3}{4}$ receive an offer. Calculate the probability that an application passes screening, proceeds to an interview and receives an offer.

::grafico{assi=0 xmin=-0.15 xmax=12.4 ymin=0 ymax=2.2 uguale=1 vettori="0.72,1.15,3.65,1.15;3.8,1.15,7.1,1.15;7.25,1.15,10.55,1.15" punti="0.65,1.15;3.73,1.15;7.18,1.15;10.63,1.15" etichette="0.18,0.62,Start;3.15,0.62,Screening;6.58,0.62,Interview;10.05,0.62,Offer;2,1.58,⅗;5.35,1.58,⅔;8.8,1.58,¾" titolo="Path of an application that leads to an offer. Each factor refers to applicants who have reached the start of the corresponding arrow."}

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(V\cap C)&=\frac{3}{5}\cdot\frac{2}{3}=\frac{2}{5},\\[6pt]
P(V\cap C\cap O)&=P(V\cap C)P(O\mid V\cap C)\\[6pt]
&=\frac{2}{5}\cdot\frac{3}{4}=\frac{3}{10}.
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(V\cap C\cap O)&=P(V)P(C\mid V)P(O\mid V\cap C)\\[6pt]
&=\frac{3\cdot2\cdot3}{5\cdot3\cdot4}=\frac{18}{60}=\frac{3}{10}.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Let $V$, $C$ and $O$ denote passing screening, proceeding to an interview and receiving an offer, respectively. The three given values are $P(V)$, $P(C\mid V)$ and $P(O\mid V\cap C)$, in that order, because each proportion is measured among applications that reached the previous stage.

The first product gives the proportion that reach an interview after screening. Since $P(V)=\dfrac35>0$, the **multiplication rule** applies and gives $P(V\cap C)=\dfrac25$. The offer rate is measured among precisely these applications, so multiply that rate by the proportion just calculated. The final factor conditions on the intersection $V\cap C$, retaining all the information about the preceding stages. Since $P(V\cap C)>0$, the second use of the rule is also valid.

The second calculation applies the multiplication rule to three events in one step. The factor $3$ appears in numerator and denominator, so the result agrees with the first calculation. The two successive conditional rates suffice to apply the multiplication rule without assuming independence. Out of $100$ applications, we would expect $60$ to pass screening, $40$ to reach an interview and $30$ to receive an offer.

The probability that an application reaches the offer stage is therefore **$\dfrac{3}{10}$**.

</div>
