## Exercise 1

A fair die is rolled once. Let $A$ be the event “the result is even” and $B$ the event “the result is at most four.” Determine whether $A$ and $B$ are independent.

$$
\begin{aligned}
A&=\{2,4,6\},\qquad B=\{1,2,3,4\},\\[6pt]
A\cap B&=\{2,4\},\qquad P(A\cap B)=\frac{2}{6}=\frac{1}{3},\\[6pt]
P(A)P(B)&=\frac{3}{6}\cdot\frac{4}{6}=\frac{12}{36}=\frac{1}{3},\\[6pt]
P(A\mid B)&=\frac{|A\cap B|}{|B|}=\frac{2}{4}=\frac{1}{2}=P(A).
\end{aligned}
$$

::venn{nomi="A,B" ambiente="Ω" zone="ab" elementi="a:6;ab:2,4;b:1,3;u:5" titolo="A contains the even die faces and B the faces numbered 4 or less. Two of the four faces in B are even, as are three of the six faces on the die."}

<div class="spiegazione">

The six faces are equally likely, so the probability of each event is its number of faces divided by $6$. Count the faces in each event and in their intersection. Only faces $2$ and $4$ are common to both, so the intersection has probability $\dfrac{2}{6}=\dfrac13$.

The product of the marginal probabilities is $\dfrac{3}{6}\cdot\dfrac{4}{6}=\dfrac{12}{36}=\dfrac13$. The probability of the intersection equals this product, so by the definition of **independence**, the events are **independent**.

The same result follows by conditioning on event $B$, which has positive probability. Two of the four faces in $B$ are even, so the proportion of even faces remains $\dfrac{1}{2}$, the same as for the whole die. Knowing that the result is at most four reduces the number of cases but preserves the proportion of even numbers. If $B$ were the event “the result is at most three,” the only even face among $1$, $2$ and $3$ would be $2$. The conditional probability would fall to $\dfrac13$, so independence depends on the precise events chosen.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Of $100$ orders, $40$ have express delivery and $30$ have a promotional code. Among express orders, $18$ have a promotional code. Let $E$ and $C$ denote “express delivery” and “promotional code.” Determine whether $E$ and $C^c$ are independent.

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(E\cap C^c)&=\frac{40-18}{100}=0.22,\\[6pt]
P(C^c)&=1-\frac{30}{100}=0.70,\\[6pt]
P(E)P(C^c)&=0.40\cdot0.70=0.28,\\[6pt]
P(C^c\mid E)&=\frac{22}{40}=0.55,\\[6pt]
P(E\cap C)&=0.18,\qquad P(E)P(C)=0.40\cdot0.30=0.12.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The orders are equally likely, so each probability is a count divided by $100$. Count express orders without a code by subtracting from the $40$ express orders the $18$ that have a code. The resulting probability, $0.22$, refers to all orders. Find the probability of $C^c$ by subtracting $P(C)=0.30$ from one.

The value $0.28$ would be the probability of the intersection under independence. Since the values differ, events $E$ and $C^c$ are **dependent**. Comparing proportions gives the same conclusion. The proportion without a code among express orders is $\dfrac{22}{40}=0.55$, compared with $0.70$ among all orders.

By the theorem on **independence of complements**, if $E$ and $C$ were independent, $E$ and $C^c$ would also be independent. Hence $E$ and $C$ are dependent too, as the comparison between $P(E\cap C)=0.18$ and $P(E)P(C)=0.12$ confirms. Express orders have a code more often than other orders, so event $C^c$ occurs less often among them.

</div>
