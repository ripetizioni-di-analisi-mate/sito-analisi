## Exercise 1

Two pumps work independently with probabilities $0.8$ and $0.7$. A circuit operates whenever at least one pump works. Calculate the reliability of the circuit.

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(F_1^c)&=1-0.8=0.2,\qquad P(F_2^c)=1-0.7=0.3,\\[6pt]
P(F_1^c\cap F_2^c)&=P(F_1^c)P(F_2^c)=0.2\cdot0.3=0.06,\\[6pt]
P(F_1\cup F_2)&=1-0.06=0.94.
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(F_1\cup F_2)&=P(F_1)+P(F_2)-P(F_1)P(F_2)\\[6pt]
&=0.8+0.7-0.56=0.94.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Let $F_i$ be the event “pump $i$ works.” The circuit then operates on event $F_1\cup F_2$. “At least one pump works” includes the case in which both work and the two cases in which only one works. Its complement is the failure of both pumps. The operating events are independent, so their complements are independent and the failure probabilities multiply. The product is $0.06$ and covers all cases in which the circuit stops. Reliability is one minus this probability and exceeds both individual reliabilities because event $F_1\cup F_2$ contains both $F_1$ and $F_2$.

A second calculation applies inclusion–exclusion to the union. The term subtracted is $P(F_1\cap F_2)$, which equals $0.8\cdot0.7=0.56$ by independence, giving the same result. Using the complement requires only one product and remains shorter when there are more pumps.

The reliability of the circuit is therefore **$0.94$**.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Three units operate mutually independently with probabilities $0.9$, $0.8$ and $0.5$. A system operates whenever at least one unit works. Given that the system is operating, calculate the probability that only the third unit works.

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(F_1^c)&=0.1,\qquad P(F_2^c)=0.2,\qquad P(F_3^c)=0.5,\\[6pt]
P(F_1^c\cap F_2^c\cap F_3^c)&=0.1\cdot0.2\cdot0.5=0.01,\\[6pt]
P(O)&=1-0.01=0.99,\\[6pt]
P(F_1^c\cap F_2^c\cap F_3)&=0.1\cdot0.2\cdot0.5=0.01,\\[6pt]
P(F_1^c\cap F_2^c\cap F_3\mid O)&=\frac{P(F_1^c\cap F_2^c\cap F_3)}{P(O)}=\frac{0.01}{0.99}=\frac{1}{99}.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Let $F_i$ denote operation of unit $i$ and $O$ operation of the system. Subtract the operating probabilities from one to obtain the failure probabilities. Replacing one or more events by their complements preserves mutual independence, by the same argument used to prove **independence of complements**. The probability of any configuration of working and failed units is therefore the product of the three corresponding factors.

The denominator is the probability that at least one unit works, found by taking the complement of total failure. The favorable event requires the first two units to fail and the third to work. Since operation of the third unit guarantees $O$, intersecting the favorable event with the condition leaves the favorable event itself. In the definition of conditional probability the numerator is therefore $0.01$ and the denominator is $0.99$.

In this example, total failure and operation of the third unit alone are equally likely because the third unit is as likely to work as it is to fail.

Given that the system operates, the probability that only the third unit works is therefore **$\dfrac{1}{99}$**, approximately $1\%$.

</div>
