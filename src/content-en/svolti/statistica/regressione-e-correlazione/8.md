## Exercise 1

A normal model with an intercept gives $n=9$, $s_e=3$, $\hat y(\bar x)=12$, and $t_{7,0.975}=2.365$. Calculate the $95\%$ margins for the mean and for a new independent response at $x_0=\bar x$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
h_0&=\frac19+\frac{(\bar x-\bar x)^2}{S_{xx}}=\frac19,\\[6pt]
SE_{\mathrm{mean}}&=s_e\sqrt{h_0}=3\sqrt{\frac19}=1,\\[6pt]
SE_{\mathrm{pred}}&=s_e\sqrt{1+h_0}=3\sqrt{\frac{10}{9}}=\sqrt{10}.
\end{aligned}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
M_{\mathrm{mean}}&=2.365\cdot1=2.365,\\[6pt]
M_{\mathrm{pred}}&=2.365\sqrt{10}\approx7.479,\\[6pt]
I_{\mathrm{mean}}&=12\pm2.365=[9.635;\,14.365],\\[6pt]
I_{\mathrm{pred}}&\approx12\pm7.479=[4.521;\,19.479].
\end{aligned}
$$

M refers to the mean response and P to a new individual response. Both intervals are centered at $12$."}

<div class="spiegazione">

At the mean predictor value the distance $x_0-\bar x$ is zero, so the second term of $h_0$ vanishes, leaving $h_0=\dfrac1n=\dfrac19$. At this point uncertainty in the slope does not enter the mean-response estimate, which depends only on $\bar y$.

An individual future response can still differ from its mean. Its new error is independent of the sample, so its variance is added to the variance of the estimate, giving $1+h_0=\dfrac{10}{9}$ under the square root.

Multiply both standard errors by the same t quantile with $n-2=7$ degrees of freedom. The margin for the mean is $2.365$ and the prediction margin is approximately $7.479$, more than three times as large. Both intervals have the **same center $12$**, and the prediction interval contains the interval for the mean.

</div>

## Exercise 2

In a normal model with an intercept, $n=16$, $\bar x=10$, $S_{xx}=64$, $s_e=4$, and $\hat y(x)=2+0.5x$. At $x_0=14$ construct both $95\%$ intervals. Use $t_{14,0.975}=2.145$ and round to two decimal places.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\hat y_0&=2+0.5\cdot14=9,\\[6pt]
h_0&=\frac1{16}+\frac{(14-10)^2}{64}=\frac1{16}+\frac{16}{64}=\frac1{16}+\frac4{16}=\frac5{16},\\[6pt]
SE_{\mathrm{mean}}&=4\sqrt{\frac5{16}}=\sqrt5\approx2.236,\\[6pt]
SE_{\mathrm{pred}}&=4\sqrt{1+\frac5{16}}=4\sqrt{\frac{21}{16}}=\sqrt{21}\approx4.583.
\end{aligned}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
2.145\sqrt5&\approx4.80,\qquad 2.145\sqrt{21}\approx9.83,\\[6pt]
I_{\mathrm{mean}}&=9\pm2.145\sqrt5\approx[4.20;\,13.80],\\[6pt]
I_{\mathrm{pred}}&=9\pm2.145\sqrt{21}\approx[-0.83;\,18.83].
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Both intervals are centered on the fitted response at the specified point, which the line gives as $9$.

The predictor value $14$ is four units from the sample mean. In addition to $\dfrac1{16}$, the weight $h_0$ includes the term $\dfrac{16}{64}=\dfrac14$ from slope uncertainty. This gives $h_0=\dfrac5{16}$, five times its value at $x_0=\bar x$.

For the mean response this weight accounts for all the variance of the estimate, giving standard error $s_e\sqrt{h_0}=\sqrt5$. A prediction also includes the variance of the new observation, giving $1+h_0=\dfrac{21}{16}$ under the square root and standard error $\sqrt{21}$.

The quantile is the same because both statistics use the same residual variance estimate with $n-2=14$ degrees of freedom. Margins $4.80$ and $9.83$ give interval $[4.20;\,13.80]$ for the mean and $[-0.83;\,18.83]$ for a new response.

</div>
