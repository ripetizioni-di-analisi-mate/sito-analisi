## Exercise 1

A regression gives $\hat y=20-0.2x$ with $x$ in seconds and $y$ in meters. Rewrite the line with $x'$ in minutes and $y'$ in centimeters and state how the correlation changes.

$$
\begin{aligned}
x&=60x',\qquad y'=100y,\\[6pt]
\hat y&=20-0.2\cdot60x'=20-12x',\\[6pt]
\hat y'&=100\,\hat y=100\left(20-12x'\right),\\[6pt]
\hat y'&=2000-1200x'.
\end{aligned}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
S_{x'y'}&=\frac{1}{60}\cdot100\,S_{xy},\qquad S_{x'x'}=\frac{1}{60^2}S_{xx},\qquad S_{y'y'}=100^2S_{yy},\\[6pt]
r'&=\frac{\frac{100}{60}S_{xy}}{\sqrt{\frac{1}{60^2}S_{xx}\cdot100^2S_{yy}}}=\frac{\frac{100}{60}S_{xy}}{\frac{100}{60}\sqrt{S_{xx}S_{yy}}}=r.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

One minute contains $60$ seconds, so the same duration satisfies $x=60x'$ and a one-unit increase on the new predictor scale corresponds to $60$ on the original scale. Substituting in the line gives slope $-0.2\cdot60=-12$ in meters per minute.

One meter contains $100$ centimeters, so multiply the entire line by $100$ to express the response in centimeters. Both intercept and slope are multiplied by $100$. The new intercept is $2000\,\mathrm{cm}$ and the new slope $-1200\,\mathrm{cm/min}$.

Both scale changes are positive, $\dfrac{1}{60}$ for the predictor and $100$ for the response. Centered deviations acquire the same factors, so $S_{x'y'}$ is multiplied by $\dfrac{100}{60}$ and the square root of $S_{x'x'}S_{y'y'}$ by the same factor. They cancel in Pearson's coefficient, so **the correlation is unchanged**, $r'=r$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

For the same sample suppose $S_{xx}=20$, $S_{yy}=45$, and $S_{xy}=18$. Calculate the slope for the regression of $y$ on $x$, the slope for $x$ on $y$, and their product.

$$
\begin{aligned}
b_{y\mid x}&=\frac{S_{xy}}{S_{xx}}=\frac{18}{20}=\frac9{10},\\[6pt]
b_{x\mid y}&=\frac{S_{xy}}{S_{yy}}=\frac{18}{45}=\frac25,\\[6pt]
b_{y\mid x}\,b_{x\mid y}&=\frac9{10}\cdot\frac25=\frac{18}{50}=\frac9{25}.
\end{aligned}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
r&=\frac{18}{\sqrt{20\cdot45}}=\frac{18}{\sqrt{900}}=\frac{18}{30}=\frac35,\\[6pt]
r^2&=\frac{9}{25},\\[6pt]
\frac{1}{b_{y\mid x}}&=\frac{10}{9}\approx1.111\neq\frac25.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Regression of $y$ on $x$ minimizes vertical deviations in $y$ and uses the predictor sum of squares $S_{xx}$ in the denominator. Reversing the variables makes $y$ the predictor and $S_{yy}$ the denominator. Both slopes share numerator $S_{xy}$ and therefore have the same sign.

The product of the two slopes is $\dfrac{S_{xy}^2}{S_{xx}S_{yy}}$, which equals $r^2$. Computing Pearson's correlation directly gives $r=\dfrac35$, whose square is indeed $\dfrac9{25}$.

Since $r^2<1$, the slope product is less than one. If the regression of $x$ on $y$ were the algebraic inverse of the first line, its slope would be $\dfrac{10}{9}$ and their product would equal $1$. The two regressions minimize deviations in different directions and **give different lines**. They coincide only when $|r|=1$, that is, when all points are collinear.

</div>
