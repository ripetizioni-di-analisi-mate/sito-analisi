## Exercise 1

In a simple linear model with an intercept, $n=10$, $SSE=18$, and $S_{xx}=25$. Calculate $s_e^2$, $s_e$, and $SE(b)$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
n-2&=10-2=8,\\[6pt]
s_e^2&=\frac{SSE}{n-2}=\frac{18}{8}=\frac94,\\[6pt]
s_e&=\sqrt{\frac94}=\frac32,\\[6pt]
\sqrt{S_{xx}}&=\sqrt{25}=5,\\[6pt]
SE(b)&=\frac{s_e}{\sqrt{S_{xx}}}=\frac{\frac32}{5}=\frac3{10}.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The **residual variance** estimates the common error variance $\sigma^2$ by dividing the residual sum of squares by $n-2$ degrees of freedom, here eight. The two degrees of freedom subtracted from $n$ account for the intercept and slope, estimated from the same data before computing residuals. This gives $s_e^2=\dfrac94$.

Taking the square root returns the dispersion to response units, so $s_e=1.5$ describes a typical deviation of an observation from the line.

The slope estimator has variance $\dfrac{\sigma^2}{S_{xx}}$, so divide $s_e$ by $\sqrt{S_{xx}}=5$ to find its estimated standard error. This gives $SE(b)=0.3$ in response units per predictor unit.

The sum $S_{xx}$ measures how far the predictor values spread about their mean. At fixed $s_e$, greater predictor spread increases $S_{xx}$ and reduces $SE(b)$. For example, with $S_{xx}=100$ and the same $s_e$, the standard error would be $0.15$, half as large.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A normal model with an intercept gives $n=18$, $SSE=64$, $S_{xx}=100$, and $b=0.7$. Test $H_0\colon\beta=0.3$ against the two-sided alternative at $5\%$ and construct the $95\%$ confidence interval. Use $t_{16,0.975}=2.120$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
s_e&=\sqrt{\frac{SSE}{n-2}}=\sqrt{\frac{64}{16}}=2,\\[6pt]
SE(b)&=\frac{2}{\sqrt{100}}=\frac{2}{10}=0.2,\\[6pt]
T&=\frac{b-\beta_0}{SE(b)}=\frac{0.7-0.3}{0.2}=2.
\end{aligned}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
|T|&=2<2.120,\\[6pt]
0.7\pm2.120\cdot0.2&=0.7\pm0.424,\\[6pt]
I_\beta&=[0.276;\,1.124].
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The model is normal with independent errors, $n=18>2$, and $S_{xx}>0$. Under $H_0$, the statistic $T$ therefore has Student's t distribution with $n-2=16$ degrees of freedom. First calculate $s_e=2$ and $SE(b)=0.2$.

The statistic expresses the difference between the estimate and the hypothesized value in standard-error units. The estimate $0.7$ is $0.4$ from $0.3$, or two standard errors away. For a two-sided alternative compare the absolute value of $T$ with cutoff $t_{16,0.975}=2.120$. Since $|T|=2<2.120$, the statistic lies in the nonrejection region and **the test fails to reject $H_0$** at $5\%$.

The interval uses the same quantile, giving margin $2.120\cdot0.2=0.424$. The value $0.3$ belongs to $[0.276;\,1.124]$. Agreement follows because the test and interval use the same t distribution and corresponding levels.

</div>
