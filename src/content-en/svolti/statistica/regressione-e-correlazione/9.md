## Exercise 1

A regression with an intercept uses predictor values $1,2,3,4,5$. Find the leverage of the observations with predictor values $3$ and $5$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\bar x&=\frac{1+2+3+4+5}{5}=3,\\[6pt]
S_{xx}&=(-2)^2+(-1)^2+0^2+1^2+2^2=10,\\[6pt]
h_{x=3}&=\frac15+\frac{(3-3)^2}{10}=\frac15,\\[6pt]
h_{x=5}&=\frac15+\frac{(5-3)^2}{10}=\frac15+\frac4{10}=\frac35.
\end{aligned}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
(h_1,h_2,h_3,h_4,h_5)&=\left(\frac35,\;\frac3{10},\;\frac15,\;\frac3{10},\;\frac35\right),\\[6pt]
\sum_i h_i&=\frac{6+3+2+3+6}{10}=2.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

In a model with an intercept, **leverage** is $h_i=\dfrac1n+\dfrac{(x_i-\bar x)^2}{S_{xx}}$. It depends only on the predictor values and measures the point's horizontal position relative to the center of the sample. The observed responses are unnecessary for this calculation.

The observation at $3$ is at the predictor mean, so its distance is zero and its leverage is just $\dfrac1n=\dfrac15$, the minimum possible for five observations. The point at $5$ is two units from the mean, adding $\dfrac4{10}$ and giving leverage $\dfrac35$.

The leverages of all five observations are symmetric about the central predictor value and sum to $2$, as the leverage-sum identity for a model with two coefficients requires. Their mean is $\dfrac25$. The observation with predictor value $5$ has leverage **one and a half times the mean**, giving it greater potential to change the line if its response is inconsistent with the other observations. Changing its observed response by $\Delta$ changes its fitted value by $\dfrac35\Delta$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

The points $(0,1)$, $(1,3)$, $(2,5)$, and $(3,7)$ lie on a line. Add $(8,20)$ and refit the regression. Find the new slope and the leverage of the added point.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\sum_i x_i&=14,\qquad \sum_i y_i=36,\\[6pt]
\sum_i x_i^2&=0+1+4+9+64=78,\qquad \sum_i x_iy_i=0+3+10+21+160=194,\\[6pt]
\bar x&=\frac{14}{5},\qquad \bar y=\frac{36}{5},\\[6pt]
S_{xx}&=78-\frac{14^2}{5}=78-\frac{196}{5}=\frac{194}{5},\\[6pt]
S_{xy}&=194-\frac{14\cdot36}{5}=194-\frac{504}{5}=\frac{466}{5}.
\end{aligned}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
b_{\mathrm{new}}&=\frac{\frac{466}{5}}{\frac{194}{5}}=\frac{466}{194}=\frac{233}{97}\approx2.40,\\[6pt]
h_{x=8}&=\frac15+\frac{\left(8-\frac{14}{5}\right)^2}{\frac{194}{5}}
=\frac15+\frac{\frac{676}{25}}{\frac{194}{5}}=\frac15+\frac{338}{485}=\frac{87}{97}\approx0.90.
\end{aligned}
$$

::grafico{f="1+2*x;(46+233*x)/97" xmin=-0.4 xmax=9 ymin=-0.5 ymax=23 punti="0,1;1,3;2,5;3,7;8,20" segmenti="8,17,8,20" legenda="\text{Before};\text{After}" titolo="The refitted line has a steeper slope. At a horizontal coordinate of 8, the vertical segment shows a deviation of 3 from the original line."}

<div class="spiegazione">

The original four points lie on $y=1+2x$, giving initial slope $2$. The new point lies far from them horizontally, and its observed response $20$ exceeds the original fitted value $17$ by $3$.

Adding the point also shifts the sample center. Recalculate the means and centered sums using all five pairs. Start from the totals and apply $S_{xx}=\sum_i x_i^2-n\bar x^2$ and $S_{xy}=\sum_i x_iy_i-n\bar x\bar y$ with $n\bar x^2=\dfrac{14^2}{5}$ and $n\bar x\bar y=\dfrac{14\cdot36}{5}$.

The new slope is $\dfrac{233}{97}\approx2.40$, larger than $2$, because the added point lies above the original line. Its predictor value is $\dfrac{26}{5}$ from the new mean, giving leverage $\dfrac{87}{97}\approx0.90$, close to the maximum $1$. The added point has **very high leverage** and, in this example, visibly changes the slope by itself.

</div>
