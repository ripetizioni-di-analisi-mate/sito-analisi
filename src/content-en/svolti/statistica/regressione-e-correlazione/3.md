## Exercise 1

For the data $(0,2)$, $(1,5)$, and $(2,5)$ the fitted line is $\hat y(x)=2.5+1.5x$. Calculate the residuals and identify the point above the line.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\hat y_1&=2.5+1.5\cdot0=2.5,\\[6pt]
\hat y_2&=2.5+1.5\cdot1=4,\\[6pt]
\hat y_3&=2.5+1.5\cdot2=5.5.
\end{aligned}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
e_1&=2-2.5=-0.5,\\[6pt]
e_2&=5-4=1,\\[6pt]
e_3&=5-5.5=-0.5.
\end{aligned}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
\sum_i e_i&=-0.5+1-0.5=0,\\[6pt]
\sum_i x_ie_i&=0\cdot(-0.5)+1\cdot1+2\cdot(-0.5)=0.
\end{aligned}
$$

::grafico{f="2.5+1.5*x" xmin=-0.5 xmax=2.8 ymin=-0.4 ymax=6.8 punti="0,2;1,5;2,5" segmenti="1,4,1,5" legenda="\hat y=2.5+1.5x" titolo="The residual for the second observation is the vertical difference from 4 to 5."}

<div class="spiegazione">

Substitute each predictor value into the line to obtain the **fitted values** $\hat y_1=2.5$, $\hat y_2=4$, and $\hat y_3=5.5$. Then subtract each fitted value from the response observed at the same predictor value to obtain the **residual**, $e_i=y_i-\hat y_i$.

At the second point the observed response $5$ exceeds the fitted value $4$, so its residual is positive and the point **lies above the line**. The first and third observed responses are below their fitted values, giving negative residuals and points below the line.

The residuals sum to zero, as do their products with the predictor values. These are the two normal equations for regression with an intercept. Both hold, confirming the given line as the least-squares fit for the data.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A least-squares line with an intercept is fitted at predictor values $0,1,2,3$. The first two residuals are $e_1=1$ and $e_2=-1$. Find the other two.

$$
\begin{cases}
e_1+e_2+e_3+e_4=0,\\
0\cdot e_1+1\cdot e_2+2e_3+3e_4=0.
\end{cases}
$$
$$
\begin{cases}
1-1+e_3+e_4=0,\\
-1+2e_3+3e_4=0,
\end{cases}
\qquad
\begin{cases}
e_3+e_4=0,\\
2e_3+3e_4=1.
\end{cases}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
e_3&=-e_4,\\[6pt]
-2e_4+3e_4&=1,\\[6pt]
e_4&=1,\qquad e_3=-1,\\[6pt]
(e_3,e_4)&=(-1,1).
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The residuals from a least-squares line with an intercept satisfy the two **normal equations** $\sum_i e_i=0$ and $\sum_i x_ie_i=0$. Substituting the known residuals gives a linear system in $e_3$ and $e_4$.

The first normal equation requires the residuals to sum to zero. The two known residuals cancel, so the remaining two must also cancel, giving $e_3+e_4=0$.

The second normal equation requires the predictor-weighted sum of residuals to be zero. The first contribution is zero because its predictor is $0$, and the second contribution is $1\cdot(-1)=-1$. The remaining contributions $2e_3+3e_4$ must therefore sum to $1$.

Substituting $e_3=-e_4$ into the second equation gives $e_4=1$ and hence $e_3=-1$. The determinant is $1\cdot3-1\cdot2=1\ne0$, so the solution is **unique**. The missing residuals are $e_3=-1$ and $e_4=1$.

</div>
