## Exercise 1

Simplify $2^{-2}\sqrt{48}-\dfrac{\sqrt3}{2}$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
2^{-2}&=\frac1{2^2}=\frac14,\\[6pt]
\sqrt{48}&=\sqrt{16\cdot3}=4\sqrt3,\\[6pt]
2^{-2}\sqrt{48}-\frac{\sqrt3}{2}
&=\frac14\cdot4\sqrt3-\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\\[6pt]
&=\frac{\sqrt3}{2}.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The exponent $-2$ denotes the reciprocal of the second power of $2$, so the first factor is $\dfrac14$. The radicand $48$ contains the perfect square $16$. Taking its square root out gives a product equal to $\sqrt3$. Subtraction combines the two terms because they share the same radical.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Find the real domain of $\dfrac{\sqrt{3-2x}}{x+1}+\dfrac1{\sqrt{x+4}}$. Evaluate it at $x=0$ if that value is admissible.

$$
\begin{cases}
3-2x\ge0,\\
x+1\ne0,\\
x+4>0
\end{cases}
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\begin{cases}
x\le\dfrac32,\\
x\ne-1,\\
x>-4.
\end{cases}
$$

$$
D=(-4,-1)\cup\left(-1,\frac32\right],
\qquad
\frac{\sqrt{3-2\cdot0}}{0+1}+\frac1{\sqrt{0+4}}
=\sqrt3+\frac12.
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The square root in the first term is in the numerator and can be zero, so its inequality is non-strict. The value $x=-1$ is excluded because it makes $x+1$ zero. In the second term the square root is in the denominator, so its argument must be strictly positive. The intersection of the three conditions contains $0$, and substitution gives the value there.

</div>
