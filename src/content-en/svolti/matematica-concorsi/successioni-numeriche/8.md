## Exercise 1

In the following table, the numbers in every row follow the same rule.
$$
\begin{array}{|c|c|c|}
\hline
2 & 3 & 13 \\ \hline
4 & 1 & 17 \\ \hline
5 & 2 & 29 \\ \hline
3 & 5 & ? \\ \hline
\end{array}
$$
What is the missing number?

- A) $15$
- B) $8$
- C) $25$
- D) $34$
- E) $64$

$$
2^2 + 3^2 = 4 + 9 = 13,
\qquad
4^2 + 1^2 = 16 + 1 = 17,
\qquad
5^2 + 2^2 = 25 + 4 = 29
$$
$$
3^2 + 5^2 = 9 + 25 = 34
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The sum $2 + 3 = 5$ and the product $2 \cdot 3 = 6$ are far from $13$, whereas $13 = 4 + 9$ is the sum of the squares of $2$ and $3$. The rule “the third number is the sum of the squares of the first two” also works for the second and third rows because $16 + 1 = 17$ and $25 + 4 = 29$, so the missing number is $9 + 25 = 34$. Choice E is the square of the sum $(3 + 5)^2 = 64$, which would give $25$ instead of $13$ for the first row. Choices A and B use the product and sum of the two numbers. The correct answer is **D**.

</div>

## Exercise 2

In the three squares shown, the center number is obtained from the numbers at the four corners using the same rule. Which number replaces the question mark?

::grafico{assi=0 uguale=1 xmin=-1 xmax=17 ymin=-1 ymax=5 linee="0,0 4,0 4,4 0,4 0,0;6,0 10,0 10,4 6,4 6,0;12,0 16,0 16,4 12,4 12,0" etichette="-0.5,4.25,5;4.1,4.25,2;-0.5,-0.55,3;4.1,-0.55,4;1.7,1.85,14;5.5,4.25,6;10.1,4.25,3;5.5,-0.55,4;10.1,-0.55,7;7.7,1.85,30;11.5,4.25,8;16.1,4.25,5;11.5,-0.55,2;16.1,-0.55,6;13.85,1.85,?" titolo="Three squares with one number at each corner and one in the center. The center number in the third square is missing"}

- A) $58$
- B) $21$
- C) $48$
- D) $28$
- E) $38$

$$
5 \cdot 4 - 2 \cdot 3 = 20 - 6 = 14,
\qquad
6 \cdot 7 - 3 \cdot 4 = 42 - 12 = 30
$$
$$
8 \cdot 6 - 5 \cdot 2 = 48 - 10 = 38
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

In the first square the corner numbers add up to $14$, but in the second they add up to $20$ instead of $30$, so the sum rule does not work for all the figures. The products of the opposite corners in the first square are $5 \cdot 4 = 20$ and $2 \cdot 3 = 6$, and their difference is $14$. In the second square the products are $42$ and $12$, and their difference is $30$. This rule therefore works for both complete **figures** and gives $48 - 10 = 38$ in the third square. Choice A adds the two products instead of subtracting them, choice B adds the corner numbers, and choice D uses the products of numbers on the same horizontal side. The correct answer is **E**.

</div>

## Exercise 3

For three teams, a warehouse manager records in a table the number of workers, the length of the shift in hours, and a third number calculated from the first two using the same rule.
$$
\begin{array}{|c|c|c|}
\hline
\text{workers} & \text{hours per shift} & \text{total} \\ \hline
4 & 6 & 20 \\ \hline
5 & 3 & 10 \\ \hline
7 & ? & 42 \\ \hline
\end{array}
$$
What is the missing number?

- A) $6$
- B) $7$
- C) $8$
- D) $5$
- E) $35$

$$
4 \cdot (6 - 1) = 20,
\qquad
5 \cdot (3 - 1) = 10
$$
$$
7 \cdot (x - 1) = 42
\iff
x - 1 = 6
\iff
x = 7
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The product of the first two numbers is $24$ in the first row and $15$ in the second. These products exceed the totals by $4$ and $5$, respectively, exactly the number of workers in each row. The total is therefore the number of workers times the shift length reduced by $1$ hour, as if each worker had a one-hour break. In the third row, $7 \cdot (x - 1) = 42$, so $x - 1 = 6$ and the missing number is $7$. Choice A, $\dfrac{42}{7} = 6$, gives the value of $x - 1$ instead of $x$. As a check, $7 \cdot 6 = 42$. The correct answer is **B**.

</div>
