## Exercise 1

A battery charger contains a transformer that reduces the grid voltage from $230\,\mathrm{V}$ to $12\,\mathrm{V}$ rms, and the primary has $N_1 = 1150$ turns. The secondary supplies a load that absorbs the rms current $I_2 = 2.0\,\mathrm{A}$ with power factor $1$. Treating the transformer as ideal, compute the number of turns of the secondary, the rms current in the primary and the power transferred.

$$
\frac{V_2}{V_1} = \frac{N_2}{N_1} \;\Longrightarrow\; N_2 = N_1\,\frac{V_2}{V_1} ,
\qquad
\frac{I_1}{I_2} = \frac{N_2}{N_1} \;\Longrightarrow\; I_1 = I_2\,\frac{V_2}{V_1} ,
\qquad
P = V_2 I_2
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
N_2 &= 1150 \cdot \frac{12}{230} = 60 \\[6pt]
I_1 &= 2.0 \cdot \frac{12}{230}\,\mathrm{A} \approx 0.10\,\mathrm{A} \\[6pt]
P &= 12 \cdot 2.0\,\mathrm{W} = 24\,\mathrm{W}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

All the values of voltage and current are rms values. By the theorem on the **ideal transformer** the ratio of the voltages equals the ratio of the numbers of turns, so the secondary has $60$ turns. The ideal transformer has no losses, therefore the power absorbed by the primary equals the power delivered to the load, $V_1 I_1 = V_2 I_2$; it follows that the current in the primary is reduced by the same factor $\dfrac{12}{230}$ by which the voltage is reduced. As a check, $230 \cdot 0.104\,\mathrm{W} \approx 24\,\mathrm{W}$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A $36\,\mathrm{W}$ lamp designed to operate at $12\,\mathrm{V}$ rms is supplied by the secondary of an ideal transformer, whose primary is connected to the grid at $230\,\mathrm{V}$. Modeling the lamp as a resistor, compute the turns ratio, the rms currents in the two windings, the resistance of the lamp, and the resistance that, connected directly to the grid, would draw the same current as the primary.

$$
\frac{N_1}{N_2} = \frac{V_1}{V_2} ,
\qquad
I_2 = \frac{P}{V_2} ,
\qquad
I_1 = \frac{P}{V_1} ,
\qquad
R = \frac{V_2^2}{P} ,
\qquad
R' = \frac{V_1}{I_1} = \left( \frac{N_1}{N_2} \right)^2 R
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{N_1}{N_2} &= \frac{230}{12} \approx 19.2 \\[6pt]
I_2 &= \frac{36}{12}\,\mathrm{A} = 3.0\,\mathrm{A} , \qquad I_1 = \frac{36}{230}\,\mathrm{A} \approx 0.16\,\mathrm{A} \\[6pt]
R &= \frac{12^2}{36}\,\Omega = 4.0\,\Omega \\[6pt]
R' &= 19.17^2 \cdot 4.0\,\Omega \approx 1.5 \cdot 10^3\,\Omega
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The turns ratio is the ratio of the voltages, by the theorem on the **ideal transformer**. The load is a resistor, so the power factor is $1$ and the power is the product of the rms values, both at the secondary and at the primary; from this follow the two currents, whose ratio $\dfrac{3.0}{0.157} \approx 19.2$ is the reciprocal of that of the voltages.

Seen from the grid, the system formed by the transformer and the lamp draws the current $I_1$ at the voltage $V_1$, like a resistor of resistance $R' = \dfrac{V_1}{I_1}$. Substituting $V_1 = \dfrac{N_1}{N_2}\,V_2$ and $I_1 = \dfrac{N_2}{N_1}\,I_2$ we obtain $R' = \left( \dfrac{N_1}{N_2} \right)^2 \dfrac{V_2}{I_2} = \left( \dfrac{N_1}{N_2} \right)^2 R$. The transformer therefore multiplies the resistance of the load by the square of the turns ratio; as a check, $\dfrac{230^2}{1469}\,\mathrm{W} \approx 36\,\mathrm{W}$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A power plant must transmit the power $P = 500\,\mathrm{kW}$ to a city through a line of total resistance $R_\ell = 4.0\,\Omega$, with power factor $1$. The generator of the plant supplies an rms voltage of $5.0\,\mathrm{kV}$. Compute the power lost in the line and the voltage drop along the line if the voltage is not transformed, and if instead it is stepped up to $50\,\mathrm{kV}$ with an ideal transformer. Also compute the turns ratio of that transformer.

$$
I = \frac{P}{V} ,
\qquad
P_{\text{lost}} = R_\ell\, I^2 = \frac{R_\ell\, P^2}{V^2} ,
\qquad
\Delta V = R_\ell\, I ,
\qquad
\frac{N_2}{N_1} = \frac{V_2}{V_1}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
V = 5.0\,\mathrm{kV} : \quad I &= \frac{5.0 \cdot 10^5}{5.0 \cdot 10^3}\,\mathrm{A} = 100\,\mathrm{A} , \qquad P_{\text{lost}} = 4.0 \cdot 100^2\,\mathrm{W} = 40\,\mathrm{kW} , \qquad \Delta V = 400\,\mathrm{V} \\[6pt]
V = 50\,\mathrm{kV} : \quad I &= \frac{5.0 \cdot 10^5}{5.0 \cdot 10^4}\,\mathrm{A} = 10\,\mathrm{A} , \qquad P_{\text{lost}} = 4.0 \cdot 10^2\,\mathrm{W} = 400\,\mathrm{W} , \qquad \Delta V = 40\,\mathrm{V} \\[6pt]
\frac{N_2}{N_1} &= \frac{50}{5.0} = 10
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The power to be transmitted is fixed, and with power factor $1$ the rms current in the line is $\dfrac{P}{V}$. The power dissipated by the Joule effect in the conductors is $R_\ell I^2$, and the voltage drop along the line is $R_\ell I$ by Ohm's law.

At $5.0\,\mathrm{kV}$ the line dissipates $40\,\mathrm{kW}$, that is, $8.0\,\%$ of the transmitted power. Stepping up the voltage by a factor of $10$ reduces the current by the same factor and, by the result on the **high-voltage transmission of electrical energy**, the power lost is reduced by a factor of $100$, to $400\,\mathrm{W}$, that is, $0.080\,\%$. The step-up transformer has a secondary with ten times as many turns as the primary; for the ideal transformer the current in the primary, $100\,\mathrm{A}$, is ten times that in the line.

</div>
