## Exercise 1

A capacitor of capacitance $C = 10\,\mu\mathrm{F}$ discharges through an inductor of inductance $L = 0.10\,\mathrm{H}$ and a resistor, connected in series. Compute the critical resistance. With $R = 20\,\Omega$, determine the damping regime and compute the coefficient $\gamma$ and the angular frequency of the damped oscillations, comparing it with the natural angular frequency.

$$
R_c = 2\sqrt{\frac{L}{C}} ,
\qquad
\gamma = \frac{R}{2L} ,
\qquad
\omega_0 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{LC}} ,
\qquad
\omega_s = \sqrt{\omega_0^2 - \gamma^2}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
R_c &= 2\sqrt{\frac{0.10}{10 \cdot 10^{-6}}}\,\Omega = 2 \cdot 100\,\Omega = 200\,\Omega \\[6pt]
\gamma &= \frac{20}{2 \cdot 0.10}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}} = 100\,\mathrm{s^{-1}} \\[6pt]
\omega_0 &= \frac{1}{\sqrt{0.10 \cdot 10 \cdot 10^{-6}}}\,\mathrm{rad/s} = 1000\,\mathrm{rad/s} \\[6pt]
\omega_s &= \sqrt{1000^2 - 100^2}\,\mathrm{rad/s} \approx 995\,\mathrm{rad/s}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

By the theorem on the **damped oscillations of the RLC circuit** the regime depends on the comparison between $R$ and the critical resistance $2\sqrt{\dfrac{L}{C}}$. Since $R = 20\,\Omega$ is smaller than $R_c = 200\,\Omega$, the damping is **weak** and the charge oscillates with an amplitude that decreases as $e^{-\gamma t}$. The angular frequency of the damped oscillations is smaller than the natural one, but the difference is only $0.5\,\%$, because $\gamma$ is one tenth of $\omega_0$ and $\sqrt{1 - 0.01} \approx 0.995$. The ratio $\sqrt{\dfrac{L}{C}}$ has the dimensions of a resistance, since $\dfrac{\mathrm{H}}{\mathrm{F}} = \dfrac{\mathrm{V \cdot s/A}}{\mathrm{A \cdot s/V}} = \Omega^2$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

In the circuit of the previous exercise, with $L = 0.10\,\mathrm{H}$ and $C = 10\,\mu\mathrm{F}$, the resistance equals $R = 5.0\,\Omega$ and the capacitor initially has the charge $Q_0 = 100\,\mu\mathrm{C}$, with zero current. Compute the time after which the amplitude of the charge oscillations has halved, the number of oscillations completed in that time, and the energy remaining in the circuit.

$$
Q_0\,e^{-\gamma t_{\frac{1}{2}}} = \frac{Q_0}{2}
\;\Longrightarrow\;
t_{\frac{1}{2}} = \frac{\ln 2}{\gamma} = \frac{2L \ln 2}{R} ,
\qquad
n = \frac{t_{\frac{1}{2}}}{T_s} = \frac{t_{\frac{1}{2}}\,\omega_s}{2\pi} ,
\qquad
U(t_{\frac{1}{2}}) \approx \frac{1}{4} \cdot \frac{Q_0^2}{2C}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
\gamma &= \frac{5.0}{2 \cdot 0.10}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}} = 25\,\mathrm{s^{-1}} \\[6pt]
t_{\frac{1}{2}} &= \frac{0.693}{25}\,\mathrm{s} \approx 2.8 \cdot 10^{-2}\,\mathrm{s} = 28\,\mathrm{ms} \\[6pt]
\omega_s &= \sqrt{1000^2 - 25^2}\,\mathrm{rad/s} \approx 1000\,\mathrm{rad/s} , \qquad T_s = \frac{2\pi}{\omega_s} \approx 6.3\,\mathrm{ms} \\[6pt]
n &= \frac{27.7}{6.28} \approx 4.4 \\[6pt]
U_0 &= \frac{(100 \cdot 10^{-6})^2}{2 \cdot 10 \cdot 10^{-6}}\,\mathrm{J} = 5.0 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{J} , \qquad U(t_{\frac{1}{2}}) \approx 1.3 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{J}
\end{aligned}
$$

::grafico{f="exp(-0.025*x)*cos(0.99969*x)" curve="t-51*floor(t/51),(1-2*floor(t/51))*exp(-0.025*(t-51*floor(t/51))),0,101.9" segmenti="27.7,0,27.7,0.5;0,0.5,27.7,0.5" punti="27.7,0.5,pieno" xmin=-0.5 xmax=50 ymin=-1.3 ymax=1.4 nomi="t,q" legenda="q(t);\pm Q_0 e^{-\gamma t}" titolo="Charge in units of $Q_0$ as a function of time in milliseconds: the amplitude halves after $\dfrac{\ln 2}{\gamma} \approx 27.7\,\mathrm{ms}$, that is, after about four and a half oscillations"}

<div class="spiegazione">

The resistance is much smaller than the critical resistance of $200\,\Omega$, so the damping is weak and the charge is $q(t) = A\,e^{-\gamma t}\cos(\omega_s t + \varphi)$. The amplitude of the oscillations is proportional to $e^{-\gamma t}$, therefore it halves when $\gamma t = \ln 2$. Since $\gamma$ is much smaller than $\omega_0$, the damped angular frequency practically coincides with the natural one, and the period is about $6.3\,\mathrm{ms}$; in $28\,\mathrm{ms}$ the circuit completes about four and a half oscillations.

By the **energy dissipated in the resistor**, with weak damping the energy is that of an $LC$ circuit with maximum charge $Q_0 e^{-\gamma t}$, and it is proportional to the square of the amplitude. When the amplitude has halved the energy is one quarter of the initial one, about $1.3 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{J}$; the missing three quarters have been dissipated by the Joule effect.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A free $RLC$ circuit has $L = 2.0\,\mathrm{mH}$, $C = 0.20\,\mu\mathrm{F}$ and $R = 1.0\,\Omega$. Compute the fraction of energy dissipated during one period of oscillation and the quality factor $Q = \dfrac{\omega_0 L}{R}$, and verify that the fraction is about $\dfrac{2\pi}{Q}$.

$$
\frac{\Delta U}{U} = 1 - e^{-2\gamma T} \approx 2\gamma T = \frac{R}{L} \cdot \frac{2\pi}{\omega_0} = \frac{2\pi}{Q} ,
\qquad
\gamma = \frac{R}{2L} ,
\qquad
T = \frac{2\pi}{\omega_0}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
\omega_0 &= \frac{1}{\sqrt{2.0 \cdot 10^{-3} \cdot 0.20 \cdot 10^{-6}}}\,\mathrm{rad/s} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{4.0 \cdot 10^{-10}}}\,\mathrm{rad/s} = 5.0 \cdot 10^4\,\mathrm{rad/s} \\[6pt]
\gamma &= \frac{1.0}{2 \cdot 2.0 \cdot 10^{-3}}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}} = 250\,\mathrm{s^{-1}} , \qquad T = \frac{2\pi}{5.0 \cdot 10^4}\,\mathrm{s} \approx 1.26 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{s} \\[6pt]
\frac{\Delta U}{U} &= 1 - e^{-2 \cdot 250 \cdot 1.26 \cdot 10^{-4}} = 1 - e^{-0.0628} \approx 0.061 \\[6pt]
Q &= \frac{5.0 \cdot 10^4 \cdot 2.0 \cdot 10^{-3}}{1.0} = 100 , \qquad \frac{2\pi}{Q} \approx 0.063
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

We have $\gamma = 250\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$, much smaller than $\omega_0 = 5.0 \cdot 10^4\,\mathrm{rad/s}$, so the damping is weak, the period of the oscillations practically coincides with $\dfrac{2\pi}{\omega_0}$ and the energy decreases as $e^{-2\gamma t}$, by the remark on the **energy dissipated in the resistor**. After one period the energy is $U\,e^{-2\gamma T}$, so the fraction dissipated is $1 - e^{-2\gamma T}$, about $6.1\,\%$.

The exponent $2\gamma T \approx 0.063$ is small, therefore $e^{-2\gamma T} \approx 1 - 2\gamma T$ and the fraction dissipated is about $2\gamma T$. Substituting $\gamma = \dfrac{R}{2L}$ and $T = \dfrac{2\pi}{\omega_0}$ we obtain $\dfrac{2\pi R}{\omega_0 L} = \dfrac{2\pi}{Q}$, about $6.3\,\%$. The quality factor therefore measures how many oscillations the circuit completes before dissipating a significant part of its energy, as in the damped mechanical oscillator.

</div>
