## Exercise 1

An electric heater, which can be modeled as a resistor, absorbs the average power $P = 1.0\,\mathrm{kW}$ when connected to the grid, with rms voltage $V_{\text{rms}} = 230\,\mathrm{V}$ and frequency $f = 50\,\mathrm{Hz}$. Compute the resistance, the rms current, the current amplitude and the maximum value of the instantaneous power, and write the expression of $i(t)$ taking $v(t) = V_0 \sin(\omega t)$.

$$
P = \frac{V_{\text{rms}}^2}{R} \;\Longrightarrow\; R = \frac{V_{\text{rms}}^2}{P} ,
\qquad
I_{\text{rms}} = \frac{V_{\text{rms}}}{R} ,
\qquad
I_0 = \sqrt{2}\, I_{\text{rms}} ,
\qquad
p_{\max} = V_0 I_0 = 2 V_{\text{rms}} I_{\text{rms}} = 2P
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
R &= \frac{230^2}{1.0 \cdot 10^3}\,\Omega \approx 52.9\,\Omega \approx 53\,\Omega \\[6pt]
I_{\text{rms}} &= \frac{230}{52.9}\,\mathrm{A} \approx 4.3\,\mathrm{A} \\[6pt]
I_0 &= \sqrt{2} \cdot 4.35\,\mathrm{A} \approx 6.1\,\mathrm{A} \\[6pt]
p_{\max} &= 2 \cdot 1.0\,\mathrm{kW} = 2.0\,\mathrm{kW} \\[6pt]
i(t) &\approx 6.1 \sin(314\,t)\,\mathrm{A} , \quad t \text{ in seconds}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

For a resistor the average power is $R\,I_{\text{rms}}^2$, by the definition of **rms value**, and since $I_{\text{rms}} = \dfrac{V_{\text{rms}}}{R}$ it can also be written as $\dfrac{V_{\text{rms}}^2}{R}$; from this we obtain $R$. The current amplitude is $\sqrt{2}$ times the rms value, by the theorem on the rms value of a sinusoidal quantity.

In the resistor the current is in phase with the voltage, so the instantaneous power $p = V_0 I_0 \sin^2(\omega t)$ oscillates between $0$ and $V_0 I_0$. The maximum value is $V_0 I_0 = \sqrt{2}\,V_{\text{rms}} \cdot \sqrt{2}\,I_{\text{rms}} = 2P$, that is, twice the average power. The angular frequency is $\omega = 2\pi \cdot 50\,\mathrm{rad/s} \approx 314\,\mathrm{rad/s}$, and the phase of the current is the same as that of the voltage.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A capacitor of capacitance $C = 15\,\mu\mathrm{F}$ is connected to the grid, with $V_{\text{rms}} = 230\,\mathrm{V}$ and $f = 50\,\mathrm{Hz}$. Compute the capacitive reactance, the rms current and the expression of $i(t)$ taking $v(t) = V_0\sin(\omega t)$. Then compute the rms current if the frequency doubles, with the same rms voltage.

$$
X_C = \frac{1}{\omega C} = \frac{1}{2\pi f C} ,
\qquad
I_{\text{rms}} = \frac{V_{\text{rms}}}{X_C} = 2\pi f C\, V_{\text{rms}} ,
\qquad
i(t) = \frac{V_0}{X_C}\sin\left( \omega t + \frac{\pi}{2} \right)
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
X_C &= \frac{1}{2\pi \cdot 50 \cdot 15 \cdot 10^{-6}}\,\Omega \approx 212\,\Omega \\[6pt]
I_{\text{rms}} &= \frac{230}{212}\,\mathrm{A} \approx 1.08\,\mathrm{A} \\[6pt]
I_0 &= \sqrt{2} \cdot 1.08\,\mathrm{A} \approx 1.53\,\mathrm{A} \\[6pt]
i(t) &\approx 1.53 \sin\left( 314\,t + \frac{\pi}{2} \right)\mathrm{A} \\[6pt]
I'_{\text{rms}} &= 2\, I_{\text{rms}} \approx 2.17\,\mathrm{A}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

By the theorem on the **capacitor in AC** the amplitudes of the voltage and the current are related by $V_0 = X_C I_0$, and dividing by $\sqrt{2}$ the same relation holds between the rms values. The current leads the voltage by $\dfrac{\pi}{2}$, so its phase is $+\dfrac{\pi}{2}$.

The rms current is proportional to the frequency, because $X_C$ is inversely proportional to $f$. Doubling the frequency halves the reactance, to about $106\,\Omega$, and the rms current **doubles**, to about $2.2\,\mathrm{A}$. In direct current, in the limit $f \to 0$, the reactance tends to infinity and in steady state the current is zero.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A coil of negligible resistance, connected to a source with $V_{\text{rms}} = 24\,\mathrm{V}$ and $f = 50\,\mathrm{Hz}$, carries the rms current $I_{\text{rms}} = 0.40\,\mathrm{A}$. Compute the reactance and the inductance of the coil, write $i(t)$ taking $v(t) = V_0 \sin(\omega t)$, and compute the rms current if the frequency of the source becomes $200\,\mathrm{Hz}$ with the same rms voltage.

$$
X_L = \frac{V_{\text{rms}}}{I_{\text{rms}}} ,
\qquad
L = \frac{X_L}{2\pi f} ,
\qquad
i(t) = \sqrt{2}\, I_{\text{rms}} \sin\left( \omega t - \frac{\pi}{2} \right) ,
\qquad
I'_{\text{rms}} = \frac{V_{\text{rms}}}{2\pi f' L} = I_{\text{rms}}\,\frac{f}{f'}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
X_L &= \frac{24}{0.40}\,\Omega = 60\,\Omega \\[6pt]
L &= \frac{60}{2\pi \cdot 50}\,\mathrm{H} \approx 0.19\,\mathrm{H} \\[6pt]
i(t) &\approx 0.57 \sin\left( 314\,t - \frac{\pi}{2} \right)\mathrm{A} \\[6pt]
I'_{\text{rms}} &= 0.40 \cdot \frac{50}{200}\,\mathrm{A} = 0.10\,\mathrm{A}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The coil has negligible resistance, so it is an ideal inductor and by the theorem on the **inductor in AC** the ratio of the rms values of voltage and current is the inductive reactance $X_L = \omega L$. From $X_L$ we obtain $L$ by dividing by the angular frequency $2\pi f$. The current lags the voltage by $\dfrac{\pi}{2}$, and its amplitude is $\sqrt{2} \cdot 0.40\,\mathrm{A} \approx 0.57\,\mathrm{A}$.

At four times the frequency the inductive reactance becomes four times larger, $240\,\Omega$, because it is proportional to $f$; with the same voltage the rms current therefore becomes one quarter of the initial one. The behavior is opposite to that of the capacitor, whose current grows with the frequency.

</div>
