## Exercise 1

An isolated conducting sphere of radius $R = 10\,\mathrm{cm}$ carries the charge $Q = 20\,\mathrm{nC}$. Compute the surface charge density, the electric field at the center of the sphere and at points just outside the surface, and the potential of the sphere, with the reference at infinity.

$$
\sigma = \frac{Q}{4\pi R^2} ,
\qquad
E_{\text{center}} = 0 ,
\qquad
E_{\text{surf}} = \frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon_0} ,
\qquad
V = \frac{kQ}{R}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
\sigma &= \frac{20 \cdot 10^{-9}}{4\pi \cdot 0.10^2}\,\mathrm{C/m^2} \approx 1.6 \cdot 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{C/m^2} \\[6pt]
E_{\text{surf}} &= \frac{1.59 \cdot 10^{-7}}{8.85 \cdot 10^{-12}}\,\mathrm{V/m} \approx 1.8 \cdot 10^{4}\,\mathrm{V/m} \\[6pt]
V &= \frac{8.99 \cdot 10^{9} \cdot 20 \cdot 10^{-9}}{0.10}\,\mathrm{V} \approx 1.8 \cdot 10^{3}\,\mathrm{V}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The sphere is in equilibrium, therefore by the theorem on the **charge on the surface** all the charge lies on the surface and, by the symmetry of the isolated sphere, it is distributed uniformly there; the density is obtained by dividing the charge by the area $4\pi R^2$. The center is a point inside the material, hence the field there is **zero**.

At points just outside the surface **Coulomb's theorem** applies, $E = \dfrac{\sigma}{\varepsilon_0}$, with the field directed radially outward because $\sigma > 0$. The result coincides with the field of a point charge $Q$ placed at the center, $\dfrac{kQ}{R^2} \approx 1.8 \cdot 10^{4}\,\mathrm{V/m}$, as it must by Gauss's law. The conductor is equipotential, and its potential is that of the surface, $\dfrac{kQ}{R}$.

</div>

$$
\begin{cases}
Q_1 + Q_2 = Q \\[4pt]
\dfrac{k Q_1}{R_1} = \dfrac{k Q_2}{R_2}
\end{cases}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
Q_1 = \frac{R_1}{R_1 + R_2}\,Q ,
\qquad
Q_2 = \frac{R_2}{R_1 + R_2}\,Q
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
Q_1 &= \frac{2.0}{8.0} \cdot 40\,\mathrm{nC} = 10\,\mathrm{nC} , \qquad Q_2 = 30\,\mathrm{nC} \\[6pt]
\sigma_1 &= \frac{Q_1}{4\pi R_1^2} = \frac{10 \cdot 10^{-9}}{4\pi \cdot 0.020^2}\,\mathrm{C/m^2} \approx 2.0 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\mathrm{C/m^2} \\[6pt]
\sigma_2 &= \frac{Q_2}{4\pi R_2^2} = \frac{30 \cdot 10^{-9}}{4\pi \cdot 0.060^2}\,\mathrm{C/m^2} \approx 6.6 \cdot 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{C/m^2} \\[6pt]
E_1 &= \frac{\sigma_1}{\varepsilon_0} \approx 2.2 \cdot 10^{5}\,\mathrm{V/m} , \qquad E_2 = \frac{\sigma_2}{\varepsilon_0} \approx 7.5 \cdot 10^{4}\,\mathrm{V/m} \\[6pt]
V &= \frac{k Q_1}{R_1} = \frac{8.99 \cdot 10^{9} \cdot 10 \cdot 10^{-9}}{0.020}\,\mathrm{V} \approx 4.5 \cdot 10^{3}\,\mathrm{V}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The two spheres and the wire form a single conductor, therefore by the theorem on the **equipotential conductor** the spheres have the same potential. The spheres are very far apart, hence the potential of each is that of an isolated sphere with its own charge, $\dfrac{kQ_i}{R_i}$; the wire is thin and its charge is negligible, therefore the total charge is divided between the two spheres. The two conditions, which must hold together, form the system, which gives charges proportional to the radii.

The large sphere carries three times the charge, while its density is one third of that of the small sphere, because the area grows with the square of the radius. The ratio $\dfrac{\sigma_1}{\sigma_2} = 3 = \dfrac{R_2}{R_1}$ is the **effect of sharp points**, and by Coulomb's theorem the field on the small sphere is also three times larger. As a check, $\dfrac{kQ_2}{R_2} = \dfrac{8.99 \cdot 10^{9} \cdot 30 \cdot 10^{-9}}{0.060}\,\mathrm{V} \approx 4.5 \cdot 10^{3}\,\mathrm{V}$, the same potential.

</div>

<div class="spiegazione">

A spherical surface of radius between $a$ and $b$ lies in the material of the shell, where the field is zero, therefore by Gauss's law it encloses zero total charge. It contains the charge $q$ and the charge of the inner surface, hence $Q_{\text{int}} = -q = +2.0\,\mathrm{nC}$, as predicted by the result on the **cavity in a conductor**. The net charge of the shell is conserved, therefore $Q_g - Q_{\text{int}} = Q_g + q$ remains on the outer surface. The charge $q$ is at the center, hence by symmetry both distributions are uniform.

The field is radial. For $r < a$ the Gaussian sphere encloses only $q$, which is negative, therefore the field is $\dfrac{k|q|}{r^2}$ and is directed toward the center. The point at $4.5\,\mathrm{cm}$ is in the material of the shell, where the field is **zero**. For $r > b$ the enclosed charge is $q + Q_{\text{int}} + Q_{\text{ext}} = Q_g + q = 4.0\,\mathrm{nC}$, therefore the field is that of a positive point charge of $4.0\,\mathrm{nC}$ placed at the center, directed outward.
