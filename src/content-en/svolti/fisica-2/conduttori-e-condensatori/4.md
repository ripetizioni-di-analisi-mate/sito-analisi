## Exercise 1

Compute the capacitance of an isolated conducting sphere of radius $10\,\mathrm{cm}$ and the capacitance of the Earth, considered as a conducting sphere of radius $R_T = 6.37 \cdot 10^{6}\,\mathrm{m}$. Also compute the charge that would bring the Earth to the potential of $1.0\,\mathrm{kV}$ relative to infinity.

$$
C = 4\pi\varepsilon_0 R = \frac{R}{k} , \qquad Q = C\,V
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
C_{\text{sphere}} &= \frac{0.10}{8.99 \cdot 10^{9}}\,\mathrm{F} \approx 1.1 \cdot 10^{-11}\,\mathrm{F} = 11\,\mathrm{pF} \\[6pt]
C_T &= \frac{6.37 \cdot 10^{6}}{8.99 \cdot 10^{9}}\,\mathrm{F} \approx 7.1 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{F} \\[6pt]
Q &= 7.09 \cdot 10^{-4} \cdot 1.0 \cdot 10^{3}\,\mathrm{C} \approx 0.71\,\mathrm{C}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

For an isolated sphere the charge is distributed uniformly over the surface, and outside the field is that of a point charge at the center; the potential of the sphere is hence $\dfrac{kQ}{R}$ and the **capacitance of the sphere** is $\dfrac{R}{k}$. The capacitance is proportional to the radius, therefore the Earth, with a radius about $6 \cdot 10^{7}$ times larger, has a capacitance slightly below one millifarad.

The charge that brings the Earth to $1.0\,\mathrm{kV}$ is about $0.71\,\mathrm{C}$. The comparison shows that capacitors of a few hundred microfarads in circuits have capacitances of the same order of magnitude as that of an isolated planet, because in a capacitor the two nearby plates of opposite sign greatly reduce the potential difference for the same charge.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A spherical capacitor consists of a conducting sphere of radius $R_1 = 5.0\,\mathrm{cm}$ and a concentric conducting shell of inner radius $R_2 = 6.0\,\mathrm{cm}$, with vacuum in between, and is charged to the potential difference $\Delta V = 2.0\,\mathrm{kV}$. Compute the capacitance, the charge and the magnitude of the electric field near the inner sphere and near the shell. Compare the capacitance with that of a parallel-plate capacitor with plates $R_2 - R_1$ apart.

$$
C = 4\pi\varepsilon_0\,\frac{R_1 R_2}{R_2 - R_1} = \frac{1}{k}\,\frac{R_1 R_2}{R_2 - R_1} , \qquad Q = C\,\Delta V , \qquad E(r) = \frac{kQ}{r^2}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
C &= \frac{1}{8.99 \cdot 10^{9}} \cdot \frac{0.050 \cdot 0.060}{0.010}\,\mathrm{F} \approx 3.3 \cdot 10^{-11}\,\mathrm{F} = 33\,\mathrm{pF} \\[6pt]
Q &= 3.34 \cdot 10^{-11} \cdot 2.0 \cdot 10^{3}\,\mathrm{C} \approx 6.7 \cdot 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{C} \\[6pt]
E(R_1) &= \frac{8.99 \cdot 10^{9} \cdot 6.67 \cdot 10^{-8}}{0.050^2}\,\mathrm{V/m} \approx 2.4 \cdot 10^{5}\,\mathrm{V/m} \\[6pt]
E(R_2) &= \frac{8.99 \cdot 10^{9} \cdot 6.67 \cdot 10^{-8}}{0.060^2}\,\mathrm{V/m} \approx 1.7 \cdot 10^{5}\,\mathrm{V/m}
\end{aligned}
$$

::grafico{f="2.4*(5/x)^2+0*sqrt((x-5)*(6-x))" punti="5,2.4,pieno;6,1.667,pieno;5,0,vuoto;6,0,vuoto" segmenti="5,0,5,2.4;6,0,6,1.667" xmin=0 xmax=8 ymin=-0.3 ymax=3 nomi="r,E" legenda="E(r)" titolo="Field of the spherical capacitor, with $r$ in centimeters and $E$ in $10^5\,\mathrm{V/m}$: it decreases from $2.4$ to $1.7$ between the plates and is zero elsewhere"}

<div class="spiegazione">

The plates are concentric, therefore the formula of the **spherical capacitor** applies, which follows from Gauss's law; indeed, between the plates the field is that of the charge $Q$ of the inner sphere, concentrated at the center. The charge is obtained from the definition of capacitance.

The field decreases as $\dfrac{1}{r^2}$, hence it is maximum near the inner sphere and the ratio of the two values is $\left( \dfrac{6.0}{5.0} \right)^2 = 1.44$. The mean value of the field, $\dfrac{\Delta V}{R_2 - R_1} = 2.0 \cdot 10^{5}\,\mathrm{V/m}$, lies between the two, as it must.

A parallel-plate capacitor with plates of area $4\pi R_1^2$ at a distance of $1.0\,\mathrm{cm}$ would have $C = \dfrac{\varepsilon_0 \cdot 4\pi R_1^2}{R_2 - R_1} \approx 28\,\mathrm{pF}$, about $17\,\%$ less. The parallel-plate approximation is therefore rough when the distance is one fifth of the radius; the exact result coincides with the parallel-plate formula if the area $4\pi R_1 R_2$ is used.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A coaxial cable of length $\ell = 10\,\mathrm{m}$ has an inner conductor of radius $a = 0.50\,\mathrm{mm}$ and an outer conductor of inner radius $b = 2.0\,\mathrm{mm}$, with vacuum in between. Compute the capacitance per unit length and the capacitance of the cable. With a potential difference $\Delta V = 100\,\mathrm{V}$ between the conductors, compute the linear charge density and the maximum field.

$$
\frac{C}{\ell} = \frac{2\pi\varepsilon_0}{\ln \dfrac{b}{a}} , \qquad \lambda = \frac{C}{\ell}\,\Delta V , \qquad E(r) = \frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0 r} = \frac{\Delta V}{r \ln \dfrac{b}{a}}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{C}{\ell} &= \frac{2\pi \cdot 8.85 \cdot 10^{-12}}{\ln 4}\,\mathrm{F/m} = \frac{5.56 \cdot 10^{-11}}{1.386}\,\mathrm{F/m} \approx 40\,\mathrm{pF/m} \\[6pt]
C &= 40.1 \cdot 10\,\mathrm{pF} \approx 4.0 \cdot 10^{2}\,\mathrm{pF} \\[6pt]
\lambda &= 4.01 \cdot 10^{-11} \cdot 100\,\mathrm{C/m} \approx 4.0 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\mathrm{C/m} \\[6pt]
E_{\max} &= E(a) = \frac{100}{0.50 \cdot 10^{-3} \cdot 1.386}\,\mathrm{V/m} \approx 1.4 \cdot 10^{5}\,\mathrm{V/m}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The cable is much longer than the outer radius, therefore edge effects are negligible and the formula of the **cylindrical capacitor** applies. The capacitance per unit length depends only on the ratio $\dfrac{b}{a} = 4$, and the capacitance of the cable is proportional to its length.

By Gauss's law the field between the conductors is $\dfrac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0 r}$, which decreases as $\dfrac{1}{r}$; the field is therefore maximum at the surface of the inner conductor. Substituting $\lambda = \dfrac{2\pi\varepsilon_0\,\Delta V}{\ln \dfrac{b}{a}}$ gives the second form of $E(r)$, which uses the voltage directly. At the outer surface the field is four times smaller, about $3.6 \cdot 10^{4}\,\mathrm{V/m}$.

</div>

## Exercise 4

A coaxial cable is to be designed with inner radius of the outer conductor $b = 2.0\,\mathrm{cm}$ and potential difference $\Delta V = 10\,\mathrm{kV}$ between the conductors. Determine the radius $a$ of the inner conductor that minimizes the maximum field in the cable, and compute this field.

$$
E_{\max}(a) = \frac{\Delta V}{a \ln \dfrac{b}{a}} , \qquad g(a) = a \ln \frac{b}{a} = a \ln b - a \ln a , \qquad 0 < a < b
$$
$$
g'(a) = \ln b - \ln a - 1 = \ln \frac{b}{a} - 1 = 0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
a = \frac{b}{e} ,
\qquad
E_{\max} = \frac{e\,\Delta V}{b}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
a &= \frac{2.0}{2.718}\,\mathrm{cm} \approx 0.74\,\mathrm{cm} \\[6pt]
E_{\max} &= \frac{2.718 \cdot 1.0 \cdot 10^{4}}{0.020}\,\mathrm{V/m} \approx 1.4 \cdot 10^{6}\,\mathrm{V/m}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The maximum field occurs at the surface of the inner conductor and is $\dfrac{\Delta V}{a \ln \dfrac{b}{a}}$, as follows from Gauss's law for the cylindrical capacitor. For fixed $\Delta V$, minimizing $E_{\max}$ is equivalent to maximizing the denominator $g(a)$. With a thin inner conductor the field near the wire is intense, by the effect of sharp points; with $a$ close to $b$ the conductors are too close. There is therefore an optimal intermediate value.

The derivative $g'(a) = \ln \dfrac{b}{a} - 1$ is positive for $a < \dfrac{b}{e}$ and negative for $a > \dfrac{b}{e}$, hence $g$ has its **maximum** at $a = \dfrac{b}{e}$ and the maximum field is minimum there. At that point $\ln \dfrac{b}{a} = 1$, therefore $E_{\max} = \dfrac{\Delta V}{a} = \dfrac{e\,\Delta V}{b}$. The value $1.4 \cdot 10^{6}\,\mathrm{V/m}$ is smaller than the dielectric strength of air, about $3 \cdot 10^{6}\,\mathrm{V/m}$; with $a = 0.50\,\mathrm{cm}$ or with $a = 1.0\,\mathrm{cm}$ the maximum field would be about $1.44 \cdot 10^{6}\,\mathrm{V/m}$, larger than the optimal value $1.36 \cdot 10^{6}\,\mathrm{V/m}$. For the study of the sign of the derivative see the topic on derivatives and maxima and minima in Analysis 1.

</div>
