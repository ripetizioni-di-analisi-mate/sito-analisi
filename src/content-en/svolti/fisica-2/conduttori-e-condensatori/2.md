## Exercise 1

A parallel-plate capacitor has square plates of side $\ell = 20\,\mathrm{cm}$ at a distance $d = 1.0\,\mathrm{mm}$, in vacuum, and is connected to a source that maintains the potential difference $\Delta V = 50\,\mathrm{V}$. Compute the capacitance, the charge on the plates, the surface charge density and the electric field between the plates.

$$
C = \frac{\varepsilon_0 A}{d} , \qquad A = \ell^2 , \qquad Q = C\,\Delta V , \qquad \sigma = \frac{Q}{A} , \qquad E = \frac{\Delta V}{d}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
C &= \frac{8.85 \cdot 10^{-12} \cdot 0.040}{1.0 \cdot 10^{-3}}\,\mathrm{F} \approx 3.5 \cdot 10^{-10}\,\mathrm{F} = 354\,\mathrm{pF} \\[6pt]
Q &= 3.54 \cdot 10^{-10} \cdot 50\,\mathrm{C} \approx 1.8 \cdot 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{C} = 18\,\mathrm{nC} \\[6pt]
\sigma &= \frac{1.77 \cdot 10^{-8}}{0.040}\,\mathrm{C/m^2} \approx 4.4 \cdot 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{C/m^2} \\[6pt]
E &= \frac{50}{1.0 \cdot 10^{-3}}\,\mathrm{V/m} = 5.0 \cdot 10^{4}\,\mathrm{V/m}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The distance between the plates is much smaller than the side, therefore edge effects are negligible and the formula of the **parallel-plate capacitor** applies, with the area $A = 0.20^2\,\mathrm{m^2} = 0.040\,\mathrm{m^2}$. The source fixes the potential difference, hence the charge follows from the definition of capacitance, $Q = C\,\Delta V$. The density is the charge divided by the area, because the charges are distributed uniformly on the facing sides.

The field between the plates is uniform, therefore it is the ratio of the potential difference to the distance. As a check, Coulomb's theorem gives $E = \dfrac{\sigma}{\varepsilon_0} = \dfrac{4.43 \cdot 10^{-7}}{8.85 \cdot 10^{-12}}\,\mathrm{V/m} = 5.0 \cdot 10^{4}\,\mathrm{V/m}$, the same value.

</div>

## Exercise 2

We want to build a parallel-plate capacitor in vacuum with capacitance $C = 1.0\,\mu\mathrm{F}$, with the plates at a distance $d = 0.10\,\mathrm{mm}$. Compute the area of the plates and the side of square plates. Also compute the charge and the field between the plates when the capacitor is connected to $12\,\mathrm{V}$.

$$
C = \frac{\varepsilon_0 A}{d}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
A = \frac{C\,d}{\varepsilon_0} , \qquad \ell = \sqrt{A}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
A &= \frac{1.0 \cdot 10^{-6} \cdot 1.0 \cdot 10^{-4}}{8.85 \cdot 10^{-12}}\,\mathrm{m^2} \approx 11\,\mathrm{m^2} \\[6pt]
\ell &= \sqrt{11.3}\,\mathrm{m} \approx 3.4\,\mathrm{m} \\[6pt]
Q &= C\,\Delta V = 1.0 \cdot 10^{-6} \cdot 12\,\mathrm{C} = 12\,\mu\mathrm{C} \\[6pt]
E &= \frac{\Delta V}{d} = \frac{12}{1.0 \cdot 10^{-4}}\,\mathrm{V/m} = 1.2 \cdot 10^{5}\,\mathrm{V/m}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The area is obtained from the formula of the **parallel-plate capacitor**, since capacitance and distance are given. About $11\,\mathrm{m^2}$ is needed, that is, two square sheets more than three meters on a side held a tenth of a millimeter apart. The result shows that the microfarad is a large capacitance for a capacitor in vacuum; in real capacitors the plates are thin rolled-up strips separated by a dielectric, which multiplies the capacitance by $\varepsilon_r$.

The charge and the field follow from the definition of capacitance and from the uniformity of the field. The field, $1.2 \cdot 10^{5}\,\mathrm{V/m}$, is smaller than the dielectric strength of air, about $3 \cdot 10^{6}\,\mathrm{V/m}$, therefore the voltage of $12\,\mathrm{V}$ can be applied even with air between the plates.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A parallel-plate capacitor with plates of area $A = 0.020\,\mathrm{m^2}$ at a distance $d_1 = 2.0\,\mathrm{mm}$ is charged by connecting it to a $\Delta V_1 = 100\,\mathrm{V}$ source. The plates are then moved to the distance $d_2 = 5.0\,\mathrm{mm}$. Compute capacitance, charge, potential difference and field after the displacement in two cases: (a) the capacitor is disconnected from the source before the displacement; (b) the capacitor remains connected to the source.

$$
C_1 = \frac{\varepsilon_0 A}{d_1} , \qquad C_2 = \frac{\varepsilon_0 A}{d_2} = \frac{d_1}{d_2}\,C_1 , \qquad Q_1 = C_1\,\Delta V_1
$$
$$
\text{(a)} \quad Q_2 = Q_1 , \qquad \Delta V_2 = \frac{Q_1}{C_2} = \frac{d_2}{d_1}\,\Delta V_1 , \qquad E_2 = \frac{\Delta V_2}{d_2} = \frac{\Delta V_1}{d_1}
$$
$$
\text{(b)} \quad \Delta V_2 = \Delta V_1 , \qquad Q_2 = C_2\,\Delta V_1 = \frac{d_1}{d_2}\,Q_1 , \qquad E_2 = \frac{\Delta V_1}{d_2}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
C_1 &= \frac{8.85 \cdot 10^{-12} \cdot 0.020}{2.0 \cdot 10^{-3}}\,\mathrm{F} = 88.5\,\mathrm{pF} , \qquad C_2 = \frac{2.0}{5.0} \cdot 88.5\,\mathrm{pF} = 35.4\,\mathrm{pF} \\[6pt]
Q_1 &= 88.5 \cdot 10^{-12} \cdot 100\,\mathrm{C} \approx 8.9\,\mathrm{nC} \\[6pt]
\text{(a)} \quad \Delta V_2 &= \frac{5.0}{2.0} \cdot 100\,\mathrm{V} = 250\,\mathrm{V} , \qquad E_2 = \frac{100}{2.0 \cdot 10^{-3}}\,\mathrm{V/m} = 5.0 \cdot 10^{4}\,\mathrm{V/m} \\[6pt]
\text{(b)} \quad Q_2 &= \frac{2.0}{5.0} \cdot 8.85\,\mathrm{nC} \approx 3.5\,\mathrm{nC} , \qquad E_2 = \frac{100}{5.0 \cdot 10^{-3}}\,\mathrm{V/m} = 2.0 \cdot 10^{4}\,\mathrm{V/m}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

In both cases the capacitance depends only on the geometry, therefore it goes from $88.5\,\mathrm{pF}$ to $35.4\,\mathrm{pF}$, that is, it is reduced by the ratio $\dfrac{d_1}{d_2}$. The problem is solved by identifying the quantity that stays constant.

In case (a) the capacitor is isolated, hence the charge is conserved. The potential difference $\dfrac{Q}{C}$ grows by the same ratio by which the capacitance decreases, and becomes $250\,\mathrm{V}$. The field **does not change**, because it depends only on the charge density, $E = \dfrac{\sigma}{\varepsilon_0}$, which stays the same.

In case (b) the source maintains $100\,\mathrm{V}$, therefore the charge drops to $C_2\,\Delta V_1 \approx 3.5\,\mathrm{nC}$; the source takes back the excess charge. The field, equal to $\dfrac{\Delta V}{d}$, decreases to $2.0 \cdot 10^{4}\,\mathrm{V/m}$.

</div>
