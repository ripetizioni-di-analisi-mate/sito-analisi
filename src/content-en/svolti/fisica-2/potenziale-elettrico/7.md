## Exercise 1

In a region of space the potential depends only on $x$ and equals $V(x) = a x^2 - b x$, with $a = 200\,\mathrm{V/m^2}$ and $b = 80\,\mathrm{V/m}$. Compute the electric field as a function of $x$, the point where the field is zero and the force on an electron placed at $x = 1.0\,\mathrm{m}$.

$$
E_x = -\frac{dV}{dx} = -(2ax - b) = b - 2ax ,
\qquad
E_y = E_z = 0
$$
$$
E_x(x_0) = 0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad x_0 = \frac{b}{2a} ,
\qquad
F_x = -e\, E_x
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
x_0 &= \frac{80}{2 \cdot 200}\,\mathrm{m} = 0.20\,\mathrm{m} \\[6pt]
E_x(1.0\,\mathrm{m}) &= (80 - 400 \cdot 1.0)\,\mathrm{V/m} = -320\,\mathrm{V/m} \\[6pt]
F_x &= -1.60 \cdot 10^{-19} \cdot (-320)\,\mathrm{N} \approx 5.1 \cdot 10^{-17}\,\mathrm{N}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The potential does not depend on $y$ and $z$, so by the **field as the gradient of the potential** the components $E_y$ and $E_z$ are zero and $E_x$ is the opposite of the derivative of $V$ with respect to $x$. The field vanishes at the point where the potential has zero derivative, that is, at the vertex of the parabola $V(x)$, which is a minimum because $a > 0$. At $x = 1.0\,\mathrm{m}$ the component of the field is negative, so the field points toward decreasing $x$; the electron has negative charge, therefore the force is opposite to the field and is directed toward **increasing** $x$, that is, toward higher potentials.

</div>

## Exercise 2

In a charge-free region the potential is $V(x, y, z) = a\,xy - b\,z$, with $a = 40\,\mathrm{V/m^2}$ and $b = 25\,\mathrm{V/m}$. Compute the electric field at the point $P = (1.0,\ 2.0,\ 0)\,\mathrm{m}$, its magnitude, the force on a charge $q = 2.0\,\mu\mathrm{C}$ placed at $P$ and the equation of the equipotential surface through $P$.

$$
\vec{E} = -\nabla V = -\left( \frac{\partial V}{\partial x}, \frac{\partial V}{\partial y}, \frac{\partial V}{\partial z} \right) = \left( -a y,\ -a x,\ b \right)
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
\vec{E}(P) &= (-40 \cdot 2.0,\ -40 \cdot 1.0,\ 25)\,\mathrm{V/m} = (-80,\ -40,\ 25)\,\mathrm{V/m} \\[6pt]
|\vec{E}(P)| &= \sqrt{80^2 + 40^2 + 25^2}\,\mathrm{V/m} = \sqrt{8625}\,\mathrm{V/m} \approx 93\,\mathrm{V/m} \\[6pt]
|\vec{F}| &= q\, |\vec{E}(P)| = 2.0 \cdot 10^{-6} \cdot 92.9\,\mathrm{N} \approx 1.9 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{N}
\end{aligned}
$$
$$
V(P) = 40 \cdot 1.0 \cdot 2.0\,\mathrm{V} = 80\,\mathrm{V}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
40\, xy - 25\, z = 80
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

By the **field as the gradient of the potential** each component of the field is the opposite of the partial derivative of $V$ with respect to the corresponding coordinate: the derivative of $axy$ with respect to $x$ is $ay$, the one with respect to $y$ is $ax$, and only the term $-bz$ depends on $z$. The force on the positive charge has the direction of the field. The equipotential surface through $P$ is the set of points where $V$ takes the value $V(P) = 80\,\mathrm{V}$, that is, with the coordinates in meters, the surface with equation $40\, xy - 25\, z = 80$; by the theorem on **field and equipotential surfaces** the field at $P$ is perpendicular to this surface, and indeed $-\vec{E}$ is the gradient of the function that defines it. As a check, the sum of the second derivatives $\dfrac{\partial^2 V}{\partial x^2} + \dfrac{\partial^2 V}{\partial y^2} + \dfrac{\partial^2 V}{\partial z^2}$ is zero, as it must be in a charge-free region.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A dipole is formed by the charges $\pm 2.0\,\mathrm{nC}$ at distance $d = 1.0\,\mathrm{mm}$, with the dipole moment directed along the $z$ axis and the center at the origin. Using the potential at large distance, compute the components of the field at the point $P = (6.0\,\mathrm{cm},\ 0,\ 8.0\,\mathrm{cm})$ and the magnitude of the field at $P$ and at the point of the $z$ axis at $10\,\mathrm{cm}$ from the center.

$$
p = q d ,
\qquad
V = \frac{k p \cos\theta}{r^2} = \frac{k p\, z}{(x^2 + y^2 + z^2)^{\frac{3}{2}}}
$$
$$
E_x = -\frac{\partial V}{\partial x} = \frac{3 k p\, x z}{r^5} ,
\qquad
E_z = -\frac{\partial V}{\partial z} = \frac{k p\, (2z^2 - x^2 - y^2)}{r^5}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
kp &= 8.99 \cdot 10^9 \cdot 2.0 \cdot 10^{-9} \cdot 1.0 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{V \cdot m} \approx 1.80 \cdot 10^{-2}\,\mathrm{V \cdot m} \\[6pt]
E_x(P) &= \frac{3 \cdot 1.80 \cdot 10^{-2} \cdot 0.060 \cdot 0.080}{0.10^5}\,\mathrm{V/m} \approx 26\,\mathrm{V/m} \\[6pt]
E_z(P) &= \frac{1.80 \cdot 10^{-2} \cdot (2 \cdot 0.0064 - 0.0036)}{0.10^5}\,\mathrm{V/m} \approx 17\,\mathrm{V/m} \\[6pt]
|\vec{E}(P)| &= \sqrt{25.9^2 + 16.5^2}\,\mathrm{V/m} \approx 31\,\mathrm{V/m} \\[6pt]
E(\text{axis}) &= \frac{2kp}{z^3} = \frac{2 \cdot 1.80 \cdot 10^{-2}}{0.10^3}\,\mathrm{V/m} \approx 36\,\mathrm{V/m}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The distance of $P$ from the center is $r = \sqrt{0.060^2 + 0.080^2}\,\mathrm{m} = 0.10\,\mathrm{m}$, one hundred times $d$, so the **potential of a dipole at large distance** is an excellent approximation. In Cartesian coordinates $\cos\theta = \dfrac{z}{r}$, and the potential becomes $\dfrac{kpz}{r^3}$. By the **field as the gradient of the potential** one differentiates with respect to $x$ and to $z$: the derivative of $r^{-3}$ with respect to $x$ is $-3x\,r^{-5}$, and in the derivative with respect to $z$ the factor $z$ of the numerator also appears. At $P$ the component $E_y$ is zero, because $y = 0$.

On the $z$ axis one has $x = y = 0$, so $E_x = 0$ and $E_z = \dfrac{2kpz^2}{z^5} = \dfrac{2kp}{z^3}$. As a check, the magnitude at $P$ can be written $\dfrac{kp}{r^3}\sqrt{3\cos^2\theta + 1}$, and with $\cos\theta = 0.80$ one obtains $18 \cdot 1.71\,\mathrm{V/m} \approx 31\,\mathrm{V/m}$. The field of the dipole decreases as $\dfrac{1}{r^3}$, one power more than the potential.

</div>
