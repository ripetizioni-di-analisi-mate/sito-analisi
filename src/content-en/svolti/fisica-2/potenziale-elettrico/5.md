## Exercise 1

A thin ring of radius $R = 6.0\,\mathrm{cm}$ carries the charge $Q = 12\,\mathrm{nC}$ distributed uniformly. Compute the potential at the center of the ring and at the point of the axis at distance $x = 8.0\,\mathrm{cm}$ from the center, and the external work needed to bring a charge $q_0 = 1.0\,\mathrm{nC}$ slowly from that point to the center.

$$
V(x) = \frac{kQ}{\sqrt{R^2 + x^2}} ,
\qquad
W_{\text{ext}} = q_0\, \big( V(0) - V(x) \big)
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
V(0) &= \frac{8.99 \cdot 10^9 \cdot 12 \cdot 10^{-9}}{0.060}\,\mathrm{V} \approx 1.80 \cdot 10^3\,\mathrm{V} \\[6pt]
V(0.080\,\mathrm{m}) &= \frac{8.99 \cdot 10^9 \cdot 12 \cdot 10^{-9}}{\sqrt{0.060^2 + 0.080^2}}\,\mathrm{V} = \frac{107.9}{0.10}\,\mathrm{V} \approx 1.08 \cdot 10^3\,\mathrm{V} \\[6pt]
W_{\text{ext}} &= 1.0 \cdot 10^{-9} \cdot (1798 - 1079)\,\mathrm{J} \approx 7.2 \cdot 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{J}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The charge is distributed uniformly on a thin ring, so the **potential of a ring on its axis** applies, in which every charge element is at the same distance $\sqrt{R^2 + x^2}$ from the point on the axis, which equals $R$ at the center and $10\,\mathrm{cm}$ at $8.0\,\mathrm{cm}$. The charge $q_0$ is positive and moves toward higher potentials, so the field does negative work on it; the external agent, which moves it slowly, does the opposite work, equal to the increase of the potential energy $q_0\,\Delta V$. The work does not depend on the path, because the electrostatic field is conservative.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A thin rod of length $L = 0.40\,\mathrm{m}$ carries the charge $Q = 8.0\,\mathrm{nC}$ distributed uniformly. Compute the potential at the point $P$ of the line of the rod at distance $d = 0.10\,\mathrm{m}$ from one end, outside the rod, and at the point $S$ of the axis of the rod at distance $y = 0.15\,\mathrm{m}$ from its center.

::grafico{assi=0 uguale=1 poligoni="0,-0.05 4,-0.05 4,0.05 0,0.05" linee="-1.6,0 0,0" segmenti="1.2,0,2,1.5;2,0,2,1.5" punti="-1,0,pieno,P;2,1.5,pieno,S;1.2,0,pieno" etichette="1.05,-0.35,dq;-0.6,-0.35,d;2.1,0.7,y;3.8,-0.35,L" xmin=-1.8 xmax=4.4 ymin=-0.6 ymax=2 titolo="Charged rod of length $L$: the point $P$ lies on the extension of the rod at distance $d$ from one end, the point $S$ on the axis at distance $y$ from the center; each element $dq$ contributes $\dfrac{k\,dq}{r}$"}

$$
\lambda = \frac{Q}{L} ,
\qquad
V(P) = \int_0^L \frac{k \lambda\, dx}{x + d} = k\lambda \ln \frac{L + d}{d}
$$
$$
V(S) = \int_{-\frac{L}{2}}^{\frac{L}{2}} \frac{k \lambda\, dx}{\sqrt{x^2 + y^2}} = 2k\lambda \ln \frac{\frac{L}{2} + \sqrt{\frac{L^2}{4} + y^2}}{y}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
\lambda &= \frac{8.0 \cdot 10^{-9}}{0.40}\,\mathrm{C/m} = 2.0 \cdot 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{C/m} ,
\qquad k\lambda = 179.8\,\mathrm{V} \\[6pt]
V(P) &= 179.8 \cdot \ln \frac{0.50}{0.10}\,\mathrm{V} = 179.8 \cdot \ln 5\,\mathrm{V} \approx 2.9 \cdot 10^2\,\mathrm{V} \\[6pt]
V(S) &= 2 \cdot 179.8 \cdot \ln \frac{0.20 + 0.25}{0.15}\,\mathrm{V} = 359.6 \cdot \ln 3\,\mathrm{V} \approx 4.0 \cdot 10^2\,\mathrm{V}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The rod is thin and the charge is uniform, so the linear density is constant and the element of length $dx$ carries the charge $dq = \lambda\,dx$. By the **potential of a continuous distribution** one integrates $\dfrac{k\,dq}{r}$ along the rod, with the origin at the end near $P$ for the first point and at the center of the rod for the second. For $P$ the distance of the element is $x + d$ and an antiderivative of $\dfrac{1}{x + d}$ is $\ln(x + d)$. For $S$ the distance is $\sqrt{x^2 + y^2}$ and an antiderivative of $\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{x^2 + y^2}}$ is $\ln\left( x + \sqrt{x^2 + y^2} \right)$; the integrand is even, therefore the integral is twice the one from $0$ to $\dfrac{L}{2}$. With the chosen data $\sqrt{0.20^2 + 0.15^2} = 0.25$, and the two arguments of the logarithms are $5$ and $3$.

As a check, if the distance is much greater than $L$ the two results must tend to $\dfrac{kQ}{r}$. For $d$ much greater than $L$ one has $\ln \dfrac{L + d}{d} \approx \dfrac{L}{d}$, so $V(P) \approx \dfrac{k\lambda L}{d} = \dfrac{kQ}{d}$, as for a point charge.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A thin disk of radius $R = 10\,\mathrm{cm}$ carries a charge distributed uniformly with density $\sigma = 2.0 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\mathrm{C/m^2}$. Compute the potential at the center of the disk and at the point of the axis at distance $x = 24\,\mathrm{cm}$, and the electric field at that point. Compare the results with those of a point charge equal to the charge of the disk.

$$
V(x) = \frac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0} \left( \sqrt{R^2 + x^2} - x \right) ,
\qquad
E_x = -\frac{dV}{dx} = \frac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0} \left( 1 - \frac{x}{\sqrt{R^2 + x^2}} \right)
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0} &= \frac{2.0 \cdot 10^{-6}}{2 \cdot 8.85 \cdot 10^{-12}}\,\mathrm{V/m} \approx 1.13 \cdot 10^5\,\mathrm{V/m} \\[6pt]
V(0) &= 1.13 \cdot 10^5 \cdot 0.10\,\mathrm{V} \approx 1.13 \cdot 10^4\,\mathrm{V} \\[6pt]
V(0.24\,\mathrm{m}) &= 1.13 \cdot 10^5 \cdot (0.26 - 0.24)\,\mathrm{V} \approx 2.26 \cdot 10^3\,\mathrm{V} \\[6pt]
E_x(0.24\,\mathrm{m}) &= 1.13 \cdot 10^5 \cdot \left( 1 - \frac{0.24}{0.26} \right)\mathrm{V/m} \approx 8.7 \cdot 10^3\,\mathrm{V/m}
\end{aligned}
$$
$$
Q = \sigma \pi R^2 \approx 6.28 \cdot 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{C} ,
\qquad
\frac{kQ}{x} \approx 2.35 \cdot 10^3\,\mathrm{V} ,
\qquad
\frac{kQ}{x^2} \approx 9.8 \cdot 10^3\,\mathrm{V/m}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The charge is uniform on a thin disk, so the **potential of a disk on its axis** applies; at the center $\sqrt{R^2 + x^2} - x = R$, and at $24\,\mathrm{cm}$ the square root equals $\sqrt{0.10^2 + 0.24^2} = 0.26\,\mathrm{m}$. By symmetry, on the axis the field is directed along the axis, and by the **field as the gradient of the potential** its component is the opposite of the derivative of $V$ with respect to $x$; the derivative of $\sqrt{R^2 + x^2}$ is $\dfrac{x}{\sqrt{R^2 + x^2}}$. The field is positive, so it points away from the disk, as expected for a positive charge.

The comparison with the point charge shows that at $24\,\mathrm{cm}$, slightly more than twice the radius, the potential of the disk differs by about $4\,\%$ from $\dfrac{kQ}{x}$ and the field by about $11\,\%$ from $\dfrac{kQ}{x^2}$. The two approximations improve moving away from the disk, and the potential approaches its limit sooner than the field.

</div>
