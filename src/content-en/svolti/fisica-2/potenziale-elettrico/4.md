## Exercise 1

Three point charges are at rest on the $x$ axis: $q_1 = +2.0\,\mu\mathrm{C}$ at $x = 0$, $q_2 = -3.0\,\mu\mathrm{C}$ at $x = 0.10\,\mathrm{m}$ and $q_3 = +4.0\,\mu\mathrm{C}$ at $x = 0.30\,\mathrm{m}$. Compute the electrostatic energy of the system and the external work needed to separate the charges to infinite distances.

$$
U = k \left( \frac{q_1 q_2}{r_{12}} + \frac{q_1 q_3}{r_{13}} + \frac{q_2 q_3}{r_{23}} \right) ,
\qquad
W_{\text{ext}} = -U
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
U &= 8.99 \cdot 10^9 \left( \frac{-6.0 \cdot 10^{-12}}{0.10} + \frac{8.0 \cdot 10^{-12}}{0.30} + \frac{-12 \cdot 10^{-12}}{0.20} \right)\mathrm{J} \\[6pt]
&= 8.99 \cdot 10^9 \cdot (-6.0 + 2.67 - 6.0) \cdot 10^{-11}\,\mathrm{J} \\[6pt]
&\approx -0.84\,\mathrm{J} \\[6pt]
W_{\text{ext}} &\approx +0.84\,\mathrm{J}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

For the **electrostatic energy of a system of charges** one term is added for each of the three pairs, with the distances $r_{12} = 0.10\,\mathrm{m}$, $r_{13} = 0.30\,\mathrm{m}$ and $r_{23} = 0.20\,\mathrm{m}$ and with the sign of the product of the charges. The two pairs with charges of opposite sign give negative terms and prevail over the single pair with charges of the same sign, because $q_2$ is close to both other charges; the total energy is therefore **negative** and the system is bound. Separating the charges to infinity brings the energy to $0$, therefore the external agent, which moves the charges slowly, must supply the work $0 - U = 0.84\,\mathrm{J}$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Four equal point charges $q = 1.0\,\mu\mathrm{C}$ occupy the vertices of a square of side $a = 5.0\,\mathrm{cm}$. Compute the electrostatic energy of the system, the external work to bring a fifth equal charge from infinity to the center of the square and the energy of the new system.

$$
U_4 = \frac{kq^2}{a} \left( 4 + \frac{2}{\sqrt{2}} \right) = \frac{kq^2}{a} \left( 4 + \sqrt{2} \right) ,
\qquad
W_5 = q\, V_O = q \cdot \frac{4kq}{\frac{a}{\sqrt{2}}} = \frac{4\sqrt{2}\, kq^2}{a}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{kq^2}{a} &= \frac{8.99 \cdot 10^9 \cdot (1.0 \cdot 10^{-6})^2}{0.050}\,\mathrm{J} \approx 0.180\,\mathrm{J} \\[6pt]
U_4 &= 0.180 \cdot 5.41\,\mathrm{J} \approx 0.97\,\mathrm{J} \\[6pt]
W_5 &= 0.180 \cdot 5.66\,\mathrm{J} \approx 1.0\,\mathrm{J} \\[6pt]
U_5 &= U_4 + W_5 \approx 2.0\,\mathrm{J}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The four charges form six pairs: four pairs of adjacent charges, at distance $a$, and two pairs of opposite charges, at distance $a\sqrt{2}$ along the diagonals. By the **electrostatic energy of a system of charges** one obtains the sum $\dfrac{kq^2}{a}\left( 4 + \dfrac{2}{\sqrt{2}} \right)$, positive because the charges all have the same sign.

The fifth charge moves in the field of the first four, and the external work to bring it slowly from infinity to the center $O$ is $q\,V_O$, where $V_O$ is the potential at $O$ produced by the four charges. The center is at distance $\dfrac{a}{\sqrt{2}}$ from each vertex, so by the **potential of a system of charges** $V_O = \dfrac{4kq}{\frac{a}{\sqrt{2}}} = \dfrac{4\sqrt{2}\, kq}{a}$. The energy of the system of five charges is the sum of the initial energy and the work supplied; as a check, the new terms are the four pairs formed by the central charge with the vertices, each with energy $\dfrac{kq^2\sqrt{2}}{a}$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Three equal point charges $q = 2.0\,\mu\mathrm{C}$ are aligned, with distance $a = 10\,\mathrm{cm}$ between consecutive charges. Compute the external work needed to arrange them at the vertices of an equilateral triangle of side $a$, moving them slowly.

::grafico{assi=0 uguale=1 linee="0,0 2,0;4,0 5,0 4.5,0.866 4,0" punti="0,0,pieno;1,0,pieno;2,0,pieno;4,0,pieno;5,0,pieno;4.5,0.866,pieno" etichette="0.45,-0.3,a;1.45,-0.3,a;4.45,-0.3,a;3.95,0.5,a;4.9,0.5,a;0.6,0.6,initial;3.8,1.25,final" xmin=-0.4 xmax=5.4 ymin=-0.6 ymax=1.5 titolo="Initial configuration with the charges aligned and final configuration with the charges at the vertices of the equilateral triangle of side $a$"}

$$
U_i = kq^2 \left( \frac{1}{a} + \frac{1}{a} + \frac{1}{2a} \right) = \frac{5}{2}\, \frac{kq^2}{a} ,
\qquad
U_f = \frac{3\, kq^2}{a} ,
\qquad
W_{\text{ext}} = U_f - U_i = \frac{1}{2}\, \frac{kq^2}{a}
$$
$$
W_{\text{ext}} = \frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{8.99 \cdot 10^9 \cdot (2.0 \cdot 10^{-6})^2}{0.10}\,\mathrm{J} \approx 0.18\,\mathrm{J}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

In the aligned configuration the two pairs of consecutive charges are at distance $a$ and the pair of end charges is at distance $2a$; in the equilateral triangle the three pairs are all at distance $a$. The charges start at rest and end at rest, so the kinetic energy does not change and the external work equals the change in potential energy, which is computed with the **electrostatic energy of a system of charges** in the two configurations. The only pair that changes distance is that of the end charges, which goes from $2a$ to $a$; the charges have the same sign, therefore bringing them closer requires **positive** external work, equal to $\dfrac{kq^2}{a} - \dfrac{kq^2}{2a}$. The result does not depend on the way the charges are moved, because the electrostatic field is conservative.

</div>

## Exercise 4

A charge $Q = 5.0\,\mu\mathrm{C}$ is distributed uniformly throughout the volume of an insulating sphere of radius $R = 10\,\mathrm{cm}$. Compute the electrostatic energy of the distribution, building the sphere layer by layer starting from zero radius, and compare it with the energy of the same charge distributed on a thin spherical shell of the same radius.

$$
q(r) = Q\, \frac{r^3}{R^3} ,
\qquad
dq = \frac{3 Q\, r^2}{R^3}\, dr ,
\qquad
dW = V(r)\, dq = \frac{k\, q(r)}{r}\, dq
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
U &= \int_0^R \frac{k Q r^3}{R^3\, r} \cdot \frac{3 Q r^2}{R^3}\, dr \\[6pt]
&= \frac{3 k Q^2}{R^6} \int_0^R r^4\, dr \\[6pt]
&= \frac{3}{5}\, \frac{k Q^2}{R}
\end{aligned}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
U &= \frac{3}{5} \cdot \frac{8.99 \cdot 10^9 \cdot (5.0 \cdot 10^{-6})^2}{0.10}\,\mathrm{J} \approx 1.3\,\mathrm{J} \\[6pt]
U_{\text{shell}} &= \frac{1}{2}\, \frac{k Q^2}{R} \approx 1.1\,\mathrm{J}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

When the sphere has radius $r$ it contains the charge $q(r)$, proportional to the volume $r^3$. By the **potential of a charged sphere**, on its surface the potential is that of a point charge $q(r)$ placed at the center, $\dfrac{k\,q(r)}{r}$. A layer of thickness $dr$ is added by bringing from infinity the charge $dq$, equal to the density $\dfrac{3Q}{4\pi R^3}$ times the volume $4\pi r^2\,dr$ of the layer; the external work is $V(r)\,dq$, as in the **energy as a sum of charges times potentials**. Integrating from $0$ to $R$ gives the energy $\dfrac{3}{5}\dfrac{kQ^2}{R}$.

For the thin shell the energy is $\dfrac{1}{2}\dfrac{kQ^2}{R}$, so the solid sphere has **greater** energy, in the ratio $\dfrac{6}{5}$. The difference is explained by the field: outside, the two fields coincide, while inside the field of the shell is zero and that of the solid sphere is not, and the energy of a distribution can be attributed to the field it produces, as seen in the topic on conductors, capacitors and dielectrics.

</div>
