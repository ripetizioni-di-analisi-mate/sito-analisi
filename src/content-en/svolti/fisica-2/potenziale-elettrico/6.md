## Exercise 1

Two thin concentric spherical shells have radii $R_1 = 5.0\,\mathrm{cm}$ and $R_2 = 15\,\mathrm{cm}$ and carry the charges $Q_1 = +6.0\,\mathrm{nC}$ and $Q_2 = -2.0\,\mathrm{nC}$, distributed uniformly. Compute the potential at distances $20\,\mathrm{cm}$ and $10\,\mathrm{cm}$ from the center, the potential at the center and the potential difference between the inner shell and the outer shell.

$$
V(r) = \frac{k Q_1}{\max(r, R_1)} + \frac{k Q_2}{\max(r, R_2)}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
V(r) =
\begin{cases}
\dfrac{k Q_1}{R_1} + \dfrac{k Q_2}{R_2} & \text{if } r \leq R_1 \\[10pt]
\dfrac{k Q_1}{r} + \dfrac{k Q_2}{R_2} & \text{if } R_1 \leq r \leq R_2 \\[10pt]
\dfrac{k\,(Q_1 + Q_2)}{r} & \text{if } r \geq R_2
\end{cases}
$$
$$
V(R_1) - V(R_2) = k Q_1 \left( \frac{1}{R_1} - \frac{1}{R_2} \right)
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
V(0.20\,\mathrm{m}) &= \frac{8.99 \cdot 10^9 \cdot 4.0 \cdot 10^{-9}}{0.20}\,\mathrm{V} \approx 1.8 \cdot 10^2\,\mathrm{V} \\[6pt]
V(0.10\,\mathrm{m}) &= 8.99 \cdot 10^9 \left( \frac{6.0 \cdot 10^{-9}}{0.10} - \frac{2.0 \cdot 10^{-9}}{0.15} \right)\mathrm{V} \approx 4.2 \cdot 10^2\,\mathrm{V} \\[6pt]
V(0) &= 8.99 \cdot 10^9 \left( \frac{6.0 \cdot 10^{-9}}{0.050} - \frac{2.0 \cdot 10^{-9}}{0.15} \right)\mathrm{V} \approx 9.6 \cdot 10^2\,\mathrm{V} \\[6pt]
V(R_1) - V(R_2) &= 8.99 \cdot 10^9 \cdot 6.0 \cdot 10^{-9} \cdot (20 - 6.67)\,\mathrm{V} \approx 7.2 \cdot 10^2\,\mathrm{V}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

By the **potential of a charged sphere**, a thin shell of radius $R$ and charge $Q$ produces the potential $\dfrac{kQ}{r}$ outside and the constant potential $\dfrac{kQ}{R}$ inside; by the **potential of a system of charges** the potential of the two shells is the sum of the two contributions. At $20\,\mathrm{cm}$ the point is outside both shells and the total charge $4.0\,\mathrm{nC}$ matters. At $10\,\mathrm{cm}$ the point is outside the inner shell and inside the outer shell, which contributes its constant potential $\dfrac{kQ_2}{R_2}$. At the center both contributions are constant, and the potential is about $960\,\mathrm{V}$ throughout the inner cavity.

In the difference between the potentials of the two shells the contribution of $Q_2$, which is the same at $R_1$ and at $R_2$, cancels, so the potential difference depends **only** on the inner charge. This is the same result obtained by integrating between $R_1$ and $R_2$ the field $\dfrac{kQ_1}{r^2}$, which by Gauss's law is produced only by $Q_1$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

An insulating sphere of radius $R = 4.0\,\mathrm{cm}$ carries the charge $Q = 8.0\,\mathrm{nC}$ distributed uniformly throughout its volume. Compute the potential at the surface and at the center. An electron is released from rest at the surface, at the entrance of a thin radial channel dug into the sphere; compute the speed with which it reaches the center.

$$
V(R) = \frac{kQ}{R} ,
\qquad
V(0) = \frac{3}{2}\, \frac{kQ}{R} ,
\qquad
\frac{1}{2} m_e v^2 = e\, \big( V(0) - V(R) \big) = \frac{e\, kQ}{2R}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
V(R) &= \frac{8.99 \cdot 10^9 \cdot 8.0 \cdot 10^{-9}}{0.040}\,\mathrm{V} \approx 1.80 \cdot 10^3\,\mathrm{V} \\[6pt]
V(0) &= 1.5 \cdot 1798\,\mathrm{V} \approx 2.70 \cdot 10^3\,\mathrm{V} \\[6pt]
v &= \sqrt{\frac{2 \cdot 1.60 \cdot 10^{-19} \cdot 899}{9.11 \cdot 10^{-31}}}\,\mathrm{m/s} \approx 1.8 \cdot 10^7\,\mathrm{m/s}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The charge is distributed uniformly throughout the volume, so by the **potential of a charged sphere** the interior potential is $\dfrac{kQ\,(3R^2 - r^2)}{2R^3}$, which equals $\dfrac{kQ}{R}$ at the surface and $\dfrac{3}{2}\dfrac{kQ}{R}$ at the center. The channel is thin, therefore it does not appreciably change the charge distribution. The electron is negative and moves toward higher potentials, that is, toward the center, where its potential energy $-eV$ is minimum. By **conservation of energy** the kinetic energy at the center is $e$ times the potential difference, about $899\,\mathrm{eV}$, and the speed is about $6\,\%$ of the speed of light. Having reached the center, the electron continues toward the opposite side of the channel and oscillates.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A very long straight wire, of negligible radius, carries a charge distributed uniformly with density $\lambda = 5.0\,\mathrm{nC/m}$. Compute the potential difference between a point at $1.0\,\mathrm{cm}$ and a point at $5.0\,\mathrm{cm}$ from the wire. An electron is released from rest at $5.0\,\mathrm{cm}$ from the wire, far from its ends; compute the speed with which it passes at $1.0\,\mathrm{cm}$ from the wire.

$$
V(r_1) - V(r_2) = \frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0} \ln \frac{r_2}{r_1} = 2k\lambda \ln \frac{r_2}{r_1} ,
\qquad
\frac{1}{2} m_e v^2 = e\, \big( V(r_1) - V(r_2) \big)
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
V(r_1) - V(r_2) &= 2 \cdot 8.99 \cdot 10^9 \cdot 5.0 \cdot 10^{-9} \cdot \ln 5\,\mathrm{V} = 89.9 \cdot 1.609\,\mathrm{V} \approx 145\,\mathrm{V} \\[6pt]
v &= \sqrt{\frac{2 \cdot 1.60 \cdot 10^{-19} \cdot 145}{9.11 \cdot 10^{-31}}}\,\mathrm{m/s} \approx 7.1 \cdot 10^6\,\mathrm{m/s}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The wire is very long and the electron is far from the ends, so the field is that of an infinite wire, radial and of magnitude $\dfrac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0\, r}$, and the **potential difference in the field of an infinite wire** applies. With $\dfrac{1}{2\pi\varepsilon_0} = 2k$ the formula becomes $2k\lambda \ln \dfrac{r_2}{r_1}$; the point closer to the wire has **higher** potential, because the charge of the wire is positive. The potential cannot be referred to infinity, but conservation of energy requires only the difference between the two points. The electron, which is negative, is attracted by the wire and moves toward higher potentials, therefore its kinetic energy increases by $e \cdot 145\,\mathrm{V} = 145\,\mathrm{eV}$.

</div>
