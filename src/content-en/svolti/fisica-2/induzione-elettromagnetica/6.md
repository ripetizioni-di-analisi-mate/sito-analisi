## Exercise 1

An ideal source of emf $\mathcal{E} = 12\,\mathrm{V}$, a resistor $R = 6.0\,\mathrm{\Omega}$ and an ideal inductor $L = 0.30\,\mathrm{H}$ are connected in series with a switch, which is closed at time $t = 0$. Compute the time constant, the steady-state current, the rate of increase of the current at the instant of closing, the current at time $t_1 = 0.10\,\mathrm{s}$, and the instant at which the current equals $1.0\,\mathrm{A}$.

$$
\mathcal{E} - L\,\frac{di}{dt} = R\,i , \qquad
i(t) = \frac{\mathcal{E}}{R} \left( 1 - e^{-\frac{t}{\tau}} \right) , \qquad \tau = \frac{L}{R}
$$
$$
\left. \frac{di}{dt} \right|_{t = 0} = \frac{\mathcal{E}}{L} , \qquad
i(t_2) = \frac{1}{2}\,\frac{\mathcal{E}}{R}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
t_2 = \tau \ln 2
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
\tau &= \frac{0.30}{6.0}\,\mathrm{s} = 0.050\,\mathrm{s} , \qquad I_\infty = \frac{12}{6.0}\,\mathrm{A} = 2.0\,\mathrm{A} \\[6pt]
\left. \frac{di}{dt} \right|_{t = 0} &= \frac{12}{0.30}\,\mathrm{A/s} = 40\,\mathrm{A/s} \\[6pt]
i(t_1) &= 2.0 \cdot \left( 1 - e^{-2} \right)\,\mathrm{A} \approx 1.7\,\mathrm{A} \\[6pt]
t_2 &= 0.050 \cdot 0.693\,\mathrm{s} \approx 35\,\mathrm{ms}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The circuit is the one of the theorem on **closing an RL circuit**, so the current grows exponentially toward the steady-state value $\dfrac{\mathcal{E}}{R}$, with time constant $\dfrac{L}{R}$. At the instant of closing the current is zero, therefore the voltage drop across the resistor is zero and the loop rule gives $L\,\dfrac{di}{dt} = \mathcal{E}$.

The instant $t_1$ corresponds to two time constants, and the current has reached $86\,\%$ of the steady-state value. The current of $1.0\,\mathrm{A}$ is half the steady-state current, so $e^{-\frac{t_2}{\tau}} = \dfrac{1}{2}$ and $t_2 = \tau \ln 2$. If the current kept growing at the initial rate of $40\,\mathrm{A/s}$, it would reach the steady-state value exactly after a time $\tau$, consistently with the geometric meaning of the time constant.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A real coil is connected to an ideal source of emf $10\,\mathrm{V}$. In steady state the current equals $0.50\,\mathrm{A}$, and the current reaches $63\,\%$ of the steady-state value $20\,\mathrm{ms}$ after closing. Compute the resistance and the inductance of the coil, and the current $40\,\mathrm{ms}$ after closing.

$$
I_\infty = \frac{\mathcal{E}}{R}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
R = \frac{\mathcal{E}}{I_\infty} , \qquad
\tau = \frac{L}{R}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
L = \tau R
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
R &= \frac{10}{0.50}\,\mathrm{\Omega} = 20\,\mathrm{\Omega} \\[6pt]
L &= 0.020 \cdot 20\,\mathrm{H} = 0.40\,\mathrm{H} \\[6pt]
i(40\,\mathrm{ms}) &= 0.50 \cdot \left( 1 - e^{-2} \right)\,\mathrm{A} \approx 0.43\,\mathrm{A}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

A real coil is represented as an ideal inductor in series with its resistance, so together with the source it forms an RL circuit. In steady state the current does not vary, the self-induced emf is zero and the current is determined by the resistance alone, from which $R = 20\,\mathrm{\Omega}$.

By the **meaning of the time constant**, the current reaches $63\,\%$ of the steady-state value after a time $\tau$, since $1 - e^{-1} \approx 0.63$; therefore $\tau = 20\,\mathrm{ms}$ and $L = \tau R = 0.40\,\mathrm{H}$. The instant $40\,\mathrm{ms}$ corresponds to $2\tau$, and the current is $86\,\%$ of the steady-state value. This procedure is a practical method for measuring the inductance of a coil with an oscilloscope.

</div>

## Exercise 3

In a circuit consisting of an ideal source $\mathcal{E} = 24\,\mathrm{V}$, a resistor $R = 8.0\,\mathrm{\Omega}$ and an ideal inductor $L = 0.40\,\mathrm{H}$ in series, the steady-state current flows. At time $t = 0$ a changeover switch cuts out the source and closes the inductor on the resistor. Compute after how much time the current drops to $0.30\,\mathrm{A}$ and the energy dissipated in the resistor from $t = 0$ onward.

$$
I_0 = \frac{\mathcal{E}}{R} , \qquad
i(t) = I_0\,e^{-\frac{t}{\tau}} , \qquad
\tau = \frac{L}{R} , \qquad
t_1 = \tau \ln\frac{I_0}{i_1}
$$
$$
W_R = \int_0^{+\infty} R\,I_0^2\,e^{-\frac{2t}{\tau}}\,dt = R I_0^2\,\frac{\tau}{2} = \frac{1}{2} L I_0^2
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
I_0 &= \frac{24}{8.0}\,\mathrm{A} = 3.0\,\mathrm{A} , \qquad \tau = \frac{0.40}{8.0}\,\mathrm{s} = 0.050\,\mathrm{s} \\[6pt]
t_1 &= 0.050 \cdot \ln\frac{3.0}{0.30}\,\mathrm{s} = 0.050 \cdot \ln 10\,\mathrm{s} \approx 0.12\,\mathrm{s} \\[6pt]
W_R &= \frac{1}{2} \cdot 0.40 \cdot 3.0^2\,\mathrm{J} = 1.8\,\mathrm{J}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Before the opening the circuit is in steady state, so the current is $\dfrac{\mathcal{E}}{R}$. After the source has been cut out the theorem on **opening an RL circuit** applies, and the current decreases exponentially; imposing $I_0\,e^{-\frac{t_1}{\tau}} = i_1$ and taking logarithms we obtain $t_1$. The current drops to one tenth of its initial value in about $2.3$ time constants.

During the transient the resistor dissipates the power $R i^2$, and the integral over the whole transient equals $\dfrac{1}{2} L I_0^2$. The result coincides with the **energy stored in the inductor** at the instant of opening, so the inductor returns all the accumulated energy, which the resistor converts into heat.

</div>

## Exercise 4

In the circuit of the figure an ideal source $\mathcal{E} = 12\,\mathrm{V}$ is in series with a switch $T$ and with a resistor $R_1 = 4.0\,\mathrm{\Omega}$; the branch thus formed supplies an ideal inductor $L = 0.60\,\mathrm{H}$ in parallel with a resistor $R_2 = 12\,\mathrm{\Omega}$. The switch is closed at time $t = 0$. Compute the current delivered by the source immediately after closing and in steady state, and the time constant with which the current in the inductor grows. Finally, in steady state, the switch is opened again; compute the potential difference across $R_2$ immediately after reopening and the new time constant.

::grafico{assi=0 curve="4+0.35*abs(sin(t)),2.2-0.35*t/pi,0,12.566" linee="0,0 0,1.4;-0.22,1.4 0.22,1.4;-0.45,1.6 0.45,1.6;0,1.6 0,3 0.9,3;0.9,3 1.5,3.4;1.6,3 2.0,3 2.1,3.2 2.3,2.8 2.5,3.2 2.7,2.8 2.9,3.2 3.1,2.8 3.2,3 6,3 6,2.2 6.25,2.1 5.75,1.9 6.25,1.7 5.75,1.5 6.25,1.3 5.75,1.1 6,1.0 6,0 0,0;4,3 4,2.2;4,0.8 4,0" punti="0.9,3,pieno;1.6,3,pieno;4,3,pieno;4,0,pieno" etichette="0.65,1.45,ℰ;-0.75,1.75,+;-0.75,1.2,−;2.6,3.55,R₁;1.15,3.7,T;4.75,1.5,L;6.5,1.6,R₂" xmin=-1.2 xmax=7.2 ymin=-0.5 ymax=4 uguale=1 titolo="Source with resistor $R_1$ supplying an inductor $L$ in parallel with a resistor $R_2$"}

$$
\begin{cases}
i_1 = i_L + i_2 \\[4pt]
\mathcal{E} - R_1 i_1 = L\,\dfrac{di_L}{dt} \\[8pt]
R_2\,i_2 = L\,\dfrac{di_L}{dt}
\end{cases}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\frac{di_L}{dt} + \frac{R_1 R_2}{L\,(R_1 + R_2)}\,i_L = \frac{R_2\,\mathcal{E}}{L\,(R_1 + R_2)}
$$
$$
\tau = \frac{L\,(R_1 + R_2)}{R_1 R_2} , \qquad
i_1(0) = \frac{\mathcal{E}}{R_1 + R_2} , \qquad
i_1(\infty) = i_L(\infty) = \frac{\mathcal{E}}{R_1} , \qquad
\tau' = \frac{L}{R_2} , \qquad
\Delta V_2 = R_2\,\frac{\mathcal{E}}{R_1}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
i_1(0) &= \frac{12}{16}\,\mathrm{A} = 0.75\,\mathrm{A} , \qquad i_1(\infty) = \frac{12}{4.0}\,\mathrm{A} = 3.0\,\mathrm{A} \\[6pt]
\tau &= \frac{0.60 \cdot 16}{4.0 \cdot 12}\,\mathrm{s} = 0.20\,\mathrm{s} \\[6pt]
\Delta V_2 &= 12 \cdot 3.0\,\mathrm{V} = 36\,\mathrm{V} , \qquad \tau' = \frac{0.60}{12}\,\mathrm{s} = 0.050\,\mathrm{s}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

We apply the junction rule and the **loop rule with an inductor**: the current $i_1$ of the source divides between the inductor and $R_2$, and the potential difference across the two branches in parallel is $L\,\dfrac{di_L}{dt}$. Solving the third equation for $i_2$ and substituting into the second we obtain a first-order linear equation in $i_L$ alone, with time constant equal to $L$ divided by the resistance $\dfrac{R_1 R_2}{R_1 + R_2}$ of the two resistors in parallel.

Immediately after closing the current in the inductor is still zero, because the inductor opposes abrupt changes in the current; so all the current flows through $R_1$ and $R_2$ in series. In steady state $\dfrac{di_L}{dt} = 0$, therefore the voltage across the parallel combination is zero, the current in $R_2$ is zero and the inductor is equivalent to a wire. On reopening the branch of the source is interrupted, and the current of $3.0\,\mathrm{A}$ in the inductor continues through $R_2$, decreasing with time constant $\dfrac{L}{R_2}$. A potential difference of $36\,\mathrm{V}$ thus appears across $R_2$, three times the emf of the source; this is an **opening overvoltage**, due to the self-induced emf.

</div>
