## Exercise 1

A solenoid of length $\ell = 25\,\mathrm{cm}$ consists of $N = 500$ circular turns of radius $r = 1.0\,\mathrm{cm}$, wound in air. Neglecting edge effects, compute the inductance of the solenoid and the magnitude of the self-induced emf when the current increases by $200\,\mathrm{A}$ per second.

$$
L = \frac{\mu_0 N^2 A}{\ell} , \qquad A = \pi r^2 , \qquad
|\mathcal{E}_L| = L \left| \frac{di}{dt} \right|
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
A &= \pi \cdot 0.010^2\,\mathrm{m^2} \approx 3.14 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m^2} \\[6pt]
L &= \frac{4\pi \cdot 10^{-7} \cdot 500^2 \cdot 3.14 \cdot 10^{-4}}{0.25}\,\mathrm{H} \approx 3.9 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{H} \\[6pt]
|\mathcal{E}_L| &= 3.95 \cdot 10^{-4} \cdot 200\,\mathrm{V} \approx 7.9 \cdot 10^{-2}\,\mathrm{V}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The solenoid is $25\,\mathrm{cm}$ long and has radius $1\,\mathrm{cm}$, so it is long compared with its radius, and edge effects are negligible; therefore the theorem on the **inductance of a solenoid** applies. By the **self-induced emf** law the magnitude is the product of the inductance and the rate of change of the current; the emf opposes the increase in the current, so it acts in the direction opposite to it.

Dimensional check: $\mathrm{T \cdot m/A} \cdot \mathrm{m^2/m} = \mathrm{T \cdot m^2/A} = \mathrm{Wb/A} = \mathrm{H}$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

In an ideal inductor of inductance $L = 50\,\mathrm{mH}$ the current increases linearly from $0$ to $2.0\,\mathrm{A}$ in $4.0\,\mathrm{ms}$, stays constant for $2.0\,\mathrm{ms}$ and then returns linearly to zero in $1.0\,\mathrm{ms}$. Compute the self-induced emf in each of the three intervals.

::grafico{linee="-0.5,0 0,0 4,2 6,2 7,0 9,0" segmenti="4,0,4,2;6,0,6,2" xmin=-0.6 xmax=9.4 ymin=-0.4 ymax=2.8 nomi="t,i" legenda=0 etichette="0.5,1.5,ℰ = −25 V;4.2,2.3,ℰ = 0;7.1,1.2,ℰ = 100 V" titolo="Current in the inductor, in amperes, with time in milliseconds, and self-induced emf in the three intervals: the emf is proportional to the slope of the graph, with the sign changed"}

$$
\mathcal{E}_L = -L\,\frac{di}{dt} , \qquad
\frac{di}{dt} = \frac{\Delta i}{\Delta t} \ \text{in each interval}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{E}_1 &= -0.050 \cdot \frac{2.0}{4.0 \cdot 10^{-3}}\,\mathrm{V} = -25\,\mathrm{V} \\[6pt]
\mathcal{E}_2 &= -0.050 \cdot 0\,\mathrm{V} = 0 \\[6pt]
\mathcal{E}_3 &= -0.050 \cdot \frac{-2.0}{1.0 \cdot 10^{-3}}\,\mathrm{V} = 100\,\mathrm{V}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

In each interval the current varies linearly, so the derivative is constant and equal to the ratio of the change in current to the duration; the **self-induced emf** is piecewise constant. In the first interval the current increases, and the emf is negative, that is, it opposes the current. In the second interval the current is constant, therefore the emf is zero and the inductor is equivalent to a wire. In the third interval the current decreases four times as fast, so the emf is positive, tends to sustain the current and has a magnitude four times as large.

The comparison between the first and the third interval shows that the emf depends on the rate of the change; indeed the same change of $2.0\,\mathrm{A}$, occurring in a time four times shorter, produces an emf four times larger.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A toroid has a rectangular cross-section, with inner radius $a = 4.0\,\mathrm{cm}$, outer radius $b = 6.0\,\mathrm{cm}$ and height $h = 2.0\,\mathrm{cm}$, and $N = 1000$ turns are wound uniformly on it. Compute the inductance of the toroid and compare it with the approximate value obtained by taking the field as uniform and equal to its value on the mean circle.

$$
B(s) = \frac{\mu_0 N i}{2\pi s} \quad (a < s < b) , \qquad
\Phi_B = N \int_a^b B(s)\,h\,ds = \frac{\mu_0 N^2 i\,h}{2\pi} \ln\frac{b}{a}
$$
$$
L = \frac{\Phi_B}{i} = \frac{\mu_0 N^2 h}{2\pi} \ln\frac{b}{a} , \qquad
L_{\text{appr}} = \frac{\mu_0 N^2 h\,(b - a)}{2\pi\,s_m} , \quad s_m = \frac{a + b}{2}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
L &= \frac{4\pi \cdot 10^{-7} \cdot 1000^2 \cdot 0.020}{2\pi} \cdot \ln 1.5\,\mathrm{H} = 4.0 \cdot 10^{-3} \cdot 0.405\,\mathrm{H} \approx 1.6 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{H} \\[6pt]
L_{\text{appr}} &= 4.0 \cdot 10^{-3} \cdot \frac{0.020}{0.050}\,\mathrm{H} = 1.6 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{H}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

By Ampère's law the field inside the toroid is tangent to the coaxial circles and equals $\dfrac{\mu_0 N i}{2\pi s}$ at distance $s$ from the axis; outside it is zero (see the topic on sources of the magnetic field). The field depends on $s$, so the flux through one turn is computed by dividing the cross-section into strips of width $ds$ and height $h$, on each of which the field is constant. The flux linkage is $N$ times the flux through one turn, and by the definition of **inductance** we divide by $i$.

The exact value is $1.62\,\mathrm{mH}$ and the approximate one $1.60\,\mathrm{mH}$; the difference is about $1\,\%$, because the thickness $b - a$ is small compared with the mean radius. Indeed $\ln\dfrac{b}{a} \approx \dfrac{b - a}{s_m}$ when $b - a$ is small compared with $s_m$, and the toroid is equivalent to a solenoid of length $2\pi s_m$ closed on itself.

</div>
