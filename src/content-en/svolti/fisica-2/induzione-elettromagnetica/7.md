## Exercise 1

An air-core solenoid of length $\ell = 40\,\mathrm{cm}$, with $N = 800$ turns of radius $r = 2.0\,\mathrm{cm}$, carries the current $I = 5.0\,\mathrm{A}$. Neglecting edge effects, compute the magnetic field inside, the magnetic energy density and the stored energy, and verify the result with the inductance of the solenoid.

$$
B = \mu_0 \frac{N}{\ell}\,I , \qquad
u_B = \frac{B^2}{2 \mu_0} , \qquad
U = u_B\,\pi r^2 \ell , \qquad
L = \frac{\mu_0 N^2 \pi r^2}{\ell} , \qquad
U = \frac{1}{2} L I^2
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
B &= 4\pi \cdot 10^{-7} \cdot \frac{800}{0.40} \cdot 5.0\,\mathrm{T} \approx 1.26 \cdot 10^{-2}\,\mathrm{T} \\[6pt]
u_B &= \frac{\left( 1.26 \cdot 10^{-2} \right)^2}{2 \cdot 4\pi \cdot 10^{-7}}\,\mathrm{J/m^3} \approx 63\,\mathrm{J/m^3} \\[6pt]
U &= 62.8 \cdot \pi \cdot 0.020^2 \cdot 0.40\,\mathrm{J} \approx 3.2 \cdot 10^{-2}\,\mathrm{J} \\[6pt]
L &= \frac{4\pi \cdot 10^{-7} \cdot 800^2 \cdot \pi \cdot 0.020^2}{0.40}\,\mathrm{H} \approx 2.53 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{H} \\[6pt]
\frac{1}{2} L I^2 &= \frac{1}{2} \cdot 2.53 \cdot 10^{-3} \cdot 5.0^2\,\mathrm{J} \approx 3.2 \cdot 10^{-2}\,\mathrm{J}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The solenoid is $40\,\mathrm{cm}$ long and has radius $2\,\mathrm{cm}$, so edge effects are negligible; the field is uniform inside and zero outside. By the **energy density of the magnetic field** the energy per unit volume is uniform in the inner volume $\pi r^2 \ell$, and the total energy is the product of $u_B$ and this volume.

The check with the **energy stored in an inductor** gives the same value, about $32\,\mathrm{mJ}$, as it must, since the formula for the energy density is derived precisely from the inductance of the solenoid. Dimensional check: $\dfrac{\mathrm{T^2}}{\mathrm{T \cdot m/A}} = \mathrm{T \cdot A/m}$, and since $1\,\mathrm{T} = 1\,\mathrm{N/(A \cdot m)}$ we obtain $\mathrm{N/m^2} = \mathrm{J/m^3}$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A solenoid of length $\ell_1 = 50\,\mathrm{cm}$, with $N_1 = 1000$ turns, contains at its center a coaxial coil of $N_2 = 200$ turns of radius $r_2 = 1.0\,\mathrm{cm}$. Compute the mutual inductance. If the current in the solenoid decreases uniformly by $50\,\mathrm{A}$ per second, compute the emf induced in the coil and determine the direction of the induced current relative to that of the current in the solenoid.

$$
M = \mu_0\,\frac{N_1}{\ell_1}\,N_2\,\pi r_2^2 , \qquad
|\mathcal{E}_2| = M \left| \frac{di_1}{dt} \right|
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
M &= 4\pi \cdot 10^{-7} \cdot \frac{1000}{0.50} \cdot 200 \cdot \pi \cdot 0.010^2\,\mathrm{H} \approx 1.6 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{H} \\[6pt]
|\mathcal{E}_2| &= 1.58 \cdot 10^{-4} \cdot 50\,\mathrm{V} \approx 7.9 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{V}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The coil is at the center of a long solenoid, where the field of the solenoid is uniform, so the theorem on the **mutual inductance of two coaxial solenoids** applies, with $n_1 = \dfrac{N_1}{\ell_1} = 2000$ turns per meter and $A_2 = \pi r_2^2$. By the definition of mutual inductance the emf in the coil is the product of $M$ and the rate of change of the current in the solenoid.

The current in the solenoid decreases, so the flux linked with the coil decreases. By **Lenz's law** the induced current generates a field in the same direction as that of the solenoid, which partly compensates for the decrease; therefore the current in the coil flows in the *same* direction as the current in the solenoid.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A circuit consists of an ideal source $\mathcal{E} = 20\,\mathrm{V}$, a resistor $R = 10\,\mathrm{\Omega}$ and an ideal inductor $L = 2.0\,\mathrm{H}$ in series, and the switch is closed at time $t = 0$. Compute the energy stored in steady state, the instant $t^*$ at which the energy in the inductor is half of its steady-state value, and how much energy the source has supplied and the resistor has dissipated up to $t^*$.

$$
i(t) = I_\infty \left( 1 - e^{-\frac{t}{\tau}} \right) , \qquad
I_\infty = \frac{\mathcal{E}}{R} , \qquad
\tau = \frac{L}{R} , \qquad
U_\infty = \frac{1}{2} L I_\infty^2
$$
$$
\frac{1}{2} L\,i(t^*)^2 = \frac{1}{2}\,U_\infty
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
1 - e^{-\frac{t^*}{\tau}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
t^* = \tau \ln\left( 2 + \sqrt{2} \right)
$$
$$
W_{\text{gen}} = \int_0^{t^*} \mathcal{E}\,i\,dt = \mathcal{E} I_\infty \left[ t^* - \tau \left( 1 - e^{-\frac{t^*}{\tau}} \right) \right] = \mathcal{E} I_\infty \left( t^* - \frac{\tau}{\sqrt{2}} \right) , \qquad
W_R = W_{\text{gen}} - \frac{1}{2}\,U_\infty
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
I_\infty &= 2.0\,\mathrm{A} , \qquad \tau = 0.20\,\mathrm{s} , \qquad U_\infty = \frac{1}{2} \cdot 2.0 \cdot 2.0^2\,\mathrm{J} = 4.0\,\mathrm{J} \\[6pt]
t^* &= 0.20 \cdot \ln 3.414\,\mathrm{s} \approx 0.246\,\mathrm{s} \\[6pt]
W_{\text{gen}} &= 20 \cdot 2.0 \cdot (0.2456 - 0.1414)\,\mathrm{J} \approx 4.2\,\mathrm{J} \\[6pt]
W_R &= (4.17 - 2.0)\,\mathrm{J} \approx 2.2\,\mathrm{J}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The energy in the inductor is proportional to the square of the current, so it is half of the steady-state value when the current is $\dfrac{I_\infty}{\sqrt{2}} \approx 0.71\,I_\infty$. From the law for **closing an RL circuit** we obtain $e^{-\frac{t^*}{\tau}} = 1 - \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}} = \dfrac{\sqrt{2} - 1}{\sqrt{2}}$, and since $\dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{2} - 1} = 2 + \sqrt{2}$ we get $t^* \approx 1.23\,\tau$.

The work of the source, denoted by $W$ because $L$ is the inductance, is the integral of the power $\mathcal{E}\,i$. By the energy balance of the RL circuit this work is divided between the energy stored in the inductor, $2.0\,\mathrm{J}$ at time $t^*$, and the heat dissipated in the resistor, about $2.2\,\mathrm{J}$. As a check, the heat can be computed directly as $\displaystyle\int_0^{t^*} R\,i^2\,dt$, and the result coincides.

</div>
