## Exercise 1

A battery connected to a resistor of $R_1 = 4.0\,\Omega$ has the terminal voltage $\Delta V_1 = 8.0\,\mathrm{V}$; connected to a resistor of $R_2 = 9.0\,\Omega$ it has the terminal voltage $\Delta V_2 = 9.0\,\mathrm{V}$. Compute the electromotive force and the internal resistance of the battery.

$$
I_k = \frac{\Delta V_k}{R_k} ,
\qquad
\Delta V_k = \mathcal{E} - r I_k
$$
$$
I_1 = \frac{8.0}{4.0}\,\mathrm{A} = 2.0\,\mathrm{A} ,
\qquad
I_2 = \frac{9.0}{9.0}\,\mathrm{A} = 1.0\,\mathrm{A}
$$
$$
\begin{cases}
\mathcal{E} - 2.0\,r = 8.0 \\[4pt]
\mathcal{E} - 1.0\,r = 9.0
\end{cases}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
r = 1.0\,\Omega ,
\qquad
\mathcal{E} = 10\,\mathrm{V}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The terminal voltage is the voltage across the external resistor, therefore Ohm's law applied to each resistor gives the current delivered in the two tests. By the model of the **real source** the terminal voltage is $\mathcal{E} - rI$, and the two tests give two linear equations that must hold together, with unknowns $\mathcal{E}$ and $r$, measured in volts and in ohms respectively. Subtracting the first equation from the second gives $r = 1.0\,\Omega$, and substituting gives $\mathcal{E} = 10\,\mathrm{V}$. The terminal voltage grows with the external resistance, because the current decreases and with it the voltage drop $rI$ inside the battery.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A source with $\mathcal{E} = 12\,\mathrm{V}$ and internal resistance $r = 0.80\,\Omega$ supplies a resistor of adjustable resistance $R$. Compute the value of $R$ for which the power dissipated in the resistor is maximum, the maximum power and the efficiency in that condition; then compute power and efficiency with $R = 3.2\,\Omega$.

::grafico{f="(x>=0?1:NaN)*144*x/(x+0.8)^2" punti="0.8,45,pieno;3.2,28.8,pieno" segmenti="0.8,0,0.8,45;3.2,0,3.2,28.8" xmin=-0.5 xmax=10.5 ymin=-0.9 ymax=52 nomi="R,P" titolo="Power on the load, in watts, as a function of $R$, in ohms, for $\mathcal{E} = 12\,\mathrm{V}$ and $r = 0.80\,\Omega$: maximum of $45\,\mathrm{W}$ for $R = r$, about $29\,\mathrm{W}$ for $R = 3.2\,\Omega$"}

$$
P(R) = \frac{\mathcal{E}^2 R}{(R + r)^2} ,
\qquad
R = r
\;\Longrightarrow\;
P_{\max} = \frac{\mathcal{E}^2}{4 r} ,
\qquad
\eta = \frac{R I^2}{\mathcal{E} I} = \frac{R}{R + r}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
R = 0.80\,\Omega : \quad
P_{\max} &= \frac{12^2}{4 \cdot 0.80}\,\mathrm{W} = 45\,\mathrm{W} ,
\qquad
\eta = \frac{0.80}{1.60} = 0.50 \\[6pt]
R = 3.2\,\Omega : \quad
I &= \frac{12}{4.0}\,\mathrm{A} = 3.0\,\mathrm{A} ,
\qquad
P = 3.2 \cdot 3.0^2\,\mathrm{W} \approx 29\,\mathrm{W} ,
\qquad
\eta = \frac{3.2}{4.0} = 0.80
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

By the theorem on **maximum power transfer** the power on the load is maximum when the load resistance equals the internal resistance, and it equals $\dfrac{\mathcal{E}^2}{4r}$. The efficiency is the ratio of the power dissipated in the load, $RI^2$, to the power delivered by the ideal source, $\mathcal{E} I$; since $\mathcal{E} = (R + r)\,I$, the efficiency is $\dfrac{R}{R + r}$, that is, $50\,\%$ when $R = r$: half of the power delivered is dissipated inside the source.

With $R = 3.2\,\Omega$, four times the internal resistance, the power on the load drops to about $29\,\mathrm{W}$ but the efficiency rises to $80\,\%$. The graph of $P(R)$ shows that the power decreases slowly to the right of the maximum, while the efficiency grows toward $1$ as $R$ increases.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A car battery with $\mathcal{E} = 12.0\,\mathrm{V}$ and internal resistance $r = 0.10\,\Omega$ is recharged with a charger that maintains the voltage $\Delta V = 14.0\,\mathrm{V}$ at its terminals, with the positive terminal of the charger connected to the positive terminal of the battery. Compute the charging current, the power supplied by the charger, the power stored as chemical energy and the power dissipated in the battery; finally compute the time needed to store $0.50\,\mathrm{kWh}$.

$$
\Delta V = \mathcal{E} + r I
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
I = \frac{\Delta V - \mathcal{E}}{r} ,
\qquad
\Delta V\, I = \mathcal{E} I + r I^2
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
I &= \frac{14.0 - 12.0}{0.10}\,\mathrm{A} = 20\,\mathrm{A} \\[6pt]
\Delta V\, I &= 14.0 \cdot 20\,\mathrm{W} = 280\,\mathrm{W} ,
\qquad
\mathcal{E} I = 240\,\mathrm{W} ,
\qquad
r I^2 = 0.10 \cdot 20^2\,\mathrm{W} = 40\,\mathrm{W} \\[6pt]
\Delta t &= \frac{0.50 \cdot 3.6 \cdot 10^{6}}{240}\,\mathrm{s} = 7.5 \cdot 10^{3}\,\mathrm{s} \approx 2.1\,\mathrm{h}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

During charging the current enters the battery at the positive terminal, that is, it crosses the ideal source from the positive to the negative terminal, in the direction opposite to the one in which the battery would deliver it. Going through the battery in the direction of the current, from the positive to the negative terminal, the potential decreases by $\mathcal{E}$ across the ideal source and by $rI$ across the internal resistance; by the **loop rule** the terminal voltage is therefore $\mathcal{E} + rI$, larger than the electromotive force.

Multiplying by $I$ gives the power balance: the power supplied by the charger, $\Delta V\, I$, is the sum of the power $\mathcal{E} I$ that the ideal source **absorbs** and converts into chemical energy and of the power $rI^2$ dissipated by the Joule effect. The charging time is obtained by dividing the energy to be stored, expressed in joules, by the power $\mathcal{E} I$.

</div>

## Exercise 4

Three identical cells, each with $\mathcal{E} = 1.5\,\mathrm{V}$ and $r = 0.50\,\Omega$, must supply a resistor $R = 1.0\,\Omega$. Compute the current in the resistor and the power dissipated in it when the cells are connected in series and when they are connected in parallel, with the positive terminals joined.

$$
\text{series:} \quad
I_s = \frac{3\mathcal{E}}{R + 3r} ,
\qquad
\text{parallel:} \quad
\mathcal{E} - r\,\frac{I_p}{3} = R I_p
\;\Longrightarrow\;
I_p = \frac{\mathcal{E}}{R + \frac{r}{3}}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
I_s &= \frac{4.5}{1.0 + 1.5}\,\mathrm{A} = 1.8\,\mathrm{A} ,
\qquad
P_s = 1.0 \cdot 1.8^2\,\mathrm{W} \approx 3.2\,\mathrm{W} \\[6pt]
I_p &= \frac{1.5}{1.0 + 0.167}\,\mathrm{A} \approx 1.3\,\mathrm{A} ,
\qquad
P_p = 1.0 \cdot 1.286^2\,\mathrm{W} \approx 1.7\,\mathrm{W}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

In series the three cells form a single loop with the resistor, carrying the same current. By the **loop rule** the electromotive forces, all traversed from the negative to the positive terminal, add, and the internal resistances are in series with $R$.

In parallel the three cells are identical and connected between the same two nodes, therefore by symmetry each delivers one third of the current $I_p$ that flows through the resistor. The loop formed by one cell and the resistor gives $\mathcal{E} - r\,\dfrac{I_p}{3} = R I_p$: the parallel combination is equivalent to a single cell with the same electromotive force and internal resistance $\dfrac{r}{3}$.

With these values the series supplies almost twice the power to the resistor. Comparing the two formulas, $I_s = \dfrac{3\mathcal{E}}{R + 3r}$ and $I_p = \dfrac{3\mathcal{E}}{3R + r}$, the parallel combination supplies a larger current if and only if $3R + r < R + 3r$, that is, if $R < r$: the parallel connection is advantageous when the external resistance is smaller than the internal resistance of each cell.

</div>
