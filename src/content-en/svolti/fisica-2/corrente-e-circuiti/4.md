## Exercise 1

A hair dryer absorbs the power $P = 1.2\,\mathrm{kW}$ when it is connected to the voltage $\Delta V = 230\,\mathrm{V}$. Compute the current through it, the resistance of its heating element and the energy consumed in $15$ minutes, in kilowatt-hours and in joules.

$$
I = \frac{P}{\Delta V} ,
\qquad
R = \frac{\Delta V^2}{P} ,
\qquad
W = P\, \Delta t
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
I &= \frac{1200}{230}\,\mathrm{A} \approx 5.2\,\mathrm{A} ,
\qquad
R = \frac{230^2}{1200}\,\Omega \approx 44\,\Omega \\[6pt]
W &= 1.2\,\mathrm{kW} \cdot 0.25\,\mathrm{h} = 0.30\,\mathrm{kWh} = 0.30 \cdot 3.6 \cdot 10^{6}\,\mathrm{J} \approx 1.1 \cdot 10^{6}\,\mathrm{J}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The power absorbed by a component is the product of the voltage and the current, therefore the current is the ratio $\dfrac{P}{\Delta V}$. The heating element is a resistor, and by the **Joule effect** $P = \dfrac{\Delta V^2}{R}$, from which the resistance follows; the same value is obtained from Ohm's law, $R = \dfrac{\Delta V}{I}$. The power is constant, hence the energy consumed, that is, the work $W$ done by the electric field on the charges, is the product of the power and the time; expressing the time in hours, $15\,\mathrm{min} = 0.25\,\mathrm{h}$, gives the result directly in kilowatt-hours, and $1\,\mathrm{kWh} = 3.6 \cdot 10^{6}\,\mathrm{J}$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Two light bulbs are rated $60\,\mathrm{W}$ and $100\,\mathrm{W}$ at $230\,\mathrm{V}$. The bulbs are connected in series to the voltage of $230\,\mathrm{V}$. Assuming that their resistances do not change, compute the power dissipated by each and determine which of the two gives more light.

$$
R_k = \frac{\Delta V_n^2}{P_{n,k}} ,
\qquad
I = \frac{\Delta V}{R_1 + R_2} ,
\qquad
P_k = R_k I^2
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
R_1 &= \frac{230^2}{60}\,\Omega \approx 882\,\Omega ,
\qquad
R_2 = \frac{230^2}{100}\,\Omega = 529\,\Omega \\[6pt]
I &= \frac{230}{882 + 529}\,\mathrm{A} \approx 0.163\,\mathrm{A} \\[6pt]
P_1 &= 882 \cdot 0.163^2\,\mathrm{W} \approx 23\,\mathrm{W} ,
\qquad
P_2 = 529 \cdot 0.163^2\,\mathrm{W} \approx 14\,\mathrm{W}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The rated values are the power absorbed by each bulb connected alone to $230\,\mathrm{V}$; by the **Joule effect** at a given voltage, $P = \dfrac{\Delta V^2}{R}$, hence the bulb with the smaller rated power has the larger resistance. In series the two bulbs carry the same current, which is obtained from the equivalent resistance $R_1 + R_2$.

With the current fixed the power is $R I^2$, therefore the bulb with the larger resistance dissipates more: the $60\,\mathrm{W}$ bulb absorbs about $23\,\mathrm{W}$ and **gives more light** than the $100\,\mathrm{W}$ one, which absorbs about $14\,\mathrm{W}$. The total, $37.5\,\mathrm{W}$, coincides with $\Delta V\, I$. In reality the resistance of a filament grows with temperature, therefore the actual values differ from those computed under the hypothesis of constant resistances.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A $1.5\,\mathrm{kW}$ electric water heater, supplied at $230\,\mathrm{V}$, contains $80\,\mathrm{L}$ of water at a temperature of $15\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$. The specific heat of water is $c = 4186\,\mathrm{J/(kg \cdot K)}$. Assuming that all the energy dissipated by the heating element heats the water, compute the time needed to bring the water to $55\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$, the energy consumed in kilowatt-hours and the current drawn.

$$
P\, \Delta t = m c\, \Delta T
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\Delta t = \frac{m c\, \Delta T}{P} ,
\qquad
I = \frac{P}{\Delta V}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
m c\, \Delta T &= 80 \cdot 4186 \cdot 40\,\mathrm{J} \approx 1.34 \cdot 10^{7}\,\mathrm{J} \\[6pt]
\Delta t &= \frac{1.34 \cdot 10^{7}}{1500}\,\mathrm{s} \approx 8.9 \cdot 10^{3}\,\mathrm{s} \approx 2.5\,\mathrm{h} \\[6pt]
W &= \frac{1.34 \cdot 10^{7}}{3.6 \cdot 10^{6}}\,\mathrm{kWh} \approx 3.7\,\mathrm{kWh} ,
\qquad
I = \frac{1500}{230}\,\mathrm{A} \approx 6.5\,\mathrm{A}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

One liter of water has a mass of one kilogram, therefore $m = 80\,\mathrm{kg}$, and the temperature increase is $\Delta T = 40\,\mathrm{K}$. By the **Joule effect** the heating element converts electrical energy into internal energy at the constant power $P$, and by the hypothesis that there are no losses all this energy becomes heat delivered to the water, which equals $m c\,\Delta T$; for specific heat see the topic on the first law of thermodynamics. Equating the two energies gives the time, about two and a half hours. The energy consumed is the same, expressed in kilowatt-hours, and the current follows from the absorbed power.

</div>

## Exercise 4

A power line with total resistance $R_\ell = 0.50\,\Omega$ must deliver the power $P = 100\,\mathrm{kW}$ to a user. Compute the power dissipated in the line and the voltage drop along the line when the voltage at the user is $\Delta V_1 = 1.0\,\mathrm{kV}$ and when it is $\Delta V_2 = 10\,\mathrm{kV}$.

$$
I = \frac{P}{\Delta V} ,
\qquad
P_\ell = R_\ell\, I^2 = \frac{R_\ell\, P^2}{\Delta V^2} ,
\qquad
\Delta V_\ell = R_\ell\, I
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta V_1 = 1.0\,\mathrm{kV} : \quad
I &= 100\,\mathrm{A} ,
\quad
P_\ell = 0.50 \cdot 100^2\,\mathrm{W} = 5.0\,\mathrm{kW} ,
\quad
\Delta V_\ell = 50\,\mathrm{V} \\[6pt]
\Delta V_2 = 10\,\mathrm{kV} : \quad
I &= 10\,\mathrm{A} ,
\quad
P_\ell = 0.50 \cdot 10^2\,\mathrm{W} = 50\,\mathrm{W} ,
\quad
\Delta V_\ell = 5.0\,\mathrm{V}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The user absorbs the power $P = \Delta V\, I$, therefore for the same power the current is inversely proportional to the voltage. The line is a resistor carrying the same current as the user, because it is in series with it, and by the **Joule effect** it dissipates $R_\ell I^2$. The dissipated power is hence inversely proportional to the *square* of the voltage: multiplying the voltage by $10$ divides the losses by $100$, and they go from $5\,\%$ to $0.05\,\%$ of the power delivered. The voltage drop along the line also decreases, from $50\,\mathrm{V}$ to $5.0\,\mathrm{V}$. For this reason electrical energy is transmitted at high voltage, and the voltage is lowered near the users with transformers, for which see the topic on electrical oscillations and alternating-current circuits.

</div>
