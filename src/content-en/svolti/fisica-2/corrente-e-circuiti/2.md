## Exercise 1

To build a $R = 50\,\Omega$ resistor a nichrome wire of diameter $d = 0.40\,\mathrm{mm}$ is used, with resistivity $\rho = 1.1 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\Omega \cdot \mathrm{m}$. Compute the length of wire needed.

$$
R = \rho\, \frac{\ell}{A}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\ell = \frac{R A}{\rho} = \frac{\pi d^2 R}{4 \rho}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
A &= \pi \cdot (0.20 \cdot 10^{-3})^2\,\mathrm{m^2} \approx 1.257 \cdot 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{m^2} \\[6pt]
\ell &= \frac{50 \cdot 1.257 \cdot 10^{-7}}{1.1 \cdot 10^{-6}}\,\mathrm{m} \approx 5.7\,\mathrm{m}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The wire is cylindrical and homogeneous, therefore the formula for the **resistance of a wire** applies, solved for the length. The cross-sectional area is computed with the radius $0.20\,\mathrm{mm} = 0.20 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m}$. The units are consistent: $\dfrac{\Omega \cdot \mathrm{m^2}}{\Omega \cdot \mathrm{m}} = \mathrm{m}$. With a copper wire of the same diameter, whose resistivity is about $65$ times smaller, more than $350\,\mathrm{m}$ of wire would be needed; for this reason high-resistivity alloys are used in heating elements.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A copper wire has resistance $R = 2.0\,\Omega$. The wire is drawn until its length triples, with no change in volume or resistivity. Compute the new resistance.

$$
\begin{cases}
\ell' = 3\,\ell \\[4pt]
\ell' A' = \ell A
\end{cases}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
A' = \frac{A}{3}
$$
$$
R' = \rho\, \frac{\ell'}{A'} = \rho\, \frac{3\,\ell}{\frac{A}{3}} = 9\, \rho\, \frac{\ell}{A} = 9 R = 18\,\Omega
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The volume of the wire is the product of the length and the cross-sectional area, and it stays constant; since the length triples, the cross-sectional area becomes one third. In the formula for the **resistance of a wire** the length appears in the numerator and the area in the denominator, therefore both changes increase the resistance by a factor $3$, and the resistance becomes $9$ times the initial one. In general, stretching the wire by a factor $k$ at constant volume increases the resistance by a factor $k^2$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A platinum resistance thermometer has resistance $R_0 = 100.0\,\Omega$ at $T_0 = 0\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ and temperature coefficient $\alpha = 3.85 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{K^{-1}}$. Immersed in a liquid, the thermometer has resistance $R = 138.5\,\Omega$. Compute the temperature of the liquid and the resistance the thermometer would have at $37.0\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$.

$$
R = R_0 \left[ 1 + \alpha\,(T - T_0) \right]
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
T = T_0 + \frac{1}{\alpha} \left( \frac{R}{R_0} - 1 \right)
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
T &= 0\,^\circ\mathrm{C} + \frac{1.385 - 1}{3.85 \cdot 10^{-3}}\,^\circ\mathrm{C} = 100\,^\circ\mathrm{C} \\[6pt]
R(37.0\,^\circ\mathrm{C}) &= 100.0 \cdot (1 + 3.85 \cdot 10^{-3} \cdot 37.0)\,\Omega \approx 114.2\,\Omega
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The empirical law of the **temperature dependence of resistivity** applies, which for a wire of constant dimensions holds for the resistance as well; the expansion of the wire is neglected. Solving for $T$ gives the temperature of the liquid, $100\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$. A temperature difference in kelvins coincides with the same difference in degrees Celsius, therefore $\alpha$ can be used with temperatures in degrees Celsius. In the second calculation the law is used in the direct direction; the resistance increases by about $0.385\,\Omega$ per degree, and a change of $0.1\,\Omega$ corresponds to about a quarter of a degree.

</div>

## Exercise 4

We want to make a resistor of $10.0\,\Omega$ at $20\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ whose resistance does not depend on temperature, by connecting in series a carbon resistor, with temperature coefficient $\alpha_{\mathrm{C}} = -5.0 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{K^{-1}}$, and a copper resistor, with $\alpha_{\mathrm{Cu}} = 3.9 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{K^{-1}}$. Compute the resistances of the two resistors at $20\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ and check the result at $70\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$.

$$
R(T) = R_{\mathrm{C}} \left[ 1 + \alpha_{\mathrm{C}}\,\Delta T \right] + R_{\mathrm{Cu}} \left[ 1 + \alpha_{\mathrm{Cu}}\,\Delta T \right]
= (R_{\mathrm{C}} + R_{\mathrm{Cu}}) + (\alpha_{\mathrm{C}} R_{\mathrm{C}} + \alpha_{\mathrm{Cu}} R_{\mathrm{Cu}})\,\Delta T
$$
$$
\begin{cases}
R_{\mathrm{C}} + R_{\mathrm{Cu}} = R_{\text{tot}} \\[4pt]
\alpha_{\mathrm{C}} R_{\mathrm{C}} + \alpha_{\mathrm{Cu}} R_{\mathrm{Cu}} = 0
\end{cases}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
R_{\mathrm{C}} = \frac{\alpha_{\mathrm{Cu}}}{\alpha_{\mathrm{Cu}} - \alpha_{\mathrm{C}}}\, R_{\text{tot}} ,
\qquad
R_{\mathrm{Cu}} = \frac{-\alpha_{\mathrm{C}}}{\alpha_{\mathrm{Cu}} - \alpha_{\mathrm{C}}}\, R_{\text{tot}}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
R_{\mathrm{C}} &= \frac{3.9 \cdot 10^{-3}}{4.4 \cdot 10^{-3}} \cdot 10.0\,\Omega \approx 8.86\,\Omega ,
\qquad
R_{\mathrm{Cu}} = \frac{0.50 \cdot 10^{-3}}{4.4 \cdot 10^{-3}} \cdot 10.0\,\Omega \approx 1.14\,\Omega \\[6pt]
R(70\,^\circ\mathrm{C}) &= \left[ 8.864 \cdot (1 - 0.025) + 1.136 \cdot (1 + 0.195) \right] \Omega = (8.642 + 1.358)\,\Omega = 10.0\,\Omega
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The resistors are in series, therefore by the rule for **resistors in series** the total resistance is the sum of the two resistances, each of which depends on temperature according to the linear law, with $\Delta T = T - 20\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$. Collecting terms, the total resistance is a first-degree polynomial in $\Delta T$. For it not to depend on temperature the coefficient of $\Delta T$ must vanish, and the constant term must equal $10.0\,\Omega$: the two conditions must hold together and form the system.

The system has a solution because the two temperature coefficients have opposite signs: the increase in the resistance of the copper compensates the decrease in that of the carbon. At $70\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$, that is, with $\Delta T = 50\,\mathrm{K}$, the carbon loses $0.222\,\Omega$ and the copper gains the same amount, therefore the total resistance remains $10.0\,\Omega$. The compensation is exact as long as the linear law holds for both materials.

</div>
