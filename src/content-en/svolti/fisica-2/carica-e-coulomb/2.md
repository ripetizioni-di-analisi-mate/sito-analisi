## Exercise 1

Two identical conducting spheres $A$ and $B$ have charges $q_A = +8.0\,\mathrm{nC}$ and $q_B = -2.0\,\mathrm{nC}$. The spheres are brought into contact and then separated. Compute the final charge of each sphere, determine in which direction the electrons move and how many there are.

$$
q_A' = q_B' = \frac{q_A + q_B}{2} ,
\qquad
\Delta q_B = q_B' - q_B ,
\qquad
n = \frac{|\Delta q_B|}{e}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
q_A' = q_B' &= \frac{8.0 - 2.0}{2}\,\mathrm{nC} = +3.0\,\mathrm{nC} \\[6pt]
\Delta q_B &= \left( 3.0 - (-2.0) \right)\,\mathrm{nC} = +5.0\,\mathrm{nC} \\[6pt]
n &= \frac{5.0 \cdot 10^{-9}}{1.60 \cdot 10^{-19}} \approx 3.1 \cdot 10^{10}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The spheres are identical conductors, so after the contact by symmetry they have the same charge, and by **conservation of charge** the sum of the final charges equals the initial sum $+6.0\,\mathrm{nC}$. Each sphere therefore ends up with $+3.0\,\mathrm{nC}$.

In metallic conductors only the electrons move. The charge of $B$ increases by $5.0\,\mathrm{nC}$, and an increasing charge corresponds to electrons leaving; hence the electrons move from sphere $B$ to sphere $A$. The charge of $A$ decreases by the same amount, as it must. The number of electrons is obtained from the **quantization of charge**, by dividing the transferred charge by $e$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Two identical small conducting spheres, with charges $q_A = +5.0\,\mu\mathrm{C}$ and $q_B = -1.0\,\mu\mathrm{C}$, are in vacuum at distance $r = 0.30\,\mathrm{m}$. The spheres are brought into contact and then returned to the same distance. Compute the force between the spheres before and after the contact, treating them as point charges.

$$
F = k\,\frac{|q_A|\,|q_B|}{r^2} ,
\qquad
q' = \frac{q_A + q_B}{2} ,
\qquad
F' = k\,\frac{q'^2}{r^2}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
F &= 8.99 \cdot 10^{9} \cdot \frac{5.0 \cdot 10^{-6} \cdot 1.0 \cdot 10^{-6}}{0.30^2}\,\mathrm{N} \approx 0.50\,\mathrm{N} \\[6pt]
q' &= \frac{5.0 - 1.0}{2}\,\mu\mathrm{C} = +2.0\,\mu\mathrm{C} \\[6pt]
F' &= 8.99 \cdot 10^{9} \cdot \frac{\left( 2.0 \cdot 10^{-6} \right)^2}{0.30^2}\,\mathrm{N} \approx 0.40\,\mathrm{N}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Before the contact the charges have opposite signs, so the force is **attractive** and its magnitude is computed with **Coulomb's law**. During the contact the total charge $+4.0\,\mu\mathrm{C}$ is conserved and divides into equal parts between the two identical spheres. After the contact the spheres have the same positive charge, therefore the force becomes **repulsive**.

The magnitude goes from $0.50\,\mathrm{N}$ to $0.40\,\mathrm{N}$. The ratio between the two magnitudes is the ratio between the products of the charges, $\dfrac{4.0}{5.0} = 0.80$, because the distance is the same; this comparison checks the two calculations.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Three identical conducting spheres $A$, $B$ and $C$ are isolated. Initially $A$ has charge $Q = +16\,\mathrm{nC}$, and $B$ and $C$ are neutral. $A$ is brought into contact with $B$, then $A$ with $C$, finally $B$ with $C$, separating the spheres after each contact. Compute the final charges and the force between $B$ and $C$ when their centers are $3.0\,\mathrm{cm}$ apart, treating them as point charges.

$$
\begin{aligned}
A \text{ with } B : \quad & q_A = q_B = \frac{16 + 0}{2}\,\mathrm{nC} = 8.0\,\mathrm{nC} \\[6pt]
A \text{ with } C : \quad & q_A = q_C = \frac{8.0 + 0}{2}\,\mathrm{nC} = 4.0\,\mathrm{nC} \\[6pt]
B \text{ with } C : \quad & q_B = q_C = \frac{8.0 + 4.0}{2}\,\mathrm{nC} = 6.0\,\mathrm{nC}
\end{aligned}
$$
$$
F = k\,\frac{q_B\, q_C}{r^2} = 8.99 \cdot 10^{9} \cdot \frac{\left( 6.0 \cdot 10^{-9} \right)^2}{\left( 3.0 \cdot 10^{-2} \right)^2}\,\mathrm{N} \approx 3.6 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{N}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

At each contact between two identical spheres the rule for **contact between two identical conducting spheres** applies, according to which each sphere takes the average of the two charges that took part in the contact. The sphere that does not take part keeps its own charge. The final charges are $q_A = 4.0\,\mathrm{nC}$ and $q_B = q_C = 6.0\,\mathrm{nC}$, with sum $16\,\mathrm{nC}$, equal to the initial charge, as **conservation of charge** requires.

The spheres $B$ and $C$ have charges of the same sign, so they repel each other, and the magnitude of the force is given by Coulomb's law with the distance in meters.

</div>

## Exercise 4

Two identical small conducting spheres, placed in vacuum at distance $r = 0.50\,\mathrm{m}$, attract each other with a force of magnitude $F_1 = 0.432\,\mathrm{N}$. The spheres are brought into contact and then returned to the same distance, and now they repel each other with a force of magnitude $F_2 = 0.144\,\mathrm{N}$. Compute the initial charges, treating the spheres as point charges.

$$
\begin{cases}
q_1 q_2 = -\dfrac{F_1 r^2}{k} \\[10pt]
\left( \dfrac{q_1 + q_2}{2} \right)^2 = \dfrac{F_2 r^2}{k}
\end{cases}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\begin{cases}
q_1 q_2 = p \\[4pt]
q_1 + q_2 = s
\end{cases}
\quad\text{with}\quad
p = -\frac{F_1 r^2}{k} ,
\quad
s = \pm 2\,r\,\sqrt{\frac{F_2}{k}}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
p &= -\frac{0.432 \cdot 0.50^2}{8.99 \cdot 10^{9}}\,\mathrm{C^2} \approx -1.20 \cdot 10^{-11}\,\mathrm{C^2} \\[6pt]
s &= \pm 2 \cdot 0.50 \cdot \sqrt{\frac{0.144}{8.99 \cdot 10^{9}}}\,\mathrm{C} \approx \pm 4.00 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\mathrm{C}
\end{aligned}
$$
$$
t^2 - s\,t + p = 0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
t = \frac{4.00 \pm \sqrt{4.00^2 + 4 \cdot 12.0}}{2}\,\mu\mathrm{C} = \frac{4.00 \pm 8.00}{2}\,\mu\mathrm{C}
$$
$$
q_1 = +6.0\,\mu\mathrm{C} , \quad q_2 = -2.0\,\mu\mathrm{C}
\qquad\text{or}\qquad
q_1 = -6.0\,\mu\mathrm{C} , \quad q_2 = +2.0\,\mu\mathrm{C}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Before the contact the force is attractive, so the charges have opposite signs and their product is negative; **Coulomb's law** then gives $q_1 q_2 = -\dfrac{F_1 r^2}{k}$. After the contact each sphere has the charge $\dfrac{q_1 + q_2}{2}$, by **contact between two identical conducting spheres**, and Coulomb's law applied to the repulsive force gives the square of that charge. The two equations must hold together and form a system in the unknowns $q_1$ and $q_2$, of which the product and the sum are known, up to the sign of the sum.

Two numbers with sum $s$ and product $p$ are the solutions of the equation $t^2 - s\,t + p = 0$. With $s = +4.00\,\mu\mathrm{C}$ and $p = -12.0\,(\mu\mathrm{C})^2$ the solutions are $+6.0\,\mu\mathrm{C}$ and $-2.0\,\mu\mathrm{C}$; with $s = -4.00\,\mu\mathrm{C}$ the opposite charges are obtained. The forces depend only on the products of the charges, therefore the force measurements do *not* distinguish the two solutions; hence the charges are, in absolute value, $6.0\,\mu\mathrm{C}$ and $2.0\,\mu\mathrm{C}$, with opposite signs.

As a check, with $+6.0\,\mu\mathrm{C}$ and $-2.0\,\mu\mathrm{C}$ the initial force is $8.99 \cdot 10^{9} \cdot \dfrac{12 \cdot 10^{-12}}{0.25}\,\mathrm{N} \approx 0.432\,\mathrm{N}$, and after the contact each charge is $+2.0\,\mu\mathrm{C}$ and the force is $8.99 \cdot 10^{9} \cdot \dfrac{4.0 \cdot 10^{-12}}{0.25}\,\mathrm{N} \approx 0.144\,\mathrm{N}$.

</div>
