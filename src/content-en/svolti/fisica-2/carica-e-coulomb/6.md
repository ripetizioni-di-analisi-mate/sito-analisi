## Exercise 1

A small sphere of mass $m = 2.0\,\mathrm{g}$ and charge $q = +0.50\,\mu\mathrm{C}$ can move without friction inside a vertical insulating tube. A point charge $Q$ is fixed at the bottom of the tube. The sphere remains at rest at height $h = 10\,\mathrm{cm}$ above $Q$. Compute $Q$ and determine whether the equilibrium is stable for displacements along the tube.

$$
k\,\frac{Qq}{h^2} - mg = 0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
Q = \frac{mg\,h^2}{kq}
$$
$$
Q = \frac{2.0 \cdot 10^{-3} \cdot 9.81 \cdot 0.10^2}{8.99 \cdot 10^{9} \cdot 0.50 \cdot 10^{-6}}\,\mathrm{C} = \frac{1.96 \cdot 10^{-4}}{4.50 \cdot 10^{3}}\,\mathrm{C} \approx 4.4 \cdot 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{C} = 44\,\mathrm{nC}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

On the sphere act the weight, the electric force of $Q$ and the reaction of the tube, which is horizontal because the tube is smooth. Along the vertical, by the **equilibrium of a charge**, the electric force must balance the weight; hence it must be directed upward, that is repulsive, and $Q$ is positive like $q$. Equating the magnitude of the Coulomb force to the weight gives $Q = 44\,\mathrm{nC}$, with the mass in kilograms and the height in meters.

If the sphere rises, the distance increases and the repulsion decreases, so the weight prevails and brings it back down; if it descends, the repulsion increases and pushes it back up. Therefore along the tube the equilibrium is **stable**. Without the tube, a horizontal displacement would give the repulsion a component that moves the sphere away from the vertical, and the equilibrium would be unstable; the reaction of the tube prevents this displacement.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Two identical small spheres of mass $m = 1.0\,\mathrm{g}$, with the same charge $q$, hang from the same point on two insulating strings of length $\ell = 50\,\mathrm{cm}$ and negligible mass. At equilibrium each string forms with the vertical the angle $\theta = 10^\circ$. Compute $q$.

$$
\begin{cases}
F - T\sin\theta = 0 \\[4pt]
T\cos\theta - mg = 0
\end{cases}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
F = mg\tan\theta ,
\qquad
r = 2\ell\sin\theta ,
\qquad
q = r\,\sqrt{\frac{F}{k}}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
r &= 2 \cdot 0.50 \cdot \sin 10^\circ\,\mathrm{m} \approx 0.174\,\mathrm{m} \\[6pt]
F &= 1.0 \cdot 10^{-3} \cdot 9.81 \cdot \tan 10^\circ\,\mathrm{N} \approx 1.73 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{N} \\[6pt]
q &= 0.174 \cdot \sqrt{\frac{1.73 \cdot 10^{-3}}{8.99 \cdot 10^{9}}}\,\mathrm{C} \approx 0.174 \cdot 4.39 \cdot 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{C} \approx 7.6 \cdot 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{C}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

On each sphere act the weight, the tension of the string and the electric repulsion of the other sphere, which is horizontal because the two spheres are at the same height. The two equilibrium conditions, along the horizontal and along the vertical, must hold together; dividing the first by the second eliminates the tension and gives $F = mg\tan\theta$, as in the **equilibrium of two charged pendulums**.

Each sphere is at distance $\ell\sin\theta$ from the vertical through the point of suspension, so the distance between the spheres is $2\ell\sin\theta$. By Coulomb's law $F = k\,\dfrac{q^2}{r^2}$, from which we obtain $q \approx 76\,\mathrm{nC}$. The calculation gives only the absolute value of the charge; the sign cannot be obtained from the angle, because two equal negative charges repel each other in the same way.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A point charge $Q = +2.0\,\mu\mathrm{C}$ is fixed on a smooth insulating inclined plane, which forms the angle $\alpha = 30^\circ$ with the horizontal. A small sphere of mass $m = 10\,\mathrm{g}$ and charge $q = +1.0\,\mu\mathrm{C}$ rests on the plane, higher than $Q$ along the line of steepest slope. Compute the distance $d$ between the charges in the equilibrium position.

::grafico{assi=0 uguale=1 poligoni="0,0 6.93,0 6.93,4" punti="1.73,1,pieno;4.33,2.5,pieno" vettori="4.33,2.5,5.63,3.25,F;4.33,2.5,4.33,-0.5,P;4.33,2.5,3.03,4.75,N" etichette="1.65,0.45,Q;3.7,2.45,q;1.1,0.2,α;2.85,2.05,d" xmin=-0.5 xmax=7.5 ymin=-0.9 ymax=5.1 titolo="On the sphere act the weight $\vec{P}$, the normal force $\vec{N}$ and the repulsion $\vec{F}$ of $Q$, parallel to the plane and directed upward"}

$$
k\,\frac{Qq}{d^2} - mg\sin\alpha = 0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
d = \sqrt{\frac{kQq}{mg\sin\alpha}}
$$
$$
d = \sqrt{\frac{8.99 \cdot 10^{9} \cdot 2.0 \cdot 10^{-6} \cdot 1.0 \cdot 10^{-6}}{1.0 \cdot 10^{-2} \cdot 9.81 \cdot 0.50}}\,\mathrm{m} = \sqrt{\frac{1.80 \cdot 10^{-2}}{4.91 \cdot 10^{-2}}}\,\mathrm{m} \approx 0.61\,\mathrm{m}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The two charges lie on the same line of steepest slope, so the electric force is parallel to the plane, and it is directed upward because the charges are positive and repel each other. The normal force is perpendicular to the plane, and the plane is smooth, therefore along the plane only the electric force and the component of the weight $mg\sin\alpha$, directed downward, act. By the **equilibrium of a charge** the two components along the plane must have equal magnitude; perpendicular to the plane the normal force balances the component $mg\cos\alpha$ of the weight.

Solving for $d$ gives $d \approx 0.61\,\mathrm{m}$. The equilibrium along the plane is stable, because if the sphere rises the repulsion decreases and the weight prevails, and if it descends the repulsion increases and pushes it back up.

</div>

## Exercise 4

Two identical small spheres of mass $m = 2.0\,\mathrm{g}$ and charge $q = 40\,\mathrm{nC}$ hang from the same point on two insulating strings of length $\ell = 1.0\,\mathrm{m}$. Compute the distance between the spheres at equilibrium using the small-angle approximation, and check that the approximation is justified.

$$
k\,\frac{q^2}{r^2} = mg\,\frac{r}{2\ell}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
r = \left( \frac{2kq^2\ell}{mg} \right)^{\frac{1}{3}} ,
\qquad
\sin\theta = \frac{r}{2\ell}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
r^3 &= \frac{2 \cdot 8.99 \cdot 10^{9} \cdot \left( 40 \cdot 10^{-9} \right)^2 \cdot 1.0}{2.0 \cdot 10^{-3} \cdot 9.81}\,\mathrm{m^3} = \frac{2.88 \cdot 10^{-5}}{1.96 \cdot 10^{-2}}\,\mathrm{m^3} \approx 1.47 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m^3} \\[6pt]
r &\approx 0.114\,\mathrm{m} \approx 11\,\mathrm{cm} \\[6pt]
\sin\theta &= \frac{0.114}{2.0} \approx 0.057 , \qquad \theta \approx 3.3^\circ
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

By the **equilibrium of two charged pendulums** we have $\tan\theta = \dfrac{F}{mg}$. For small angles the tangent is approximately equal to the sine, and the sine equals $\dfrac{r}{2\ell}$; substituting the Coulomb force gives an equation in the single unknown $r$, which gives $r^3$ and hence $r$ as a cube root.

The angle turns out to be about $3.3^\circ$, that is $0.057\,\mathrm{rad}$; for angles of this size tangent and sine differ by less than $0.2\,\%$, so the approximation is justified. Solving numerically the exact equation $\tan\theta = \dfrac{kq^2}{mg\,(2\ell\sin\theta)^2}$ gives $r \approx 0.1135\,\mathrm{m}$, which coincides with the approximate value within the significant figures of the data.

</div>
