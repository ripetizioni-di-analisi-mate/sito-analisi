## Exercise 1

Two point charges $Q = +2.0\,\mu\mathrm{C}$ are fixed in vacuum at the points $(-0.30\,\mathrm{m};\,0)$ and $(0.30\,\mathrm{m};\,0)$. Compute the force on the charge $q_0 = +1.0\,\mu\mathrm{C}$ placed at the point $(0;\,0.40\,\mathrm{m})$.

$$
\vec{F} = \frac{2kQq_0\,y}{\left( a^2 + y^2 \right)^{\frac{3}{2}}}\,\hat{\jmath}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
a^2 + y^2 &= (0.090 + 0.160)\,\mathrm{m^2} = 0.25\,\mathrm{m^2} , \qquad \left( a^2 + y^2 \right)^{\frac{3}{2}} = 0.50^3\,\mathrm{m^3} = 0.125\,\mathrm{m^3} \\[6pt]
F &= \frac{2 \cdot 8.99 \cdot 10^{9} \cdot 2.0 \cdot 10^{-6} \cdot 1.0 \cdot 10^{-6} \cdot 0.40}{0.125}\,\mathrm{N} \approx 0.115\,\mathrm{N} \approx 0.12\,\mathrm{N}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The two charges are equal and $q_0$ lies on the perpendicular bisector of the segment joining them, so the **force on the axis of two equal charges** applies, with $a = 0.30\,\mathrm{m}$ and $y = 0.40\,\mathrm{m}$. Each charge is $0.50\,\mathrm{m}$ from $q_0$; the horizontal components of the two forces cancel by symmetry, and the vertical components remain.

The force is directed in the positive direction of the $y$-axis, that is away from the segment, because all the charges are positive. As a check, each force has magnitude $k\,\dfrac{Qq_0}{0.25} \approx 0.0719\,\mathrm{N}$ and vertical component $0.0719 \cdot \dfrac{0.40}{0.50}\,\mathrm{N} \approx 0.0575\,\mathrm{N}$, and twice that is $0.115\,\mathrm{N}$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

With the charges of the previous exercise, $Q = +2.0\,\mu\mathrm{C}$ at $(\pm a, 0)$ with $a = 0.30\,\mathrm{m}$ and $q_0 = +1.0\,\mu\mathrm{C}$ on the axis at $(0, y)$ with $y > 0$, determine the point of the axis at which the magnitude of the force on $q_0$ is maximum and the maximum value.

$$
F(y) = 2kQq_0\,\frac{y}{\left( a^2 + y^2 \right)^{\frac{3}{2}}}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
F'(y) &= 2kQq_0\,\frac{\left( a^2 + y^2 \right)^{\frac{3}{2}} - y \cdot \dfrac{3}{2}\left( a^2 + y^2 \right)^{\frac{1}{2}} \cdot 2y}{\left( a^2 + y^2 \right)^3} \\[6pt]
&= 2kQq_0\,\frac{a^2 + y^2 - 3y^2}{\left( a^2 + y^2 \right)^{\frac{5}{2}}} \\[6pt]
&= 2kQq_0\,\frac{a^2 - 2y^2}{\left( a^2 + y^2 \right)^{\frac{5}{2}}}
\end{aligned}
$$
$$
F'(y) = 0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad y^* = \frac{a}{\sqrt{2}} ,
\qquad
F(y^*) = 2kQq_0\,\frac{\dfrac{a}{\sqrt{2}}}{\left( \dfrac{3}{2}\,a^2 \right)^{\frac{3}{2}}} = \frac{4kQq_0}{3\sqrt{3}\,a^2}
$$
$$
y^* = \frac{0.30}{\sqrt{2}}\,\mathrm{m} \approx 0.21\,\mathrm{m} ,
\qquad
F(y^*) = \frac{4 \cdot 8.99 \cdot 10^{9} \cdot 2.0 \cdot 10^{-12}}{3\sqrt{3} \cdot 0.090}\,\mathrm{N} \approx 0.15\,\mathrm{N}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

By the **force on the axis of two equal charges**, the magnitude of the force as a function of $y$ is the function $F(y)$, which is zero at $y = 0$ and tends to zero as $y \to +\infty$; hence it has a maximum for some $y > 0$. The derivative is computed with the quotient rule and the derivative of a power, and factoring out $\left( a^2 + y^2 \right)^{\frac{1}{2}}$ the numerator reduces to $a^2 - 2y^2$.

The sign of the derivative is the sign of $a^2 - 2y^2$, positive for $0 < y < \dfrac{a}{\sqrt{2}}$ and negative for $y > \dfrac{a}{\sqrt{2}}$. Therefore $F$ increases and then decreases, and $y^* = \dfrac{a}{\sqrt{2}} \approx 0.21\,\mathrm{m}$ is the point of **maximum**, as indicated by the maximum force on the axis. The maximum value, about $0.15\,\mathrm{N}$, is greater than the force $0.115\,\mathrm{N}$ found at $y = 0.40\,\mathrm{m}$, as it must be.

</div>

## Exercise 3

The point charges $+q$ and $-q$, with $q = 2.0\,\mu\mathrm{C}$, are fixed in vacuum at the points $(-a, 0)$ and $(a, 0)$, with $a = 0.10\,\mathrm{m}$. Compute the force on the charge $q_0 = +1.0\,\mu\mathrm{C}$ placed at the point $(0, y)$, for $y = 0.10\,\mathrm{m}$ and for $y = 1.0\,\mathrm{m}$, and compare the second value with the approximation for $y \gg a$.

$$
\vec{F} = \frac{2kq\,q_0\,a}{\left( a^2 + y^2 \right)^{\frac{3}{2}}}\,\hat{\imath} ,
\qquad
F \approx \frac{2kq\,q_0\,a}{y^3} \quad (y \gg a)
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
y = 0.10\,\mathrm{m} : \quad F &= \frac{2 \cdot 8.99 \cdot 10^{9} \cdot 2.0 \cdot 10^{-12} \cdot 0.10}{(0.020)^{\frac{3}{2}}}\,\mathrm{N} = \frac{3.60 \cdot 10^{-3}}{2.83 \cdot 10^{-3}}\,\mathrm{N} \approx 1.3\,\mathrm{N} \\[6pt]
y = 1.0\,\mathrm{m} : \quad F &= \frac{3.60 \cdot 10^{-3}}{(1.01)^{\frac{3}{2}}}\,\mathrm{N} \approx 3.54 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{N} \\[6pt]
F_{\text{appr}} &= \frac{3.60 \cdot 10^{-3}}{1.0^3}\,\mathrm{N} = 3.60 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{N}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

For the **two opposite charges**, the charge $q_0$ on the perpendicular bisector of the segment is equidistant from $+q$ and from $-q$. The force of $+q$ is repulsive and points from $(-a, 0)$ toward $q_0$; the force of $-q$ is attractive and points from $q_0$ toward $(a, 0)$. The components along $y$ are opposite and cancel, while the components along $x$ are both positive and add up; therefore the force is parallel to the $x$-axis and directed from $+q$ toward $-q$.

At $y = 1.0\,\mathrm{m}$, ten times farther than $a$, the force is about $3.5 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{N}$, and the approximation $\dfrac{2kq\,q_0\,a}{y^3}$ exceeds it by about $1.5\,\%$, because it neglects $a^2$ compared with $y^2$. The distance has increased by a factor of $10$ and the force has decreased by a factor of about $360$, more than the force of a single charge would, which would decrease by a factor of $100$; for $y \gg a$ the force decreases as $\dfrac{1}{y^3}$.

</div>

## Exercise 4

Five equal point charges $q = +1.0\,\mu\mathrm{C}$ occupy five of the six vertices of a regular hexagon of side $a = 0.10\,\mathrm{m}$, in vacuum; the sixth vertex is empty. Compute the force on the charge $q_0 = +0.50\,\mu\mathrm{C}$ placed at the center of the hexagon.

::grafico{assi=0 uguale=1 linee="2,0 1,1.732 -1,1.732 -2,0 -1,-1.732 1,-1.732 2,0" punti="2,0,vuoto;1,1.732,pieno,+q;-1,1.732,pieno,+q;-2,0,pieno,+q;-1,-1.732,pieno,+q;1,-1.732,pieno,+q;0,0,pieno" vettori="0,0,1.2,0,F" etichette="-0.35,-0.45,q₀" xmin=-2.7 xmax=3.2 ymin=-2.2 ymax=2.3 titolo="Five equal charges at the vertices of a regular hexagon: the force on $q_0$ at the center is directed toward the empty vertex"}

$$
\vec{F}_{\text{five}} = \vec{F}_{\text{six}} - \vec{F}_{\text{empty vertex}} = \vec{0} - \vec{F}_{\text{empty vertex}}
$$
$$
F = k\,\frac{q\,q_0}{a^2} = 8.99 \cdot 10^{9} \cdot \frac{1.0 \cdot 10^{-6} \cdot 0.50 \cdot 10^{-6}}{0.10^2}\,\mathrm{N} \approx 0.45\,\mathrm{N}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

In a regular hexagon the distance of each vertex from the center equals the side, so each charge is at distance $a$ from $q_0$. If there were six charges, the forces on $q_0$ would be six vectors of equal magnitude, opposite in pairs, because the vertices of the hexagon are symmetric in pairs with respect to the center; their sum would be zero.

By the **superposition principle**, the force of the five charges is the force of the six charges minus the force that a charge $q$ placed at the empty vertex would exert. The first is zero, so the total force is the opposite of the force of that charge. A positive charge at the empty vertex would repel $q_0$, pushing it away from that vertex; therefore the force of the five charges is directed **toward the empty vertex**, with magnitude $k\,\dfrac{q\,q_0}{a^2} \approx 0.45\,\mathrm{N}$. The argument avoids adding five vectors in components.

</div>
