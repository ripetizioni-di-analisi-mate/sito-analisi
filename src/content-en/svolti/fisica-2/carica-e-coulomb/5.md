## Exercise 1

Two point charges $q_1 = +2.0\,\mu\mathrm{C}$ and $q_2 = +8.0\,\mu\mathrm{C}$ are fixed in vacuum, on the $x$-axis, at $x = 0$ and at $x = d = 0.60\,\mathrm{m}$. Determine the point at which a third charge, subject only to the forces of $q_1$ and $q_2$, is in equilibrium.

$$
k\,\frac{q_1\,|q_0|}{x_0^2} = k\,\frac{q_2\,|q_0|}{(d - x_0)^2}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\frac{d - x_0}{x_0} = \sqrt{\frac{q_2}{q_1}}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
x_0 = \frac{\sqrt{q_1}}{\sqrt{q_1} + \sqrt{q_2}}\,d
$$
$$
x_0 = \frac{\sqrt{2.0}}{\sqrt{2.0} + \sqrt{8.0}} \cdot 0.60\,\mathrm{m} = \frac{1}{1 + 2} \cdot 0.60\,\mathrm{m} = 0.20\,\mathrm{m}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The two fixed charges have the same sign, so by the **equilibrium position between two fixed charges** equilibrium occurs only between the two charges, where the forces on $q_0$ have opposite orientations. Outside the segment the two forces have the same orientation, and off the axis they have different directions; in neither case is the sum zero.

When the magnitudes are set equal the factor $k\,|q_0|$ cancels, therefore the point does not depend on the third charge. Since $\sqrt{\dfrac{q_2}{q_1}} = \sqrt{4.0} = 2.0$, the charge in equilibrium is twice as far from $q_2$ as from $q_1$, and the equilibrium point is $x_0 = 0.20\,\mathrm{m}$, closer to the smaller charge.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Two point charges $q_1 = +9.0\,\mathrm{nC}$ and $q_2 = -4.0\,\mathrm{nC}$ are fixed in vacuum, on the $x$-axis, at $x = 0$ and at $x = d = 0.10\,\mathrm{m}$. Determine the point of the axis at which a third charge is in equilibrium.

$$
k\,\frac{|q_1|\,|q_0|}{x^2} = k\,\frac{|q_2|\,|q_0|}{(x - d)^2} , \quad x > d
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\frac{x - d}{x} = \sqrt{\frac{|q_2|}{|q_1|}} = \frac{2}{3}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
x = 3d
$$
$$
x = 3 \cdot 0.10\,\mathrm{m} = 0.30\,\mathrm{m}
$$
$$
\frac{|q_1|}{x^2} = \frac{9.0}{0.090} = 100 , \qquad \frac{|q_2|}{(x - d)^2} = \frac{4.0}{0.040} = 100
$$

::grafico{assi=0 uguale=1 punti="0,0,pieno;1,0,pieno;3,0,pieno" vettori="3,0,3.85,0,F₁;3,0,2.15,0,F₂" segmenti="0,-0.5,3,-0.5" etichette="-0.15,0.25,q₁;0.85,0.25,q₂;2.9,-0.3,q₀;1.2,-0.85,x = 0.30 m" xmin=-0.6 xmax=4.4 ymin=-1.1 ymax=0.6 titolo="With $q_1 = +9.0\,\mathrm{nC}$ and $q_2 = -4.0\,\mathrm{nC}$ equilibrium occurs beyond the smaller charge, where the forces $\vec{F}_1$ and $\vec{F}_2$ on a positive charge $q_0$ are opposite and of equal magnitude"}

<div class="spiegazione">

The fixed charges have opposite signs, so the **equilibrium near two charges of opposite sign** applies. Between the two charges a test charge is repelled by one and attracted by the other, and the two forces have the same orientation; therefore there is no equilibrium there. To the left of $q_1$ the forces have opposite orientations, but the larger charge $q_1$ is also the closer one, and its force always prevails. The equilibrium is therefore sought to the right of $q_2$, the charge of smaller absolute value.

For $x > d$ the distances are $x$ from $q_1$ and $x - d$ from $q_2$, and equating the magnitudes gives $\dfrac{x - d}{x} = \dfrac{2}{3}$, that is $x = 3d$. The final substitution checks that the two ratios $\dfrac{|q|}{r^2}$ coincide. The equilibrium point is $x = 0.30\,\mathrm{m}$.

Pay attention to the region in which the solution is sought. The equation $\dfrac{9}{x^2} = \dfrac{4}{(x - d)^2}$, written without the condition $x > d$, also has the solution $x = 0.06\,\mathrm{m}$, which lies between the two charges. At that point the magnitudes are equal, but the two forces have the same orientation and the total force is twice each of them, so $x = 0.06\,\mathrm{m}$ is *not* an equilibrium position.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Two point charges $q_1 = +9.0\,\mathrm{nC}$ and $q_2 = +1.0\,\mathrm{nC}$ are placed in vacuum at $x = 0$ and at $x = d = 0.40\,\mathrm{m}$, and are free to move. Determine the value and position of a third charge $q_3$ that puts all three charges in equilibrium.

$$
x_0 = \frac{\sqrt{q_1}}{\sqrt{q_1} + \sqrt{q_2}}\,d ,
\qquad
q_3 = -\frac{q_1 q_2}{\left( \sqrt{q_1} + \sqrt{q_2} \right)^2}
$$
$$
x_0 = \frac{3}{3 + 1} \cdot 0.40\,\mathrm{m} = 0.30\,\mathrm{m} ,
\qquad
q_3 = -\frac{9.0 \cdot 1.0}{(3 + 1)^2}\,\mathrm{nC} = -\frac{9}{16}\,\mathrm{nC} \approx -0.56\,\mathrm{nC}
$$
$$
\text{check on } q_2 : \quad
\frac{q_1}{d^2} = \frac{9.0}{0.16} = 56.25 , \qquad
\frac{|q_3|}{(d - x_0)^2} = \frac{0.5625}{0.010} = 56.25
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

By the **equilibrium of three collinear charges**, the third charge must lie at the equilibrium position between $q_1$ and $q_2$, because otherwise $q_3$ itself is not in equilibrium. The point is $x_0 = 0.30\,\mathrm{m}$, at distance $0.10\,\mathrm{m}$ from $q_2$, the smaller charge.

The charge $q_1$ is repelled by $q_2$ to the left, so $q_3$ must attract it to the right and is negative; its absolute value is obtained by equating the magnitudes of the two forces on $q_1$, and equals $\dfrac{9}{16}\,\mathrm{nC}$. The equilibrium of $q_2$ follows from the sum of the internal forces. The check confirms it: $q_2$ is repelled by $q_1$ to the right and attracted by $q_3$ to the left, and the two ratios $\dfrac{|q|}{r^2}$ are equal, so the two forces have the same magnitude.

</div>

## Exercise 4

Two point charges $q_1 = +4.0\,\mu\mathrm{C}$ and $q_2 = +1.0\,\mu\mathrm{C}$ are fixed in vacuum at $(0, 0)$ and at $(d, 0)$, with $d = 0.30\,\mathrm{m}$. A charge $q_0 = +1.0\,\mu\mathrm{C}$ is in equilibrium at the point of the $x$-axis between the two charges. Compute the force on $q_0$ when it is displaced by $\delta = 1.0\,\mathrm{mm}$ along the $x$-axis, toward $q_2$, and when it is displaced by $\delta$ perpendicular to the axis, and determine in which directions the equilibrium is stable.

$$
x_0 = \frac{\sqrt{q_1}}{\sqrt{q_1} + \sqrt{q_2}}\,d = \frac{2}{3} \cdot 0.30\,\mathrm{m} = 0.20\,\mathrm{m} ,
\qquad
d - x_0 = 0.10\,\mathrm{m}
$$
$$
F_x(x) = k q_0 \left( \frac{q_1}{x^2} - \frac{q_2}{(d - x)^2} \right) ,
\qquad
F_x(x_0 + \delta) \approx F_x'(x_0)\,\delta = -2 k q_0 \left( \frac{q_1}{x_0^3} + \frac{q_2}{(d - x_0)^3} \right) \delta
$$
$$
F_y \approx k q_0 \left( \frac{q_1}{x_0^2} \cdot \frac{\delta}{x_0} + \frac{q_2}{(d - x_0)^2} \cdot \frac{\delta}{d - x_0} \right) = k q_0 \left( \frac{q_1}{x_0^3} + \frac{q_2}{(d - x_0)^3} \right) \delta
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{q_1}{x_0^3} + \frac{q_2}{(d - x_0)^3} &= \left( \frac{4.0 \cdot 10^{-6}}{0.20^3} + \frac{1.0 \cdot 10^{-6}}{0.10^3} \right)\,\mathrm{C/m^3} = 1.5 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{C/m^3} \\[6pt]
F_x &\approx -2 \cdot 8.99 \cdot 10^{9} \cdot 1.0 \cdot 10^{-6} \cdot 1.5 \cdot 10^{-3} \cdot 1.0 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{N} \approx -0.027\,\mathrm{N} \\[6pt]
F_y &\approx 8.99 \cdot 10^{9} \cdot 1.0 \cdot 10^{-6} \cdot 1.5 \cdot 10^{-3} \cdot 1.0 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{N} \approx +0.013\,\mathrm{N}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The equilibrium point is found with the **equilibrium position between two fixed charges** and is $x_0 = 0.20\,\mathrm{m}$. On the axis the force on $q_0$ has only the component $F_x(x)$, the sum of the repulsion of $q_1$ to the right and the repulsion of $q_2$ to the left. Since $F_x(x_0) = 0$, for a small displacement the force is approximated by the linear term $F_x'(x_0)\,\delta$, with the derivative computed term by term. The derivative is negative, so for $\delta > 0$ the force is negative and brings $q_0$ back toward $x_0$; therefore along the axis the equilibrium is **stable**. The exact calculation, $F_x(0.201\,\mathrm{m}) \approx -0.0272\,\mathrm{N}$, confirms the approximation.

With a displacement $\delta$ perpendicular to the axis, the distances from $q_1$ and from $q_2$ remain $x_0$ and $d - x_0$ up to terms in $\delta^2$, and each repulsive force acquires a component along $y$ equal to its magnitude times the sine of the angle with the axis, about $\dfrac{\delta}{x_0}$ and $\dfrac{\delta}{d - x_0}$. Both components move $q_0$ away from the axis, so $F_y > 0$ and in the perpendicular direction the equilibrium is **unstable**, as predicted by the stability of electrostatic equilibrium.

</div>
