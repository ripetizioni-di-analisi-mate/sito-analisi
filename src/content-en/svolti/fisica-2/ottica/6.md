## Exercise 1

A slit of width $a = 0.11\,\mathrm{mm}$ is illuminated perpendicularly with light of wavelength $\lambda = 550\,\mathrm{nm}$, and the diffraction pattern is observed on a screen at $L = 1.5\,\mathrm{m}$. Compute the direction of the first minimum and the width of the central maximum on the screen.

$$
a \sin\theta_1 = \lambda , \qquad
y_1 = L \tan\theta_1 \approx L\,\theta_1 , \qquad
w = 2 y_1 = \frac{2 \lambda L}{a}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
\sin\theta_1 &= \frac{5.50 \cdot 10^{-7}}{1.1 \cdot 10^{-4}} = 5.0 \cdot 10^{-3}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\theta_1 \approx 5.0 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{rad} \\[6pt]
y_1 &= 1.5 \cdot 5.0 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m} = 7.5\,\mathrm{mm} , \qquad
w = 15\,\mathrm{mm}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The first diffraction minimum corresponds to $m = 1$ in the condition for **single-slit diffraction minima**. The sine is $5.0 \cdot 10^{-3}$, so the angle is small and, in radians, coincides with its sine and its tangent to within $0.01\,\%$; the distance of the minimum from the center of the screen is therefore $L\,\theta_1$.

The central maximum extends from the first minimum on one side to the first minimum on the other, which are symmetric with respect to the center, so it is $2 y_1 = 15\,\mathrm{mm}$ wide. The secondary maxima are half as wide, because the subsequent minima are spaced $\dfrac{\lambda L}{a} = 7.5\,\mathrm{mm}$ apart.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A laser beam with $\lambda = 650\,\mathrm{nm}$ passes through a thin slit, and on a wall at $L = 2.0\,\mathrm{m}$ the central diffraction maximum is $w = 2.6\,\mathrm{cm}$ wide. Compute the width of the slit. By how much does the width of the central maximum change if the width of the slit is halved?

$$
w = \frac{2 \lambda L}{a}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
a = \frac{2 \lambda L}{w}
$$
$$
a = \frac{2 \cdot 6.50 \cdot 10^{-7} \cdot 2.0}{2.6 \cdot 10^{-2}}\,\mathrm{m} = 1.0 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m} = 0.10\,\mathrm{mm} , \qquad
a' = \frac{a}{2}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
w' = 2w = 5.2\,\mathrm{cm}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The width of the central maximum is obtained from the **single-slit diffraction minima** with $m = \pm 1$, in the small-angle approximation, which can be verified afterwards: $\sin\theta_1 = \dfrac{\lambda}{a} = 6.5 \cdot 10^{-3}$. Inverting the formula gives the width of the slit, $0.10\,\mathrm{mm}$, that is, about one hundred and fifty wavelengths.

The width of the central maximum is inversely proportional to $a$, so halving the slit **doubles** the central maximum. This is the typical behavior of diffraction: the narrower the aperture, the more the light spreads out beyond it.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A grating with $500$ lines per millimeter is illuminated perpendicularly by the yellow light of sodium, with $\lambda = 589\,\mathrm{nm}$. Compute the grating spacing, the directions of the principal maxima and the total number of visible principal maxima.

$$
d = \frac{1}{500}\,\mathrm{mm} = 2.00 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\mathrm{m} , \qquad
\sin\theta_m = \frac{m \lambda}{d} = 0.2945\, m
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
m = 1 : &\quad \sin\theta_1 = 0.2945 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \theta_1 \approx 17.1^\circ \\[6pt]
m = 2 : &\quad \sin\theta_2 = 0.589 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \theta_2 \approx 36.1^\circ \\[6pt]
m = 3 : &\quad \sin\theta_3 = 0.8835 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \theta_3 \approx 62.1^\circ \\[6pt]
m = 4 : &\quad \sin\theta_4 = 1.178 > 1
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The spacing is the distance between two adjacent lines, that is, the reciprocal of the number of lines per unit length. By the condition for the **principal maxima of the grating** the sine of the angle of the maximum of order $m$ is $m$ times $\dfrac{\lambda}{d} = 0.2945$. For $m = 4$ the sine would exceed $1$, so the fourth order does not exist; the maximum order is the integer part of $\dfrac{d}{\lambda} \approx 3.40$, that is, $3$.

The angles here are not small, so they are computed with the arcsine. The visible principal maxima are the central maximum and, on each side, those of orders $1$, $2$ and $3$, **seven** maxima in all.

</div>

## Exercise 4

A grating with $400$ lines per millimeter is illuminated perpendicularly with white light, which contains all wavelengths between $400\,\mathrm{nm}$ and $700\,\mathrm{nm}$. Compute the angular width of the first-order spectrum. Then show that, for any grating spacing, the second-order and third-order spectra partly overlap.

$$
d = \frac{1}{400}\,\mathrm{mm} = 2.50 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\mathrm{m} , \qquad
\sin\theta = \frac{\lambda}{d}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
\lambda = 400\,\mathrm{nm} : &\quad \sin\theta_V = 0.160 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \theta_V \approx 9.21^\circ \\[6pt]
\lambda = 700\,\mathrm{nm} : &\quad \sin\theta_R = 0.280 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \theta_R \approx 16.26^\circ \\[6pt]
\Delta\theta &= \theta_R - \theta_V \approx 7.05^\circ
\end{aligned}
$$
$$
2 \cdot 700\,\mathrm{nm} = 1400\,\mathrm{nm} > 3 \cdot 400\,\mathrm{nm} = 1200\,\mathrm{nm}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\frac{2 \lambda_R}{d} > \frac{3 \lambda_V}{d}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

In the first order each wavelength has its maximum in the direction $\sin\theta = \dfrac{\lambda}{d}$, increasing with $\lambda$, so the spectrum goes from violet, deviated by about $9.2^\circ$, to red, deviated by about $16.3^\circ$, and spans about $7.1^\circ$.

The second-order spectrum ends in the direction of red, with $\sin\theta = \dfrac{2\lambda_R}{d}$; the third-order spectrum begins in the direction of violet, with $\sin\theta = \dfrac{3\lambda_V}{d}$. Since $2\lambda_R = 1400\,\mathrm{nm}$ exceeds $3\lambda_V = 1200\,\mathrm{nm}$, the red end of the second order is deviated more than the beginning of the third, and the two spectra **overlap**. The comparison does not depend on $d$, which divides both sides; for example, in the direction in which the orange at $600\,\mathrm{nm}$ of the second order falls, the violet at $400\,\mathrm{nm}$ of the third order also falls, because $2 \cdot 600 = 3 \cdot 400$.

</div>
