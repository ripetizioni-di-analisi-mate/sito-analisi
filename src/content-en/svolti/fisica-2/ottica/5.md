## Exercise 1

The light of a helium-neon laser, with $\lambda = 633\,\mathrm{nm}$, illuminates perpendicularly two narrow slits at distance $d = 0.25\,\mathrm{mm}$. Interference fringes are observed on a screen at $L = 2.0\,\mathrm{m}$. Compute the fringe spacing, the distance from the center of the third-order bright fringe and that of the first dark fringe.

$$
\Delta y = \frac{\lambda L}{d} , \qquad
y_m = m\,\Delta y , \qquad
y_{\text{dark}} = \left( m + \frac{1}{2} \right) \Delta y
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta y &= \frac{6.33 \cdot 10^{-7} \cdot 2.0}{2.5 \cdot 10^{-4}}\,\mathrm{m} \approx 5.1 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m} = 5.1\,\mathrm{mm} \\[6pt]
y_3 &= 3\,\Delta y \approx 15\,\mathrm{mm} \\[6pt]
y_{\text{dark}} &= \frac{1}{2}\,\Delta y \approx 2.5\,\mathrm{mm}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The slits are illuminated by the same wavefront, so they are two coherent in-phase sources, and the screen is at a distance much greater than $d$. The fringes near the center correspond to small angles: the third-order fringe is in the direction $\sin\theta = \dfrac{3\lambda}{d} \approx 7.6 \cdot 10^{-3}$. The small-angle formulas of **two-slit interference** therefore hold, with the bright fringes equally spaced by the fringe spacing $\dfrac{\lambda L}{d}$.

The bright fringe of order $3$ is three fringe spacings from the center. The first dark fringe corresponds to a path difference of half a wavelength, so it lies halfway between the central fringe and the first bright fringe. The computation shows how the experiment makes a wavelength of fractions of a micrometer measurable: a small distance between the slits and a distant screen produce fringes millimeters apart.

</div>

## Exercise 2

In a Young experiment the slits are $d = 0.30\,\mathrm{mm}$ apart and the screen is at $L = 1.20\,\mathrm{m}$. The distance between the first and the sixth bright fringe, on the same side of the center, is $10.0\,\mathrm{mm}$. Compute the wavelength of the light.

$$
y_6 - y_1 = 5\,\Delta y = \frac{5 \lambda L}{d}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\lambda = \frac{d\,(y_6 - y_1)}{5 L}
$$
$$
\lambda = \frac{3.0 \cdot 10^{-4} \cdot 1.00 \cdot 10^{-2}}{5 \cdot 1.20}\,\mathrm{m} = 5.0 \cdot 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{m} = 500\,\mathrm{nm}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The bright fringes are equally spaced by the fringe spacing $\Delta y$, and between the first and the sixth there are five intervals, so the measured distance is $5\,\Delta y$ and the fringe spacing is $2.0\,\mathrm{mm}$. From the formula of **two-slit interference** one obtains $\lambda$ in terms of measurable quantities. Measuring the distance between distant fringes, instead of a single fringe spacing, reduces the relative error of the measurement. The result, $500\,\mathrm{nm}$, is a blue-green light, in the visible range.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Two slits at distance $d = 0.40\,\mathrm{mm}$ are illuminated simultaneously by two lights of wavelengths $\lambda_1 = 480\,\mathrm{nm}$ and $\lambda_2 = 600\,\mathrm{nm}$, and the screen is at $L = 1.0\,\mathrm{m}$. At what minimum nonzero distance from the center does a bright fringe of one light fall exactly on a bright fringe of the other?

$$
\begin{cases}
d \sin\theta = m_1 \lambda_1 \\[4pt]
d \sin\theta = m_2 \lambda_2
\end{cases}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
m_1 \lambda_1 = m_2 \lambda_2
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\frac{m_1}{m_2} = \frac{\lambda_2}{\lambda_1} = \frac{600}{480} = \frac{5}{4}
$$
$$
m_1 = 5 , \quad m_2 = 4 , \qquad
y = \frac{m_1 \lambda_1 L}{d} = \frac{5 \cdot 4.80 \cdot 10^{-7} \cdot 1.0}{4.0 \cdot 10^{-4}}\,\mathrm{m} = 6.0 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m} = 6.0\,\mathrm{mm}
$$

::grafico{f="cos(pi*x/1.2)^2;cos(pi*x/1.5)^2" segmenti="6,0,6,1.15" nomi="y,I" legenda="\lambda_1 = 480\,\mathrm{nm};\lambda_2 = 600\,\mathrm{nm}" xmin=-0.6 xmax=7.4 ymin=-0.3 ymax=1.4 titolo="Relative intensities of the two interference patterns as functions of $y$, in millimeters: the fringe spacings are $1.2\,\mathrm{mm}$ and $1.5\,\mathrm{mm}$, and the first common maximum is at $6.0\,\mathrm{mm}$"}

<div class="spiegazione">

The two lights have different frequencies, so they do not interfere with each other, and each produces its own interference pattern; on the screen the intensities add. A bright fringe of the first light coincides with one of the second if the same direction $\theta$ satisfies *both* conditions for a maximum, which must therefore be combined in a system. Eliminating $d\sin\theta$ gives $m_1 \lambda_1 = m_2 \lambda_2$, and the ratio of the orders is $\dfrac{5}{4}$, in lowest terms.

The smallest pair of integers with this ratio is $m_1 = 5$, $m_2 = 4$, and it corresponds to the path difference $2400\,\mathrm{nm}$, the least common multiple of $480\,\mathrm{nm}$ and $600\,\mathrm{nm}$. The angle is small, $\sin\theta = 6.0 \cdot 10^{-3}$, so the position on the screen is computed with the small-angle formula: the first **coincidence** is at $6.0\,\mathrm{mm}$ from the center, where the fifth fringe of the blue light and the fourth of the orange light fall.

</div>

## Exercise 4

In a Young experiment with $\lambda = 600\,\mathrm{nm}$, $d = 0.20\,\mathrm{mm}$ and $L = 1.0\,\mathrm{m}$, the intensity at the center of the screen is $I_{\max}$. Compute the intensity at the point at distance $y = 1.0\,\mathrm{mm}$ from the center and the minimum distance from the center at which the intensity is $\dfrac{I_{\max}}{2}$.

$$
I(y) = I_{\max} \cos^2\left( \frac{\pi\, d\, y}{\lambda L} \right)
$$
$$
\frac{\pi\, d\, y}{\lambda L} = \frac{\pi \cdot 2.0 \cdot 10^{-4} \cdot 1.0 \cdot 10^{-3}}{6.00 \cdot 10^{-7} \cdot 1.0} = \frac{\pi}{3}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
I = I_{\max} \cos^2\frac{\pi}{3} = \frac{I_{\max}}{4}
$$
$$
\cos^2\left( \frac{\pi\, d\, y}{\lambda L} \right) = \frac{1}{2}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\frac{\pi\, d\, y}{\lambda L} = \frac{\pi}{4}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
y = \frac{\lambda L}{4 d} = \frac{6.00 \cdot 10^{-7} \cdot 1.0}{8.0 \cdot 10^{-4}}\,\mathrm{m} = 0.75\,\mathrm{mm}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The fringe spacing is $\dfrac{\lambda L}{d} = 3.0\,\mathrm{mm}$, so all the points considered are close to the center and the small-angle formula for the **intensity of the interference pattern** holds. At the point at $1.0\,\mathrm{mm}$ the argument of the cosine is $\dfrac{\pi}{3}$, that is, the phase difference between the two waves is $\dfrac{2\pi}{3}$, and the intensity is a quarter of the maximum.

For the second question we look for the smallest $y > 0$ with $\cos^2 = \dfrac{1}{2}$. As $y$ increases from $0$ the argument increases from $0$, and the squared cosine equals $\dfrac{1}{2}$ for the first time when the argument is $\dfrac{\pi}{4}$; hence $y = \dfrac{\lambda L}{4d} = 0.75\,\mathrm{mm}$, that is, a quarter of a fringe spacing, halfway between the center of the bright fringe and the first dark fringe, which is at $1.5\,\mathrm{mm}$.

</div>
