## Exercise 1

A beam of unpolarized light, of intensity $I_0 = 80\,\mathrm{W/m^2}$, passes through a polarizer and then through a second polarizer, called the analyzer, whose transmission axis forms an angle of $30^\circ$ with that of the first. Compute the intensity of the light after each polarizer.

$$
I_1 = \frac{I_0}{2} , \qquad I_2 = I_1 \cos^2\theta
$$
$$
I_1 = \frac{80}{2}\,\mathrm{W/m^2} = 40\,\mathrm{W/m^2} , \qquad
I_2 = 40 \cdot \cos^2 30^\circ\,\mathrm{W/m^2} = 40 \cdot \frac{3}{4}\,\mathrm{W/m^2} = 30\,\mathrm{W/m^2}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The incident light is unpolarized, so the first polarizer transmits half of it, whatever the orientation of its axis, and the light exits polarized along the axis of the first polarizer. Linearly polarized light therefore reaches the analyzer, with its direction forming $30^\circ$ with the transmission axis, and **Malus's law** applies with $\cos^2 30^\circ = \dfrac{3}{4}$. After the two polarizers $37.5\,\%$ of the initial intensity remains.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Two polarizers are crossed, that is, with perpendicular transmission axes, and unpolarized light of intensity $I_0 = 80\,\mathrm{W/m^2}$ is incident on them. A third polarizer is inserted between the two, with its axis forming the angle $\theta$ with that of the first. Express the transmitted intensity as a function of $\theta$, compute it for $\theta = 30^\circ$ and find the angle for which it is maximum.

$$
I = \frac{I_0}{2} \cos^2\theta \cos^2(90^\circ - \theta) = \frac{I_0}{2} \cos^2\theta \sin^2\theta = \frac{I_0}{8} \sin^2(2\theta)
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
I(30^\circ) &= \frac{80}{8} \cdot \sin^2 60^\circ\,\mathrm{W/m^2} = 10 \cdot \frac{3}{4}\,\mathrm{W/m^2} = 7.5\,\mathrm{W/m^2} \\[6pt]
I_{\max} &= \frac{I_0}{8} = 10\,\mathrm{W/m^2} \quad \text{for} \quad \theta = 45^\circ
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Without the middle polarizer no light passes, because the light leaving the first is polarized perpendicular to the axis of the last and **Malus's law** with $\cos 90^\circ = 0$ gives zero intensity. With the middle polarizer, Malus's law is applied twice: the light leaving the first forms the angle $\theta$ with the axis of the second, and the light leaving the second forms the angle $90^\circ - \theta$ with the axis of the third. The product $\cos\theta\sin\theta$ is equal to $\dfrac{1}{2}\sin 2\theta$, by the double-angle formula for the sine, and the final form follows.

The function $\sin^2(2\theta)$ is at most $1$, for $2\theta = 90^\circ$, so the intensity is maximum with the middle polarizer at $45^\circ$ and equals $\dfrac{I_0}{8}$. The added polarizer absorbs part of the light and yet **increases** the transmitted intensity, because it rotates the direction of polarization halfway between the two crossed axes.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Sunlight is reflected by the surface of a lake ($n = 1.33$). Compute the angle of incidence for which the reflected light is totally polarized and the corresponding angle of refraction. Then compute Brewster's angle for light that, propagating in the water, is reflected by the surface of an immersed glass plate, with $n_v = 1.50$.

$$
\tan\theta_B = \frac{n_2}{n_1} , \qquad \theta_2 = 90^\circ - \theta_B
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{air-water:} \quad \theta_B &= \arctan 1.33 \approx 53.1^\circ , \qquad \theta_2 \approx 36.9^\circ \\[6pt]
\text{water-glass:} \quad \theta_B &= \arctan\frac{1.50}{1.33} = \arctan 1.128 \approx 48.4^\circ
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Sunlight is unpolarized, and the reflected light is totally polarized when the angle of incidence is **Brewster's angle**, with $n_1 = 1.00$ for air and $n_2 = 1.33$. In that condition the reflected ray and the refracted ray are perpendicular, so the angle of refraction is the complement of $\theta_B$. As a check, Snell's law gives $\sin\theta_2 = \dfrac{\sin 53.1^\circ}{1.33} \approx 0.601$, that is, $\theta_2 \approx 36.9^\circ$, the same value.

For the reflection on the immersed glass the first medium is water, hence the ratio $\dfrac{n_v}{n_{\text{water}}}$ appears in the formula. The ratio is closer to $1$ than in the case of glass in air, and Brewster's angle is smaller than the $56.3^\circ$ that one would have in air.

</div>

## Exercise 4

Unpolarized light of intensity $I_0 = 80\,\mathrm{W/m^2}$ passes through a first polarizer and then through $N$ further polarizers in succession; each of these has its axis rotated by $\dfrac{90^\circ}{N}$ with respect to the previous one, so that the last is perpendicular to the first. Express the transmitted intensity as a function of $N$, compute it for $N = 3$ and for $N = 6$, and determine its limit as $N \to \infty$.

$$
I_N = \frac{I_0}{2} \left( \cos^2\frac{90^\circ}{N} \right)^{N} = \frac{I_0}{2} \cos^{2N}\left( \frac{\pi}{2N} \right)
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
I_3 &= 40 \cdot \cos^6 30^\circ\,\mathrm{W/m^2} = 40 \cdot \frac{27}{64}\,\mathrm{W/m^2} \approx 16.9\,\mathrm{W/m^2} \\[6pt]
I_6 &= 40 \cdot \cos^{12} 15^\circ\,\mathrm{W/m^2} \approx 40 \cdot 0.660\,\mathrm{W/m^2} \approx 26.4\,\mathrm{W/m^2}
\end{aligned}
$$
$$
\ln\frac{2 I_N}{I_0} = 2N \ln\cos\frac{\pi}{2N} \sim 2N \cdot \left( -\frac{\pi^2}{8N^2} \right) = -\frac{\pi^2}{4N} \to 0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\lim_{N \to \infty} I_N = \frac{I_0}{2} = 40\,\mathrm{W/m^2}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The first polarizer transmits half of the unpolarized light. Each of the $N$ subsequent polarizers receives light polarized along the axis of the previous one, which forms the angle $\dfrac{90^\circ}{N}$ with its own axis, so by **Malus's law** it multiplies the intensity by $\cos^2\dfrac{90^\circ}{N}$. For $N = 3$ the steps are $30^\circ$ and about $42\,\%$ of the light leaving the first polarizer passes; for $N = 6$ the steps are $15^\circ$ and about $66\,\%$ passes. For $N = 2$ the formula gives $\dfrac{I_0}{8}$, the intensity of three polarizers with the middle one at $45^\circ$.

For the limit we take the logarithm, which turns the power into a product. As $N \to \infty$ the angle $\dfrac{\pi}{2N}$ tends to zero, and from the expansions $\cos x = 1 - \dfrac{x^2}{2} + o(x^2)$ and $\ln(1 + u) = u + o(u)$ it follows that $\ln\cos x \sim -\dfrac{x^2}{2}$. The logarithm of the relative intensity therefore tends to zero, and the intensity tends to $\dfrac{I_0}{2}$: with many small steps the direction of polarization **rotates** by $90^\circ$ with almost no losses beyond that of the first polarizer.

</div>
