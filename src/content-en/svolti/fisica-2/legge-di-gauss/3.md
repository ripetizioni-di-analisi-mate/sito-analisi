## Exercise 1

An insulating sphere of radius $R = 5.0\,\mathrm{cm}$ has the charge $Q = 8.0\,\mathrm{nC}$ distributed uniformly throughout its volume. Compute the magnitude of the electric field at distances $r_1 = 2.0\,\mathrm{cm}$ and $r_2 = 10\,\mathrm{cm}$ from the center and the maximum value of the field.

$$
E(r) \cdot 4\pi r^2 = \frac{q_{\text{int}}(r)}{\varepsilon_0} ,
\qquad
q_{\text{int}}(r) =
\begin{cases}
Q\,\dfrac{r^3}{R^3} & \text{if } r \leq R \\[12pt]
Q & \text{if } r \geq R
\end{cases}
$$
$$
E(r_1) = \frac{k Q\, r_1}{R^3} ,
\qquad
E(r_2) = \frac{k Q}{r_2^2} ,
\qquad
E_{\max} = E(R) = \frac{k Q}{R^2}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
E(r_1) &= \frac{8.99 \cdot 10^9 \cdot 8.0 \cdot 10^{-9} \cdot 0.020}{0.050^3}\,\mathrm{N/C} \approx 1.2 \cdot 10^4\,\mathrm{N/C} \\[6pt]
E(r_2) &= \frac{8.99 \cdot 10^9 \cdot 8.0 \cdot 10^{-9}}{0.10^2}\,\mathrm{N/C} \approx 7.2 \cdot 10^3\,\mathrm{N/C} \\[6pt]
E_{\max} &= \frac{8.99 \cdot 10^9 \cdot 8.0 \cdot 10^{-9}}{0.050^2}\,\mathrm{N/C} \approx 2.9 \cdot 10^4\,\mathrm{N/C}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The distribution has spherical symmetry, so the field is radial and its magnitude depends only on $r$; as the **Gaussian surface** one chooses the sphere of radius $r$ concentric with the charge, through which the flux is $E(r) \cdot 4\pi r^2$. The point at $2.0\,\mathrm{cm}$ is inside the charged sphere, so the Gaussian sphere encloses the fraction $\left( \dfrac{2.0}{5.0} \right)^3$ of the volume; since the density is uniform, it encloses the same fraction of the charge. The point at $10\,\mathrm{cm}$ is outside, the Gaussian sphere encloses the whole charge and the field is that of a point charge $Q$ at the center.

Inside, the field grows in proportion to $r$ and outside it decreases as $\dfrac{1}{r^2}$, so the **maximum** occurs at the surface of the sphere. As a check, the field at $2.0\,\mathrm{cm}$ is $\dfrac{2.0}{5.0} = 0.40$ times the value at the surface, and $0.40 \cdot 2.9 \cdot 10^4\,\mathrm{N/C} \approx 1.2 \cdot 10^4\,\mathrm{N/C}$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A thin spherical shell of radius $R = 0.20\,\mathrm{m}$ carries the charge $Q = -6.0\,\mathrm{nC}$ distributed uniformly, and at its center there is a point charge $q = +2.0\,\mathrm{nC}$. Compute the electric field at distances $r_1 = 0.10\,\mathrm{m}$ and $r_2 = 0.40\,\mathrm{m}$ from the center.

$$
E(r) \cdot 4\pi r^2 = \frac{q_{\text{int}}(r)}{\varepsilon_0} ,
\qquad
q_{\text{int}}(r) =
\begin{cases}
q & \text{if } r < R \\[4pt]
q + Q & \text{if } r > R
\end{cases}
$$
$$
E(r_1) = \frac{k\,q}{r_1^2} ,
\qquad
E(r_2) = \frac{k\,(q + Q)}{r_2^2}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
E(r_1) &= \frac{8.99 \cdot 10^9 \cdot 2.0 \cdot 10^{-9}}{0.10^2}\,\mathrm{N/C} \approx 1.8 \cdot 10^3\,\mathrm{N/C} \\[6pt]
E(r_2) &= \frac{8.99 \cdot 10^9 \cdot (-4.0 \cdot 10^{-9})}{0.40^2}\,\mathrm{N/C} \approx -2.2 \cdot 10^2\,\mathrm{N/C}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The central charge and the shell have the same center, so the overall distribution has spherical symmetry and the field is $\vec{E} = E(r)\,\hat{r}$, where $E(r)$ is the component along the outward radial unit vector. By **Gauss's law** applied to the sphere of radius $r$, the component $E(r)$ depends only on the enclosed charge.

At $0.10\,\mathrm{m}$ the Gaussian sphere is inside the shell and encloses only $q$; the shell, by the theorem on the field of a spherical shell, produces no field inside it. The field is therefore that of the central charge, directed outward. At $0.40\,\mathrm{m}$ the Gaussian sphere also encloses the shell and the enclosed charge is $-4.0\,\mathrm{nC}$; the radial component is negative, therefore the field, of magnitude about $2.2 \cdot 10^2\,\mathrm{N/C}$, is **directed toward the center**.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A hollow insulating sphere has inner radius $a = 2.0\,\mathrm{cm}$ and outer radius $b = 4.0\,\mathrm{cm}$, and the region between the two surfaces is charged with uniform density $\rho = 2.0 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\mathrm{C/m^3}$. Compute the total charge and the magnitude of the electric field at distances $1.0\,\mathrm{cm}$, $3.0\,\mathrm{cm}$ and $6.0\,\mathrm{cm}$ from the center.

::grafico{f="x<0 ? NaN : x<2 ? 0 : (x<4 ? 0.7533*(x^3-8)/x^2 : 42.1846/x^2)" xmin=-0.4 xmax=9 ymin=-0.3 ymax=3.2 nomi="r,E" segmenti="2,0,2,3;4,0,4,2.637" punti="3,1.590,pieno;6,1.172,pieno" legenda="E(r)" titolo="Magnitude of the field of the thick hollow sphere, with $r$ in centimeters and $E$ in $10^3\,\mathrm{N/C}$; the field is zero in the cavity and maximum at the outer surface"}

$$
q_{\text{int}}(r) =
\begin{cases}
0 & \text{if } r \leq a \\[6pt]
\dfrac{4}{3}\pi\rho\,(r^3 - a^3) & \text{if } a \leq r \leq b \\[12pt]
\dfrac{4}{3}\pi\rho\,(b^3 - a^3) = Q & \text{if } r \geq b
\end{cases}
\qquad
E(r) = \frac{q_{\text{int}}(r)}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2}
$$
$$
E(r) = \frac{\rho\,(r^3 - a^3)}{3\varepsilon_0 r^2} \quad (a \leq r \leq b) ,
\qquad
E(r) = \frac{\rho\,(b^3 - a^3)}{3\varepsilon_0 r^2} \quad (r \geq b)
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
Q &= \frac{4}{3}\pi \cdot 2.0 \cdot 10^{-6} \cdot (0.040^3 - 0.020^3)\,\mathrm{C} \approx 4.7 \cdot 10^{-10}\,\mathrm{C} \\[6pt]
E(1.0\,\mathrm{cm}) &= 0 \\[6pt]
E(3.0\,\mathrm{cm}) &= \frac{2.0 \cdot 10^{-6} \cdot (0.030^3 - 0.020^3)}{3 \cdot 8.85 \cdot 10^{-12} \cdot 0.030^2}\,\mathrm{N/C} \approx 1.6 \cdot 10^3\,\mathrm{N/C} \\[6pt]
E(6.0\,\mathrm{cm}) &= \frac{2.0 \cdot 10^{-6} \cdot (0.040^3 - 0.020^3)}{3 \cdot 8.85 \cdot 10^{-12} \cdot 0.060^2}\,\mathrm{N/C} \approx 1.2 \cdot 10^3\,\mathrm{N/C}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The density depends only on the distance from the center, so the distribution has spherical symmetry and on every concentric Gaussian sphere the flux is $E(r) \cdot 4\pi r^2$. The enclosed charge depends on the region in which the Gaussian sphere lies. In the cavity the Gaussian sphere contains no charge, therefore the field is **zero**. In the charged layer the Gaussian sphere contains the material between the radii $a$ and $r$, whose volume is the difference of the volumes of two spheres, $\dfrac{4}{3}\pi\,(r^3 - a^3)$. Outside, it contains the whole charge $Q$.

Setting the flux equal to $\dfrac{q_{\text{int}}}{\varepsilon_0}$ gives the expressions of $E(r)$ in the three regions. Outside, the field is that of a point charge $Q$ at the center; as a check, $\dfrac{k Q}{r^2} = \dfrac{8.99 \cdot 10^9 \cdot 4.7 \cdot 10^{-10}}{0.060^2}\,\mathrm{N/C} \approx 1.2 \cdot 10^3\,\mathrm{N/C}$. The field is continuous at $r = a$ and at $r = b$, because the charge is distributed in a volume and not on a surface.

</div>
