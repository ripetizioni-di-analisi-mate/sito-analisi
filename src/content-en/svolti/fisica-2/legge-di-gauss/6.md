## Exercise 1

Two very large parallel planes, at distance $d$ from each other, are charged uniformly with surface densities $\sigma_1 = +6.0\,\mathrm{nC/m^2}$ and $\sigma_2 = -2.0\,\mathrm{nC/m^2}$. Compute the electric field in region I to the left of the first plane, in region II between the planes and in region III to the right of the second plane, far from the edges.

::grafico{unicolore=1 assi=0 uguale=1 linee="0,-1.6 0,1.6;3,-1.6 3,1.6" vettori="-0.3,0,-1.3,0;0.5,0,2.5,0,E;3.3,0,4.3,0" etichette="-0.3,1.85,σ₁;2.75,1.85,σ₂;-1.5,-0.9,I;1.35,-0.9,II;4,-0.9,III" xmin=-2.2 xmax=5.2 ymin=-1.4 ymax=2.2 titolo="In the three regions the field is $226\,\mathrm{N/C}$ to the left in I, $452\,\mathrm{N/C}$ to the right in II and $226\,\mathrm{N/C}$ to the right in III"}

$$
E_1 = \frac{|\sigma_1|}{2\varepsilon_0} ,
\qquad
E_2 = \frac{|\sigma_2|}{2\varepsilon_0}
$$
$$
E_{x,\text{I}} = -\frac{\sigma_1 + \sigma_2}{2\varepsilon_0} ,
\qquad
E_{x,\text{II}} = \frac{\sigma_1 - \sigma_2}{2\varepsilon_0} ,
\qquad
E_{x,\text{III}} = \frac{\sigma_1 + \sigma_2}{2\varepsilon_0}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
E_1 &= \frac{6.0 \cdot 10^{-9}}{2 \cdot 8.85 \cdot 10^{-12}}\,\mathrm{N/C} \approx 339\,\mathrm{N/C} \\[6pt]
E_2 &= \frac{2.0 \cdot 10^{-9}}{2 \cdot 8.85 \cdot 10^{-12}}\,\mathrm{N/C} \approx 113\,\mathrm{N/C} \\[6pt]
E_{x,\text{I}} &= -(339 - 113)\,\mathrm{N/C} = -226\,\mathrm{N/C} \\[6pt]
E_{x,\text{II}} &= (339 + 113)\,\mathrm{N/C} = 452\,\mathrm{N/C} \\[6pt]
E_{x,\text{III}} &= (339 - 113)\,\mathrm{N/C} = 226\,\mathrm{N/C}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Far from the edges each plane can be treated as infinite, and by the **field of an infinite charged plane** it produces a uniform field of magnitude $\dfrac{|\sigma|}{2\varepsilon_0}$ in each half-space, pointing away from the plane if the charge is positive and toward it if it is negative. Choose the $x$ axis perpendicular to the planes, oriented from the first toward the second, and by the **superposition principle** add the $x$ components of the two fields.

In region I the field of the positive plane points to the left and that of the negative plane points to the right, toward the negative plane; the sum is $226\,\mathrm{N/C}$ to the left. In region II both fields point to the right, from the positive plane to the negative plane, and the magnitudes add. In region III the field of the positive plane points to the right and that of the negative plane to the left, and the sum is $226\,\mathrm{N/C}$ to the right. The result does not depend on the distance $d$ between the planes. As a check, the jump of the $x$ component across the first plane is $452 - (-226) = 678\,\mathrm{N/C}$, equal to $\dfrac{\sigma_1}{\varepsilon_0}$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A small insulating ball of mass $m = 1.0\,\mathrm{g}$ and charge $q = +20\,\mathrm{nC}$ hangs from a light insulating thread, near a large vertical insulating sheet charged uniformly with density $\sigma = +2.0\,\mathrm{\mu C/m^2}$. Compute the magnitude of the field produced by the sheet, the electric force on the ball and the angle that the thread makes with the vertical at equilibrium.

$$
E = \frac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0} ,
\qquad
F = q E
$$
$$
\begin{cases}
T \sin\theta = q E \\[4pt]
T \cos\theta = m g
\end{cases}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\tan\theta = \frac{q E}{m g} = \frac{q\,\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0\, m g}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
E &= \frac{2.0 \cdot 10^{-6}}{2 \cdot 8.85 \cdot 10^{-12}}\,\mathrm{N/C} \approx 1.1 \cdot 10^5\,\mathrm{N/C} \\[6pt]
F &= 20 \cdot 10^{-9} \cdot 1.13 \cdot 10^5\,\mathrm{N} \approx 2.3 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{N} \\[6pt]
\tan\theta &= \frac{2.26 \cdot 10^{-3}}{1.0 \cdot 10^{-3} \cdot 9.81} \approx 0.23
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\theta \approx 13^\circ
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The ball is close to the sheet compared with the size of the sheet, so the sheet can be treated as an infinite plane, and the field is perpendicular to the sheet, that is, horizontal, and uniform. The sheet is insulating, therefore its charge remains distributed uniformly even in the presence of the ball. The ball is small and is treated as a point charge; since $q$ and $\sigma$ are positive, the electric force is horizontal and pushes the ball away from the sheet.

The forces acting on the ball are the weight, vertical, the electric force, horizontal, and the tension of the thread. At equilibrium the resultant is zero, so the horizontal component of the tension balances the electric force and the vertical component balances the weight; the two conditions must hold together. Dividing one equation by the other eliminates the tension and gives the tangent of the angle. The angle does not depend on the distance of the ball from the sheet, because the field of the infinite plane is uniform.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A very large insulating slab of thickness $d = 2.0\,\mathrm{cm}$ is charged uniformly with density $\rho = 4.0 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\mathrm{C/m^3}$. Compute the magnitude of the field outside the slab, far from the edges, and the distance from the midplane of the slab at which the field is half of the outside value.

$$
E_{\text{ext}} = \frac{\rho\, d}{2\varepsilon_0} ,
\qquad
E(x) = \frac{\rho\, x}{\varepsilon_0} \quad \left( 0 \leq x \leq \frac{d}{2} \right)
$$
$$
\frac{\rho\, x}{\varepsilon_0} = \frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{\rho\, d}{2\varepsilon_0}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
x = \frac{d}{4}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
E_{\text{ext}} &= \frac{4.0 \cdot 10^{-6} \cdot 0.020}{2 \cdot 8.85 \cdot 10^{-12}}\,\mathrm{N/C} \approx 4.5 \cdot 10^3\,\mathrm{N/C} \\[6pt]
x &= \frac{2.0}{4}\,\mathrm{cm} = 0.50\,\mathrm{cm}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Far from the edges the slab is treated as infinite, and the theorem on the **field of a uniformly charged slab** applies, which is obtained from Gauss's law with a Gaussian cylinder symmetric with respect to the midplane. Outside, the field is uniform and equal to that of a plane with surface density $\rho d = 8.0 \cdot 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{C/m^2}$, the charge contained in one square meter of slab.

Inside, the field grows linearly from the midplane, where it is zero, to the surface, where it reaches the outside value. The field is therefore half of the outside value halfway between the midplane and the surface, that is, at $\dfrac{d}{4}$ from the midplane, on both sides. As a check, $\dfrac{\rho}{\varepsilon_0} \cdot \dfrac{d}{4} = \dfrac{4.0 \cdot 10^{-6} \cdot 0.0050}{8.85 \cdot 10^{-12}}\,\mathrm{N/C} \approx 2.3 \cdot 10^3\,\mathrm{N/C}$, half of $4.5 \cdot 10^3\,\mathrm{N/C}$.

</div>

## Exercise 4

A very large plane is charged uniformly with density $\sigma = +8.85\,\mathrm{nC/m^2}$, and a point charge $q = -2.0\,\mathrm{nC}$ is at distance $d = 0.30\,\mathrm{m}$ from the plane. Determine the points where the total electric field is zero.

::grafico{assi=0 uguale=1 linee="0,-1.6 0,1.6" punti="3,0,pieno,q;4.9,0,vuoto,P" vettori="4.9,0.45,5.9,0.45,E₁;4.9,-0.45,3.9,-0.45,E₂" segmenti="0,-1.1,3,-1.1;3,-1.1,4.9,-1.1" etichette="1.3,-1.45,d;3.75,-1.45,s;-0.35,1.8,σ" xmin=-0.8 xmax=6.4 ymin=-1.8 ymax=2.1 titolo="At the point $P$, beyond the charge with respect to the plane, the field of the plane and that of the charge have opposite directions and equal magnitudes"}

$$
E_1 = \frac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0} ,
\qquad
E_2 = \frac{k\,|q|}{s^2} ,
\qquad
E_1 = E_2
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
s = \sqrt{\frac{2\varepsilon_0\, k\,|q|}{\sigma}} = \sqrt{\frac{|q|}{2\pi\sigma}}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
E_1 &= \frac{8.85 \cdot 10^{-9}}{2 \cdot 8.85 \cdot 10^{-12}}\,\mathrm{N/C} = 500\,\mathrm{N/C} \\[6pt]
s &= \sqrt{\frac{2.0 \cdot 10^{-9}}{2\pi \cdot 8.85 \cdot 10^{-9}}}\,\mathrm{m} \approx 0.19\,\mathrm{m} \\[6pt]
d + s &\approx 0.49\,\mathrm{m}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The field of the plane, $\vec{E}_1$, is perpendicular to the plane, pointing away from it and of constant magnitude; the field of the negative charge, $\vec{E}_2$, is directed toward the charge. Off the line perpendicular to the plane through the charge, $\vec{E}_2$ has a component parallel to the plane that $\vec{E}_1$ cannot cancel, so the points of zero field lie on that line. On the line three regions are distinguished.

- Between the plane and the charge, $\vec{E}_1$ and $\vec{E}_2$ are both directed from the plane toward the charge, and the sum is nonzero.
- On the side of the plane opposite to the charge, $\vec{E}_1$ points away from the plane and $\vec{E}_2$ points toward the charge, so they have opposite directions; however, the distance from the charge exceeds $d$, and $E_2 < \dfrac{k\,|q|}{d^2} \approx 200\,\mathrm{N/C} < 500\,\mathrm{N/C}$, therefore the magnitudes are never equal.
- Beyond the charge, $\vec{E}_1$ points away from the plane and $\vec{E}_2$ points back toward the charge; the directions are opposite, and the magnitudes are equal at the distance $s$ from the charge.

The total field is therefore zero at **only one point** $P$, on the perpendicular to the plane through the charge, at $0.19\,\mathrm{m}$ from the charge and at $0.49\,\mathrm{m}$ from the plane. As a check, $\dfrac{8.99 \cdot 10^9 \cdot 2.0 \cdot 10^{-9}}{0.19^2}\,\mathrm{N/C} \approx 498\,\mathrm{N/C}$, equal to $500\,\mathrm{N/C}$ within rounding.

</div>
