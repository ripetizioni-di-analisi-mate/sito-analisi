## Exercise 1

A very long straight wire is charged uniformly with linear density $\lambda = +4.0\,\mathrm{nC/m}$. Compute the magnitude of the electric field at distance $r = 5.0\,\mathrm{cm}$ from the wire, far from its ends, and the force acting on an electron placed at that point.

$$
E(r) \cdot 2\pi r h = \frac{\lambda h}{\varepsilon_0}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
E = \frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0 r} = \frac{2k\lambda}{r} ,
\qquad
F = e\,E
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
E &= \frac{2 \cdot 8.99 \cdot 10^9 \cdot 4.0 \cdot 10^{-9}}{0.050}\,\mathrm{N/C} \approx 1.4 \cdot 10^3\,\mathrm{N/C} \\[6pt]
F &= 1.60 \cdot 10^{-19} \cdot 1.44 \cdot 10^3\,\mathrm{N} \approx 2.3 \cdot 10^{-16}\,\mathrm{N}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The point is far from the ends compared with its distance from the wire, so the wire can be treated as infinite. The field is radial and its magnitude depends only on $r$; by **Gauss's law** applied to a coaxial cylinder of radius $r$ and height $h$, the flux through the bases is zero and the flux through the lateral surface equals the enclosed charge $\lambda h$ divided by $\varepsilon_0$. The field points away from the wire, because $\lambda > 0$.

The electron has charge $-e$, so the force is opposite to the field, that is, **directed toward the wire**, and has magnitude $eE$. As a dimensional check, $\dfrac{k\lambda}{r}$ is measured in $\mathrm{N \cdot m^2/C^2} \cdot \mathrm{C/m} \cdot \mathrm{m^{-1}} = \mathrm{N/C}$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A very long insulating cylinder, of radius $R = 2.0\,\mathrm{cm}$, is charged uniformly with density $\rho = 5.0 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\mathrm{C/m^3}$. Compute the charge per unit length and the magnitude of the electric field at distances $r_1 = 1.0\,\mathrm{cm}$ and $r_2 = 4.0\,\mathrm{cm}$ from the axis.

$$
\lambda = \rho\,\pi R^2 ,
\qquad
E(r_1) = \frac{\rho\, r_1}{2\varepsilon_0} \quad (r_1 \leq R) ,
\qquad
E(r_2) = \frac{\rho R^2}{2\varepsilon_0 r_2} \quad (r_2 \geq R)
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
\lambda &= 5.0 \cdot 10^{-6} \cdot \pi \cdot 0.020^2\,\mathrm{C/m} \approx 6.3 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\mathrm{C/m} \\[6pt]
E(r_1) &= \frac{5.0 \cdot 10^{-6} \cdot 0.010}{2 \cdot 8.85 \cdot 10^{-12}}\,\mathrm{N/C} \approx 2.8 \cdot 10^3\,\mathrm{N/C} \\[6pt]
E(r_2) &= \frac{5.0 \cdot 10^{-6} \cdot 0.020^2}{2 \cdot 8.85 \cdot 10^{-12} \cdot 0.040}\,\mathrm{N/C} \approx 2.8 \cdot 10^3\,\mathrm{N/C}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The distribution has cylindrical symmetry, so Gauss's law is applied to a coaxial cylinder of radius $r$ and height $h$, through which the flux is $E(r) \cdot 2\pi r h$. At $1.0\,\mathrm{cm}$ from the axis the Gaussian cylinder is inside the material and encloses the charge $\rho\,\pi r_1^2 h$; at $4.0\,\mathrm{cm}$ it encloses the whole charged cross-section, with charge $\lambda h$, and the field is that of a wire on the axis. The two expressions of the **field of a uniformly charged cylinder** thus apply.

The two values coincide. Indeed the two expressions are equal when $r_1 = \dfrac{R^2}{r_2}$, and with the data $\dfrac{R^2}{r_2} = \dfrac{2.0^2}{4.0}\,\mathrm{cm} = 1.0\,\mathrm{cm}$. The maximum value of the field, reached at the surface of the cylinder, is twice as large, about $5.6 \cdot 10^3\,\mathrm{N/C}$.

</div>

Inside the inner surface the Gaussian cylinder encloses no charge, so the field is zero. Between the two surfaces it encloses only the inner surface, and the field is that of a wire with density $\lambda_1 > 0$, pointing away from the axis. Outside both it encloses the total density $-2.0\,\mathrm{nC/m}$, so the radial component is negative and the field is **directed toward the axis**, with magnitude about $7.2 \cdot 10^2\,\mathrm{N/C}$. If the two densities were opposite the outside field would be zero, as in the coaxial cable.

## Exercise 4

An electron travels on a circular orbit around a very long straight wire, charged uniformly with linear density $\lambda = +4.0\,\mathrm{nC/m}$, in a plane perpendicular to the wire. Compute the speed of the electron and show that it does not depend on the radius of the orbit. Then compute the period for an orbit of radius $r = 1.0\,\mathrm{cm}$.

$$
e\,E(r) = m_e\,\frac{v^2}{r} ,
\qquad
E(r) = \frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0 r}
$$
$$
\frac{e\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0 r} = \frac{m_e v^2}{r}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
v = \sqrt{\frac{e\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0 m_e}} = \sqrt{\frac{2 k e \lambda}{m_e}}
$$
$$
T = \frac{2\pi r}{v}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
v &= \sqrt{\frac{2 \cdot 8.99 \cdot 10^9 \cdot 1.60 \cdot 10^{-19} \cdot 4.0 \cdot 10^{-9}}{9.11 \cdot 10^{-31}}}\,\mathrm{m/s} \approx 3.6 \cdot 10^6\,\mathrm{m/s} \\[6pt]
T &= \frac{2\pi \cdot 0.010}{3.55 \cdot 10^6}\,\mathrm{s} \approx 1.8 \cdot 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{s}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The wire is positive and the electron is negative, so the electric force is attractive, directed toward the wire and perpendicular to the velocity at every point of the orbit; the weight of the electron is negligible compared with the electric force. The magnitude of the force is constant on the circle, because it depends only on the distance from the wire. By Newton's second law, in uniform circular motion the force equals the mass times the centripetal acceleration $\dfrac{v^2}{r}$; see the topic on motion in the plane in Physics 1.

The field of the wire is proportional to $\dfrac{1}{r}$, as is the centripetal acceleration at fixed speed, so the radius cancels and the speed **does not depend on the radius** of the orbit; the period, instead, is proportional to the radius. The speed is about $1.2\,\%$ of the speed of light, so the nonrelativistic treatment is adequate.

</div>
