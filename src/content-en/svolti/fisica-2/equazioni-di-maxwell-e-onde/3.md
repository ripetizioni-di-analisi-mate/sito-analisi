## Exercise 1

A laser pointer emits a beam of power $P = 5.0\,\mathrm{mW}$ with a circular cross section of diameter $D = 2.0\,\mathrm{mm}$. Assuming that the power is distributed uniformly over the cross section and that the beam is a harmonic plane wave in vacuum, compute the intensity of the beam and the amplitudes of the electric field and of the magnetic field.

$$
I = \frac{P}{\pi \left( \dfrac{D}{2} \right)^2} ,
\qquad
I = \frac{1}{2}\, c\, \varepsilon_0 E_0^2
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
E_0 = \sqrt{\frac{2I}{c\, \varepsilon_0}} ,
\qquad
B_0 = \frac{E_0}{c}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
I &= \frac{5.0 \cdot 10^{-3}}{\pi \cdot \left( 1.0 \cdot 10^{-3} \right)^2}\,\mathrm{W/m^2} \approx 1.6 \cdot 10^{3}\,\mathrm{W/m^2} \\[6pt]
E_0 &= \sqrt{\frac{2 \cdot 1.59 \cdot 10^{3}}{3.00 \cdot 10^{8} \cdot 8.85 \cdot 10^{-12}}}\,\mathrm{V/m} \approx 1.1 \cdot 10^{3}\,\mathrm{V/m} \\[6pt]
B_0 &= \frac{1.09 \cdot 10^{3}}{3.00 \cdot 10^{8}}\,\mathrm{T} \approx 3.6 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\mathrm{T}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The **intensity** is the average power per unit area, and the power is distributed uniformly over a disk of radius $1.0\,\mathrm{mm}$, so it suffices to divide the power by the area of the cross section. For a harmonic wave the intensity is related to the amplitude of the electric field by $I = \dfrac{1}{2} c\, \varepsilon_0 E_0^2$, and solving for $E_0$ gives about $1.1 \cdot 10^{3}\,\mathrm{V/m}$; the amplitude of the magnetic field follows from $E_0 = cB_0$. The beam of a pointer of a few milliwatts has an intensity comparable to that of sunlight, because the power is concentrated on a very small cross section.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A lamp, which can be treated as an isotropic point source, emits electromagnetic waves with average power $P = 100\,\mathrm{W}$. Assuming that air does not absorb energy, compute at distance $r = 2.0\,\mathrm{m}$ from the lamp the intensity, the amplitude and the rms value of the electric field, and the distance at which the intensity is halved.

$$
I(r) = \frac{P}{4\pi r^2} ,
\qquad
E_0 = \sqrt{\frac{2I}{c\, \varepsilon_0}} ,
\qquad
E_{\text{rms}} = \frac{E_0}{\sqrt{2}}
$$
$$
\frac{I(r')}{I(r)} = \frac{r^2}{r'^2} = \frac{1}{2}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
r' = \sqrt{2}\, r
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
I &= \frac{100}{4\pi \cdot 2.0^2}\,\mathrm{W/m^2} \approx 1.99\,\mathrm{W/m^2} \\[6pt]
E_0 &= \sqrt{\frac{2 \cdot 1.99}{3.00 \cdot 10^{8} \cdot 8.85 \cdot 10^{-12}}}\,\mathrm{V/m} \approx 38.7\,\mathrm{V/m} \\[6pt]
E_{\text{rms}} &= \frac{38.7}{\sqrt{2}}\,\mathrm{V/m} \approx 27.4\,\mathrm{V/m} \\[6pt]
r' &= \sqrt{2} \cdot 2.0\,\mathrm{m} \approx 2.8\,\mathrm{m}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The source is isotropic and the medium does not absorb energy, so the formula for the **intensity of an isotropic source** applies: the power is distributed uniformly over the sphere of radius $r$, of area $4\pi r^2$. From the intensity the amplitude is obtained as for harmonic waves, and the rms value is the amplitude divided by $\sqrt{2}$, so that $I = c\, \varepsilon_0 E_{\text{rms}}^2$. The intensity is proportional to $\dfrac{1}{r^2}$, hence it is halved when $r^2$ doubles, that is, at the distance $\sqrt{2}\, r \approx 2.8\,\mathrm{m}$; at that point the amplitude of the field has decreased only by the factor $\sqrt{2}$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

The intensity of solar radiation, measured outside the Earth's atmosphere at the distance $d_T = 1.50 \cdot 10^{11}\,\mathrm{m}$ from the Sun, is $I_T = 1.36\,\mathrm{kW/m^2}$. Treating the Sun as an isotropic source and neglecting absorption in interplanetary space, compute the total power emitted by the Sun, the amplitudes of the fields of a harmonic wave with the same intensity, and the intensity of solar radiation at the orbit of Mars, at the distance $d_M = 2.28 \cdot 10^{11}\,\mathrm{m}$ from the Sun.

$$
P = 4\pi d_T^2\, I_T ,
\qquad
E_0 = \sqrt{\frac{2 I_T}{c\, \varepsilon_0}} ,
\qquad
B_0 = \frac{E_0}{c} ,
\qquad
I_M = I_T \left( \frac{d_T}{d_M} \right)^2
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
P &= 4\pi \cdot \left( 1.50 \cdot 10^{11} \right)^2 \cdot 1.36 \cdot 10^{3}\,\mathrm{W} \approx 3.85 \cdot 10^{26}\,\mathrm{W} \\[6pt]
E_0 &= \sqrt{\frac{2 \cdot 1.36 \cdot 10^{3}}{3.00 \cdot 10^{8} \cdot 8.85 \cdot 10^{-12}}}\,\mathrm{V/m} \approx 1.01 \cdot 10^{3}\,\mathrm{V/m} \\[6pt]
B_0 &= \frac{1.01 \cdot 10^{3}}{3.00 \cdot 10^{8}}\,\mathrm{T} \approx 3.37 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\mathrm{T} \\[6pt]
I_M &= 1.36 \cdot 10^{3} \cdot \left( \frac{1.50}{2.28} \right)^2\,\mathrm{W/m^2} \approx 589\,\mathrm{W/m^2}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

By the **intensity of an isotropic source**, $I_T = \dfrac{P}{4\pi d_T^2}$, so the emitted power is the intensity multiplied by the area of the sphere whose radius is the Earth-Sun distance. Sunlight contains many frequencies, hence the computed amplitudes are those of a harmonic wave that carries the same intensity, and they give the order of magnitude of the fields: about $1\,\mathrm{kV/m}$ and $3\,\mu\mathrm{T}$. The ratio of the intensities at two different distances is the inverse of the ratio of the squares of the distances, because the power $P$ is the same; Mars therefore receives less than half of the intensity that the Earth receives.

</div>
