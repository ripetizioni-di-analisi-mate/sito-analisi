## Exercise 1

A harmonic plane wave propagates in vacuum with electric field amplitude $E_0 = 50\,\mathrm{V/m}$. Compute the average energy density, the intensity, the average energy contained in a volume of $1.0\,\mathrm{m^3}$, and the energy that in one minute crosses a surface of area $A = 0.50\,\mathrm{m^2}$ perpendicular to the direction of propagation.

$$
\langle u \rangle = \frac{1}{2}\, \varepsilon_0 E_0^2 ,
\qquad
I = c \langle u \rangle ,
\qquad
U_V = \langle u \rangle V ,
\qquad
U_A = I A\, \Delta t
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
\langle u \rangle &= \frac{1}{2} \cdot 8.85 \cdot 10^{-12} \cdot 50^2\,\mathrm{J/m^3} \approx 1.1 \cdot 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{J/m^3} \\[6pt]
I &= 3.00 \cdot 10^{8} \cdot 1.106 \cdot 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{W/m^2} \approx 3.3\,\mathrm{W/m^2} \\[6pt]
U_V &= 1.1 \cdot 10^{-8} \cdot 1.0\,\mathrm{J} \approx 1.1 \cdot 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{J} \\[6pt]
U_A &= 3.32 \cdot 0.50 \cdot 60\,\mathrm{J} \approx 1.0 \cdot 10^{2}\,\mathrm{J}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

By the **energy density of a wave**, the instantaneous value is $\varepsilon_0 E^2$, half electric and half magnetic, and the average value over one period is $\dfrac{1}{2}\varepsilon_0 E_0^2$, because the average of $\sin^2$ is $\dfrac{1}{2}$. By the theorem on the energy flux of a plane wave, the power per unit area is the energy density times $c$, so the intensity is $c\langle u \rangle$, about $3.3\,\mathrm{W/m^2}$.

One cubic meter contains a tiny energy, about $11\,\mathrm{nJ}$, while about $100\,\mathrm{J}$ pass through half a square meter in one minute. The difference is due to the speed at which the energy moves: in one minute the energy contained in a cylinder of length $c\, \Delta t = 1.8 \cdot 10^{10}\,\mathrm{m}$ crosses the surface.

</div>

## Exercise 2

At a point crossed by a plane traveling wave in vacuum, at a certain instant, the electric field has magnitude $E = 300\,\mathrm{V/m}$. Compute at that instant the magnitude of the magnetic field, the electric and magnetic energy densities, and the magnitude of the Poynting vector, verifying that it is equal to the product of the total energy density and $c$.

$$
B = \frac{E}{c} ,
\qquad
u_E = \frac{1}{2}\, \varepsilon_0 E^2 ,
\qquad
u_B = \frac{B^2}{2\mu_0} ,
\qquad
S = \frac{E B}{\mu_0}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
B &= \frac{300}{3.00 \cdot 10^{8}}\,\mathrm{T} = 1.00 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\mathrm{T} \\[6pt]
u_E &= \frac{1}{2} \cdot 8.85 \cdot 10^{-12} \cdot 300^2\,\mathrm{J/m^3} \approx 3.98 \cdot 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{J/m^3} \\[6pt]
u_B &= \frac{\left( 1.00 \cdot 10^{-6} \right)^2}{2 \cdot 4\pi \cdot 10^{-7}}\,\mathrm{J/m^3} \approx 3.98 \cdot 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{J/m^3} \\[6pt]
S &= \frac{300 \cdot 1.00 \cdot 10^{-6}}{4\pi \cdot 10^{-7}}\,\mathrm{W/m^2} \approx 239\,\mathrm{W/m^2} \\[6pt]
(u_E + u_B)\, c &= 7.96 \cdot 10^{-7} \cdot 3.00 \cdot 10^{8}\,\mathrm{W/m^2} \approx 239\,\mathrm{W/m^2}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The magnetic field follows from the **relation between the electric field and the magnetic field**, $E = cB$, valid at every point and every instant of a plane traveling wave. The two energy densities turn out to be equal, as stated by the theorem on the energy density of a wave: the magnetic field is numerically small, but the factor $\dfrac{1}{\mu_0}$ is large and compensates exactly. The Poynting vector has magnitude $\dfrac{EB}{\mu_0}$, because in a wave the two fields are perpendicular, and its value coincides with $u\,c$ within rounding, as required by the theorem on the **energy flux** of a plane wave.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A laser emits a pulse of energy $U = 0.20\,\mathrm{J}$ and duration $\Delta t = 10\,\mathrm{ns}$, in a beam of cross section $A = 1.0\,\mathrm{mm^2}$. Assuming that energy and intensity are uniform over the pulse and over the cross section, compute the length of the pulse in space, the average energy density, the intensity, and the amplitude of the electric field of a harmonic wave with the same intensity.

$$
\ell = c\, \Delta t ,
\qquad
\langle u \rangle = \frac{U}{A\, \ell} ,
\qquad
I = \frac{U}{A\, \Delta t} = c \langle u \rangle ,
\qquad
E_0 = \sqrt{\frac{2I}{c\, \varepsilon_0}}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
\ell &= 3.00 \cdot 10^{8} \cdot 1.0 \cdot 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{m} = 3.0\,\mathrm{m} \\[6pt]
\langle u \rangle &= \frac{0.20}{1.0 \cdot 10^{-6} \cdot 3.0}\,\mathrm{J/m^3} \approx 6.7 \cdot 10^{4}\,\mathrm{J/m^3} \\[6pt]
I &= \frac{0.20}{1.0 \cdot 10^{-6} \cdot 1.0 \cdot 10^{-8}}\,\mathrm{W/m^2} = 2.0 \cdot 10^{13}\,\mathrm{W/m^2} \\[6pt]
E_0 &= \sqrt{\frac{2 \cdot 2.0 \cdot 10^{13}}{3.00 \cdot 10^{8} \cdot 8.85 \cdot 10^{-12}}}\,\mathrm{V/m} \approx 1.2 \cdot 10^{8}\,\mathrm{V/m}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The pulse lasts $10\,\mathrm{ns}$ and moves at the speed $c$, so in space it occupies a stretch of length $c\,\Delta t = 3.0\,\mathrm{m}$; the energy is contained in the cylinder with base $A$ and length $\ell$, and the average density is the energy divided by the volume. The intensity is the power per unit area, that is, the energy divided by the area and by the duration; the same value is obtained by multiplying the energy density by $c$, as stated by the theorem on the **energy flux** of a plane wave.

From the formula for the intensity of a harmonic wave one obtains an amplitude of about $1.2 \cdot 10^{8}\,\mathrm{V/m}$, much greater than the dielectric strength of air, about $3 \cdot 10^{6}\,\mathrm{V/m}$; hence such a concentrated pulse **ionizes** the air at the point where it is focused.

</div>

## Exercise 4

An open container holds $m = 2.0\,\mathrm{kg}$ of water and has a free surface of area $A = 0.20\,\mathrm{m^2}$, exposed perpendicularly to solar radiation of intensity $I = 1.0\,\mathrm{kW/m^2}$. Assuming that the water absorbs all the incident radiation and does not give off heat to the surroundings, compute the time needed to raise the temperature of the water by $\Delta T = 10\,\mathrm{K}$. The specific heat of water is $c_{\text{water}} = 4186\,\mathrm{J/(kg\cdot K)}$.

$$
P = I A ,
\qquad
Q = m\, c_{\text{water}}\, \Delta T ,
\qquad
P\, t = Q
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
t = \frac{m\, c_{\text{water}}\, \Delta T}{I A}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
P &= 1.0 \cdot 10^{3} \cdot 0.20\,\mathrm{W} = 2.0 \cdot 10^{2}\,\mathrm{W} \\[6pt]
Q &= 2.0 \cdot 4186 \cdot 10\,\mathrm{J} \approx 8.37 \cdot 10^{4}\,\mathrm{J} \\[6pt]
t &= \frac{8.37 \cdot 10^{4}}{2.0 \cdot 10^{2}}\,\mathrm{s} \approx 4.2 \cdot 10^{2}\,\mathrm{s}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The intensity is the power per unit area perpendicular to the direction of propagation, and the free surface is perpendicular to the radiation, so the absorbed power is $IA = 200\,\mathrm{W}$. The heat needed to raise the temperature by $10\,\mathrm{K}$ is $m\, c_{\text{water}}\, \Delta T$; see the topic on the first law of thermodynamics. There are no losses, so the energy absorbed in the time $t$ is all used to heat the water, and equating $Pt$ to the heat gives about **7 minutes**. In a real situation the water reflects part of the light and gives off heat to the air, hence the time is longer.

</div>
