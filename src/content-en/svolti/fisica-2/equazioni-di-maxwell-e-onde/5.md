## Exercise 1

Solar radiation, of intensity $I = 1.36\,\mathrm{kW/m^2}$, is incident perpendicularly on a surface. Compute the radiation pressure if the surface absorbs all the radiation and if it reflects all of it, and the force on the panel of area $A = 2.0\,\mathrm{m^2}$ of a satellite, treated as a perfectly absorbing surface perpendicular to the light.

$$
p_{\text{abs}} = \frac{I}{c} ,
\qquad
p_{\text{refl}} = \frac{2I}{c} ,
\qquad
F = p_{\text{abs}}\, A = \frac{I A}{c}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
p_{\text{abs}} &= \frac{1.36 \cdot 10^{3}}{3.00 \cdot 10^{8}}\,\mathrm{Pa} \approx 4.5 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\mathrm{Pa} \\[6pt]
p_{\text{refl}} &= 2 \cdot 4.53 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\mathrm{Pa} \approx 9.1 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\mathrm{Pa} \\[6pt]
F &= 4.53 \cdot 10^{-6} \cdot 2.0\,\mathrm{N} \approx 9.1 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\mathrm{N}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The radiation is incident perpendicularly, so the theorem on **radiation pressure** applies. An absorbing surface receives all the momentum of the radiation, and the pressure is $\dfrac{I}{c}$; a reflecting surface reverses the momentum of the radiation and receives twice as much, hence the pressure doubles. The force is the pressure times the area, about $9\,\mu\mathrm{N}$: it is negligible for an object on the Earth, but on a satellite it acts continuously for years and slowly modifies its orbit.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A probe of total mass $m = 50\,\mathrm{kg}$ is pushed by a perfectly reflecting solar sail of area $A = 800\,\mathrm{m^2}$, oriented perpendicular to sunlight. The probe is at the Earth's distance from the Sun, where the intensity of the radiation is $I = 1.36\,\mathrm{kW/m^2}$. Compute the force due to the radiation and the acceleration it produces, and the change in velocity due to radiation alone in $30$ days, assuming that the force remains constant.

$$
F = \frac{2 I A}{c} ,
\qquad
a = \frac{F}{m} ,
\qquad
\Delta v = a\, \Delta t
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
F &= \frac{2 \cdot 1.36 \cdot 10^{3} \cdot 800}{3.00 \cdot 10^{8}}\,\mathrm{N} \approx 7.3 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{N} \\[6pt]
a &= \frac{7.25 \cdot 10^{-3}}{50}\,\mathrm{m/s^2} \approx 1.5 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m/s^2} \\[6pt]
\Delta t &= 30 \cdot 86400\,\mathrm{s} = 2.59 \cdot 10^{6}\,\mathrm{s} \\[6pt]
\Delta v &= 1.45 \cdot 10^{-4} \cdot 2.59 \cdot 10^{6}\,\mathrm{m/s} \approx 3.8 \cdot 10^{2}\,\mathrm{m/s}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The sail reflects all the radiation and the light is incident perpendicularly, so the **radiation pressure** is $\dfrac{2I}{c}$ and the force is the pressure times the area. The force is a few millinewtons, and divided by the mass it gives a very small acceleration, about $1.5 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$. By Newton's second law, if the force remains constant the acceleration is constant and the velocity grows linearly in time; in one month the change in velocity is **about 380 m/s**. A small but continuous thrust, which requires no fuel, therefore produces large changes in velocity over long times. The assumption of constant force requires that the probe remain at an approximately constant distance from the Sun.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A laser beam of power $P = 10\,\mathrm{W}$ is incident on a perfectly reflecting flat mirror with angle of incidence $\theta = 30^\circ$, measured from the normal to the mirror, and is completely reflected. Compute the force that the beam exerts on the mirror, and compare it with the force in the case of perpendicular incidence.

::grafico{assi=0 uguale=1 poligoni="-3,0 3,0 3,-0.35 -3,-0.35" vettori="0,-0.35,0,-1.6,F;-1.5,2.598,-0.03,0.052;0.03,0.052,1.5,2.598" segmenti="0,0,0,2.9" etichette="-0.62,1.65,θ;0.38,1.65,θ;-2.2,2.8,incident;1.3,2.95,reflected" xmin=-3.4 xmax=3.4 ymin=-1.9 ymax=3.4 titolo="The beam is incident at angle $\theta$ from the normal and is reflected at the same angle: the component of the momentum parallel to the mirror is unchanged, the normal component is reversed, and the mirror receives a force $\vec{F}$ perpendicular to its surface"}

$$
\frac{dp}{dt} = \frac{P}{c} ,
\qquad
\left| \frac{d\vec{p}_{\text{rad}}}{dt} \right| = 2\,\frac{P}{c}\cos\theta
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
F = \frac{2P\cos\theta}{c}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
F &= \frac{2 \cdot 10 \cdot \cos 30^\circ}{3.00 \cdot 10^{8}}\,\mathrm{N} \approx 5.8 \cdot 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{N} \\[6pt]
F_\perp &= \frac{2 \cdot 10}{3.00 \cdot 10^{8}}\,\mathrm{N} \approx 6.7 \cdot 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{N}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

By the theorem on the **momentum of an electromagnetic wave**, the beam carries the energy $P\,dt$ and therefore the momentum $\dfrac{P}{c}\,dt$ in the time $dt$, directed along the incident beam. Decompose this momentum into a component parallel to the mirror, $\dfrac{P}{c}\sin\theta\, dt$, and a perpendicular one, $\dfrac{P}{c}\cos\theta\, dt$. In the reflection the parallel component stays the same and the perpendicular one changes direction, so the momentum of the radiation changes by $2\,\dfrac{P}{c}\cos\theta\, dt$, in the direction perpendicular to the mirror and pointing outward.

By the impulse-momentum theorem the mirror receives an equal and opposite momentum, hence the force is perpendicular to the mirror, directed into it, and has magnitude $\dfrac{2P\cos\theta}{c}$, about $5.8 \cdot 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{N}$. With perpendicular incidence $\cos\theta = 1$ and the force is **maximum**, about $6.7 \cdot 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{N}$.

</div>

## Exercise 4

A grain of interplanetary dust is a sphere of radius $r$ and density $\rho = 2.0 \cdot 10^{3}\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}$, which absorbs all the solar radiation incident on its cross section $\pi r^2$. The Sun emits the power $P = 3.85 \cdot 10^{26}\,\mathrm{W}$ and has mass $M = 1.99 \cdot 10^{30}\,\mathrm{kg}$; the universal gravitational constant is $G = 6.67 \cdot 10^{-11}\,\mathrm{N \cdot m^2/kg^2}$. Show that the ratio between the force of the radiation and the gravitational attraction of the Sun does not depend on the distance from the Sun, and compute the radius for which the two forces have the same magnitude.

$$
F_{\text{rad}} = \frac{I}{c}\,\pi r^2 = \frac{P}{4\pi d^2 c}\,\pi r^2 = \frac{P r^2}{4 d^2 c} ,
\qquad
F_g = \frac{G M m}{d^2} = \frac{4\pi G M \rho\, r^3}{3 d^2}
$$
$$
\frac{F_{\text{rad}}}{F_g} = \frac{P r^2}{4 d^2 c} \cdot \frac{3 d^2}{4\pi G M \rho\, r^3} = \frac{3P}{16\pi c\, G M \rho\, r}
$$
$$
\frac{F_{\text{rad}}}{F_g} = 1
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
r^* = \frac{3P}{16\pi c\, G M \rho}
$$
$$
r^* = \frac{3 \cdot 3.85 \cdot 10^{26}}{16\pi \cdot 3.00 \cdot 10^{8} \cdot 6.67 \cdot 10^{-11} \cdot 1.99 \cdot 10^{30} \cdot 2.0 \cdot 10^{3}}\,\mathrm{m} \approx 2.9 \cdot 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{m}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The Sun is an isotropic source, so at the distance $d$ the intensity is $\dfrac{P}{4\pi d^2}$. The grain absorbs the radiation incident on its cross section, and by the **radiation pressure** on an absorbing surface the force is $\dfrac{I}{c}$ times the area $\pi r^2$, directed away from the Sun. The gravitational attraction is computed with the law of universal gravitation, with the mass $m = \dfrac{4}{3}\pi r^3 \rho$; see the topic on gravitation.

Both forces are proportional to $\dfrac{1}{d^2}$, so in the ratio the distance cancels, and the ratio is inversely proportional to the radius. For $r = r^* \approx 0.29\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ the two forces balance; for smaller grains the radiation prevails and the grain is **pushed away** from the Sun, for larger grains gravity prevails. The result is an order-of-magnitude estimate, because a grain with a radius comparable to the wavelength of light does *not* absorb exactly the radiation incident on its geometric cross section.

</div>
