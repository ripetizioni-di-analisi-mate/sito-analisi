## Exercise 1

In vacuum the electric field of a plane wave is
$$
\vec{E}(y,t) = E_0 \sin(ky + \omega t)\,\hat{k} ,
\qquad
E_0 = 15\,\mathrm{V/m} ,
\qquad
k = 4.0\pi \cdot 10^{6}\,\mathrm{rad/m} ,
$$
with $y$ in meters and $t$ in seconds. Determine the direction and sense of propagation, the wavelength, frequency and angular frequency, write the magnetic field $\vec{B}(y,t)$ and compute the intensity.

$$
\sin(ky + \omega t) = \sin\left( k \left( y + \frac{\omega}{k}\, t \right) \right)
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\text{propagation toward } -\hat{\jmath}
$$
$$
\lambda = \frac{2\pi}{k} ,
\qquad
f = \frac{c}{\lambda} ,
\qquad
\omega = c\, k
$$
$$
\hat{k} \times \left( -\hat{\imath} \right) = -\hat{\jmath}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\vec{B}(y,t) = -\frac{E_0}{c}\, \sin(ky + \omega t)\,\hat{\imath} ,
\qquad
I = \frac{E_0^2}{2\mu_0 c}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
\lambda &= \frac{2\pi}{4.0\pi \cdot 10^{6}}\,\mathrm{m} = 5.0 \cdot 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{m} \\[6pt]
f &= \frac{3.00 \cdot 10^{8}}{5.0 \cdot 10^{-7}}\,\mathrm{Hz} = 6.0 \cdot 10^{14}\,\mathrm{Hz} \\[6pt]
\omega &= 3.00 \cdot 10^{8} \cdot 4.0\pi \cdot 10^{6}\,\mathrm{rad/s} \approx 3.8 \cdot 10^{15}\,\mathrm{rad/s} \\[6pt]
\frac{E_0}{c} &= \frac{15}{3.00 \cdot 10^{8}}\,\mathrm{T} = 5.0 \cdot 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{T} \\[6pt]
I &= \frac{15^2}{2 \cdot 4\pi \cdot 10^{-7} \cdot 3.00 \cdot 10^{8}}\,\mathrm{W/m^2} \approx 0.30\,\mathrm{W/m^2}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The field depends only on the coordinate $y$, so it is a plane wave propagating along the $y$ axis. The argument can be written $k\left( y + \dfrac{\omega}{k}\, t \right)$, that is, it has the form $g(y + vt)$, which by the theorem on **traveling waves** describes a profile that moves toward decreasing $y$, toward $-\hat{\jmath}$. Wavelength, frequency and angular frequency follow from the relations of the harmonic wave; the wavelength of $500\,\mathrm{nm}$ is that of green light.

The magnetic field has magnitude $\dfrac{E}{c}$, oscillates in phase with $\vec{E}$ and is perpendicular both to $\hat{k}$ and to $\hat{\jmath}$, so it is directed along the $x$ axis. Its sense is fixed by the condition that $\vec{E} \times \vec{B}$ point in the direction of propagation: since $\hat{k} \times \left( -\hat{\imath} \right) = -\hat{\jmath}$, when $E_z > 0$ the magnetic field points toward $-\hat{\imath}$. As a check one can use the third Maxwell equation in local form: the $x$ component of the curl of $\vec{E}$ is $\dfrac{\partial E_z}{\partial y} = E_0 k \cos(ky + \omega t)$, and $-\dfrac{\partial B_x}{\partial t} = \dfrac{E_0\, \omega}{c} \cos(ky + \omega t)$ coincides with it, because $\dfrac{\omega}{c} = k$. The intensity is computed from the amplitude of the electric field, and equals **about 0.30 W/m²**.

</div>

## Exercise 2

In vacuum the electric field of a plane electromagnetic pulse is
$$
\vec{E}(x,t) = E_0\, e^{-\frac{(x - ct)^2}{a^2}}\,\hat{\jmath} ,
\qquad
E_0 = 100\,\mathrm{V/m} ,
\qquad
a = 1.0\,\mathrm{m} .
$$
Verify that the field satisfies the wave equation, compute the position of the maximum of the pulse at the instant $t = 2.0\,\mu\mathrm{s}$, write the magnetic field, and compute the energy per unit area that crosses a plane perpendicular to the $x$ axis during the passage of the pulse.

$$
s = x - ct ,
\qquad
f(s) = E_0\, e^{-\frac{s^2}{a^2}} ,
\qquad
\frac{\partial^2 E}{\partial x^2} = f''(s) ,
\qquad
\frac{\partial^2 E}{\partial t^2} = c^2 f''(s)
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\frac{\partial^2 E}{\partial x^2} = \frac{1}{c^2}\,\frac{\partial^2 E}{\partial t^2}
$$
$$
x_{\max}(t) = ct = 3.00 \cdot 10^{8} \cdot 2.0 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\mathrm{m} = 6.0 \cdot 10^{2}\,\mathrm{m}
$$
$$
\vec{B}(x,t) = \frac{E_0}{c}\, e^{-\frac{(x - ct)^2}{a^2}}\,\hat{k} ,
\qquad
\frac{E_0}{c} = \frac{100}{3.00 \cdot 10^{8}}\,\mathrm{T} \approx 3.3 \cdot 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{T}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{U}{A} &= \int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} S\, dt = \int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} \frac{E_0^2}{\mu_0 c}\, e^{-\frac{2(x - ct)^2}{a^2}}\, dt \\[6pt]
&= \frac{E_0^2}{\mu_0 c} \cdot \frac{a}{\sqrt{2}\, c} \int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} e^{-w^2}\, dw \\[6pt]
&= \frac{E_0^2\, a\, \sqrt{\pi}}{\sqrt{2}\, \mu_0 c^2} = \varepsilon_0 E_0^2\, a\, \sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}} \\[6pt]
&= 8.85 \cdot 10^{-12} \cdot 100^2 \cdot 1.0 \cdot 1.253\,\mathrm{J/m^2} \approx 1.1 \cdot 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{J/m^2}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The field has the form $f(x - ct)$ with $f$ of class $C^2$, so by the theorem on **traveling waves** it satisfies the wave equation: the second derivatives with respect to $x$ and with respect to $t$ differ by the factor $c^2$, because the derivative of the inner function with respect to $t$ is $-c$. The profile is translated with speed $c$ without changing shape, so the maximum, which at the initial instant is at $x = 0$, at the instant $t$ is at $x = ct$, that is, at $600\,\mathrm{m}$.

The pulse propagates toward $\hat{\imath}$ with $\vec{E}$ along $\hat{\jmath}$, so by the **relation between the electric field and the magnetic field** we have $\vec{B} = \dfrac{E}{c}\,\hat{k}$, with the same Gaussian profile and maximum value $0.33\,\mu\mathrm{T}$.

The magnitude of the Poynting vector is the power per unit area, so the energy per unit area that crosses the plane is its integral over time; at a fixed point $S = \dfrac{E^2}{\mu_0 c}$. With the change of variable $w = \dfrac{\sqrt{2}\,(ct - x)}{a}$, for which $dt = \dfrac{a}{\sqrt{2}\,c}\, dw$, the integral reduces to the Gaussian integral $\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} e^{-w^2}\, dw = \sqrt{\pi}$, for which see the Analisi 1 topic on improper integrals. Finally $\dfrac{1}{\mu_0 c^2} = \varepsilon_0$, and one obtains about $1.1 \cdot 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{J/m^2}$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

In vacuum the electric field in a region bounded by two metal mirrors is
$$
\vec{E}(x,t) = 2E_0 \sin(kx)\cos(\omega t)\,\hat{\jmath} ,
\qquad
E_0 = 10\,\mathrm{V/m} ,
\qquad
\lambda = \frac{2\pi}{k} = 2.0\,\mathrm{cm} .
$$
Determine the relation between $\omega$ and $k$ for which the field satisfies the wave equation and the corresponding frequency, find the magnetic field assuming that there are no static fields, find the points at which the electric field is zero at every instant, and compute the time average of the Poynting vector.

$$
\frac{\partial^2 E_y}{\partial x^2} = -k^2 E_y ,
\qquad
\frac{\partial^2 E_y}{\partial t^2} = -\omega^2 E_y
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
-k^2 E_y = -\frac{\omega^2}{c^2}\, E_y
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\omega = c k
$$
$$
f = \frac{c}{\lambda} = \frac{3.00 \cdot 10^{8}}{2.0 \cdot 10^{-2}}\,\mathrm{Hz} = 1.5 \cdot 10^{10}\,\mathrm{Hz}
$$
$$
\frac{\partial B_z}{\partial t} = -\frac{\partial E_y}{\partial x} = -2E_0 k \cos(kx)\cos(\omega t)
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
B_z(x,t) = -\frac{2E_0}{c}\, \cos(kx)\sin(\omega t)
$$
$$
\frac{2E_0}{c} = \frac{20}{3.00 \cdot 10^{8}}\,\mathrm{T} \approx 6.7 \cdot 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{T}
$$
$$
\sin(kx) = 0
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
x = n\, \frac{\lambda}{2} = n \cdot 1.0\,\mathrm{cm} , \qquad n \in \mathbb{Z}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
S_x &= \frac{E_y B_z}{\mu_0} = -\frac{4E_0^2}{\mu_0 c}\, \sin(kx)\cos(kx)\, \sin(\omega t)\cos(\omega t) \\[6pt]
&= -\frac{E_0^2}{\mu_0 c}\, \sin(2kx)\, \sin(2\omega t)
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\langle S_x \rangle = 0
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The second derivatives of the field with respect to $x$ and with respect to $t$ are proportional to the field itself, with coefficients $-k^2$ and $-\omega^2$; so the **wave equation** $\dfrac{\partial^2 E_y}{\partial x^2} = \dfrac{1}{c^2}\, \dfrac{\partial^2 E_y}{\partial t^2}$ is satisfied at every point and every instant if and only if $k^2 = \dfrac{\omega^2}{c^2}$, that is, $\omega = ck$, the same relation as for traveling waves. Indeed, the field is the sum of two harmonic waves of amplitude $E_0$ traveling in opposite directions, as in the remark on the superposition of waves.

The magnetic field is obtained from the first relation of the wave equation, $\dfrac{\partial E}{\partial x} = -\dfrac{\partial B}{\partial t}$; integrating with respect to time, with $\displaystyle\int \cos(\omega t)\, dt = \dfrac{\sin(\omega t)}{\omega}$ and $\dfrac{k}{\omega} = \dfrac{1}{c}$, and setting the constant to zero because there are no static fields, one obtains $B_z$. One checks that the second relation is also satisfied: $\dfrac{\partial B_z}{\partial x} = \dfrac{2E_0 k}{c}\sin(kx)\sin(\omega t)$ and $-\dfrac{1}{c^2}\dfrac{\partial E_y}{\partial t} = \dfrac{2E_0\, \omega}{c^2}\sin(kx)\sin(\omega t)$ coincide.

The electric field is zero at every instant where $\sin(kx) = 0$, that is, every $1.0\,\mathrm{cm}$; the mirrors are located at two of these points, because on the surface of a conductor the electric field parallel to the surface is zero. Unlike in a traveling wave, the two fields are a quarter of a period out of phase in time, and the Poynting vector is proportional to $\sin(2\omega t)$, which has zero average over one period; hence the standing wave **carries no energy** on average, and the energy oscillates back and forth between points a quarter of a wavelength apart.

</div>
