## Exercise 1

A thin disk of radius $R = 10\,\mathrm{cm}$ has uniform surface charge density $\sigma = 3.0 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\mathrm{C/m^2}$. Compute the field on the axis of the disk at distance $z = 5.0\,\mathrm{cm}$ and at distance $z = 1.0\,\mathrm{mm}$ from the center, and compare them with the field of an infinite plane with the same density.

$$
E_z = \frac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0} \left( 1 - \frac{z}{\sqrt{z^2 + R^2}} \right) , \qquad E_{\text{plane}} = \frac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0} &= \frac{3.0 \cdot 10^{-6}}{2 \cdot 8.85 \cdot 10^{-12}}\,\mathrm{N/C} \approx 1.69 \cdot 10^{5}\,\mathrm{N/C} \\[6pt]
E_z(5.0\,\mathrm{cm}) &= 1.69 \cdot 10^{5} \left( 1 - \frac{0.050}{\sqrt{0.050^2 + 0.10^2}} \right)\,\mathrm{N/C} = 1.69 \cdot 10^{5} \cdot 0.553\,\mathrm{N/C} \approx 9.4 \cdot 10^{4}\,\mathrm{N/C} \\[6pt]
E_z(1.0\,\mathrm{mm}) &= 1.69 \cdot 10^{5} \left( 1 - \frac{0.0010}{\sqrt{0.0010^2 + 0.10^2}} \right)\,\mathrm{N/C} \approx 1.69 \cdot 10^{5} \cdot 0.990\,\mathrm{N/C} \approx 1.68 \cdot 10^{5}\,\mathrm{N/C}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The point is on the axis of the disk and the density is uniform, so the formula for the **field on the axis of a charged disk** applies; the field is directed along the axis, pointing away from the disk because $\sigma > 0$. At $5.0\,\mathrm{cm}$, that is at half the radius, the field is about $55\,\%$ of that of the infinite plane: at this distance the disk is too small to be treated as a plane. At $1.0\,\mathrm{mm}$ the fraction $\dfrac{z}{\sqrt{z^2 + R^2}}$ is about $0.010$ and the field differs by $1\,\%$ from the **field of the infinite plane**. The result confirms that the infinite-plane model describes a slab well at points that are close compared with its dimensions.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Two parallel infinite planes, $3.0\,\mathrm{cm}$ apart, have uniform charge densities $\sigma_1 = 4.0\,\mathrm{nC/m^2}$ and $\sigma_2 = -2.0\,\mathrm{nC/m^2}$. Compute the electric field in the three regions into which the planes divide space.

::grafico{assi=0 uguale=1 linee="0,-1.4 0,1.4;3,-1.4 3,1.4;-1,0 -1.6,0;-1.45,-0.1 -1.6,0 -1.45,0.1;0.6,0 2.4,0;2.25,0.1 2.4,0 2.25,-0.1;4,0 4.6,0;4.45,0.1 4.6,0 4.45,-0.1" etichette="-0.3,1.6,σ₁;2.7,1.6,σ₂;-1.9,0.3,113 N/C;0.9,0.3,339 N/C;3.85,0.3,113 N/C" xmin=-2.6 xmax=5.4 ymin=-1.7 ymax=2 titolo="Resultant field in the three regions: on the left it points to the left, between the planes and on the right it points to the right"}

$$
E_1 = \frac{\sigma_1}{2\varepsilon_0} , \qquad E_2 = \frac{|\sigma_2|}{2\varepsilon_0}
$$
$$
E_x =
\begin{cases}
-E_1 + E_2 & \text{to the left of plane 1} \\[4pt]
E_1 + E_2 & \text{between the planes} \\[4pt]
E_1 - E_2 & \text{to the right of plane 2}
\end{cases}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
E_1 &= \frac{4.0 \cdot 10^{-9}}{2 \cdot 8.85 \cdot 10^{-12}}\,\mathrm{N/C} \approx 226\,\mathrm{N/C} , \qquad E_2 = \frac{2.0 \cdot 10^{-9}}{2 \cdot 8.85 \cdot 10^{-12}}\,\mathrm{N/C} \approx 113\,\mathrm{N/C} \\[6pt]
E_x &\approx -1.1 \cdot 10^{2}\,\mathrm{N/C} , \quad 3.4 \cdot 10^{2}\,\mathrm{N/C} , \quad 1.1 \cdot 10^{2}\,\mathrm{N/C}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Choose the $x$-axis perpendicular to the planes, oriented from plane 1 to plane 2. By the **field of an infinite plane** each plane produces a uniform field, perpendicular to the plane, of magnitude $\dfrac{|\sigma|}{2\varepsilon_0}$, which does not depend on the distance. Plane 1 is positive, so its field points away from it: toward $-x$ on its left, toward $+x$ on its right. Plane 2 is negative, so its field points toward it: toward $+x$ on its left, toward $-x$ on its right.

By the **superposition principle** in each region the $x$ components are added. Between the planes the two fields have the same orientation and the field is about $3.4 \cdot 10^{2}\,\mathrm{N/C}$; outside they have opposite orientations and the field is $1.1 \cdot 10^{2}\,\mathrm{N/C}$, pointing outward on both sides, that is to the left on the left of plane 1 and to the right on the right of plane 2, because the plane with the larger density in absolute value prevails. The distance between the planes does not enter the result. With opposite densities, $\sigma_2 = -\sigma_1$, the external field would be zero, as in the case of parallel planes with opposite charges.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A thin annulus, that is a disk of radius $R_2 = 6.0\,\mathrm{cm}$ with a concentric hole of radius $R_1 = 2.0\,\mathrm{cm}$, has uniform surface density $\sigma = 1.0 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\mathrm{C/m^2}$. Compute the field on the axis at distance $z = 4.0\,\mathrm{cm}$ from the center.

$$
E_z = E_{\text{disk}}(R_2) - E_{\text{disk}}(R_1) = \frac{\sigma\,z}{2\varepsilon_0} \left( \frac{1}{\sqrt{z^2 + R_1^2}} - \frac{1}{\sqrt{z^2 + R_2^2}} \right)
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0} &= \frac{1.0 \cdot 10^{-6}}{2 \cdot 8.85 \cdot 10^{-12}}\,\mathrm{N/C} \approx 5.65 \cdot 10^{4}\,\mathrm{N/C} \\[6pt]
\frac{1}{\sqrt{z^2 + R_1^2}} &= \frac{1}{\sqrt{0.0016 + 0.0004}}\,\mathrm{m^{-1}} \approx 22.36\,\mathrm{m^{-1}} \\[6pt]
\frac{1}{\sqrt{z^2 + R_2^2}} &= \frac{1}{\sqrt{0.0016 + 0.0036}}\,\mathrm{m^{-1}} \approx 13.87\,\mathrm{m^{-1}} \\[6pt]
E_z &\approx 5.65 \cdot 10^{4} \cdot 0.040 \cdot (22.36 - 13.87)\,\mathrm{N/C} \approx 1.9 \cdot 10^{4}\,\mathrm{N/C}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The annulus is obtained by removing from a disk of radius $R_2$ a disk of radius $R_1$ with the same density. By the **superposition principle**, the field of the annulus plus the field of the small disk equals the field of the large disk; hence the field of the annulus is the difference of the fields on the axis of the two disks, both directed along the axis. In the difference the terms $\dfrac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0}$ cancel and the two terms with the roots remain. The same result is obtained by integrating the contributions of the rings as in the proof of the field of the disk, with the lower limit $R_1$ in place of $0$. The field is about $1.9 \cdot 10^{4}\,\mathrm{N/C}$, directed along the axis and pointing away from the annulus.

</div>

## Exercise 4

An infinite plane has uniform charge density $\sigma = 4.0\,\mathrm{nC/m^2}$, and a point charge $Q = 1.0\,\mathrm{nC}$ is at distance $D = 30\,\mathrm{cm}$ from the plane. Find the point at which the electric field is zero.

$$
k\,\frac{Q}{r^2} = \frac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
r = \sqrt{\frac{2\varepsilon_0\,k\,Q}{\sigma}} = \sqrt{\frac{Q}{2\pi\sigma}}
$$
$$
r = \sqrt{\frac{1.0 \cdot 10^{-9}}{2\pi \cdot 4.0 \cdot 10^{-9}}}\,\mathrm{m} = \sqrt{0.0398}\,\mathrm{m} \approx 0.20\,\mathrm{m} , \qquad D - r \approx 0.10\,\mathrm{m}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The field of the plane is uniform, of magnitude $\dfrac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0} \approx 226\,\mathrm{N/C}$, perpendicular to the plane and pointing away from it. The field of the charge is radial. For the sum to be zero the two fields must be opposite, so the point lies on the perpendicular to the plane through $Q$. On the stretch between the plane and the charge the field of the plane points toward $Q$ and the field of $Q$ points toward the plane, that is they are opposite; beyond the charge and beyond the plane the two fields have the same orientation.

Equating the magnitudes, by the **field of a point charge** and the **field of an infinite plane**, we obtain $r$, the distance of the point from $Q$; since $k = \dfrac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}$, we have $2\varepsilon_0 k = \dfrac{1}{2\pi}$. The result is $r \approx 20\,\mathrm{cm}$, smaller than $D$, so the point does fall between the charge and the plane: the field is zero $20\,\mathrm{cm}$ from the charge and $10\,\mathrm{cm}$ from the plane.

</div>
