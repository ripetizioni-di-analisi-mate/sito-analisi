## Exercise 1

A thin ring of radius $R = 5.0\,\mathrm{cm}$ has charge $Q = 12\,\mathrm{nC}$ uniformly distributed. Compute the electric field on the axis of the ring at distance $z = 12\,\mathrm{cm}$ from the center, the distance from the center at which the field on the axis is maximum and the maximum value.

$$
E_z = \frac{k\,Q\,z}{\left( z^2 + R^2 \right)^{\frac{3}{2}}} , \qquad
z_{\max} = \frac{R}{\sqrt{2}} , \qquad
E_{\max} = \frac{k\,Q\,\dfrac{R}{\sqrt{2}}}{\left( \dfrac{3}{2} R^2 \right)^{\frac{3}{2}}} = \frac{2\,k\,Q}{3\sqrt{3}\,R^2}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
E_z &= \frac{8.99 \cdot 10^{9} \cdot 12 \cdot 10^{-9} \cdot 0.12}{\left( 0.12^2 + 0.050^2 \right)^{\frac{3}{2}}}\,\mathrm{N/C} = \frac{12.95}{0.13^3}\,\mathrm{N/C} \approx 5.9 \cdot 10^{3}\,\mathrm{N/C} \\[6pt]
z_{\max} &= \frac{5.0}{\sqrt{2}}\,\mathrm{cm} \approx 3.5\,\mathrm{cm} \\[6pt]
E_{\max} &= \frac{2 \cdot 8.99 \cdot 10^{9} \cdot 12 \cdot 10^{-9}}{3\sqrt{3} \cdot 0.050^2}\,\mathrm{N/C} \approx 1.7 \cdot 10^{4}\,\mathrm{N/C}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The charge is uniformly distributed on the ring and the point is on the axis, so the formula for the **field on the axis of a charged ring** applies; the field is directed along the axis, pointing away from the ring because $Q > 0$. With $z = 12\,\mathrm{cm}$ and $R = 5.0\,\mathrm{cm}$ the distance from every point of the ring is $\sqrt{z^2 + R^2} = 13\,\mathrm{cm}$, and the calculation simplifies.

For the maximum we differentiate $E_z$ with respect to $z$: the derivative is proportional to $(z^2 + R^2) - 3z^2 = R^2 - 2z^2$, which vanishes for $z = \dfrac{R}{\sqrt{2}}$, is positive before and negative after. At that point $z^2 + R^2 = \dfrac{3}{2}R^2$, and substituting gives $E_{\max} \approx 1.7 \cdot 10^{4}\,\mathrm{N/C}$. At $12\,\mathrm{cm}$ the field is about one third of the maximum; at even greater distances the ring behaves as a point charge.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A very long straight wire has uniform linear charge density $\lambda = 3.0\,\mathrm{nC/m}$. Compute the field at distance $r = 4.0\,\mathrm{cm}$ from the wire. Then consider a segment of wire $20\,\mathrm{cm}$ long, with the same density, and the point $4.0\,\mathrm{cm}$ from its midpoint on the perpendicular bisector of the segment: compute the field and the error made by approximating the segment with an infinite wire.

$$
E_\infty = \frac{2k\,\lambda}{r} , \qquad
E_{\ell} = \frac{2k\,\lambda\,\ell}{r\sqrt{r^2 + \ell^2}} = E_\infty \cdot \frac{\ell}{\sqrt{r^2 + \ell^2}} , \qquad \ell = 10\,\mathrm{cm}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
E_\infty &= \frac{2 \cdot 8.99 \cdot 10^{9} \cdot 3.0 \cdot 10^{-9}}{0.040}\,\mathrm{N/C} \approx 1.35 \cdot 10^{3}\,\mathrm{N/C} \\[6pt]
\frac{\ell}{\sqrt{r^2 + \ell^2}} &= \frac{0.10}{\sqrt{0.0016 + 0.010}} \approx 0.928 \\[6pt]
E_{\ell} &\approx 1.35 \cdot 10^{3} \cdot 0.928\,\mathrm{N/C} \approx 1.25 \cdot 10^{3}\,\mathrm{N/C} \\[6pt]
\frac{E_\infty - E_\ell}{E_\ell} &\approx 0.077
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

For the very long wire we use the formula for the **infinite wire**: the field is radial, perpendicular to the wire, and is about $1.35 \cdot 10^{3}\,\mathrm{N/C}$. For the finite segment the length is $2\ell = 20\,\mathrm{cm}$, so $\ell = 10\,\mathrm{cm}$, and the point is on the perpendicular bisector of the segment, where the formula for the **field of a straight wire** of finite length holds. The field of the segment equals the field of the infinite wire multiplied by the factor $\dfrac{\ell}{\sqrt{r^2 + \ell^2}} < 1$, because the contributions of the parts of the wire beyond the ends are missing. Approximating the segment with an infinite wire overestimates the field by about $8\,\%$: the distance $4.0\,\mathrm{cm}$ is not small enough compared with the half-length $10\,\mathrm{cm}$ for the approximation to be accurate.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A thin rod of length $\ell = 20\,\mathrm{cm}$, with charge $Q = 4.0\,\mathrm{nC}$ uniformly distributed, lies on the $x$-axis between $x = 0$ and $x = \ell$. Compute the electric field at the point $P$ of the $x$-axis that is at distance $b = 5.0\,\mathrm{cm}$ from the end $x = \ell$, outside the rod.

$$
\lambda = \frac{Q}{\ell} , \qquad dq = \lambda\,dx , \qquad dE_x = k\,\frac{\lambda\,dx}{(\ell + b - x)^2}
$$
$$
E_x = k\,\lambda \int_0^{\ell} \frac{dx}{(\ell + b - x)^2} = k\,\lambda \left[ \frac{1}{\ell + b - x} \right]_0^{\ell} = k\,\lambda \left( \frac{1}{b} - \frac{1}{\ell + b} \right) = \frac{k\,Q}{b\,(\ell + b)}
$$
$$
E_x = \frac{8.99 \cdot 10^{9} \cdot 4.0 \cdot 10^{-9}}{0.050 \cdot 0.25}\,\mathrm{N/C} \approx 2.9 \cdot 10^{3}\,\mathrm{N/C}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

We apply the method for the **field of a continuous distribution**. The linear density is uniform, $\lambda = \dfrac{Q}{\ell} = 2.0 \cdot 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{C/m}$. The point $P$ has coordinate $\ell + b$, so the element between $x$ and $x + dx$ is at distance $\ell + b - x$ from $P$. All the elements lie on the same line as $P$ and to its left, therefore their fields are all directed along $\hat{\imath}$ and add up without being resolved into components. An antiderivative of $\dfrac{1}{(\ell + b - x)^2}$ is $\dfrac{1}{\ell + b - x}$, as can be verified by differentiating, and the field is $2.9 \cdot 10^{3}\,\mathrm{N/C}$, directed toward increasing $x$.

Check: for $b \gg \ell$ we have $E_x \approx \dfrac{kQ}{b^2}$, the field of a point charge $Q$, as expected when the rod is seen from far away. The result is also the field that would be obtained by concentrating the charge at distance $\sqrt{b\,(\ell + b)} \approx 11\,\mathrm{cm}$ from $P$, which is the geometric mean of the distances of the two ends.

</div>

## Exercise 4

A thin wire bent into a semicircle of radius $R = 10\,\mathrm{cm}$ has charge $Q = 5.0\,\mathrm{nC}$ uniformly distributed. Compute the electric field at the center $O$ of the semicircle.

::grafico{assi=0 uguale=1 curve="2*cos(t),2*sin(t),0,3.1416" punti="0,0,pieno,O;1,1.732,pieno,dq;-1,1.732,pieno" segmenti="1,1.732,0,0;-1,1.732,0,0;0,0,2.4,0" vettori="0,0,-0.5,-0.866;0,0,0.5,-0.866" etichette="-1.6,1.95,dq′;0.45,0.2,θ;-1.05,-0.9,dE;0.65,-0.9,dE′;1.05,0.65,R" legenda=0 xmin=-2.6 xmax=2.8 ymin=-1.4 ymax=2.5 titolo="The elements $dq$ and $dq'$, symmetric with respect to the $y$-axis, produce at $O$ fields whose $x$ components cancel"}

$$
\lambda = \frac{Q}{\pi R} , \qquad dq = \lambda\,R\,d\theta , \qquad dE = k\,\frac{\lambda\,R\,d\theta}{R^2} = \frac{k\,\lambda}{R}\,d\theta
$$
$$
E_y = -\int_0^{\pi} \frac{k\,\lambda}{R}\,\sin\theta\,d\theta = -\frac{k\,\lambda}{R} \Big[ -\cos\theta \Big]_0^{\pi} = -\frac{2\,k\,\lambda}{R} = -\frac{2\,k\,Q}{\pi R^2}
$$
$$
|E_y| = \frac{2 \cdot 8.99 \cdot 10^{9} \cdot 5.0 \cdot 10^{-9}}{\pi \cdot 0.10^2}\,\mathrm{N/C} \approx 2.9 \cdot 10^{3}\,\mathrm{N/C}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The semicircle has length $\pi R$, so the linear density is $\dfrac{Q}{\pi R}$. The point of the arc is described by the angle $\theta \in [0, \pi]$ measured from the $x$-axis; the arc corresponding to $d\theta$ has length $R\,d\theta$ and charge $\lambda R\,d\theta$. All the elements are at distance $R$ from the center, therefore the magnitude $dE$ is the same for each of them; the field of the element points away from the element, so at $O$ it is directed as the unit vector $(-\cos\theta, -\sin\theta)$.

The elements at $\theta$ and at $\pi - \theta$ have opposite $x$ components, which cancel by symmetry; the $y$ component remains, which equals $-dE\sin\theta$ for each element. Integrating gives a field of magnitude $\dfrac{2k\lambda}{R} \approx 2.9 \cdot 10^{3}\,\mathrm{N/C}$, directed **from the center toward the side opposite to the arc**. The result coincides with the field of an infinite wire with the same $\lambda$ at distance $R$, $\dfrac{2k\lambda}{R}$; a complete ring would instead give zero field at the center, because the other half would produce the opposite field.

</div>
