## Exercise 1

An oil drop of mass $m = 4.9 \cdot 10^{-15}\,\mathrm{kg}$ remains suspended at rest between two horizontal plates, where there is a uniform vertical electric field of magnitude $E = 1.0 \cdot 10^{5}\,\mathrm{N/C}$ directed downward. Compute the charge of the drop and express it as a multiple of the elementary charge. The buoyant force of the air is negligible.

$$
q\,\vec{E} + m\vec{g} = \vec{0}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
|q|\,E = m g
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
|q| = \frac{m g}{E}
$$
$$
|q| = \frac{4.9 \cdot 10^{-15} \cdot 9.81}{1.0 \cdot 10^{5}}\,\mathrm{C} \approx 4.8 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{C} , \qquad
\frac{|q|}{e} = \frac{4.81 \cdot 10^{-19}}{1.60 \cdot 10^{-19}} \approx 3.0
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The drop is at rest, so the resultant of the forces is zero: the electric force balances the weight and points upward. The field points downward and the force $q\vec{E}$ has orientation opposite to the field, therefore the charge is **negative**, $q \approx -4.8 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{C}$. The ratio with the elementary charge is $3.0$, so the drop carries three excess electrons. This is the principle of Millikan's experiment, which by measuring the charges of many drops showed that they are all integer multiples of $e$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A small sphere of mass $m = 2.0\,\mathrm{g}$ and charge $q = 50\,\mathrm{nC}$ hangs from an inextensible insulating string, in a region where there is a uniform horizontal electric field of magnitude $E = 2.0 \cdot 10^{5}\,\mathrm{N/C}$. Compute the angle that the string forms with the vertical at equilibrium and the tension of the string.

::grafico{assi=0 uguale=1 linee="-1,0 1.2,0;0,0 1.36,-2.67;-1,-4.1 0.6,-4.1;0.45,-4 0.6,-4.1 0.45,-4.2" segmenti="0,0,0,-3" punti="1.36,-2.67,pieno" vettori="1.36,-2.67,0.56,-1.1,T;1.36,-2.67,1.36,-4.24,P;1.36,-2.67,2.16,-2.67,F" etichette="0.12,-1.05,θ;-0.4,-3.85,E" xmin=-1.4 xmax=3.4 ymin=-4.6 ymax=0.6 titolo="At equilibrium the tension $\vec{T}$ of the string balances the sum of the weight $\vec{P}$ and the electric force $\vec{F} = q\vec{E}$"}

$$
\begin{cases}
T\sin\theta = q\,E \\[4pt]
T\cos\theta = m g
\end{cases}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\tan\theta = \frac{q\,E}{m g} , \qquad T = \sqrt{(m g)^2 + (q E)^2}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
q\,E &= 50 \cdot 10^{-9} \cdot 2.0 \cdot 10^{5}\,\mathrm{N} = 1.0 \cdot 10^{-2}\,\mathrm{N} , \qquad m g = 2.0 \cdot 10^{-3} \cdot 9.81\,\mathrm{N} \approx 1.96 \cdot 10^{-2}\,\mathrm{N} \\[6pt]
\tan\theta &= \frac{1.0 \cdot 10^{-2}}{1.96 \cdot 10^{-2}} \approx 0.510 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \theta \approx 27^\circ \\[6pt]
T &= \sqrt{(1.96 \cdot 10^{-2})^2 + (1.0 \cdot 10^{-2})^2}\,\mathrm{N} \approx 2.2 \cdot 10^{-2}\,\mathrm{N}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Three forces act on the sphere: the weight, vertical and downward; the electric force $q\vec{E}$, horizontal and with the same orientation as the field because $q > 0$; the tension of the string, directed along the string toward the point of suspension. At equilibrium the resultant is zero, so the horizontal and vertical components must vanish *simultaneously*, and we obtain the system written above. Dividing the first equation by the second eliminates $T$ and gives $\tan\theta$; adding the squares eliminates $\theta$ and gives $T$. The string is inclined by about $27^\circ$ **in the direction of the field**, and the tension is greater than the weight, because it must also balance the electric force.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A small sphere of mass $m = 1.0\,\mathrm{g}$ and charge $q = 20\,\mathrm{nC}$ hangs from an insulating string near a large vertical slab with uniform charge density $\sigma$. At equilibrium the string forms an angle $\theta = 15^\circ$ with the vertical, moving away from the slab. Compute $\sigma$.

$$
E = \frac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0} , \qquad \tan\theta = \frac{q\,E}{m g}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\sigma = \frac{2\varepsilon_0\, m g \tan\theta}{q}
$$
$$
\sigma = \frac{2 \cdot 8.85 \cdot 10^{-12} \cdot 1.0 \cdot 10^{-3} \cdot 9.81 \cdot \tan 15^\circ}{20 \cdot 10^{-9}}\,\mathrm{C/m^2} = \frac{4.65 \cdot 10^{-14}}{2.0 \cdot 10^{-8}}\,\mathrm{C/m^2} \approx 2.3 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\mathrm{C/m^2}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The slab is large and the sphere is close to it, far from the edges, so the **infinite plane** model can be used: the field is uniform, horizontal, of magnitude $\dfrac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0}$, and does not depend on the distance of the sphere from the slab. The situation is therefore the same as in the previous exercise, with a uniform horizontal field, and the balance of forces gives $\tan\theta = \dfrac{qE}{mg}$. The sphere, which is positive, is repelled by the slab, so the slab is also positively charged, with $\sigma \approx 2.3\,\mu\mathrm{C/m^2}$; the corresponding field is about $1.3 \cdot 10^{5}\,\mathrm{N/C}$.

</div>

## Exercise 4

A thin ring of radius $R = 5.0\,\mathrm{cm}$ has charge $Q = 1.0\,\mathrm{nC}$ uniformly distributed and is at rest in vacuum. An electron is released from rest on the axis of the ring, at a distance from the center much smaller than $R$. Show that the electron performs harmonic oscillations about the center and compute their period.

$$
F_z = -e\,E_z = -\frac{k\,Q\,e\,z}{\left( z^2 + R^2 \right)^{\frac{3}{2}}} \approx -\frac{k\,Q\,e}{R^3}\,z \qquad (|z| \ll R)
$$
$$
m_e\,\ddot{z} = -\frac{k\,Q\,e}{R^3}\,z
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\omega = \sqrt{\frac{k\,Q\,e}{m_e R^3}} , \qquad T = \frac{2\pi}{\omega}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
\omega^2 &= \frac{8.99 \cdot 10^{9} \cdot 1.0 \cdot 10^{-9} \cdot 1.60 \cdot 10^{-19}}{9.11 \cdot 10^{-31} \cdot 0.050^3}\,\mathrm{s^{-2}} \approx 1.26 \cdot 10^{16}\,\mathrm{s^{-2}} \\[6pt]
\omega &\approx 1.12 \cdot 10^{8}\,\mathrm{rad/s} \\[6pt]
T &= \frac{2\pi}{1.12 \cdot 10^{8}}\,\mathrm{s} \approx 5.6 \cdot 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{s}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

By the **field on the axis of a charged ring** the field at the point with coordinate $z$ is directed along the axis, with component $E_z$ of the same sign as $z$. The electron has charge $-e$, so the force has a component of sign opposite to $z$: it is always directed toward the center of the ring and tends to bring the electron back there. Since the electron starts from rest on the axis and the force is directed along the axis, the motion takes place along the axis.

For $|z| \ll R$ the term $z^2$ is negligible compared with $R^2$, so the denominator is approximately $R^3$ and the force is proportional to the displacement and opposite to it, like the elastic force of a spring with constant $\dfrac{kQe}{R^3}$. Newton's second law then gives the equation of **harmonic motion**, with angular frequency $\omega \approx 1.12 \cdot 10^{8}\,\mathrm{rad/s}$ and period $T \approx 56\,\mathrm{ns}$. The weight of the electron is negligible compared with the electric force. If the amplitude were not small compared with $R$ the motion would remain periodic, with a period depending on the amplitude.

</div>
