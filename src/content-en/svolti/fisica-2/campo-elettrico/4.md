## Exercise 1

A dipole is formed by the charges $\pm q$, with $q = 3.0\,\mathrm{nC}$, placed at distance $d = 2.0\,\mathrm{mm}$. Compute the dipole moment, the field on the axis of the dipole at $r = 6.0\,\mathrm{cm}$ from the center, both with the exact formula and with the approximation for $r \gg d$, and the field on the perpendicular bisector at the same distance.

$$
p = q\,d , \qquad
E_{\text{axis}} = \frac{2k\,p\,r}{\left( r^2 - \dfrac{d^2}{4} \right)^2} \approx \frac{2k\,p}{r^3} , \qquad
E_{\text{bis}} \approx \frac{k\,p}{r^3}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
p &= 3.0 \cdot 10^{-9} \cdot 2.0 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{C\cdot m} = 6.0 \cdot 10^{-12}\,\mathrm{C\cdot m} \\[6pt]
E_{\text{axis}} &= \frac{2 \cdot 8.99 \cdot 10^{9} \cdot 6.0 \cdot 10^{-12} \cdot 0.060}{\left( 0.0036 - 1.0 \cdot 10^{-6} \right)^2}\,\mathrm{N/C} \approx 499.7\,\mathrm{N/C} \\[6pt]
\frac{2k\,p}{r^3} &= \frac{2 \cdot 8.99 \cdot 10^{9} \cdot 6.0 \cdot 10^{-12}}{0.060^3}\,\mathrm{N/C} \approx 499.4\,\mathrm{N/C} \\[6pt]
E_{\text{bis}} &\approx \frac{8.99 \cdot 10^{9} \cdot 6.0 \cdot 10^{-12}}{0.060^3}\,\mathrm{N/C} \approx 2.5 \cdot 10^{2}\,\mathrm{N/C}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The dipole moment has magnitude $q\,d$ and is directed from the negative charge to the positive one. On the axis the formula for the **field of the dipole on its axis** applies: the field is parallel to $\vec{p}$ and has the same orientation. The distance $r = 6.0\,\mathrm{cm}$ is $30$ times $d$, so the term $\dfrac{d^2}{4} = 1.0 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\mathrm{m^2}$ is negligible compared with $r^2 = 3.6 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m^2}$, and indeed the two values differ by less than $0.1\,\%$: with two significant figures the field is $5.0 \cdot 10^{2}\,\mathrm{N/C}$. On the perpendicular bisector the field is **opposite** to $\vec{p}$ and, at equal distance, is half as large.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A rigid dipole with $p = 4.0 \cdot 10^{-12}\,\mathrm{C\cdot m}$ is in a uniform field $E = 2.5 \cdot 10^{4}\,\mathrm{N/C}$, with $\vec{p}$ inclined at $\theta = 60^\circ$ with respect to $\vec{E}$. Compute the resultant force, the torque, the maximum torque that the field can exert on the dipole and the work $W$ done by the field when the dipole rotates until it is aligned with the field.

$$
\vec{F}_{\text{tot}} = q\vec{E} - q\vec{E} = \vec{0} , \qquad
\tau = p\,E\sin\theta , \qquad
\tau_{\max} = p\,E , \qquad
W = p\,E \left( \cos 0 - \cos\theta \right)
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
p\,E &= 4.0 \cdot 10^{-12} \cdot 2.5 \cdot 10^{4}\,\mathrm{N\cdot m} = 1.0 \cdot 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{N\cdot m} \\[6pt]
\tau &= 1.0 \cdot 10^{-7} \cdot \sin 60^\circ\,\mathrm{N\cdot m} \approx 8.7 \cdot 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{N\cdot m} \\[6pt]
W &= 1.0 \cdot 10^{-7} \cdot (1 - 0.50)\,\mathrm{J} = 5.0 \cdot 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{J}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The field is uniform, so equal and opposite forces act on the two charges and the resultant is **zero**: the dipole does not translate. By the theorem on the **dipole in a uniform field** the torque is $\vec{p} \times \vec{E}$, of magnitude $pE\sin\theta$; it tends to reduce the angle $\theta$ and is maximum when $\vec{p}$ is perpendicular to the field. During the rotation from $60^\circ$ to $0^\circ$ the torque has the same direction as the rotation, so it does positive work, which by the formula for the work on the dipole equals $pE(\cos\theta_2 - \cos\theta_1)$ with $\theta_1 = 60^\circ$ and $\theta_2 = 0$. The same result is obtained as the decrease of the potential energy $U = -pE\cos\theta$, which goes from $-5.0 \cdot 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{J}$ to $-1.0 \cdot 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{J}$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

For a dipole of length $d$, determine the minimum distance $r$ from the center, on the axis of the dipole, at which the approximate formula $E \approx \dfrac{2kp}{r^3}$ differs from the exact value by no more than $1\,\%$. Compute the distance for $d = 2.0\,\mathrm{mm}$.

$$
\frac{E_{\text{exact}}}{E_{\text{appr}}} = \frac{2k\,p\,r}{\left( r^2 - \dfrac{d^2}{4} \right)^2} \cdot \frac{r^3}{2k\,p} = \frac{1}{\left( 1 - u \right)^2} , \qquad u = \frac{d^2}{4 r^2}
$$
$$
\frac{1}{(1 - u)^2} \leq 1.01
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
1 - u \geq \frac{1}{\sqrt{1.01}}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
u \leq 1 - \frac{1}{\sqrt{1.01}} \approx 4.96 \cdot 10^{-3}
$$
$$
\frac{d}{2r} \leq \sqrt{4.96 \cdot 10^{-3}} \approx 0.0704
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
r \geq \frac{d}{2 \cdot 0.0704} \approx 7.1\,d
$$
$$
r \geq 7.1 \cdot 2.0\,\mathrm{mm} \approx 1.4\,\mathrm{cm}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The ratio between the exact value of the **field of the dipole on its axis** and the approximate value depends only on the ratio $\dfrac{d}{r}$: dividing numerator and denominator by $r^4$ gives $\dfrac{1}{(1 - u)^2}$, with $u = \dfrac{d^2}{4r^2}$. The ratio is greater than $1$, so the approximate formula underestimates the field, and the relative error is $\dfrac{1}{(1-u)^2} - 1$. Requiring an error not greater than $1\,\%$ is equivalent to the inequality written above; since $0 < u < 1$, we can take the square root and invert without reversing the inequality. We obtain $r \geq 7.1\,d$: already at a distance of about seven times the length of the dipole the approximation is **accurate** to $1\,\%$, and for $d = 2.0\,\mathrm{mm}$ it suffices that $r \geq 1.4\,\mathrm{cm}$. The expansion $\dfrac{1}{(1 - u)^2} \approx 1 + 2u$ gives the same result with $u \leq 0.005$.

</div>

## Exercise 4

Two small spheres of mass $m = 1.0\,\mathrm{g}$ each, with charges $+q$ and $-q$, where $q = 50\,\mathrm{nC}$, are fixed to the ends of an insulating rod of negligible mass and length $d = 4.0\,\mathrm{cm}$, free to rotate on a smooth horizontal plane about a vertical axis through its center. A uniform horizontal field $E = 2.0 \cdot 10^{4}\,\mathrm{N/C}$ acts in the plane. Compute the period of the small oscillations of the rod about the position of stable equilibrium.

$$
I\,\ddot{\theta} = -p\,E\sin\theta \approx -p\,E\,\theta
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\omega = \sqrt{\frac{p\,E}{I}} , \qquad T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{I}{p\,E}}
$$
$$
p = q\,d , \qquad I = 2\,m \left( \frac{d}{2} \right)^2 = \frac{m\,d^2}{2}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
p\,E &= 50 \cdot 10^{-9} \cdot 0.040 \cdot 2.0 \cdot 10^{4}\,\mathrm{N\cdot m} = 4.0 \cdot 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{N\cdot m} \\[6pt]
I &= \frac{1.0 \cdot 10^{-3} \cdot 0.040^2}{2}\,\mathrm{kg\cdot m^2} = 8.0 \cdot 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{kg\cdot m^2} \\[6pt]
T &= 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{8.0 \cdot 10^{-7}}{4.0 \cdot 10^{-5}}}\,\mathrm{s} = 2\pi\sqrt{0.020}\,\mathrm{s} \approx 0.89\,\mathrm{s}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The system is a rigid dipole with $p = q\,d$ in a uniform field. The plane is horizontal and smooth, so the weight and the reaction of the plane balance each other and have no torque about the vertical axis; the only torque is the electric one, which by the theorem on the **dipole in a uniform field** equals $pE\sin\theta$ and tends to bring $\vec{p}$ back parallel to $\vec{E}$. The position of stable equilibrium is $\theta = 0$.

The rotational equation of motion about the fixed axis gives $I\ddot{\theta} = -pE\sin\theta$, where the minus sign indicates that the torque opposes the angular displacement; the moment of inertia is that of the two spheres, treated as point masses, at distance $\dfrac{d}{2}$ from the axis. For small angles $\sin\theta \approx \theta$, therefore the equation is that of **harmonic motion** with angular frequency $\sqrt{\dfrac{pE}{I}} \approx 7.1\,\mathrm{rad/s}$, and the period is about $0.89\,\mathrm{s}$. For rigid-body dynamics and harmonic motion we refer to the topics in Physics 1.

</div>
