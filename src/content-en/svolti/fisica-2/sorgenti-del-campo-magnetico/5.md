## Exercise 1

A long straight wire with circular cross section of radius $R = 2.0\,\mathrm{mm}$ carries the current $I = 30\,\mathrm{A}$, distributed uniformly over the cross section. Compute the magnitude of the magnetic field at the distances $r_1 = 1.0\,\mathrm{mm}$, $r_2 = R$ and $r_3 = 6.0\,\mathrm{mm}$ from the axis.

$$
B(r) =
\begin{cases}
\dfrac{\mu_0 I\, r}{2\pi R^2} & 0 \leq r \leq R \\[10pt]
\dfrac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi r} & r \geq R
\end{cases}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
B(r_1) &= \frac{2 \cdot 10^{-7} \cdot 30 \cdot 1.0 \cdot 10^{-3}}{(2.0 \cdot 10^{-3})^2}\,\mathrm{T} = \frac{6.0 \cdot 10^{-9}}{4.0 \cdot 10^{-6}}\,\mathrm{T} = 1.5 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{T} \\[6pt]
B(R) &= \frac{2 \cdot 10^{-7} \cdot 30}{2.0 \cdot 10^{-3}}\,\mathrm{T} = 3.0 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{T} \\[6pt]
B(r_3) &= \frac{2 \cdot 10^{-7} \cdot 30}{6.0 \cdot 10^{-3}}\,\mathrm{T} = 1.0 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{T}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The wire is long and the current is uniform over the cross section, so the theorem on the **field of a cylindrical wire** holds, which is derived from Ampère's law applied to coaxial circles. At $1.0\,\mathrm{mm}$ from the axis, that is inside, the circle encloses only the fraction $\dfrac{r_1^2}{R^2} = \dfrac{1}{4}$ of the current, and the field grows linearly with $r$: it equals half the value at the surface. At the surface the field is maximum, $3.0 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{T}$. Outside, the wire behaves like a thin wire on the axis, and at three times the radius the field is one third of the value at the surface. The two branches of the formula give the same value for $r = R$, as they must.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A coaxial cable consists of an inner conductor of radius $a = 1.0\,\mathrm{mm}$ and a hollow cylindrical outer conductor with radii $b = 4.0\,\mathrm{mm}$ and $c = 5.0\,\mathrm{mm}$. The two conductors carry the current $I = 12\,\mathrm{A}$ in opposite senses, distributed uniformly over the cross sections. Compute the magnitude of the magnetic field at distances $0.50\,\mathrm{mm}$, $2.0\,\mathrm{mm}$, $4.5\,\mathrm{mm}$ and $7.0\,\mathrm{mm}$ from the axis.

$$
B\,2\pi r = \mu_0\, I_{\text{enc}}(r) ,
\qquad
I_{\text{enc}}(r) =
\begin{cases}
I\,\dfrac{r^2}{a^2} & 0 \leq r \leq a \\[10pt]
I & a \leq r \leq b \\[10pt]
I\,\dfrac{c^2 - r^2}{c^2 - b^2} & b \leq r \leq c \\[10pt]
0 & r \geq c
\end{cases}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
B(0.50\,\mathrm{mm}) &= \frac{2 \cdot 10^{-7} \cdot 12 \cdot 0.50 \cdot 10^{-3}}{(1.0 \cdot 10^{-3})^2}\,\mathrm{T} = 1.2 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{T} \\[6pt]
B(2.0\,\mathrm{mm}) &= \frac{2 \cdot 10^{-7} \cdot 12}{2.0 \cdot 10^{-3}}\,\mathrm{T} = 1.2 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{T} \\[6pt]
I_{\text{enc}}(4.5\,\mathrm{mm}) &= 12 \cdot \frac{25 - 20.25}{25 - 16}\,\mathrm{A} \approx 6.33\,\mathrm{A} \\[6pt]
B(4.5\,\mathrm{mm}) &= \frac{2 \cdot 10^{-7} \cdot 6.33}{4.5 \cdot 10^{-3}}\,\mathrm{T} \approx 2.8 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{T} \\[6pt]
B(7.0\,\mathrm{mm}) &= 0
\end{aligned}
$$

::grafico{f="x<=1?2.4*x:(x<=4?2.4/x:(x<=5?2.4/x*(25-x*x)/9:0))" segmenti="1,0,1,2.4;4,0,4,0.6;5,0,5,0.2" legenda="B(r)" nomi="r,B" xmin=0 xmax=7.5 ymin=0 ymax=2.8 titolo="Field of the coaxial cable as a function of the distance from the axis, with $r$ in millimeters and $B$ in millitesla: it grows in the inner conductor, decreases between the conductors and vanishes outside the cable"}

<div class="spiegazione">

The cable has cylindrical symmetry, so we apply **Ampère's law** to the coaxial circles, along which the field is tangent and of constant magnitude; the circulation equals $B\,2\pi r$, and the problem reduces to the calculation of the enclosed current, counting the current of the inner conductor as positive.

At $0.50\,\mathrm{mm}$ the circle is inside the inner conductor and encloses a quarter of its current. At $2.0\,\mathrm{mm}$ it is in the space between the conductors and encloses the whole inner current; by coincidence the field has the same value as at the previous point. At $4.5\,\mathrm{mm}$ the circle passes through the outer conductor: the annulus between $b$ and $r$ carries the fraction $\dfrac{r^2 - b^2}{c^2 - b^2} = \dfrac{4.25}{9}$ of the outer current, which is opposite, and the enclosed current drops to $6.33\,\mathrm{A}$. At $7.0\,\mathrm{mm}$ the two currents compensate each other completely and the field is **zero**, as at every point outside the cable.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A long cylindrical conductor of radius $R = 5.0\,\mathrm{mm}$ carries the current $I = 20\,\mathrm{A}$ with current density parallel to the axis and of magnitude $J(r) = J_0\,\dfrac{r}{R}$, where $r$ is the distance from the axis. Compute $J_0$ and the magnitude of the magnetic field at distance $\dfrac{R}{2}$, $R$ and $2R$ from the axis.

$$
I_{\text{enc}}(r) = \int_0^r J_0\,\frac{r'}{R}\,2\pi r'\,dr' = \frac{2\pi J_0\, r^3}{3R} ,
\qquad
I = I_{\text{enc}}(R) = \frac{2\pi J_0 R^2}{3}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
J_0 = \frac{3I}{2\pi R^2}
$$
$$
B\,2\pi r = \mu_0\, I\,\frac{r^3}{R^3}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
B(r) = \frac{\mu_0 I\, r^2}{2\pi R^3} \quad (r \leq R) ,
\qquad
B(r) = \frac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi r} \quad (r \geq R)
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
J_0 &= \frac{3 \cdot 20}{2\pi \cdot (5.0 \cdot 10^{-3})^2}\,\mathrm{A/m^2} \approx 3.8 \cdot 10^{5}\,\mathrm{A/m^2} \\[6pt]
B\!\left( \frac{R}{2} \right) &= \frac{2 \cdot 10^{-7} \cdot 20}{5.0 \cdot 10^{-3}} \cdot \frac{1}{4}\,\mathrm{T} = 2.0 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{T} \\[6pt]
B(R) &= \frac{2 \cdot 10^{-7} \cdot 20}{5.0 \cdot 10^{-3}}\,\mathrm{T} = 8.0 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{T} \\[6pt]
B(2R) &= \frac{2 \cdot 10^{-7} \cdot 20}{1.0 \cdot 10^{-2}}\,\mathrm{T} = 4.0 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{T}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The current density is not uniform, so the current enclosed by a circle of radius $r$ is computed by integrating $J$ over the disk. The disk is divided into annuli of radius $r'$ and thickness $dr'$, of area $2\pi r'\,dr'$, on which $J$ is constant; for double integrals in polar coordinates see the topic on double and triple integrals in Mathematical Analysis 2. Requiring the total current to be $I$ gives $J_0$.

The distribution has cylindrical symmetry, therefore the field is tangent to the coaxial circles and by **Ampère's law** $B\,2\pi r = \mu_0 I_{\text{enc}}$. Inside, the enclosed current grows as $r^3$, so the field grows as $r^2$, more slowly near the axis than in the uniform case, because the current is concentrated toward the surface. At half the radius the field is one quarter of the value at the surface. Outside, the field depends only on the total current and coincides with that of a thin wire, as in the uniform case.

</div>
