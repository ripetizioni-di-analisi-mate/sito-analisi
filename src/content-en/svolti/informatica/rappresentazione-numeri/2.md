## Exercise 1

Write the decimal integer $39$ in base two using the fewest possible digits.

$$
\begin{aligned}
39 &= 2\cdot 19+1 \\
19 &= 2\cdot 9+1 \\
9 &= 2\cdot 4+1 \\
4 &= 2\cdot 2+0 \\
2 &= 2\cdot 1+0 \\
1 &= 2\cdot 0+1 .
\end{aligned}
$$
$$
39=(100111)_2 .
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The first division gives a remainder of $1$. It occupies the binary units position, so it is the rightmost digit. The quotient $19$ gives the value of the remaining digits with their weights divided by $2$. The next division therefore extracts the digit of weight $2$, and each further division extracts the digit at the next weight. The process stops when the quotient reaches $0$.

Write the remainders starting with the last one, since it is the most significant bit. The first bit of the resulting word is $1$, so all six digits are needed in the shortest representation. Summing the weights of the four bits equal to $1$ gives $32+4+2+1=39$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Encode the decimal integer $190$ as a ten-bit unsigned binary word.

$$
\begin{aligned}
190 &= 2\cdot 95+0 \\
95 &= 2\cdot 47+1 \\
47 &= 2\cdot 23+1 \\
23 &= 2\cdot 11+1 \\
11 &= 2\cdot 5+1 \\
5 &= 2\cdot 2+1 \\
2 &= 2\cdot 1+0 \\
1 &= 2\cdot 0+1 .
\end{aligned}
$$
$$
(10111110)_2=(0010111110)_2=190 .
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Since $190$ is even, the first remainder is $0$ and the least significant bit is $0$. Reading the remainders from the bottom up gives the eight-bit word $10111110$. The problem specifies a ten-bit word, so two more positions are needed. Set the bits at indices $9$ and $8$, to the left of the digits already obtained, to zero. The existing bits keep their weights.

Check the unsigned value by summing the powers of two at the positions where the bit is set. The bits equal to $1$ in the ten-bit word have indices $7$, $5$, $4$, $3$, $2$ and $1$. Their weights add up to $128+32+16+8+4+2=190$, as required.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Write $1000$ in base two by repeated division. Then, from the same divisions and without computing new ones, find the minimal representations of $500$ and $2000$ and the minimum widths of the three integers.

$$
\begin{aligned}
1000 &= 2\cdot 500+0 \\
500 &= 2\cdot 250+0 \\
250 &= 2\cdot 125+0 \\
125 &= 2\cdot 62+1 \\
62 &= 2\cdot 31+0 \\
31 &= 2\cdot 15+1 \\
15 &= 2\cdot 7+1 \\
7 &= 2\cdot 3+1 \\
3 &= 2\cdot 1+1 \\
1 &= 2\cdot 0+1 .
\end{aligned}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
1000 &= (1111101000)_2 \\
500 &= (111110100)_2 \\
2000 &= (11111010000)_2 .
\end{aligned}
$$

The minimum widths are **ten bits** for $1000$, nine bits for $500$ and eleven bits for $2000$.

<div class="spiegazione">

Reading the remainders from the last to the first gives the representation of $1000$, which has ten digits. The first row shows that $500$ is the quotient of $1000$ divided by $2$. By the theorem on **repeated division**, the digits of the quotient are those of the dividend without the digit at index $0$, with every index decreased by one. Therefore the representation of $500$ is obtained by removing the final zero and has nine digits. Conversely, $2000=2\cdot1000+0$, so dividing $2000$ by $2$ gives remainder $0$ and quotient $1000$. Hence the representation of $2000$ is that of $1000$ followed by a zero and has eleven digits.

All three representations begin with $1$, so no digit is superfluous and each length equals the minimum width. As a check, the bits equal to $1$ in $1000$ have weights adding up to $512+256+128+64+32+8=1000$. In the representation of $2000$ the same bits occupy positions with twice the weight, so the sum doubles.

</div>
