## Exercise 1

Merge runs $(3,8,12,14)$ and $(1,9)$, counting key comparisons and all output writes.

| Heads compared | Value written |
|---|---:|
| 3 and 1 | 1 |
| 3 and 9 | 3 |
| 8 and 9 | 8 |
| 12 and 9 | 9 |

Copy the tail $(12,14)$. The result is $(1,3,8,9,12,14)$, with $4$ comparisons and $6$ writes.

<div class="spiegazione">

The first value written comes from the right run, so the second comparison still has $3$ on the left. The next comparisons select $3$ and $8$, both smaller than the right head $9$. When the left head becomes $12$, the algorithm selects $9$ and exhausts the right run. The left tail then completes the output without key comparisons.

Verify that each input element occurs once in the output and that all keys are nondecreasing. The six elements split into two copied from the tail and four produced by comparisons. This gives the comparison count a second way.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Merge $(1,A),(4,B),(5,C)$ and $(1,D),(4,E),(4,F)$. Give the tag order when ties choose the left run. Trace it again when ties choose the right run.

| Tie rule | Tag order |
|---|---|
| left run comes first | $A,D,B,E,F,C$ |
| right run comes first | $D,A,E,F,B,C$ |

<div class="spiegazione">

With the first rule, comparisons of $A$ with $D$ and of $B$ with $E$ give priority to the record that had the smaller index before splitting the array. Thus $A$ precedes $D$ and $B$ precedes the pair $E,F$. With the second rule, $D$ is written first. Once both heads have key $4$, the two right-run records are written first, retaining their internal order $E,F$.

As a check, the key groups are $1$ for $A,D$, $4$ for $B,E,F$, and $5$ for $C$. Both rules give the same nondecreasing key sequence. The internal order of the first two groups shows that only the first rule preserves the relative order of the original concatenation.

</div>
