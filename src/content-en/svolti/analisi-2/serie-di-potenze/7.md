## Exercise 1

Determine the Taylor series of $f(x) = e^x$ centered at $x_0 = 2$ and its region of validity.

$$
e^x = e^{2 + (x - 2)} = e^2 \, e^{x - 2}
$$
$$
e^t = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{t^n}{n!} \quad (t \in \mathbb{R}),
\qquad t = x - 2:
\qquad
e^x = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{e^2 \, (x - 2)^n}{n!}
\qquad (x \in \mathbb{R})
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

For an expansion centered at $x_0 = 2$ the series must be written in powers of $x - 2$, so the quantity $x - 2$ is introduced in the exponent by writing $x = 2 + (x - 2)$. The property of the exponential of a sum separates the constant factor $e^2$, and for the exponential of $x - 2$ the **standard expansion** with $t = x - 2$ is used. The starting expansion holds for every real $t$, so the one obtained also holds for every $x$. By the **uniqueness of the expansion** the coefficients found are $\dfrac{f^{(n)}(2)}{n!}$, in agreement with the fact that *all* the derivatives of $e^x$ at $2$ equal $e^2$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Determine the Taylor series of $f(x) = \dfrac{1}{x + 2}$ centered at $x_0 = 1$ and its region of validity.

$$
\frac{1}{x + 2}
= \frac{1}{3 + (x - 1)}
= \frac{1}{3} \cdot \frac{1}{1 + \dfrac{x - 1}{3}}
$$
$$
\frac{1}{1 + t} = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} (-1)^n \, t^n \quad (|t| < 1),
\qquad t = \frac{x - 1}{3}:
$$
$$
\frac{1}{x + 2}
= \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^n \, (x - 1)^n}{3^{n+1}}
\qquad (|x - 1| < 3, \ \text{that is, } -2 < x < 4)
$$

::grafico{f="1/(x+2);(1-(-(x-1)/3)^4)/(x+2);(1-(-(x-1)/3)^10)/(x+2)" legenda=0 xmin=-3 xmax=5.5 ymin=-2 ymax=3 asintoti="x=-2" segmenti="4,-2,4,3" punti="1,0.3333,pieno" titolo="$y = \dfrac{1}{x + 2}$ with the partial sums up to ${(x - 1)^3}$ (dashed) and up to ${(x - 1)^9}$ (red): they approximate the function only between $x = -2$ and $x = 4$, that is, at distance less than $3$ from the center $1$"}

<div class="spiegazione">

The denominator is written in terms of $x - 1$: since $x + 2 = (x - 1) + 3$, the $3$ is factored out and the form $\dfrac{1}{1 + t}$ with $t = \dfrac{x - 1}{3}$ appears, which is the **geometric series** with ratio $-t$. The condition $|t| < 1$ becomes $|x - 1| < 3$: the **radius of convergence** is $3$ and the interval is centered at $1$. The radius has a geometric meaning that can be read off the function, since the distance between the center $x_0 = 1$ and the point $x = -2$ at which the function is not defined equals *exactly* $3$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Determine the Taylor series of $f(x) = \ln x$ centered at $x_0 = 3$ and its region of validity.

$$
\ln x = \ln\!\bigl( 3 + (x - 3) \bigr)
= \ln 3 + \ln\!\left( 1 + \frac{x - 3}{3} \right)
$$
$$
\ln(1 + t) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^{n+1} \, t^n}{n} \quad (-1 < t \leq 1),
\qquad t = \frac{x - 3}{3}:
$$
$$
\ln x
= \ln 3 + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^{n+1} \, (x - 3)^n}{n \, 3^n}
\qquad (0 < x \leq 6)
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The argument of the logarithm is written as $3 + (x - 3)$, the $3$ is factored out and the **logarithm of the product** is split: the constant $\ln 3$ is the degree-zero term. For the remaining logarithm the standard expansion with $t = \dfrac{x - 3}{3}$ is used, valid for $-1 < t \leq 1$. The condition on $x$ is derived from the double inequality: $-1 < \dfrac{x - 3}{3} \leq 1$ is equivalent to $-3 < x - 3 \leq 3$, that is, $0 < x \leq 6$. The radius is $R = 3$; the right endpoint $x = 6$ is included because it corresponds to $t = 1$, a point at which the expansion of the logarithm holds by **Abel's theorem**, while at $x = 0$ the series is the opposite of the harmonic series, which **diverges**, in agreement with the fact that $\ln x$ is *not* defined at $0$.

</div>
