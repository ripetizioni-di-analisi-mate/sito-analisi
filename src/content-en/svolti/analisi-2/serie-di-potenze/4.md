## Exercise 1

Determine the set of convergence and the sum of the series
$$
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{x^{2n}}{9^n} .
$$

$$
t = x^2: \qquad
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{x^{2n}}{9^n} = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \left( \frac{t}{9} \right)^{\! n}
$$
$$
\left| \frac{t}{9} \right| < 1
\iff x^2 < 9
\iff |x| < 3
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad R = 3, \quad E = (-3,\, 3)
$$
$$
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{x^{2n}}{9^n}
= \frac{1}{1 - \dfrac{x^2}{9}}
= \frac{9}{9 - x^2}
\qquad (|x| < 3)
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The series contains only even powers, so it is **lacunary** and the ratio test on the coefficients does not apply directly. With the substitution $t = x^2$ the series becomes a geometric series with ratio $\dfrac{t}{9}$, which converges *if and only if* the absolute value of the ratio is less than $1$, that is, $x^2 < 9$, that is, $|x| < 3$. The radius is therefore $R = 3$, and *not* $9$ as the denominator alone would suggest. At the endpoints $x = \pm 3$ the ratio equals $1$ and the general term does not tend to $0$, so the series **does not converge**. The sum is obtained from the formula for the **geometric series** with first term $1$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Determine the set of convergence and the sum of the series
$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{x^{2n+1}}{n \, 4^n} .
$$

$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{x^{2n+1}}{n \, 4^n}
= x \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{n} \left( \frac{x^2}{4} \right)^{\! n},
\qquad t = \frac{x^2}{4} \geq 0
$$
$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{t^n}{n} \ \text{ converges} \iff -1 \leq t < 1
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
\frac{x^2}{4} < 1 \iff |x| < 2
$$
$$
R = 2, \qquad E = (-2,\, 2)
$$
$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{t^n}{n} = -\ln(1 - t)
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{x^{2n+1}}{n \, 4^n}
= -x \ln\!\left( 1 - \frac{x^2}{4} \right)
\qquad (|x| < 2)
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The series contains only odd powers. A factor $x$ is factored out, so the remaining exponents are all even and the substitution $t = \dfrac{x^2}{4}$ turns the series into $\sum \dfrac{t^n}{n}$. This series converges for $-1 \leq t < 1$: for $|t| < 1$ the radius is $1$, at $t = -1$ it converges by the **Leibniz test** and at $t = 1$ it is the harmonic series, which diverges. In our case $t$ is a square divided by $4$, so it is always greater than or equal to $0$ and the case $t = -1$ *never* occurs: the condition becomes $t < 1$, that is, $|x| < 2$. At $x = \pm 2$ we have $t = 1$ and the series **diverges**. For the sum we use the equality $\sum\limits_{n \geq 1} \dfrac{t^n}{n} = -\ln(1 - t)$, which is the expansion of $\ln(1+x)$ evaluated at $-t$ with the sign changed, and multiply by the factor $x$ factored out at the beginning.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Determine the set of convergence and the sum of the series
$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{n \, x^{3n}}{8^n} .
$$

$$
t = \frac{x^3}{8}: \qquad
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{n \, x^{3n}}{8^n} = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} n \, t^n
$$
$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} n \, t^n \ \text{ converges} \iff |t| < 1
\iff |x|^3 < 8 \iff |x| < 2
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad R = 2, \quad E = (-2,\, 2)
$$
$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} n \, t^n = \frac{t}{(1 - t)^2}
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{n \, x^{3n}}{8^n}
= \frac{\dfrac{x^3}{8}}{\left( 1 - \dfrac{x^3}{8} \right)^{\! 2}}
= \frac{8 \, x^3}{(8 - x^3)^2}
\qquad (|x| < 2)
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Only the powers with exponent a multiple of $3$ appear, so the suitable substitution is $t = \dfrac{x^3}{8}$; unlike the square, the cube preserves the sign of $x$, therefore $t$ can be negative and $|t| < 1$ must be required. The series $\sum n \, t^n$ has radius $1$ by the **ratio test** and at the points $t = \pm 1$ the general term has absolute value $n$, which does not tend to $0$, so convergence holds *exactly* for $|t| < 1$. The condition $\left| \dfrac{x^3}{8} \right| < 1$ is equivalent to $|x| < 2$, and at the endpoints $x = \pm 2$ we have $t = \pm 1$, where the series **does not converge**. The sum follows from the equality $\sum n \, t^n = \dfrac{t}{(1-t)^2}$, obtained by **differentiating term by term** the geometric series; the last step multiplies numerator and denominator by $64$.

</div>
