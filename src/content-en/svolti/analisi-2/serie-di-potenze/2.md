## Exercise 1

Determine the radius and the set of convergence of the series
$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \left( \frac{2n+1}{3n+1} \right)^{\! n} x^n .
$$

$$
a_n = \left( \frac{2n+1}{3n+1} \right)^{\! n}, \qquad
\sqrt[n]{|a_n|} = \frac{2n+1}{3n+1} \xrightarrow[n \to \infty]{} \frac{2}{3}
$$
$$
R = \frac{3}{2}
$$
$$
x = \pm \frac{3}{2}: \quad
|a_n| \left( \frac{3}{2} \right)^{\! n}
= \left( \frac{6n+3}{6n+2} \right)^{\! n}
= \left( 1 + \frac{1}{6n+2} \right)^{\! n}
\xrightarrow[n \to \infty]{} e^{\frac{1}{6}} \neq 0
$$
$$
E = \left( -\frac{3}{2},\; \frac{3}{2} \right)
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The coefficient $a_n$ is an $n$-th power, so the **root test** is the natural choice: the $n$-th root removes the exponent and leaves the ratio $\dfrac{2n+1}{3n+1}$, which tends to $\dfrac{2}{3}$. The radius is the reciprocal, that is, $R = \dfrac{3}{2}$. At the endpoints the absolute value of the general term is $\left( 1 + \dfrac{1}{6n+2} \right)^{\! n}$; since $n \ln\!\left( 1 + \dfrac{1}{6n+2} \right) \to \dfrac{1}{6}$, this absolute value tends to $e^{\frac{1}{6}}$, which is different from $0$. The **necessary condition for convergence** is not satisfied at *either* of the two endpoints, so the set of convergence is the **open interval**.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Determine the radius and the set of convergence of the series
$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{x^n}{\bigl( \ln(n+1) \bigr)^n} .
$$

$$
a_n = \frac{1}{\bigl( \ln(n+1) \bigr)^n}, \qquad
\sqrt[n]{|a_n|} = \frac{1}{\ln(n+1)} \xrightarrow[n \to \infty]{} 0
$$
$$
R = +\infty, \qquad E = \mathbb{R}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The denominator is the $n$-th power of $\ln(n+1)$, so the $n$-th root of the absolute value of the coefficient is $\dfrac{1}{\ln(n+1)}$, which tends to $0$ because the logarithm tends to $+\infty$. By the convention of the root test, a zero limit means **infinite radius**: the series converges absolutely for *every* real $x$ and there are no endpoints to study.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Determine the radius and the set of convergence of the series
$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \left( 1 + \frac{1}{n} \right)^{\! n^2} x^n .
$$

$$
a_n = \left( 1 + \frac{1}{n} \right)^{\! n^2}, \qquad
\sqrt[n]{|a_n|} = \left( 1 + \frac{1}{n} \right)^{\! n} \xrightarrow[n \to \infty]{} e
$$
$$
R = \frac{1}{e}
$$
$$
x = \pm \frac{1}{e}: \quad
|a_n| \, e^{-n} = \exp\!\left( n^2 \ln\!\left( 1 + \frac{1}{n} \right) - n \right)
$$
$$
n^2 \ln\!\left( 1 + \frac{1}{n} \right) - n
= n^2 \left( \frac{1}{n} - \frac{1}{2n^2} + o\!\left( \frac{1}{n^2} \right) \right) - n
= -\frac{1}{2} + o(1)
$$
$$
|a_n| \, e^{-n} \xrightarrow[n \to \infty]{} e^{-\frac{1}{2}} \neq 0
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
E = \left( -\frac{1}{e},\; \frac{1}{e} \right)
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The $n$-th root of $\left( 1 + \dfrac{1}{n} \right)^{\! n^2}$ is $\left( 1 + \dfrac{1}{n} \right)^{\! n}$, which tends to $e$ by the **standard limit**, so the radius is $R = \dfrac{1}{e}$. At the endpoints the absolute value of the general term is $|a_n| \, e^{-n}$, which is conveniently written as the exponential of $n^2 \ln\!\left( 1 + \dfrac{1}{n} \right) - n$. Substituting the expansion $\ln(1 + t) = t - \dfrac{t^2}{2} + o(t^2)$ with $t = \dfrac{1}{n}$, the terms in $n$ cancel and the exponent tends to $-\dfrac{1}{2}$; hence the absolute value of the general term tends to $e^{-\frac{1}{2}}$, which is different from $0$. The **necessary condition for convergence** is not satisfied at the endpoints, therefore the set of convergence is the **open interval**.

</div>
