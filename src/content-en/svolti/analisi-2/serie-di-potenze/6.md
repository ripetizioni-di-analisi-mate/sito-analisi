## Exercise 1

Determine the Maclaurin expansion of $f(x) = \dfrac{1}{(2 - x)^2}$ and its region of validity.

$$
\frac{1}{(2 - x)^2} = \frac{d}{dx} \left( \frac{1}{2 - x} \right)
$$
$$
\frac{1}{2 - x} = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{x^n}{2^{n+1}}
\qquad (|x| < 2)
$$
$$
\frac{1}{(2 - x)^2}
= \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{n \, x^{n-1}}{2^{n+1}}
= \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{(n+1) \, x^{n}}{2^{n+2}}
\qquad (|x| < 2)
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The function is the derivative of $\dfrac{1}{2 - x}$, which is expanded with the geometric series by factoring out the $2$ in the denominator, as in the sheet on expansions by substitution. By the **term-by-term differentiation theorem**, the expansion of $f$ is obtained by differentiating each summand: the power $x^n$ becomes $n \, x^{n-1}$ and the constant term disappears, so the sum starts from $n = 1$. The radius of the differentiated series is the *same* as that of the starting series, that is, $2$. In the last equality the index is shifted with $n \to n + 1$ to write the series in the powers $x^n$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Determine the Maclaurin expansion of $f(x) = \arctan(3x)$ and its region of validity.

$$
\arctan(3x) = \int_0^x \frac{3 \, dt}{1 + 9t^2}
$$
$$
\frac{3}{1 + 9t^2}
= 3 \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} (-1)^n \, 9^n \, t^{2n}
\qquad \left( 9t^2 < 1, \ \text{that is, } |t| < \frac{1}{3} \right)
$$
$$
\arctan(3x)
= 3 \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^n \, 9^n \, x^{2n+1}}{2n+1}
= \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^n \, 3^{2n+1} \, x^{2n+1}}{2n+1}
\qquad \left( |x| \leq \frac{1}{3} \right)
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The arctangent is the integral function of its own derivative, so the derivative is expanded first and then **integrated term by term**. The derivative is $\dfrac{3}{1 + 9t^2}$, which is a geometric series with ratio $-9t^2$, convergent for $|t| < \dfrac{1}{3}$. Integrating from $0$ to $x$, each power $t^{2n}$ produces $\dfrac{x^{2n+1}}{2n+1}$, and the radius remains $\dfrac{1}{3}$. At the endpoint $x = \dfrac{1}{3}$ the powers $3^{2n+1}$ and $\left( \dfrac{1}{3} \right)^{2n+1}$ cancel and the series $\sum \dfrac{(-1)^n}{2n+1}$ remains, which converges by the **Leibniz test**, since $\dfrac{1}{2n+1}$ is decreasing and infinitesimal; at $x = -\dfrac{1}{3}$ the same series with the opposite sign is obtained. By **Abel's theorem** the sum at the endpoints coincides with $\arctan(\pm 1)$. The validity therefore extends to the closed interval $\left[ -\dfrac{1}{3}, \dfrac{1}{3} \right]$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Determine the Maclaurin expansion of the integral function
$$
F(x) = \int_0^x \frac{e^t - 1}{t} \, dt .
$$

$$
e^t - 1 = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{t^n}{n!}
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
\frac{e^t - 1}{t} = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{t^{n-1}}{n!}
\qquad (t \in \mathbb{R})
$$
$$
F(x)
= \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{n!} \int_0^x t^{n-1} \, dt
= \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{x^n}{n \cdot n!}
\qquad (x \in \mathbb{R})
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The antiderivative of $\dfrac{e^t - 1}{t}$ *cannot* be expressed in terms of elementary functions, so the series expansion is the tool for describing $F$. The degree-zero term is subtracted from the expansion of the exponential, so the series starts from $n = 1$ and every term contains *at least one* factor $t$: division by $t$ is then legitimate and produces the series of the powers $t^{n-1}$, which equals $1$ at $t = 0$, consistent with the limit of the integrand. **Term-by-term integration** from $0$ to $x$ turns $t^{n-1}$ into $\dfrac{x^n}{n}$, which gives the coefficient $\dfrac{1}{n \cdot n!}$. The radius of the starting series is infinite and integration preserves it, so the expansion holds for every real $x$.

</div>
