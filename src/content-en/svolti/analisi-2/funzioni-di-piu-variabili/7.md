## Exercise 1

Determine whether the function
$$
f(x, y) = \frac{\sin(x^2 + y^2)}{x^2 + y^2},
\qquad (x, y) \neq (0, 0),
$$
can be extended continuously to the origin.

$$
x^2 + y^2 = \rho^2, \qquad t = \rho^2 \to 0^+
$$
$$
\frac{\sin(x^2 + y^2)}{x^2 + y^2} = \frac{\sin t}{t} \xrightarrow[t \to 0]{} 1
$$
$$
\tilde{f}(x, y) =
\begin{cases}
\dfrac{\sin(x^2 + y^2)}{x^2 + y^2} & (x, y) \neq (0, 0) \\[8pt]
1 & (x, y) = (0, 0)
\end{cases}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The function is defined on $\mathbb{R}^2 \setminus \{(0,0)\}$ and the origin is an accumulation point of the domain, so the question is equivalent to **computing the limit** at the origin. The function depends on $x$ and $y$ only through $x^2 + y^2$, which in polar coordinates becomes $\rho^2$; with the substitution $t = \rho^2$, which tends to $0^+$, the expression becomes $\dfrac{\sin t}{t}$, and by the **standard limit of the sine** this ratio tends to $1$. The expression does not contain $\theta$, so the convergence is uniform in all directions. The limit exists, is finite and equals $1$, therefore the function **extends continuously** by assigning the value $1$ at the origin.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Determine whether the function
$$
f(x, y) = \frac{x^3 + x y^2}{x^2 + y^2},
\qquad (x, y) \neq (0, 0),
$$
can be extended continuously to the origin.

$$
\frac{x^3 + x y^2}{x^2 + y^2}
= \frac{x \left( x^2 + y^2 \right)}{x^2 + y^2}
= x
$$
$$
\lim_{(x, y) \to (0, 0)} f(x, y) = \lim_{(x, y) \to (0, 0)} x = 0
$$
$$
\tilde{f}(0, 0) = 0
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Before resorting to upper bounds it is convenient to look at the structure of the numerator: factoring out $x$ gives $x(x^2 + y^2)$, so the factor $x^2 + y^2$ **cancels** with the denominator, which is different from zero at all points of the domain. Away from the origin the function therefore coincides with $f(x, y) = x$, which is continuous and tends to $0$ as $(x, y) \to (0, 0)$. The limit exists, is finite and equals $0$, so the function **extends continuously** by assigning the value $0$ at the origin; the extension coincides with the function $\tilde{f}(x, y) = x$ on all of $\mathbb{R}^2$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Determine whether the function
$$
f(x, y) = \frac{2xy}{x^2 + 3y^2},
\qquad (x, y) \neq (0, 0),
$$
can be extended continuously to the origin.

$$
y = mx: \qquad f(x, mx) = \frac{2m x^2}{x^2 + 3m^2 x^2} = \frac{2m}{1 + 3m^2}
$$
$$
m = 0: \ 0, \qquad m = 1: \ \frac{2}{4} = \frac{1}{2}, \qquad \frac{1}{2} \neq 0
$$
$$
\text{the limit does not exist} \quad \Longrightarrow \quad f \ \text{cannot be extended continuously}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

A continuous extension requires the limit at the origin to *exist and be finite*, so one begins with restrictions. Along the line $y = mx$ the function is constantly $\dfrac{2m}{1 + 3m^2}$: the value depends on the slope of the line, since for $m = 0$ one obtains $0$ and for $m = 1$ one obtains $\dfrac{1}{2}$. Since two restrictions produce different limits, by the **restriction theorem** the limit at the origin **does not exist**. Without a finite limit no value assigned at the origin makes the function continuous, therefore a continuous extension **is not possible**.

</div>
