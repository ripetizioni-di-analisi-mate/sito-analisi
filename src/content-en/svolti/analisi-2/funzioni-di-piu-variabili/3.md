## Exercise 1

Determine whether the limit
$$
\lim_{(x, y) \to (0, 0)} \frac{x^2 - y^2}{x^2 + y^2} .
$$
exists.

$$
y = 0: \qquad f(x, 0) = \frac{x^2}{x^2} = 1 \xrightarrow[x \to 0]{} 1
$$
$$
x = 0: \qquad f(0, y) = \frac{-y^2}{y^2} = -1 \xrightarrow[y \to 0]{} -1
$$
$$
1 \neq -1 \quad \Longrightarrow \quad \text{the limit does not exist}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

One computes the restrictions along the two axes. On the $x$-axis one sets $y = 0$: the function is constantly $1$, so the restriction tends to $1$. On the $y$-axis one sets $x = 0$: the function is constantly $-1$, so the restriction tends to $-1$. By the **restriction theorem**, if the limit existed *every* restriction would have to tend to the same value; since two restrictions produce different values, the limit **does not exist**.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Determine whether the limit
$$
\lim_{(x, y) \to (0, 0)} \frac{xy}{x^2 + y^2} .
$$
exists.

$$
y = 0: \qquad f(x, 0) = 0 \xrightarrow[x \to 0]{} 0
$$
$$
y = mx: \qquad f(x, mx) = \frac{m x^2}{x^2 + m^2 x^2} = \frac{m}{1 + m^2}
$$
$$
m = 1: \ \frac{1}{2}, \qquad m = 0: \ 0, \qquad \frac{1}{2} \neq 0
\quad \Longrightarrow \quad \text{the limit does not exist}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Along the axes the function is identically zero, because the numerator $xy$ vanishes when one of the two coordinates is zero; the axes alone are therefore *not* enough to reach a conclusion. One then restricts the function to the **pencil of lines** $y = mx$: substituting gives $\dfrac{m x^2}{(1 + m^2) x^2} = \dfrac{m}{1 + m^2}$, a value that is constant along each line but **depends on the slope** $m$. For $m = 0$ the restriction tends to $0$, while for $m = 1$ it tends to $\dfrac{1}{2}$. Since two restrictions produce different limits, the limit **does not exist**.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Determine whether the limit
$$
\lim_{(x, y) \to (0, 0)} \frac{x y^2}{x^2 + y^4} .
$$
exists.

$$
y = mx: \qquad f(x, mx) = \frac{m^2 x^3}{x^2 + m^4 x^4} = \frac{m^2 x}{1 + m^4 x^2} \xrightarrow[x \to 0]{} 0
$$
$$
x = 0: \qquad f(0, y) = 0 \xrightarrow[y \to 0]{} 0
$$
$$
x = y^2: \qquad f(y^2, y) = \frac{y^2 \cdot y^2}{y^4 + y^4} = \frac{1}{2}
$$
$$
\frac{1}{2} \neq 0 \quad \Longrightarrow \quad \text{the limit does not exist}
$$

::grafico{curve="t^2,t,-1.5,1.5" segmenti="-1.5,-1.5,1.5,1.5;-1.5,1.5,1.5,-1.5;-1.5,-0.75,2.3,1.15;-1.5,0.75,2.3,-1.15" punti="0,0,vuoto" etichette="0.5,1.3,x = y²" xmin=-1.5 xmax=2.3 ymin=-1.5 ymax=1.5 uguale=1 titolo="Restrictions at the origin: along the lines $y = mx$ (dashed) $f$ tends to $0$, along the parabola $x = y^2$ it is constantly $\dfrac{1}{2}$"}

<div class="spiegazione">

Along every line $y = mx$ the restriction equals $\dfrac{m^2 x}{1 + m^4 x^2}$, which tends to $0$ as $x \to 0$ whatever $m$ is; the restriction to the $y$-axis is also zero. All lines through the origin therefore produce the same value $0$, but this does *not* prove the existence of the limit, because the restriction theorem provides only *necessary* conditions. The denominator contains $y^4$, so one tries the **parabola** $x = y^2$, which balances the degrees: substituting gives the constant value $\dfrac{1}{2}$. Since the restriction to the parabola tends to $\dfrac{1}{2}$ and the restrictions to the lines tend to $0$, the limit **does not exist**.

</div>
