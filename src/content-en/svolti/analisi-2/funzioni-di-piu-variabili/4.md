## Exercise 1

Compute the limit
$$
\lim_{(x, y) \to (0, 0)} \frac{x^2 y}{x^2 + y^2} .
$$

$$
x = \rho \cos\theta, \qquad y = \rho \sin\theta
$$
$$
\frac{x^2 y}{x^2 + y^2}
= \frac{\rho^3 \cos^2\theta \sin\theta}{\rho^2}
= \rho \cos^2\theta \sin\theta
$$
$$
\left| \rho \cos^2\theta \sin\theta \right| \leq \rho = g(\rho) \xrightarrow[\rho \to 0^+]{} 0
$$
$$
\lim_{(x, y) \to (0, 0)} \frac{x^2 y}{x^2 + y^2} = 0
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

One switches to **polar coordinates** centered at the origin. The numerator $x^2 y$ becomes $\rho^3 \cos^2\theta \sin\theta$ and the denominator $x^2 + y^2$ becomes $\rho^2$, since $\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta = 1$; after simplification $\rho \cos^2\theta \sin\theta$ remains. The factors $\cos^2\theta$ and $|\sin\theta|$ are bounded above by $1$, so the absolute value of the expression is bounded above by $g(\rho) = \rho$, which does *not* depend on $\theta$ and tends to $0$ as $\rho \to 0^+$. Since the bound is **uniform** in $\theta$, the limit **exists and equals** $0$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Compute the limit
$$
\lim_{(x, y) \to (0, 0)} \frac{x^3 + y^3}{x^2 + y^2} .
$$

$$
\frac{x^3 + y^3}{x^2 + y^2}
= \frac{\rho^3 (\cos^3\theta + \sin^3\theta)}{\rho^2}
= \rho \left( \cos^3\theta + \sin^3\theta \right)
$$
$$
\left| \rho \left( \cos^3\theta + \sin^3\theta \right) \right|
\leq \rho \left( |\cos\theta|^3 + |\sin\theta|^3 \right)
\leq 2\rho \xrightarrow[\rho \to 0^+]{} 0
$$
$$
\lim_{(x, y) \to (0, 0)} \frac{x^3 + y^3}{x^2 + y^2} = 0
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

In polar coordinates the numerator becomes $\rho^3(\cos^3\theta + \sin^3\theta)$ and the denominator becomes $\rho^2$, so the expression reduces to $\rho(\cos^3\theta + \sin^3\theta)$. By the **triangle inequality** the absolute value of the sum is bounded above by the sum of the absolute values, and each of the two terms $|\cos\theta|^3$ and $|\sin\theta|^3$ is bounded above by $1$; therefore the whole expression is bounded above by $g(\rho) = 2\rho$. Since $2\rho \to 0$ as $\rho \to 0^+$ and the bound does *not* depend on $\theta$, the limit **equals** $0$. The numerator has degree $3$ and the denominator degree $2$: when the degree of the numerator exceeds that of the denominator a **positive power** of $\rho$ remains, and the limit is $0$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Compute the limit
$$
\lim_{(x, y) \to (0, 0)} \frac{1 - \cos(x^2 + y^2)}{(x^2 + y^2)^2} .
$$

$$
x^2 + y^2 = \rho^2
$$
$$
\frac{1 - \cos(x^2 + y^2)}{(x^2 + y^2)^2} = \frac{1 - \cos \rho^2}{\rho^4}
$$
$$
t = \rho^2 \to 0^+: \qquad \frac{1 - \cos t}{t^2} \xrightarrow[t \to 0]{} \frac{1}{2}
$$
$$
\lim_{(x, y) \to (0, 0)} \frac{1 - \cos(x^2 + y^2)}{(x^2 + y^2)^2} = \frac{1}{2}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The function depends on $x$ and $y$ *only* through the quantity $x^2 + y^2$, which in polar coordinates becomes $\rho^2$. The expression therefore reduces to $\dfrac{1 - \cos \rho^2}{\rho^4}$, which does not contain $\theta$: the distance from a possible limit is automatically a function of $\rho$ alone, and the convergence is **uniform** in all directions. With the substitution $t = \rho^2$, which tends to $0^+$ as $\rho \to 0^+$, the expression becomes $\dfrac{1 - \cos t}{t^2}$, and by the **standard limit of the cosine** this ratio tends to $\dfrac{1}{2}$. Therefore the limit equals $\dfrac{1}{2}$.

</div>
