## Exercise 1

Study the continuity on $\mathbb{R}^2$ of the function
$$
f(x, y) =
\begin{cases}
\dfrac{x^3}{x^2 + y^2} & (x, y) \neq (0, 0) \\[8pt]
0 & (x, y) = (0, 0)
\end{cases}
$$

$$
\left| \frac{x^3}{x^2 + y^2} \right|
= \frac{x^2}{x^2 + y^2} \cdot |x|
\leq |x| \xrightarrow[(x, y) \to (0, 0)]{} 0
$$
$$
\lim_{(x, y) \to (0, 0)} f(x, y) = 0 = f(0, 0)
$$
$$
f \ \text{is continuous on } \mathbb{R}^2
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Away from the origin the function is a quotient of polynomials with a denominator that never vanishes, so it is continuous by the theorems on elementary functions. It remains to study the origin, where continuity is *equivalent* to the condition that the limit of $f$ exists and coincides with $f(0, 0) = 0$. The absolute value of the function is written as the product of the ratio $\dfrac{x^2}{x^2 + y^2}$, bounded above by $1$, and the factor $|x|$, which tends to $0$; by the **comparison theorem** the limit equals $0$. Since the limit coincides with the value assigned at the origin, the function is **continuous** on all of $\mathbb{R}^2$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Study the continuity on $\mathbb{R}^2$ of the function
$$
f(x, y) =
\begin{cases}
\dfrac{x y^3}{x^4 + y^4} & (x, y) \neq (0, 0) \\[8pt]
0 & (x, y) = (0, 0)
\end{cases}
$$

$$
y = x: \qquad f(x, x) = \frac{x^4}{2x^4} = \frac{1}{2} \xrightarrow[x \to 0]{} \frac{1}{2}
$$
$$
\frac{1}{2} \neq f(0, 0) = 0
$$
$$
f \ \text{is continuous on } \mathbb{R}^2 \setminus \{(0, 0)\} \ \text{and is not continuous at } (0, 0)
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Away from the origin the denominator $x^4 + y^4$ does not vanish, so the function is continuous because it is a quotient of polynomials. At the origin one tries the **restriction** to the bisector $y = x$: substituting gives the constant value $\dfrac{1}{2}$, so the restriction tends to $\dfrac{1}{2}$. If the function were continuous at the origin, by the **restriction theorem** *every* restriction would have to tend to $f(0, 0) = 0$; since the restriction to the bisector tends to $\dfrac{1}{2} \neq 0$, the function **is not continuous** at the origin. In fact the limit at the origin does not exist, since the restriction to the $x$-axis is identically zero and tends to $0 \neq \dfrac{1}{2}$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Study the continuity on $\mathbb{R}^2$ of the function
$$
f(x, y) =
\begin{cases}
(x^2 + y^2) \sin\!\left( \dfrac{1}{x^2 + y^2} \right) & (x, y) \neq (0, 0) \\[8pt]
0 & (x, y) = (0, 0)
\end{cases}
$$

$$
\left| (x^2 + y^2) \sin\!\left( \frac{1}{x^2 + y^2} \right) \right|
\leq x^2 + y^2 \xrightarrow[(x, y) \to (0, 0)]{} 0
$$
$$
\lim_{(x, y) \to (0, 0)} f(x, y) = 0 = f(0, 0)
$$
$$
f \ \text{is continuous on } \mathbb{R}^2
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Away from the origin the function is a composition and product of continuous functions with a well-defined argument of the sine, so it is continuous. At the origin the factor $\sin\!\left(\dfrac{1}{x^2 + y^2}\right)$ has *no* limit, but it is **bounded** in absolute value by $1$; the factor $x^2 + y^2$ tends to $0$. The product of a bounded function and an infinitesimal one is **infinitesimal**, so the limit of $f$ at the origin equals $0$ and coincides with $f(0, 0)$. Therefore the function is **continuous** on all of $\mathbb{R}^2$.

</div>
