## Exercise 1

Compute the double integral
$$
\iint_R (x + 3y^2)\, dx\, dy ,
\qquad R = [0, 1] \times [0, 2] .
$$

$$
\iint_R (x + 3y^2)\, dx\, dy
= \int_0^1 \left( \int_0^2 (x + 3y^2)\, dy \right) dx
$$
$$
\int_0^2 (x + 3y^2)\, dy
= \Bigl[ x y + y^3 \Bigr]_{y=0}^{y=2}
= 2x + 8
$$
$$
\int_0^1 (2x + 8)\, dx
= \Bigl[ x^2 + 8x \Bigr]_0^1
= 1 + 8 = 9
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The domain is a rectangle and the function is continuous, so **Fubini's theorem** applies and the double integral reduces to two successive single integrals. In the inner integral the variable $x$ is fixed and we integrate with respect to $y$: the antiderivative of $x + 3y^2$ with respect to $y$ is $xy + y^3$, therefore the inner integral equals $2x + 8$. The result is a function of $x$ *alone*, which is integrated over $[0, 1]$ and gives $9$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Compute the double integral
$$
\iint_R x\, e^{xy}\, dx\, dy ,
\qquad R = [0, 1] \times [0, 1] .
$$

$$
\iint_R x\, e^{xy}\, dx\, dy
= \int_0^1 \left( \int_0^1 x\, e^{xy}\, dy \right) dx
$$
$$
\int_0^1 x\, e^{xy}\, dy
= \Bigl[ e^{xy} \Bigr]_{y=0}^{y=1}
= e^{x} - 1
$$
$$
\int_0^1 (e^{x} - 1)\, dx
= \Bigl[ e^{x} - x \Bigr]_0^1
= (e - 1) - 1 = e - 2
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The two orders of integration give the **same value**, therefore we choose the one with the simpler computations. Integrating first with respect to $y$, the factor $x$ is a constant and is *exactly* the derivative of the exponent $xy$ with respect to $y$, so the antiderivative of $x\, e^{xy}$ is $e^{xy}$ and the inner integral equals $e^x - 1$. Integrating first with respect to $x$ would instead require **integration by parts**, since $x\, e^{xy}$, as a function of $x$, is the product of a polynomial and an exponential. With the choice made, what remains is the integral of $e^x - 1$ over $[0, 1]$, which equals $e - 2$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Compute the double integral
$$
\iint_R \frac{1}{(x + y)^2}\, dx\, dy ,
\qquad R = [0, 1] \times [1, 2] .
$$

$$
\iint_R \frac{1}{(x + y)^2}\, dx\, dy
= \int_0^1 \left( \int_1^2 \frac{1}{(x + y)^2}\, dy \right) dx
$$
$$
\int_1^2 \frac{dy}{(x + y)^2}
= \left[ -\frac{1}{x + y} \right]_{y=1}^{y=2}
= \frac{1}{x + 1} - \frac{1}{x + 2}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_0^1 \left( \frac{1}{x + 1} - \frac{1}{x + 2} \right) dx
&= \Bigl[ \ln(x + 1) - \ln(x + 2) \Bigr]_0^1 \\[6pt]
&= (\ln 2 - \ln 3) - (\ln 1 - \ln 2) \\[6pt]
&= 2\ln 2 - \ln 3 = \ln \frac{4}{3}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

On $R$ the sum $x + y$ is *always* greater than or equal to $1$, so the function is **continuous** on the rectangle and **Fubini's theorem** applies. In the inner integral $x$ is a constant, therefore the antiderivative of $\dfrac{1}{(x + y)^2}$ with respect to $y$ is $-\dfrac{1}{x + y}$. Evaluating at the limits gives the difference $\dfrac{1}{x + 1} - \dfrac{1}{x + 2}$, which is integrated with logarithms. Collecting the terms we obtain $2\ln 2 - \ln 3$, which by the **properties of logarithms** is $\ln \dfrac{4}{3}$.

</div>
