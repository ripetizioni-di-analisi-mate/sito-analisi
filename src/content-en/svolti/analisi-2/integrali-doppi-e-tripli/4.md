## Exercise 1

Compute the double integral
$$
\iint_D (x^2 + y^2)\, dx\, dy ,
\qquad D = \{ (x, y) : x^2 + y^2 \leq 4 \} .
$$

$$
\begin{cases}
x = \rho \cos\theta \\
y = \rho \sin\theta
\end{cases}
\qquad 0 \leq \rho \leq 2, \quad 0 \leq \theta \leq 2\pi
$$
$$
\iint_D (x^2 + y^2)\, dx\, dy
= \int_0^{2\pi} \left( \int_0^2 \rho^2 \cdot \rho \, d\rho \right) d\theta
$$
$$
\int_0^{2\pi} d\theta \cdot \int_0^2 \rho^3 \, d\rho
= 2\pi \cdot \frac{16}{4} = 8\pi
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The domain is a disk centered at the origin and the function depends *only* on $x^2 + y^2$, so **polar coordinates** are the natural choice. In polar coordinates $x^2 + y^2 = \rho^2$, the disk of radius $2$ becomes the rectangle $0 \leq \rho \leq 2$, $0 \leq \theta \leq 2\pi$, and the area element carries the factor $\rho$ of the Jacobian. The integrand $\rho^3$ does not depend on $\theta$ and the limits are constant, therefore the double integral is the **product of the two single integrals**, that is, $2\pi \cdot 4 = 8\pi$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Compute the double integral
$$
\iint_D e^{-(x^2 + y^2)}\, dx\, dy ,
\qquad D = \{ (x, y) : 1 \leq x^2 + y^2 \leq 4 \} .
$$

$$
1 \leq \rho \leq 2, \qquad 0 \leq \theta \leq 2\pi
$$
$$
\iint_D e^{-(x^2 + y^2)}\, dx\, dy
= \int_0^{2\pi} \left( \int_1^2 e^{-\rho^2} \rho \, d\rho \right) d\theta
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
2\pi \int_1^2 \rho\, e^{-\rho^2} d\rho
&= 2\pi \left[ -\frac{e^{-\rho^2}}{2} \right]_1^2 \\[6pt]
&= \pi \left( e^{-1} - e^{-4} \right)
= \pi \left( \frac{1}{e} - \frac{1}{e^4} \right)
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The domain is the **annulus** with radii $1$ and $2$, which in polar coordinates is the rectangle $1 \leq \rho \leq 2$, $0 \leq \theta \leq 2\pi$. The function $e^{-x^2}$ does *not* have an elementary antiderivative, so in Cartesian coordinates the integral cannot be computed; in polar coordinates the factor $\rho$ of the Jacobian is, up to the constant $-2$, the **derivative of the exponent** $-\rho^2$, therefore the antiderivative of $\rho\, e^{-\rho^2}$ is $-\dfrac{e^{-\rho^2}}{2}$. The function does not depend on $\theta$, so the integral in $\theta$ gives the factor $2\pi$ and the result is $\pi \left( \dfrac{1}{e} - \dfrac{1}{e^4} \right)$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Compute the double integral
$$
\iint_D x \, dx\, dy ,
\qquad D = \{ (x, y) : x^2 + y^2 \leq 2x \} .
$$

$$
x^2 + y^2 \leq 2x
\iff (x - 1)^2 + y^2 \leq 1
$$
$$
\rho^2 \leq 2 \rho \cos\theta
\iff \rho \leq 2\cos\theta ,
\qquad -\frac{\pi}{2} \leq \theta \leq \frac{\pi}{2}
$$
$$
\iint_D x \, dx\, dy
= \int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2} \left( \int_0^{2\cos\theta} \rho \cos\theta \cdot \rho \, d\rho \right) d\theta
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2} \cos\theta \cdot \frac{(2\cos\theta)^3}{3} \, d\theta
&= \frac{8}{3} \int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2} \cos^4\theta \, d\theta \\[6pt]
&= \frac{8}{3} \cdot \frac{3\pi}{8} = \pi
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

**Completing the square** shows that the domain is the disk with center $(1, 0)$ and radius $1$, tangent to the $y$-axis at the origin. Since the center is *not* the origin, in polar coordinates the radius is not constant: substituting into the inequality gives $\rho^2 \leq 2\rho\cos\theta$, so $\rho \leq 2\cos\theta$, and the angle varies where $\cos\theta \geq 0$, that is, in $\left[ -\dfrac{\pi}{2}, \dfrac{\pi}{2} \right]$. In the inner integral the factor $\cos\theta$ is constant and the antiderivative of $\rho^2$ is $\dfrac{\rho^3}{3}$, evaluated at $\rho = 2\cos\theta$. What remains is the integral of $\cos^4\theta$ over the interval, which by the **half-angle formulas** equals $\dfrac{3\pi}{8}$: since $\cos^4\theta = \dfrac{3}{8} + \dfrac{\cos 2\theta}{2} + \dfrac{\cos 4\theta}{8}$, the two cosines have zero integral over the interval and the constant term survives. The result is $\pi$.

</div>
