## Exercise 1

Compute the double integral
$$
\iint_D (x + y)\, dx\, dy ,
$$
where $D$ is the parallelogram bounded by the lines $x + y = 1$, $x + y = 3$, $x - y = 0$ and $x - y = 2$.

$$
u = x + y, \qquad v = x - y
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
x = \frac{u + v}{2}, \qquad y = \frac{u - v}{2}
$$
$$
J = \det
\begin{pmatrix}
\dfrac{1}{2} & \dfrac{1}{2} \\[8pt]
\dfrac{1}{2} & -\dfrac{1}{2}
\end{pmatrix}
= -\frac{1}{2},
\qquad |J| = \frac{1}{2}
$$
$$
\iint_D (x + y)\, dx\, dy
= \int_1^3 \left( \int_0^2 u \cdot \frac{1}{2}\, dv \right) du
= \int_1^3 u \, du
= \frac{9 - 1}{2} = 4
$$

::grafico{xmin=-0.5 xmax=3.2 ymin=-1 ymax=2.2 uguale=1 area="x;1-x;0.5;1.5|3-x;x-2;1.5;2.5" segmenti="0.5,0.5,1.5,-0.5;1.5,1.5,2.5,0.5;0.5,0.5,1.5,1.5;1.5,-0.5,2.5,0.5" etichette="0.05,-0.3,x + y = 1;2.15,1.2,x + y = 3;0.1,1.25,x − y = 0;2.15,-0.3,x − y = 2" titolo="The parallelogram $D$: in the variables $u = x + y$, $v = x - y$ it becomes the rectangle $[1, 3] \times [0, 2]$"}

<div class="spiegazione">

The four sides of the parallelogram are **level curves** of the expressions $x + y$ and $x - y$, so the natural variables are $u = x + y$ and $v = x - y$: in the $uv$-plane the domain becomes the rectangle $[1, 3] \times [0, 2]$. Solving the system gives $x$ and $y$ as functions of $u$ and $v$, and the **Jacobian** of the transformation has absolute value $\dfrac{1}{2}$. The integrand is *exactly* $u$, therefore the integral reduces to $\dfrac{1}{2} \displaystyle\int_1^3 \int_0^2 u \, dv\, du$; the inner integral equals $2u \cdot \dfrac{1}{2} = u$ and the result is $4$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Compute the area of the region in the first quadrant bounded by the hyperbolas $xy = 1$ and $xy = 4$ and by the lines $y = x$ and $y = 4x$.

$$
u = x y, \qquad v = \frac{y}{x}
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
1 \leq u \leq 4, \qquad 1 \leq v \leq 4
$$
$$
\det \frac{\partial(u, v)}{\partial(x, y)}
= \det
\begin{pmatrix}
y & x \\[4pt]
-\dfrac{y}{x^2} & \dfrac{1}{x}
\end{pmatrix}
= \frac{y}{x} + \frac{y}{x} = 2v
$$
$$
dx\, dy = \frac{1}{2v}\, du\, dv
$$
$$
\operatorname{Area}(D)
= \int_1^4 \left( \int_1^4 \frac{1}{2v}\, dv \right) du
= 3 \cdot \frac{\ln 4}{2} = 3 \ln 2
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The four boundary curves are **level curves** of $xy$ and of $\dfrac{y}{x}$, so in the variables $u = xy$ and $v = \dfrac{y}{x}$ the domain becomes the square $[1, 4] \times [1, 4]$; indeed, the line $y = 4x$ corresponds to $v = 4$ and the hyperbola $xy = 4$ to $u = 4$. For the Jacobian it is convenient to differentiate the given formulas: the determinant of the matrix of the derivatives of $(u, v)$ with respect to $(x, y)$ equals $2 \dfrac{y}{x} = 2v$, and in the first quadrant it is positive. The Jacobian of the inverse transformation is the **reciprocal**, so $dx\, dy = \dfrac{du\, dv}{2v}$. The area is the integral of the constant $1$: the integral in $u$ gives the factor $3$, the integral of $\dfrac{1}{2v}$ over $[1, 4]$ equals $\dfrac{\ln 4}{2} = \ln 2$, therefore the **area** is $3\ln 2$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Compute the double integral
$$
\iint_D x^2 \, dx\, dy ,
\qquad D = \left\{ (x, y) : \frac{x^2}{4} + \frac{y^2}{9} \leq 1 \right\} .
$$

$$
x = 2u, \qquad y = 3v, \qquad J = \det
\begin{pmatrix}
2 & 0 \\
0 & 3
\end{pmatrix} = 6
$$
$$
\iint_D x^2 \, dx\, dy
= \iint_{u^2 + v^2 \leq 1} 4u^2 \cdot 6 \, du\, dv
= 24 \iint_{u^2 + v^2 \leq 1} u^2 \, du\, dv
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
\iint_{u^2 + v^2 \leq 1} u^2 \, du\, dv
&= \int_0^{2\pi} \left( \int_0^1 \rho^2 \cos^2\theta \cdot \rho \, d\rho \right) d\theta \\[6pt]
&= \frac{1}{4} \int_0^{2\pi} \cos^2\theta \, d\theta
= \frac{1}{4} \cdot \pi = \frac{\pi}{4}
\end{aligned}
$$
$$
\iint_D x^2 \, dx\, dy = 24 \cdot \frac{\pi}{4} = 6\pi
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The ellipse with semi-axes $2$ and $3$ becomes the unit disk under the **linear transformation** $x = 2u$, $y = 3v$, whose Jacobian is the product of the semi-axes, that is, $6$. The function $x^2$ becomes $4u^2$ and the constant $24$ comes out of the integral sign. The integral of $u^2$ over the unit disk is computed in polar coordinates: the integral in $\rho$ of $\rho^3$ equals $\dfrac{1}{4}$ and the integral of $\cos^2\theta$ over one period equals $\pi$, since $\cos^2\theta = \dfrac{1 + \cos 2\theta}{2}$ and the cosine has zero integral over the period. The overall result is $24 \cdot \dfrac{\pi}{4} = 6\pi$. The exercise combines **two changes of variables** in succession: first the linear transformation that simplifies the domain, then polar coordinates on the disk.

</div>
