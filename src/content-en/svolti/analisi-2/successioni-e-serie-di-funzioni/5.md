## Exercise 1

Compute
$$
\lim_{n \to +\infty} \int_0^1 \frac{\cos\!\left(\dfrac{x}{n}\right)}{1 + x^2} \, dx .
$$

$$
f_n(x) = \frac{\cos\!\left(\dfrac{x}{n}\right)}{1 + x^2}
\qquad
f(x) = \frac{1}{1 + x^2}
$$
$$
|f_n(x) - f(x)|
= \frac{1 - \cos\!\left(\dfrac{x}{n}\right)}{1 + x^2}
\leq 1 - \cos\!\left(\frac{x}{n}\right)
\leq \frac{x^2}{2 n^2}
\leq \frac{1}{2 n^2}
\qquad \text{on } [0, 1]
$$
$$
M_n \leq \frac{1}{2 n^2} \to 0
\ \implies \ f_n \to f \ \text{uniformly on } [0, 1]
$$
$$
\lim_{n \to +\infty} \int_0^1 f_n(x)\,dx
= \int_0^1 \frac{dx}{1 + x^2}
= \bigl[ \arctan x \bigr]_0^1
= \frac{\pi}{4}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

For fixed $x \in [0, 1]$ the argument $\dfrac{x}{n}$ tends to $0$ and the cosine is continuous, so the pointwise limit is $f(x) = \dfrac{1}{1 + x^2}$. **Passing to the limit under the integral sign** requires uniform convergence, which is verified by bounding the difference: the denominator $1 + x^2$ is at least $1$, and the inequality $1 - \cos t \leq \dfrac{t^2}{2}$ applied with $t = \dfrac{x}{n}$ gives a bound by $\dfrac{x^2}{2n^2} \leq \dfrac{1}{2n^2}$ on $[0, 1]$. The sup is therefore bounded by an infinitesimal sequence *independent* of $x$, so the convergence is **uniform** and the theorem applies. The limit of the integrals then coincides with the integral of the limit, which equals $\arctan 1 - \arctan 0 = \dfrac{\pi}{4}$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Compute
$$
\lim_{n \to +\infty} \int_{-1}^{1} \sqrt{x^2 + \frac{1}{n^2}} \; dx .
$$

$$
f_n(x) = \sqrt{x^2 + \frac{1}{n^2}}
\qquad
f(x) = |x|
$$
$$
|x| \leq \sqrt{x^2 + \frac{1}{n^2}} \leq \sqrt{x^2} + \sqrt{\frac{1}{n^2}} = |x| + \frac{1}{n}
$$
$$
M_n = \sup_{x \in [-1,1]} \bigl| f_n(x) - |x| \bigr| \leq \frac{1}{n} \to 0
\ \implies \ \text{uniform convergence on } [-1, 1]
$$
$$
\lim_{n \to +\infty} \int_{-1}^{1} f_n(x)\,dx
= \int_{-1}^{1} |x| \, dx
= 2 \int_0^1 x \, dx
= 1
$$

::grafico{f="abs(x);sqrt(x*x+0.25);sqrt(x*x+0.04)" xmin=-1.3 xmax=1.3 ymin=-0.2 ymax=1.4 uguale=1 titolo="$y = |x|$ (in color) with $f_2$ (dashed) and $f_5$ (red): every $f_n$ lies above $|x|$ and the maximum gap, at $x = 0$, equals $\dfrac{1}{n}$"}

<div class="spiegazione">

For fixed $x$ the term $\dfrac{1}{n^2}$ under the square root tends to $0$ and the square root is continuous, so the pointwise limit is $\sqrt{x^2} = |x|$. Uniform convergence follows from the **subadditivity of the square root**: for $a, b \geq 0$ we have $\sqrt{a + b} \leq \sqrt{a} + \sqrt{b}$, so $f_n(x)$ lies between $|x|$ and $|x| + \dfrac{1}{n}$, and the difference $f_n(x) - |x|$ lies between $0$ and $\dfrac{1}{n}$ for every $x$. The sup is therefore bounded by $\dfrac{1}{n}$, which tends to $0$, so **passing to the limit under the integral sign** applies. The integral of $|x|$ on $[-1, 1]$ is computed by symmetry, since $|x|$ is even, and equals $2 \cdot \dfrac{1}{2} = 1$. The example also shows that the uniform limit of differentiable functions **need not be differentiable**: every $f_n$ is smooth, while $|x|$ has a corner point at $0$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Compute
$$
\lim_{n \to +\infty} \int_0^1 n x \,(1 - x^2)^n \, dx
$$
and compare the result with the integral of the pointwise limit.

$$
t = 1 - x^2, \qquad dt = -2x\,dx
$$
$$
\int_0^1 n x (1 - x^2)^n dx
= \frac{n}{2} \int_0^1 t^n \, dt
= \frac{n}{2(n+1)}
\xrightarrow[n \to +\infty]{} \frac{1}{2}
$$
$$
0 < x \leq 1: \ (1 - x^2)^n \to 0, \quad f_n(0) = 0
\ \implies \ f(x) = 0 \ \text{ on } [0, 1]
$$
$$
\lim_{n \to +\infty} \int_0^1 f_n(x)\,dx = \frac{1}{2}
\neq 0 = \int_0^1 f(x)\,dx
\ \implies \ \text{convergence not uniform on } [0, 1]
$$

::grafico{f="2*x*(1-x*x)^2;8*x*(1-x*x)^8;30*x*(1-x*x)^30" area="30*x*(1-x*x)^30;0;0;1" xmin=0 xmax=1.02 ymin=-0.2 ymax=2.6 etichette="0.6,0.85,n = 2;0.3,1.3,n = 8;0.16,2.45,n = 30" titolo="$y = nx(1 - x^2)^n$ for $n = 2, 8, 30$: the peak rises and narrows near $0$, and the shaded area ($n = 30$) equals $\dfrac{n}{2(n + 1)}$, close to $\dfrac{1}{2}$"}

<div class="spiegazione">

The integral is computed exactly with the substitution $t = 1 - x^2$: the factor $x\,dx$ becomes $-\dfrac{1}{2}\,dt$ and the limits of integration are swapped, so the integral equals $\dfrac{n}{2} \cdot \dfrac{1}{n+1}$, which tends to $\dfrac{1}{2}$. The pointwise limit, *instead*, is **zero**: for $0 < x \leq 1$ the base $1 - x^2$ lies between $0$ and $1$, so $(1 - x^2)^n$ tends to $0$ geometrically and dominates the factor $n$, while at $x = 0$ every term equals $0$. The limit of the integrals equals $\dfrac{1}{2}$ and the integral of the limit equals $0$: the two values are different, so by the **theorem on passing to the limit under the integral sign** the convergence **cannot be uniform** on $[0, 1]$. The geometric reason is a peak near $0$ that becomes ever taller and narrower and carries an area that does not vanish.

</div>
