## Exercise 1

Study the pointwise and total convergence on $\mathbb{R}$ of the series of functions
$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{\sin(n x)}{n^2} .
$$

$$
\left| \frac{\sin(n x)}{n^2} \right| \leq \frac{1}{n^2} = M_n
\qquad \text{for every } x \in \mathbb{R}
$$
$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{n^2} \ \text{ converges}
\quad (\text{generalized harmonic series, } \alpha = 2 > 1)
$$
$$
\text{the series converges totally on } \mathbb{R},
\ \text{hence uniformly, and pointwise at every point}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The sine is bounded by $1$ in absolute value for every value of the argument, so every term of the series is bounded in absolute value by $M_n = \dfrac{1}{n^2}$, a constant independent of $x$. The numerical series $\sum \dfrac{1}{n^2}$ is a **generalized harmonic series** with exponent $2 > 1$, therefore it **converges**. *Both* conditions of total convergence are verified on all of $\mathbb{R}$, so by the **Weierstrass test** the series *also* converges uniformly on $\mathbb{R}$ and, in particular, pointwise at every point.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Study the pointwise and total convergence on $\mathbb{R}$ of the series of functions
$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{n^2 + x^2} .
$$

$$
0 < \frac{1}{n^2 + x^2} \leq \frac{1}{n^2}
\qquad \text{for every } x \in \mathbb{R}
$$
$$
\sup_{x \in \mathbb{R}} \frac{1}{n^2 + x^2} = \frac{1}{n^2} = M_n
\qquad (\text{value attained at } x = 0)
$$
$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{n^2} \ \text{ converges}
\ \implies \
\text{total convergence on } \mathbb{R}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The general term is positive and the denominator satisfies $n^2 + x^2 \geq n^2$ for every $x$, so the term is bounded by $\dfrac{1}{n^2}$. In this case the bound is also the *best* possible, since for $x = 0$ the term equals exactly $\dfrac{1}{n^2}$, hence $M_n = \sup_{\mathbb{R}} |f_n| = \dfrac{1}{n^2}$. The series $\sum \dfrac{1}{n^2}$ converges, therefore the series of functions **converges totally** on $\mathbb{R}$, hence uniformly and pointwise. Moreover, the sum is a **continuous** function on $\mathbb{R}$, as a uniform sum of continuous functions.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Study the pointwise and total convergence on $\mathbb{R}$ of the series of functions
$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{x}{n (1 + n x^2)} .
$$

$$
f_n(x) = \frac{x}{n (1 + n x^2)},
\qquad
f_n'(x) = \frac{1 - n x^2}{n (1 + n x^2)^2}
$$
$$
f_n'(x) = 0 \iff x = \pm \frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}
$$
$$
M_n = \sup_{x \in \mathbb{R}} |f_n(x)|
= f_n\!\left( \frac{1}{\sqrt{n}} \right)
= \frac{\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{n}}}{n \cdot 2}
= \frac{1}{2\, n^{\frac{3}{2}}}
$$
$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{2\,n^{\frac{3}{2}}} \ \text{ converges}
\quad \left( \alpha = \frac{3}{2} > 1 \right)
\ \implies \
\text{total convergence on } \mathbb{R}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Here an immediate bound on the general term is not available, so the sup is computed by **studying the derivative**. The function $f_n$ is odd, therefore the *positive* half-line suffices. The derivative has the sign of the numerator $1 - n x^2$, so $f_n$ increases up to $x = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{n}}$ and then decreases, and the maximum of the absolute value equals $f_n\!\left(\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right) = \dfrac{1}{2 n^{\frac{3}{2}}}$, since at the maximum point the factor $1 + n x^2$ equals $2$. The numerical series $\sum \dfrac{1}{2 n^{\frac{3}{2}}}$ is a generalized harmonic series with exponent $\dfrac{3}{2} > 1$, so it converges. Therefore the series of functions **converges totally** on all of $\mathbb{R}$, and consequently uniformly and pointwise.

</div>
