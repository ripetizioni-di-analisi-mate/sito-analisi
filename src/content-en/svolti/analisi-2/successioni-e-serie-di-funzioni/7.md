## Exercise 1

Prove that the function
$$
f(x) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{\cos(n x)}{n^3}
$$
is of class $C^1$ on $\mathbb{R}$ and compute its derivative.

$$
\left| \frac{\cos(n x)}{n^3} \right| \leq \frac{1}{n^3},
\qquad
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{n^3} \ \text{converges}
\ \implies \ \text{total convergence on } \mathbb{R}
$$
$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \left( \frac{\cos(n x)}{n^3} \right)^{\!\prime}
= \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \left( -\frac{\sin(n x)}{n^2} \right),
\qquad
\left| \frac{\sin(n x)}{n^2} \right| \leq \frac{1}{n^2}
\ \implies \ \text{total convergence on } \mathbb{R}
$$
$$
f \in C^1(\mathbb{R}),
\qquad
f'(x) = -\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{\sin(n x)}{n^2}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The series **converges totally** on $\mathbb{R}$, since the cosine is bounded by $1$ and the series $\sum \dfrac{1}{n^3}$ converges; hence $f$ is defined on all of $\mathbb{R}$ and continuous, as the sum of a uniformly convergent series of continuous functions. For differentiability we apply the **term-by-term differentiation theorem** on an arbitrary interval $[-a, a]$: the series converges at *at least one* point, since it converges everywhere, and the series of derivatives $\sum \left(-\dfrac{\sin(nx)}{n^2}\right)$ converges totally on $\mathbb{R}$ with bounds $\dfrac{1}{n^2}$, hence uniformly. *Both* hypotheses of the theorem are satisfied, therefore $f$ is of class $C^1$ on every interval $[-a, a]$, and hence on $\mathbb{R}$, with derivative equal to the sum of the series of derivatives.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Justify term-by-term integration and write, as the sum of a numerical series, the integral
$$
\int_0^{\pi} \left( \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{\sin(n x)}{n^3} \right) dx .
$$

$$
\left| \frac{\sin(n x)}{n^3} \right| \leq \frac{1}{n^3}
\ \implies \ \text{total, hence uniform, convergence on } [0, \pi]
$$
$$
\int_0^{\pi} \frac{\sin(n x)}{n^3}\,dx
= \frac{1}{n^3} \left[ -\frac{\cos(n x)}{n} \right]_0^{\pi}
= \frac{1 - \cos(n \pi)}{n^4}
= \frac{1 - (-1)^n}{n^4}
$$
$$
\frac{1 - (-1)^n}{n^4} =
\begin{cases}
0 & \text{if } n \text{ is even} \\[4pt]
\dfrac{2}{n^4} & \text{if } n \text{ is odd}
\end{cases}
$$
$$
\int_0^{\pi} \left( \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{\sin(n x)}{n^3} \right) dx
= \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{1 - (-1)^n}{n^4}
= 2 \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \frac{1}{(2k+1)^4}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The series converges totally on $[0, \pi]$, with the same bound $\dfrac{1}{n^3}$ used in the previous exercise, so it converges **uniformly** and the **term-by-term integration theorem** applies: the integral of the sum is the series of the integrals. Each integral is computed with the antiderivative $-\dfrac{\cos(nx)}{n}$ of the sine, and since $\cos(n\pi) = (-1)^n$ the $n$-th term equals $\dfrac{1 - (-1)^n}{n^4}$. This value is $0$ for even $n$ and $\dfrac{2}{n^4}$ for odd $n$, so the series of the integrals is rewritten as a sum over the odd indices $n = 2k + 1$ *alone*. The result is a **convergent numerical series**, comparable with $\sum \dfrac{2}{n^4}$, which represents the required integral exactly.

</div>
