## Exercise 1

Study the pointwise convergence of the series of functions
$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{x^2}{n^2 + x^4}
$$
on $\mathbb{R}$, and the total convergence on $\mathbb{R}$ and on the intervals $[-a, a]$.

$$
x \ \text{fixed}: \quad
0 \leq \frac{x^2}{n^2 + x^4} \leq \frac{x^2}{n^2},
\qquad
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{x^2}{n^2} \ \text{converges}
\ \implies \ \text{pointwise convergence on } \mathbb{R}
$$
$$
f_n'(x) = \frac{2x\,(n^2 - x^4)}{(n^2 + x^4)^2},
\qquad
\sup_{x \in \mathbb{R}} f_n(x) = f_n(\sqrt{n}\,)
= \frac{n}{2 n^2} = \frac{1}{2n}
$$
$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{2n} \ \text{diverges}
\ \implies \ \text{convergence not total on } \mathbb{R}
$$
$$
\text{on } [-a, a]: \quad
0 \leq f_n(x) \leq \frac{a^2}{n^2} = M_n,
\qquad
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{a^2}{n^2} \ \text{converges}
\ \implies \ \text{total convergence on } [-a, a]
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

For pointwise convergence we fix $x$ and compare with a numerical series: the term is bounded by $\dfrac{x^2}{n^2}$, where $x^2$ is a constant, so the **comparison test** with the generalized harmonic series of exponent $2$ gives convergence at every point. For total convergence on $\mathbb{R}$ we compute the exact sup: the function is even, the derivative on the positive half-line has the sign of $n^2 - x^4$, so the maximum is at $x = \sqrt{n}$ and equals $\dfrac{n}{2n^2} = \dfrac{1}{2n}$. The series of the sups is a multiple of the harmonic series, which diverges, therefore total convergence on all of $\mathbb{R}$ **does not hold**. On a bounded interval $[-a, a]$, instead, the bound $f_n(x) \leq \dfrac{a^2}{n^2}$ gives a convergent series, so the convergence is total on *every* compact set. Since every point of $\mathbb{R}$ is interior to an interval $[-a, a]$, the sum of the series is **continuous** on all of $\mathbb{R}$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Study the pointwise convergence of the series of functions
$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} n \, e^{-n x}
$$
on $(0, +\infty)$, and the total convergence on $(0, +\infty)$ and on the half-lines $[\delta, +\infty)$ with $\delta > 0$.

$$
x > 0 \ \text{fixed}: \quad
\sqrt[n]{\,n\,e^{-n x}} = \sqrt[n]{n} \; e^{-x}
\xrightarrow[n \to +\infty]{} e^{-x} < 1
\ \implies \ \text{pointwise convergence on } (0, +\infty)
$$
$$
\sup_{x > 0} \, n\,e^{-n x} = \lim_{x \to 0^+} n\,e^{-n x} = n
\ \implies \ \text{convergence not total on } (0, +\infty)
$$
$$
\text{on } [\delta, +\infty): \quad
M_n = \sup_{x \geq \delta} n\,e^{-n x} = n\,e^{-n \delta},
\qquad
\sqrt[n]{\,n\,e^{-n\delta}} \to e^{-\delta} < 1
\ \implies \ \text{total convergence on } [\delta, +\infty)
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

For fixed $x > 0$ the series is a numerical series with positive terms and is handled with the **root test**: the $n$-th root of the term tends to $e^{-x}$, which is less than $1$ because $x > 0$, so the series converges at every point of the open half-line. The term $n\,e^{-nx}$ is decreasing in $x$, therefore on $(0, +\infty)$ its supremum is found as $x \to 0^+$ and equals $n$: the condition $|f_n(x)| \leq M_n$ with $\sum M_n$ convergent cannot be satisfied, since every constant upper bound of $f_n$ is *at least* $n$, so total convergence on $(0, +\infty)$ **does not hold**. On $[\delta, +\infty)$ the same monotonicity gives $M_n = n\,e^{-n\delta}$, and the root test applied to the numerical series $\sum n\,e^{-n\delta}$ gives the limit $e^{-\delta} < 1$, so the series of the sups converges. Therefore the convergence is **total**, and hence uniform, on every half-line $[\delta, +\infty)$, and the sum is continuous on $(0, +\infty)$ because every point belongs to one of these half-lines.

</div>
