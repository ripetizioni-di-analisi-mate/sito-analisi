## Exercise 1

Study the pointwise and uniform convergence on $[0, +\infty)$ of the sequence of functions
$$
f_n(x) = x\,e^{-2 n x} .
$$

$$
f_n(0) = 0, \qquad
x > 0: \ e^{-2 n x} \to 0
\ \implies \ f(x) = 0 \ \text{ su } [0, +\infty)
$$
$$
f_n'(x) = e^{-2 n x} (1 - 2 n x)
\qquad
f_n'(x) > 0 \iff x < \frac{1}{2n}
$$
$$
M_n = \sup_{x \geq 0} |f_n(x) - 0|
= f_n\!\left( \frac{1}{2n} \right)
= \frac{1}{2 n e}
\xrightarrow[n \to +\infty]{} 0
$$
$$
f_n \to 0 \ \text{ uniformemente su } [0, +\infty)
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The pointwise limit is computed for fixed $x$: at $x = 0$ every $f_n$ equals $0$, and for $x > 0$ the exponential $e^{-2nx}$ tends to $0$ faster than any constant, so $f(x) = 0$ at all points. By the **supremum criterion** we study $|f_n - f| = f_n$, which is nonnegative. The derivative $f_n'(x) = e^{-2nx}(1 - 2nx)$ vanishes at $x = \dfrac{1}{2n}$, is positive before and negative after, so that point is the **absolute maximum** on $[0, +\infty)$. The maximum value is $M_n = \dfrac{1}{2n}\,e^{-1} = \dfrac{1}{2ne}$. Since $M_n \to 0$, the convergence is **uniform** on the *whole* half-line.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Study the pointwise and uniform convergence on $\mathbb{R}$ of the sequence of functions
$$
f_n(x) = \frac{x}{1 + n^2 x^2} .
$$

$$
f_n(0) = 0, \qquad
x \neq 0: \ |f_n(x)| \leq \frac{|x|}{n^2 x^2} = \frac{1}{n^2 |x|} \to 0
\ \implies \ f(x) = 0 \ \text{ su } \mathbb{R}
$$
$$
f_n'(x) = \frac{1 - n^2 x^2}{(1 + n^2 x^2)^2}
\qquad
f_n'(x) = 0 \iff x = \pm \frac{1}{n}
$$
$$
M_n = \sup_{x \in \mathbb{R}} |f_n(x)|
= f_n\!\left( \frac{1}{n} \right)
= \frac{\dfrac{1}{n}}{2}
= \frac{1}{2n}
\xrightarrow[n \to +\infty]{} 0
$$
$$
f_n \to 0 \ \text{ uniformemente su } \mathbb{R}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

For the pointwise limit we fix $x$: at $x = 0$ all the $f_n$ equal $0$, and for $x \neq 0$ the denominator grows like $n^2$, so the absolute value of $f_n(x)$ is bounded by $\dfrac{1}{n^2 |x|}$, which tends to $0$. The function $f_n$ is **odd**, therefore the supremum of the absolute value on $\mathbb{R}$ coincides with the maximum on $[0, +\infty)$. The derivative vanishes at $x = \dfrac{1}{n}$, with $f_n$ increasing before and decreasing after, so the maximum equals $f_n\!\left(\dfrac{1}{n}\right) = \dfrac{1}{2n}$. Since $M_n = \dfrac{1}{2n} \to 0$, the **supremum criterion** guarantees **uniform convergence** on all of $\mathbb{R}$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Study the pointwise and uniform convergence on $\mathbb{R}$ of the sequence of functions
$$
f_n(x) = n x \, e^{-n x^2} .
$$

$$
f_n(0) = 0, \qquad
x \neq 0: \ n\,e^{-n x^2} \to 0
\ \implies \ f(x) = 0 \ \text{ su } \mathbb{R}
$$
$$
f_n'(x) = n \, e^{-n x^2} (1 - 2 n x^2)
\qquad
f_n'(x) = 0 \iff x = \pm \frac{1}{\sqrt{2n}}
$$
$$
M_n = \sup_{x \in \mathbb{R}} |f_n(x)|
= f_n\!\left( \frac{1}{\sqrt{2n}} \right)
= \frac{n}{\sqrt{2n}} \, e^{-\frac{1}{2}}
= \sqrt{\frac{n}{2e}}
\xrightarrow[n \to +\infty]{} +\infty
$$
$$
f_n \to 0 \ \text{ puntualmente ma non uniformemente su } \mathbb{R}
$$

::grafico{f="x*exp(-x*x);4*x*exp(-4*x*x);16*x*exp(-16*x*x)" xmin=-2 xmax=2 ymin=-2 ymax=2 etichette="0.75,0.55,n = 1;0.45,1,n = 4;0.25,1.85,n = 16" titolo="$y = nx\,e^{-nx^2}$ for $n = 1, 4, 16$: the maximum, at $x = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2n}}$, equals $\sqrt{\dfrac{n}{2e}}$ and grows without bound"}

<div class="spiegazione">

For fixed $x \neq 0$ the quantity $n\,e^{-n x^2}$ tends to $0$, since the exponential $e^{-n x^2}$ decreases faster than the factor $n$ grows; at $x = 0$ every term equals $0$. Hence the pointwise limit is the **zero function** on all of $\mathbb{R}$. For the supremum we use the oddness of $f_n$ and study the positive half-line: the derivative vanishes at $x = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2n}}$, which is the maximum point, and the maximum value is $\sqrt{\dfrac{n}{2e}}$. This sequence tends to $+\infty$, so the supremum criterion **is not satisfied** and the convergence **is not uniform** on $\mathbb{R}$. Near the origin the graphs have an ever taller peak, whose maximum point $\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2n}}$ tends to $0$: this is why the limit at every fixed point is zero, while the maximum diverges.

</div>
