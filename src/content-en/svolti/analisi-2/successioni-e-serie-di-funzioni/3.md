## Exercise 1

Study the pointwise and uniform convergence of $f_n(x) = x^n$ on $[0, 1]$, on $[0, 1)$ and on $[0, a]$ with $0 < a < 1$.

$$
f(x) =
\begin{cases}
0 & \text{if } 0 \leq x < 1 \\[4pt]
1 & \text{if } x = 1
\end{cases}
$$
$$
[0, 1]: \quad f \ \text{discontinuous at } 1
\ \implies \ \text{convergence not uniform}
$$
$$
[0, 1): \quad
M_n = \sup_{0 \leq x < 1} x^n = 1 \not\to 0
\ \implies \ \text{convergence not uniform}
$$
$$
[0, a]: \quad
M_n = \sup_{0 \leq x \leq a} x^n = a^n \xrightarrow[n \to +\infty]{} 0
\ \implies \ \text{uniform convergence}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The pointwise limit distinguishes two cases: for $0 \leq x < 1$ the geometric sequence $x^n$ tends to $0$, while at $x = 1$ every term equals $1$. On $[0, 1]$ the $f_n$ are continuous and the pointwise limit is discontinuous, so by the **theorem on the continuity of the uniform limit** the convergence **cannot be uniform**. On $[0, 1)$ the limit is the zero function, which is continuous, but the sup criterion is *not* satisfied: since $x^n \to 1$ as $x \to 1^-$, the supremum of $x^n$ on $[0, 1)$ equals $1$ for every $n$, and it does not tend to $0$. On $[0, a]$ the function $x^n$ is increasing, so the maximum is $a^n$, which tends to $0$ because $0 < a < 1$; therefore the convergence is **uniform** on *every* interval $[0, a]$ with $a < 1$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Study the pointwise and uniform convergence of $f_n(x) = \arctan(n x)$ on $\mathbb{R}$ and on $[\delta, +\infty)$ with $\delta > 0$.

$$
f(x) =
\begin{cases}
\dfrac{\pi}{2} & \text{if } x > 0 \\[8pt]
0 & \text{if } x = 0 \\[4pt]
-\dfrac{\pi}{2} & \text{if } x < 0
\end{cases}
$$
$$
\mathbb{R}: \quad f \ \text{discontinuous at } 0
\ \implies \ \text{convergence not uniform}
$$
$$
x \geq \delta: \quad
0 \leq \frac{\pi}{2} - \arctan(n x) \leq \frac{\pi}{2} - \arctan(n \delta)
$$
$$
M_n = \sup_{x \geq \delta} \left| f_n(x) - \frac{\pi}{2} \right|
= \frac{\pi}{2} - \arctan(n \delta)
\xrightarrow[n \to +\infty]{} 0
\ \implies \ \text{uniform convergence on } [\delta, +\infty)
$$

::grafico{f="atan(x);atan(4*x);atan(20*x)" xmin=-3 xmax=3 ymin=-2 ymax=2 asintoti="y=1.5708;y=-1.5708" titolo="$y = \arctan(nx)$ for $n = 1, 4, 20$: for large $n$ the graph approaches $y = \dfrac{\pi}{2}$ for $x > 0$ and $y = -\dfrac{\pi}{2}$ for $x < 0$ (dashed lines), with the transition concentrated near the origin"}

<div class="spiegazione">

For fixed $x > 0$ the argument $n x$ tends to $+\infty$, so $\arctan(n x) \to \dfrac{\pi}{2}$; for $x < 0$ the limit is $-\dfrac{\pi}{2}$, and at $x = 0$ every term equals $0$. The pointwise limit is therefore discontinuous at the origin, while every $f_n$ is continuous, so the convergence **is not uniform** on $\mathbb{R}$. On the half-line $[\delta, +\infty)$ the difference $\dfrac{\pi}{2} - \arctan(n x)$ is positive and decreasing in $x$, since the arctangent is increasing, so the sup is attained at the first point $x = \delta$. Since $n \delta \to +\infty$, the quantity $\dfrac{\pi}{2} - \arctan(n \delta)$ tends to $0$, and the **sup criterion** guarantees **uniform convergence** on $[\delta, +\infty)$ for *every* $\delta > 0$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Study the pointwise and uniform convergence of $f_n(x) = e^{-n x}$ on $(0, +\infty)$ and on $[\delta, +\infty)$ with $\delta > 0$.

$$
x > 0: \quad e^{-n x} \xrightarrow[n \to +\infty]{} 0
\ \implies \ f(x) = 0 \ \text{ on } (0, +\infty)
$$
$$
(0, +\infty): \quad
M_n = \sup_{x > 0} e^{-n x} = \lim_{x \to 0^+} e^{-n x} = 1 \not\to 0
\ \implies \ \text{convergence not uniform}
$$
$$
[\delta, +\infty): \quad
M_n = \sup_{x \geq \delta} e^{-n x} = e^{-n \delta}
\xrightarrow[n \to +\infty]{} 0
\ \implies \ \text{uniform convergence}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

For each fixed $x > 0$ the exponent $-n x$ tends to $-\infty$, so $f_n(x) \to 0$ and the pointwise limit is the zero function. The function $e^{-n x}$ is decreasing in $x$, therefore on $(0, +\infty)$ the supremum is found at the left endpoint: as $x \to 0^+$ the values tend to $1$, so $M_n = 1$ for every $n$ and the convergence **is not uniform**. In this case the pointwise limit is continuous on $(0, +\infty)$, so the example shows that the theorem on the continuity of the uniform limit does *not* have a converse. On $[\delta, +\infty)$ monotonicity gives $M_n = e^{-n \delta}$, which tends to $0$ because $\delta > 0$ is fixed, therefore the convergence is **uniform** on *every* half-line $[\delta, +\infty)$.

</div>
