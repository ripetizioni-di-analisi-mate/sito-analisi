## Exercise 1

Determine the pointwise limit on $\mathbb{R}$ of the sequence of functions
$$
f_n(x) = \frac{n x^2 + 1}{n} .
$$

$$
f_n(x) = x^2 + \frac{1}{n}
$$
$$
\lim_{n \to +\infty} f_n(x)
= x^2 + \lim_{n \to +\infty} \frac{1}{n}
= x^2
$$
$$
f(x) = x^2 \qquad \text{for every } x \in \mathbb{R}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The point $x$ is treated as a constant and the limit is computed in the variable $n$ *alone*. Dividing numerator and denominator by $n$ gives $f_n(x) = x^2 + \dfrac{1}{n}$, so the term $x^2$ does not depend on $n$ and the term $\dfrac{1}{n}$ tends to $0$. Therefore, for each fixed $x \in \mathbb{R}$, $f_n(x)$ tends to $x^2$, and the **pointwise limit** is the function $f(x) = x^2$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Determine the pointwise limit on $[0, +\infty)$ of the sequence of functions
$$
f_n(x) = \frac{x^n}{1 + x^n} .
$$

$$
0 \leq x < 1: \quad x^n \to 0,
\qquad
f_n(x) = \frac{x^n}{1 + x^n} \to \frac{0}{1 + 0} = 0
$$
$$
x = 1: \quad f_n(1) = \frac{1}{2} \ \text{ for every } n
$$
$$
x > 1: \quad f_n(x) = \frac{1}{x^{-n} + 1} \to \frac{1}{0 + 1} = 1
$$
$$
f(x) =
\begin{cases}
0 & \text{if } 0 \leq x < 1 \\[4pt]
\dfrac{1}{2} & \text{if } x = 1 \\[8pt]
1 & \text{if } x > 1
\end{cases}
$$

::grafico{f="x/(1+x);x^4/(1+x^4);x^20/(1+x^20)" xmin=0 xmax=2.6 ymin=-0.1 ymax=1.2 punti="1,0.5,pieno" etichette="2.1,0.58,n = 1;1.75,0.83,n = 4" titolo="$y = \dfrac{x^n}{1 + x^n}$ for $n = 1, 4, 20$: all the graphs pass through $\left(1, \dfrac{1}{2}\right)$ and for large $n$ they approach $0$ for $x < 1$ and $1$ for $x > 1$"}

<div class="spiegazione">

The behavior of $x^n$ as $n \to +\infty$ depends on the base, so the limit is computed by distinguishing three cases. For $0 \leq x < 1$ the geometric sequence $x^n$ tends to $0$, therefore the ratio tends to $0$. For $x = 1$ every $f_n$ equals exactly $\dfrac{1}{2}$, so the limit is $\dfrac{1}{2}$. For $x > 1$ both the numerator and the denominator tend to $+\infty$; dividing numerator and denominator by $x^n$ the ratio is rewritten as $\dfrac{1}{x^{-n} + 1}$, and since $x^{-n} \to 0$ the limit is $1$. The pointwise limit is a function that is **discontinuous** at $x = 1$, even though *every* $f_n$ is continuous.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Determine the pointwise limit on $\mathbb{R}$ of the sequence of functions
$$
f_n(x) = \left( 1 + \frac{x}{n} \right)^{\! n} .
$$

$$
f_n(x) = e^{\,n \ln\left(1 + \frac{x}{n}\right)}
\qquad \text{for } n > |x|
$$
$$
n \ln\!\left( 1 + \frac{x}{n} \right)
= x \cdot \frac{\ln\!\left( 1 + \dfrac{x}{n} \right)}{\dfrac{x}{n}}
\xrightarrow[n \to +\infty]{} x \cdot 1 = x
\qquad (x \neq 0)
$$
$$
\lim_{n \to +\infty} f_n(x) = e^{x}
\qquad \text{for every } x \in \mathbb{R}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

For each fixed $x$ and for $n > |x|$ the base $1 + \dfrac{x}{n}$ is positive, so the power is rewritten in **exponential form** as $f_n(x) = e^{\,n \ln\left(1 + \frac{x}{n}\right)}$. The exponent reduces to the **standard limit of the logarithm**: setting $t = \dfrac{x}{n}$, as $n \to +\infty$ we have $t \to 0$, so $\dfrac{\ln(1 + t)}{t} \to 1$ and the exponent tends to $x$. For $x = 0$ every $f_n$ equals $1 = e^0$, so the formula remains valid. Since the exponential is a continuous function, the limit of the exponent gives $f_n(x) \to e^x$ for every $x \in \mathbb{R}$.

</div>
