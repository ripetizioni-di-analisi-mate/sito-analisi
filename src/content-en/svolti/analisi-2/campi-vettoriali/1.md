## Exercise 1

Determine whether the field
$$
F(x, y) = \bigl( 2xy + 3x^2, \; x^2 - 4y \bigr)
$$
is conservative on $\mathbb{R}^2$ and, if so, determine its potentials.

$$
\frac{\partial F_1}{\partial y} = 2x, \qquad \frac{\partial F_2}{\partial x} = 2x
$$
$$
U(x, y) = \int (2xy + 3x^2) \, dx + c(y) = x^2 y + x^3 + c(y)
$$
$$
U_y = x^2 + c'(y) = x^2 - 4y \implies c'(y) = -4y \implies c(y) = -2y^2 + k
$$
$$
U(x, y) = x^2 y + x^3 - 2y^2 + k, \qquad k \in \mathbb{R}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The cross derivatives coincide at *every* point, hence the field is irrotational on $\mathbb{R}^2$. Since $\mathbb{R}^2$ is simply connected, the theorem on irrotational fields on simply connected domains applies and the field is **conservative**. The potential is constructed by **successive integration**: integrating the first component with respect to $x$, with $y$ treated as a constant, one obtains $x^2 y + x^3$ plus a function $c(y)$ of $y$ alone. Differentiating with respect to $y$ and imposing that the result coincide with the second component one finds $c'(y) = -4y$, therefore $c(y) = -2y^2 + k$. The final check confirms that the gradient of $U$ is $F$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Determine whether the field
$$
F(x, y) = \bigl( x - y^2, \; 2xy \bigr)
$$
is conservative on $\mathbb{R}^2$.

$$
\frac{\partial F_1}{\partial y} = -2y, \qquad \frac{\partial F_2}{\partial x} = 2y
$$
$$
-2y \neq 2y \quad \text{for } y \neq 0
$$
The field **is not conservative** on $\mathbb{R}^2$.

<div class="spiegazione">

A conservative field of class $C^1$ is irrotational, hence the equality of the cross derivatives is a *necessary* condition. Here the derivative of $F_1$ with respect to $y$ equals $-2y$ and the derivative of $F_2$ with respect to $x$ equals $2y$; the two functions coincide only at the points with $y = 0$, not on the whole of $\mathbb{R}^2$. Since the necessary condition is not satisfied, the field **is not conservative** and the construction of the potential is not to be attempted.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Determine whether the field
$$
F(x, y) = \bigl( y \cos(xy) + 2x, \; x \cos(xy) - 3y^2 \bigr)
$$
is conservative on $\mathbb{R}^2$ and, if so, determine its potentials.

$$
\frac{\partial F_1}{\partial y} = \cos(xy) - xy \sin(xy), \qquad
\frac{\partial F_2}{\partial x} = \cos(xy) - xy \sin(xy)
$$
$$
U(x, y) = \int \bigl( y \cos(xy) + 2x \bigr) dx + c(y) = \sin(xy) + x^2 + c(y)
$$
$$
U_y = x \cos(xy) + c'(y) = x \cos(xy) - 3y^2 \implies c'(y) = -3y^2
$$
$$
U(x, y) = \sin(xy) + x^2 - y^3 + k, \qquad k \in \mathbb{R}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The derivative of $F_1$ with respect to $y$ is computed with the **product rule** on the term $y \cos(xy)$ and equals $\cos(xy) - xy \sin(xy)$; the same computation on $x \cos(xy)$ gives the derivative of $F_2$ with respect to $x$, which is identical. The field is therefore irrotational on the simply connected domain $\mathbb{R}^2$, so it is **conservative**. In the integration with respect to $x$ the term $y \cos(xy)$ has antiderivative $\sin(xy)$, since the derivative of $\sin(xy)$ with respect to $x$ is $y \cos(xy)$. Imposing $U_y = F_2$ the term $x \cos(xy)$ cancels from both sides and what remains is $c'(y) = -3y^2$, hence $c(y) = -y^3 + k$.

</div>

## Exercise 4

Determine whether the field
$$
F(x, y, z) = \bigl( yz + 2x, \; xz, \; xy + 1 \bigr)
$$
is conservative on $\mathbb{R}^3$ and, if so, determine its potentials.

$$
\operatorname{curl} F =
\bigl( x - x, \;\; y - y, \;\; z - z \bigr) = (0, 0, 0)
$$
$$
U(x, y, z) = \int (yz + 2x) \, dx + c(y, z) = xyz + x^2 + c(y, z)
$$
$$
U_y = xz + c_y = xz \implies c_y = 0 \implies c = c(z)
$$
$$
U_z = xy + c'(z) = xy + 1 \implies c'(z) = 1 \implies c(z) = z + k
$$
$$
U(x, y, z) = xyz + x^2 + z + k, \qquad k \in \mathbb{R}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

In space the necessary condition is the **vanishing of the curl**. The first component of the curl is the derivative of $xy + 1$ with respect to $y$ minus the derivative of $xz$ with respect to $z$, that is, $x - x = 0$; the other two components vanish with the same computation. Since $\mathbb{R}^3$ is simply connected, the field is **conservative**. Successive integration has three steps: integrating $F_1$ with respect to $x$ one obtains $xyz + x^2$ plus a function $c(y, z)$ of the other two variables; imposing $U_y = F_2$ one gets $c_y = 0$, hence $c$ depends on $z$ *alone*; imposing $U_z = F_3$ one gets $c'(z) = 1$, therefore $c(z) = z + k$.

</div>
