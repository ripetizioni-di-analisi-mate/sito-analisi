## Exercise 1

Compute with Green's formula the area of the region enclosed by the ellipse
$$
\frac{x^2}{9} + \frac{y^2}{4} = 1 .
$$

$$
r(t) = (3 \cos t, \; 2 \sin t), \quad t \in [0, 2\pi]
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{area}
&= \frac{1}{2} \oint_{\partial D} (x \, dy - y \, dx) \\[6pt]
&= \frac{1}{2} \int_0^{2\pi} \bigl( 3 \cos t \cdot 2 \cos t - 2 \sin t \cdot (-3 \sin t) \bigr) \, dt \\[6pt]
&= \frac{1}{2} \int_0^{2\pi} 6 \, (\cos^2 t + \sin^2 t) \, dt
= \frac{6}{2} \cdot 2\pi = 6\pi
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The ellipse has semi-axes $3$ and $2$ and is parametrized by $x = 3\cos t$, $y = 2\sin t$; for increasing $t$ the direction of travel is counterclockwise, hence the boundary is **positively oriented** and the **area formula** applies with the correct sign. In the integrand of the third formula one substitutes $dy = 2\cos t \, dt$ and $dx = -3\sin t \, dt$; the term $x \, dy$ gives $6\cos^2 t$ and the term $-y \, dx$ gives $6\sin^2 t$, therefore the sum equals $6$ by the trigonometric identity. The integral of the constant $6$ over $[0, 2\pi]$, divided by $2$, gives $6\pi$, in agreement with the general formula $\pi a b$ for the area of the ellipse.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Compute the area of the region enclosed by the astroid
$$
r(t) = \bigl( \cos^3 t, \; \sin^3 t \bigr), \quad t \in [0, 2\pi] .
$$

$$
dx = -3 \cos^2 t \sin t \, dt, \qquad dy = 3 \sin^2 t \cos t \, dt
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
x \, dy - y \, dx
&= 3 \cos^4 t \sin^2 t \, dt + 3 \sin^4 t \cos^2 t \, dt \\[4pt]
&= 3 \sin^2 t \cos^2 t \, (\cos^2 t + \sin^2 t) \, dt
= \frac{3}{4} \sin^2 (2t) \, dt
\end{aligned}
$$
$$
\operatorname{area}
= \frac{1}{2} \int_0^{2\pi} \frac{3}{4} \sin^2 (2t) \, dt
= \frac{3}{8} \cdot \pi = \frac{3\pi}{8}
$$

::grafico{xmin=-1.6 xmax=1.6 ymin=-1.3 ymax=1.3 uguale=1 area="(1-cbrt(x*x))*sqrt(1-cbrt(x*x));-(1-cbrt(x*x))*sqrt(1-cbrt(x*x));-1;1" curve="cos(t)^3,sin(t)^3,0,6.29" titolo="The region enclosed by the astroid $r(t) = (\cos^3 t, \sin^3 t)$"}

<div class="spiegazione">

The astroid is a closed curve that is *not* the graph of a function, hence a Cartesian description of the region is inconvenient and **Green's formula** is the suitable tool. The differentials are computed by differentiating the components; the product $x \, dy$ contains $\cos^4 t \sin^2 t$ and the product $y \, dx$ contains $-\sin^4 t \cos^2 t$, therefore in the difference one factors out the common factor $3\sin^2 t \cos^2 t$ and the trigonometric identity eliminates the second factor. With the **double-angle formula** $\sin t \cos t = \dfrac{1}{2} \sin(2t)$ the integrand becomes $\dfrac{3}{4} \sin^2(2t)$. The integral of $\sin^2(2t)$ over $[0, 2\pi]$ equals $\pi$, since the mean value of $\sin^2$ over an integer number of periods is $\dfrac{1}{2}$; multiplying by $\dfrac{3}{4}$ and dividing by $2$ one obtains $\dfrac{3\pi}{8}$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Compute with the formula $\operatorname{area}(D) = \displaystyle\oint_{\partial D} x \, dy$ the area of the region between the parabola $y = x^2$ and the line $y = x$.

$$
D = \{ (x, y) : 0 \leq x \leq 1, \ x^2 \leq y \leq x \}
$$
Piece on the parabola, from $(0,0)$ to $(1,1)$: $\ r(t) = (t, t^2)$, $\ dy = 2t \, dt$
$$
\int_0^1 t \cdot 2t \, dt = \frac{2}{3}
$$
Piece on the line, from $(1,1)$ to $(0,0)$: $\ r(t) = (t, t)$, $\ t$ from $1$ to $0$, $\ dy = dt$
$$
\int_1^0 t \, dt = -\frac{1}{2}
$$
$$
\operatorname{area}(D) = \frac{2}{3} - \frac{1}{2} = \frac{1}{6}
$$

::grafico{xmin=-0.4 xmax=1.6 ymin=-0.4 ymax=1.4 uguale=1 area="x;x^2;0;1" curve="t,t^2,0,1" vettori="0.55,0.3025,0.75,0.5625;1,1,0,0" etichette="1.05,0.75,y = x²;0.12,0.6,y = x" titolo="The boundary of $D$ traversed counterclockwise: the arc of the parabola from $(0, 0)$ to $(1, 1)$, then the segment of the line $y = x$ back to the origin"}

<div class="spiegazione">

The two curves intersect where $x^2 = x$, that is, at the points $(0,0)$ and $(1,1)$, and between them the line lies above the parabola. The **positively oriented** boundary traverses the parabola from left to right, since it is the *lower* piece, and the line from right to left. On the parabola piece one substitutes $x = t$ and $dy = 2t \, dt$ and the integral equals $\dfrac{2}{3}$; on the line piece the integral of $t$ with the limits swapped equals $-\dfrac{1}{2}$. The sum of the two contributions is $\dfrac{1}{6}$, the same value as the integral $\displaystyle\int_0^1 (x - x^2) \, dx$ with which the area is computed in Cartesian form: the comparison **confirms the result**.

</div>
