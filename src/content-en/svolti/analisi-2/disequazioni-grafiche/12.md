## Exercise 1

Write as a normal domain with respect to the $x$-axis and with respect to the $y$-axis the region $D$ lying between the parabola $y = x^2$ and the line $y = 4$.

$$
x^2 = 4 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad x = \pm 2
$$
$$
(-2, 4), \qquad (2, 4)
$$
$$
\begin{gathered}
a = -2, \qquad b = 2 \\[6pt]
g_1(x) = x^2, \qquad g_2(x) = 4
\end{gathered}
$$
$$
D = \left\{ (x, y) :
\begin{cases}
-2 \leq x \leq 2 \\[4pt]
x^2 \leq y \leq 4
\end{cases}
\right\}
$$
$$
y = x^2 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad x = \pm\sqrt{y}
$$
$$
\begin{gathered}
c = 0, \qquad d = 4 \\[6pt]
h_1(y) = -\sqrt{y}, \qquad h_2(y) = \sqrt{y}
\end{gathered}
$$
$$
D = \left\{ (x, y) :
\begin{cases}
0 \leq y \leq 4 \\[4pt]
-\sqrt{y} \leq x \leq \sqrt{y}
\end{cases}
\right\}
$$

</div>

<div class="spiegazione">

The parabola $y = x^2$ and the line $y = 4$ meet where $x^2 = 4$, that is, at the points $(-2, 4)$ and $(2, 4)$, and the region is formed by the points above the parabola and below the line. For the form with respect to the $x$-axis we project the region onto the $x$-axis and obtain the interval $[-2, 2]$. Every vertical segment with $x$-coordinate between $-2$ and $2$ enters the region through the parabola and exits through the line, hence $g_1(x) = x^2$ and $g_2(x) = 4$. The projection onto the $y$-axis is the interval $[0, 4]$, because the lowest $y$-coordinate of the region is that of the vertex of the parabola. Isolating $x$ in the equation of the parabola we obtain the left half $x = -\sqrt{y}$ and the right half $x = \sqrt{y}$. Every horizontal segment enters through the left half and exits through the right half, hence $h_1(y) = -\sqrt{y}$ and $h_2(y) = \sqrt{y}$. The region is a **normal domain with respect to both axes**, and from each of the two descriptions we read off the limits of integration of a double integral over $D$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Write as a normal domain with respect to the $x$-axis and with respect to the $y$-axis the triangle $D$ with vertices $(0, 0)$, $(1, 1)$ and $(2, 0)$.

$$
(0, 0),\ (1, 1): \qquad y = x
$$
$$
(1, 1),\ (2, 0): \qquad m = \frac{0 - 1}{2 - 1} = -1
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
y - 1 &= -(x - 1) \\[6pt]
y &= 2 - x
\end{aligned}
$$
$$
(0, 0),\ (2, 0): \qquad y = 0
$$
$$
D_1 = \left\{ (x, y) :
\begin{cases}
0 \leq x \leq 1 \\[4pt]
0 \leq y \leq x
\end{cases}
\right\}
$$
$$
D_2 = \left\{ (x, y) :
\begin{cases}
1 \leq x \leq 2 \\[4pt]
0 \leq y \leq 2 - x
\end{cases}
\right\}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
y = x &\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad x = y \\[6pt]
y = 2 - x &\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad x = 2 - y
\end{aligned}
$$
$$
\begin{gathered}
c = 0, \qquad d = 1 \\[6pt]
h_1(y) = y, \qquad h_2(y) = 2 - y
\end{gathered}
$$
$$
D = \left\{ (x, y) :
\begin{cases}
0 \leq y \leq 1 \\[4pt]
y \leq x \leq 2 - y
\end{cases}
\right\}
$$

</div>

<div class="spiegazione">

The sides of the triangle are the segment of the line $y = x$ between $(0, 0)$ and $(1, 1)$, the segment of the line $y = 2 - x$ between $(1, 1)$ and $(2, 0)$ and the segment of the $x$-axis between $(0, 0)$ and $(2, 0)$. The projection onto the $x$-axis is the interval $[0, 2]$, and every vertical segment enters the triangle through the $x$-axis, hence $g_1(x) = 0$. The exit curve is the line $y = x$ for $0 \leq x \leq 1$ and the line $y = 2 - x$ for $1 \leq x \leq 2$, so the function $g_2$ is **defined piecewise**. The triangle is therefore split into **two normal domains** $D_1$ and $D_2$, one for each piece, separated by the vertical segment with $x$-coordinate $1$. The projection onto the $y$-axis is the interval $[0, 1]$. Isolating $x$ in the equations of the two oblique sides we obtain $x = y$ and $x = 2 - y$, and every horizontal segment enters through the first side and exits through the second. With respect to the $y$-axis the functions $h_1(y) = y$ and $h_2(y) = 2 - y$ therefore have **a single expression**, and a double integral over the triangle is computed with a single iterated integral.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Write as a normal domain with respect to the $x$-axis and with respect to the $y$-axis the region $D$ in the first quadrant lying between the parabola $y = x^2$ and the parabola $x = y^2$.

$$
\begin{cases}
y = x^2 \\[4pt]
x = y^2
\end{cases}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
x &= x^4 \\[6pt]
x(x^3 - 1) &= 0 \\[6pt]
x = 0 \quad &\text{or} \quad x = 1
\end{aligned}
$$
$$
(0, 0), \qquad (1, 1)
$$
$$
\begin{cases}
x = y^2 \\[4pt]
y \geq 0
\end{cases}
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
y = \sqrt{x}
$$
$$
x = \frac{1}{4}: \qquad x^2 = \frac{1}{16} < \frac{1}{2} = \sqrt{x}
$$
$$
\begin{gathered}
a = 0, \qquad b = 1 \\[6pt]
g_1(x) = x^2, \qquad g_2(x) = \sqrt{x}
\end{gathered}
$$
$$
D = \left\{ (x, y) :
\begin{cases}
0 \leq x \leq 1 \\[4pt]
x^2 \leq y \leq \sqrt{x}
\end{cases}
\right\}
$$
$$
\begin{cases}
y = x^2 \\[4pt]
x \geq 0
\end{cases}
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
x = \sqrt{y}
$$
$$
\begin{gathered}
c = 0, \qquad d = 1 \\[6pt]
h_1(y) = y^2, \qquad h_2(y) = \sqrt{y}
\end{gathered}
$$
$$
D = \left\{ (x, y) :
\begin{cases}
0 \leq y \leq 1 \\[4pt]
y^2 \leq x \leq \sqrt{y}
\end{cases}
\right\}
$$

</div>

<div class="spiegazione">

Substituting $y = x^2$ into $x = y^2$ we obtain $x = x^4$, so the two parabolas meet at $(0, 0)$ and $(1, 1)$, and the projection of the region onto the $x$-axis is the interval $[0, 1]$. In the first quadrant the parabola $x = y^2$ is written $y = \sqrt{x}$, which is its upper half. The two curves meet only at the endpoints of the interval, so between $0$ and $1$ one of them always stays below the other. For $x = \dfrac{1}{4}$ the parabola $y = x^2$ has $y$-coordinate $\dfrac{1}{16}$ and the curve $y = \sqrt{x}$ has $y$-coordinate $\dfrac{1}{2}$, hence the lower curve is $y = x^2$. Every vertical segment enters through the parabola $y = x^2$ and exits through the curve $y = \sqrt{x}$, hence $g_1(x) = x^2$ and $g_2(x) = \sqrt{x}$. Exchanging $x$ and $y$ swaps the equations of the two parabolas, so the form with respect to the $y$-axis is obtained from the form with respect to the $x$-axis by exchanging the variables. The projection onto the $y$-axis is $[0, 1]$, the curve on the left is $x = y^2$ and the curve on the right is $x = \sqrt{y}$. The region is a **normal domain with respect to both axes**.

</div>

## Exercise 4

Write as a normal domain with respect to the $x$-axis and with respect to the $y$-axis, and then in polar coordinates, the quarter disk $D$ described by the system
$$
\begin{cases}
x^2 + y^2 \leq 4 \\[4pt]
x \geq 0 \\[4pt]
y \geq 0
\end{cases}
$$

$$
\begin{cases}
x^2 + y^2 \leq 4 \\[4pt]
y \geq 0
\end{cases}
\Longleftrightarrow
0 \leq y \leq \sqrt{4 - x^2}
$$
$$
\begin{gathered}
a = 0, \qquad b = 2 \\[6pt]
g_1(x) = 0, \qquad g_2(x) = \sqrt{4 - x^2}
\end{gathered}
$$
$$
D = \left\{ (x, y) :
\begin{cases}
0 \leq x \leq 2 \\[4pt]
0 \leq y \leq \sqrt{4 - x^2}
\end{cases}
\right\}
$$
$$
\begin{cases}
x^2 + y^2 \leq 4 \\[4pt]
x \geq 0
\end{cases}
\Longleftrightarrow
0 \leq x \leq \sqrt{4 - y^2}
$$
$$
\begin{gathered}
c = 0, \qquad d = 2 \\[6pt]
h_1(y) = 0, \qquad h_2(y) = \sqrt{4 - y^2}
\end{gathered}
$$
$$
D = \left\{ (x, y) :
\begin{cases}
0 \leq y \leq 2 \\[4pt]
0 \leq x \leq \sqrt{4 - y^2}
\end{cases}
\right\}
$$
$$
x = \rho\cos\theta, \qquad y = \rho\sin\theta
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
x^2 + y^2 \leq 4 &\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \rho^2 \leq 4 \\[6pt]
&\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad 0 \leq \rho \leq 2
\end{aligned}
$$
$$
\begin{cases}
\cos\theta \geq 0 \\[4pt]
\sin\theta \geq 0
\end{cases}
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
0 \leq \theta \leq \frac{\pi}{2}
$$
$$
\begin{cases}
0 \leq \rho \leq 2 \\[4pt]
0 \leq \theta \leq \dfrac{\pi}{2}
\end{cases}
$$

</div>

<div class="spiegazione">

The boundary of $x^2 + y^2 \leq 4$ is the circle with center the origin and radius $2$. The conditions $x \geq 0$ and $y \geq 0$ are satisfied at the points of the first quadrant, axes included, so $D$ is the part of the disk that lies in the first quadrant. The projection onto the $x$-axis is the interval $[0, 2]$, and isolating $y$ with $y \geq 0$ we obtain the upper semicircle $y = \sqrt{4 - x^2}$. Every vertical segment enters through the $x$-axis and exits through the semicircle, hence $g_1(x) = 0$ and $g_2(x) = \sqrt{4 - x^2}$. The system stays the same when $x$ and $y$ are exchanged, so the form with respect to the $y$-axis is obtained by exchanging the two variables. In polar coordinates we have $x^2 + y^2 = \rho^2$, so the first inequality becomes $0 \leq \rho \leq 2$. The conditions $x \geq 0$ and $y \geq 0$ require $\cos\theta \geq 0$ and $\sin\theta \geq 0$, that is, $0 \leq \theta \leq \dfrac{\pi}{2}$. In polar coordinates the quarter disk therefore has **constant limits** for both variables, while in the two normal forms one of the limits contains a root.

</div>
