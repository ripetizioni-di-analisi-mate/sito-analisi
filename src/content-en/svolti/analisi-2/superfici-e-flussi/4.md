## Exercise 1

Compute
$$
\iint_\Sigma z \; d\sigma,
$$
where $\Sigma$ is the portion of the plane $z = 1 - x - y$ that projects onto the triangle $T$ with vertices $(0, 0)$, $(1, 0)$, $(0, 1)$.

$$
f(x, y) = 1 - x - y, \qquad d\sigma = \sqrt{1 + 1 + 1} \; dx \, dy = \sqrt{3} \; dx \, dy
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
\iint_\Sigma z \; d\sigma
&= \sqrt{3} \int_0^1 \!\! \int_0^{1 - x} (1 - x - y) \; dy \, dx \\[6pt]
&= \sqrt{3} \int_0^1 \frac{(1 - x)^2}{2} \; dx \\[6pt]
&= \sqrt{3} \cdot \frac{1}{6} = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{6}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The surface is the graph of $f(x, y) = 1 - x - y$, so the area element is $\sqrt{1 + f_x^2 + f_y^2} \, dx \, dy = \sqrt{3} \, dx \, dy$, **constant** because the graph is a plane. On the surface the function to be integrated equals $z = 1 - x - y$, therefore the surface integral reduces to the **double integral** of $(1 - x - y)$ over the triangle, multiplied by $\sqrt{3}$. The triangle is normal with respect to the $x$-axis, with $y$ varying between $0$ and $1 - x$: the inner integral equals $\dfrac{(1-x)^2}{2}$, since the integrand vanishes on the upper boundary. The outer integral gives $\displaystyle\int_0^1 \frac{(1-x)^2}{2} dx = \frac{1}{6}$, so the result is $\dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{6}$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Compute
$$
\iint_\Sigma \bigl( x^2 + y^2 \bigr) \, d\sigma,
$$
where $\Sigma$ is the sphere $x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 4$.

$$
\mathbf{r}(\varphi, \theta) = (2 \sin\varphi \cos\theta,\; 2 \sin\varphi \sin\theta,\; 2 \cos\varphi),
\qquad
d\sigma = 4 \sin\varphi \; d\varphi \, d\theta
$$
$$
x^2 + y^2 = 4 \sin^2\varphi
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
\iint_\Sigma \bigl( x^2 + y^2 \bigr) \, d\sigma
&= \int_0^{2\pi} \!\! \int_0^{\pi} 4 \sin^2\varphi \cdot 4 \sin\varphi \; d\varphi \, d\theta \\[6pt]
&= 32\pi \int_0^{\pi} \sin^3\varphi \; d\varphi \\[6pt]
&= 32\pi \cdot \frac{4}{3} = \frac{128\pi}{3}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

On the sphere parametrized in spherical coordinates we have $x^2 + y^2 = 4\sin^2\varphi$, since $\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta = 1$, and the area element is $4 \sin\varphi \, d\varphi \, d\theta$. The integrand does *not* depend on $\theta$, so the integral in $\theta$ produces the factor $2\pi$. For the integral of $\sin^3\varphi$ we write $\sin^3\varphi = (1 - \cos^2\varphi) \sin\varphi$ and integrate with the **substitution** $w = \cos\varphi$: the result is $\left[ -\cos\varphi + \dfrac{\cos^3\varphi}{3} \right]_0^{\pi} = \dfrac{4}{3}$. The final value is $\dfrac{128\pi}{3}$. A **check by symmetry**: the integrals of $x^2$, $y^2$ and $z^2$ over the sphere are equal to one another, so the integral of $x^2 + y^2$ is $\dfrac{2}{3}$ of the integral of $x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 4$, that is, $\dfrac{2}{3} \cdot 4 \cdot 16\pi = \dfrac{128\pi}{3}$, since the area of the sphere is $4\pi R^2 = 16\pi$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Compute
$$
\iint_\Sigma x^2 z \; d\sigma,
$$
where $\Sigma$ is the lateral surface of the cylinder $x^2 + y^2 = 4$ with $0 \leq z \leq 3$.

$$
\mathbf{r}(\theta, z) = (2 \cos\theta,\; 2 \sin\theta,\; z), \qquad d\sigma = 2 \; d\theta \, dz
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
\iint_\Sigma x^2 z \; d\sigma
&= \int_0^3 \!\! \int_0^{2\pi} 4\cos^2\theta \cdot z \cdot 2 \; d\theta \, dz \\[6pt]
&= 8 \int_0^{2\pi} \cos^2\theta \; d\theta \, \int_0^3 z \; dz \\[6pt]
&= 8 \cdot \pi \cdot \frac{9}{2} = 36\pi
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

On the cylinder of radius $2$ we have $x = 2\cos\theta$, so the function to be integrated becomes $4 \cos^2\theta \, z$; the area element of the cylinder is $d\sigma = R \, d\theta \, dz = 2 \, d\theta \, dz$. The integrand is the product of a function of $\theta$ and a function of $z$ over a parameter rectangle, therefore the double integral factors into the **product of the two single integrals**. The integral of $\cos^2\theta$ over one period equals $\pi$, by the formula $\cos^2\theta = \dfrac{1 + \cos 2\theta}{2}$; the integral of $z$ between $0$ and $3$ equals $\dfrac{9}{2}$. The result is $8 \cdot \pi \cdot \dfrac{9}{2} = 36\pi$.

</div>
