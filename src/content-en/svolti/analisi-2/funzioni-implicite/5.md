## Exercise 1

Verify that the equation
$$
x^2 + y^2 + z^3 + z = 4
$$
implicitly defines a function $z = g(x, y)$ near the point $(1, 1, 1)$, compute $g_x(1, 1)$ and $g_y(1, 1)$ and write the equation of the tangent plane.

$$
F(x, y, z) = x^2 + y^2 + z^3 + z - 4, \qquad F(1, 1, 1) = 1 + 1 + 1 + 1 - 4 = 0
$$
$$
F_z(1, 1, 1) = 3z^2 + 1 \big|_{(1,1,1)} = 4 \neq 0
$$
$$
g_x(1, 1) = -\,\frac{F_x}{F_z} = -\,\frac{2}{4} = -\,\frac{1}{2},
\qquad
g_y(1, 1) = -\,\frac{F_y}{F_z} = -\,\frac{2}{4} = -\,\frac{1}{2}
$$
$$
2(x - 1) + 2(y - 1) + 4(z - 1) = 0
\qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad
x + y + 2z = 4
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The point belongs to the surface, since $F(1, 1, 1) = 0$, and the partial derivative with respect to $z$ at the point equals $4$, which is nonzero, therefore **Dini's theorem in three variables** applies: near the point the surface is the **graph of a function** $z = g(x, y)$ with $g(1, 1) = 1$. The partial derivatives of $g$ are obtained from the formulas $g_x = -\dfrac{F_x}{F_z}$ and $g_y = -\dfrac{F_y}{F_z}$ with the values at the point $F_x = 2$, $F_y = 2$, $F_z = 4$, and both equal $-\dfrac{1}{2}$. The tangent plane is written in the **gradient form**, $F_x(x - 1) + F_y(y - 1) + F_z(z - 1) = 0$; dividing by $2$ we obtain $x + y + 2z = 4$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Verify that the equation
$$
e^{z} + xz + y^2 = 2
$$
implicitly defines a function $z = g(x, y)$ near the point $(1, 1, 0)$ and write the equation of the tangent plane to the surface at that point.

$$
F(x, y, z) = e^{z} + xz + y^2 - 2, \qquad F(1, 1, 0) = 1 + 0 + 1 - 2 = 0
$$
$$
F_z(1, 1, 0) = e^{z} + x \big|_{(1,1,0)} = 1 + 1 = 2 \neq 0
$$
$$
F_x(1, 1, 0) = z \big|_{(1,1,0)} = 0, \qquad F_y(1, 1, 0) = 2y \big|_{(1,1,0)} = 2
$$
$$
0 \cdot (x - 1) + 2(y - 1) + 2(z - 0) = 0
\qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad
z = 1 - y
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

At the point the exponential equals $e^0 = 1$, hence $F(1, 1, 0) = 0$ and the point belongs to the surface. The derivative with respect to $z$ is $e^z + x$, which equals $2$ at the point, which is nonzero, therefore **Dini's theorem** applies and the surface is locally the **graph of a function** $z = g(x, y)$. The other partial derivatives at the point are $F_x = z = 0$ and $F_y = 2y = 2$. The tangent plane $F_x(x - 1) + F_y(y - 1) + F_z\,z = 0$ becomes $2(y - 1) + 2z = 0$, that is $z = 1 - y$. From the same equation one reads off the derivatives of the implicit function, $g_x(1, 1) = 0$ and $g_y(1, 1) = -1$: the plane does *not* depend on $x$ because at the point the surface has **zero slope** in the $x$ direction.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Verify that the equation
$$
x^2 z^3 + x y^2 - z = 1
$$
implicitly defines a function $z = g(x, y)$ near the point $(1, 1, 1)$, compute $\nabla g(1, 1)$ and write the equation of the tangent plane.

$$
F(x, y, z) = x^2 z^3 + x y^2 - z - 1, \qquad F(1, 1, 1) = 1 + 1 - 1 - 1 = 0
$$
$$
F_z(1, 1, 1) = 3x^2 z^2 - 1 \big|_{(1,1,1)} = 2 \neq 0
$$
$$
F_x(1, 1, 1) = 2x z^3 + y^2 \big|_{(1,1,1)} = 3, \qquad F_y(1, 1, 1) = 2xy \big|_{(1,1,1)} = 2
$$
$$
\nabla g(1, 1) = \left( -\,\frac{3}{2},\; -1 \right)
$$
$$
3(x - 1) + 2(y - 1) + 2(z - 1) = 0
\qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad
3x + 2y + 2z = 7
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The point belongs to the surface and $F_z(1, 1, 1) = 2$ is nonzero, hence the surface is locally the **graph of a function** $z = g(x, y)$ with $g(1, 1) = 1$. In computing the partial derivatives, the term $x^2 z^3$ contributes $2x z^3$ to $F_x$ and $3x^2 z^2$ to $F_z$, while the term $x y^2$ contributes $y^2$ to $F_x$ and $2xy$ to $F_y$. From the **formulas of the theorem** it follows that $g_x(1, 1) = -\dfrac{3}{2}$ and $g_y(1, 1) = -\dfrac{2}{2} = -1$, which form the required gradient. The tangent plane, written with the gradient of $F$ at the point, is $3(x-1) + 2(y-1) + 2(z-1) = 0$; moving the constant terms to the right-hand side we obtain $3x + 2y + 2z = 7$. The *same* plane is obtained from the form $z = 1 + g_x(1,1)(x - 1) + g_y(1,1)(y - 1)$, multiplying by $2$.

</div>
