## Exercise 1

Find the points of the ellipse
$$
x^2 + 4y^2 = 4
$$
at which Dini's theorem does not apply with respect to the variable $y$, and study the curve near those points.

$$
F(x, y) = x^2 + 4y^2 - 4, \qquad F_y(x, y) = 8y
$$
$$
F_y = 0 \quad \Longrightarrow \quad y = 0 \quad \Longrightarrow \quad x^2 = 4 \quad \Longrightarrow \quad (2, 0), \ (-2, 0)
$$
$$
F_x(\pm 2, 0) = 2x \big|_{x = \pm 2} = \pm 4 \neq 0
$$
$$
\psi'(0) = -\,\frac{F_y(\pm 2, 0)}{F_x(\pm 2, 0)} = 0,
\qquad \text{tangent lines: } x = 2 \ \text{ and } \ x = -2
$$

::grafico{curve="2*cos(t),sin(t),0,6.29" segmenti="2,-1.4,2,1.4;-2,-1.4,-2,1.4" punti="2,0,pieno;-2,0,pieno" xmin=-3 xmax=3 ymin=-1.6 ymax=1.8 uguale=1 titolo="The ellipse $x^2 + 4y^2 = 4$: at the points $(\pm 2, 0)$ the tangent is vertical and Dini's theorem applies with respect to $x$"}

<div class="spiegazione">

The condition of the theorem with respect to $y$ is $F_y \neq 0$, hence the points sought are those of the curve where $F_y = 8y = 0$. From $y = 0$ and the equation of the ellipse it follows that $x = \pm 2$: the points are $(2, 0)$ and $(-2, 0)$. At these points $F_x = \pm 4$ is nonzero, therefore **Dini's theorem** applies with the roles of the variables **exchanged**: near each of the two points the ellipse is the graph of a function $x = \psi(y)$ with $\psi'(0) = -\dfrac{F_y}{F_x} = 0$. Since the derivative of $\psi$ is zero, the tangent line is **vertical**: $x = 2$ at one point and $x = -2$ at the other, as the shape of the ellipse suggests.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Determine whether the equation
$$
x^2 - y^2 = 0
$$
implicitly defines a function $y = \varphi(x)$ near the point $(0, 0)$.

$$
F(x, y) = x^2 - y^2, \qquad F(0, 0) = 0
$$
$$
\nabla F(x, y) = (2x, -2y), \qquad \nabla F(0, 0) = (0, 0)
$$
$$
x^2 - y^2 = 0 \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad y = x \ \text{ or } \ y = -x
$$
$$
\text{No function } y = \varphi(x) \text{ is implicitly defined near } (0,0).
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

At the point $(0, 0)$ *both* partial derivatives are zero, hence the point is **singular** and Dini's theorem provides no information, neither with respect to $y$ nor with respect to $x$. The existence of the implicit function must therefore be decided by studying the zero set directly. Since $x^2 - y^2 = (x - y)(x + y)$, the set is the **union of the two lines** $y = x$ and $y = -x$. In every neighborhood of the origin, each value $x \neq 0$ corresponds to the two distinct points $(x, x)$ and $(x, -x)$ of the set, therefore **no function** $y = \varphi(x)$ can describe it near the origin. For the same reason, with the roles exchanged, there is no function $x = \psi(y)$ either.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Determine whether the equation
$$
(y - x)^3 = 0
$$
implicitly defines a function $y = \varphi(x)$ near the point $(0, 0)$, and compare the answer with the hypotheses of Dini's theorem.

$$
F(x, y) = (y - x)^3, \qquad F(0, 0) = 0
$$
$$
\nabla F(x, y) = \bigl( -3(y - x)^2,\; 3(y - x)^2 \bigr), \qquad \nabla F(0, 0) = (0, 0)
$$
$$
(y - x)^3 = 0 \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad y = x
$$
$$
\varphi(x) = x \ \text{ implicitly defined at every point, with } \varphi'(x) = 1
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Here too the gradient is zero at the origin, and moreover $\nabla F = \bigl( -3(y-x)^2, 3(y-x)^2 \bigr)$ vanishes at every point of the zero set, hence Dini's theorem **does not apply** at any point of the curve. The zero set, however, can be determined directly: a cube is zero *if and only if* its base is zero, therefore the equation is equivalent to $y = x$. The set is thus the **graph of the function** $\varphi(x) = x$, which is smooth everywhere. The comparison with the previous exercise shows that the condition $F_y \neq 0$ is **sufficient but not necessary**: when it is not satisfied, the implicit function may fail to exist, as for $x^2 - y^2 = 0$, or it may exist, as in this case.

</div>
