## Exercise 1

Verify that the equation
$$
x^3 + y^3 - 2xy = 0
$$
implicitly defines a function $y = \varphi(x)$ near the point $(1, 1)$ and compute $\varphi'(1)$.

$$
F(x, y) = x^3 + y^3 - 2xy, \qquad F(1, 1) = 1 + 1 - 2 = 0
$$
$$
F_y(x, y) = 3y^2 - 2x, \qquad F_y(1, 1) = 3 - 2 = 1 \neq 0
$$
$$
F_x(x, y) = 3x^2 - 2y, \qquad F_x(1, 1) = 3 - 2 = 1
$$
$$
\varphi'(1) = -\,\frac{F_x(1, 1)}{F_y(1, 1)} = -\,\frac{1}{1} = -1
$$

::grafico{curve="2*tan(t)/(1+tan(t)^3),2*tan(t)^2/(1+tan(t)^3),-1.53,1.56;t,2-t,-0.2,2.2" punti="1,1,pieno" xmin=-1.5 xmax=2.2 ymin=-1.3 ymax=2.2 uguale=1 titolo="The curve $x^3 + y^3 - 2xy = 0$ and the tangent line $y = 2 - x$ at the point $(1, 1)$ (dashed), with slope $\varphi'(1) = -1$"}

<div class="spiegazione">

We check the two hypotheses of **Dini's theorem** at the point. The point belongs to the curve, since $F(1, 1) = 0$; the partial derivative with respect to $y$ at the point is $F_y(1, 1) = 1$, which is nonzero, therefore the theorem applies and near $(1, 1)$ the curve is the **graph of a function** $y = \varphi(x)$ of class $C^1$ with $\varphi(1) = 1$. The derivative is computed with the formula of the theorem, which requires *only* the partial derivatives of $F$ at the point: since $F_x(1, 1) = 1$ and $F_y(1, 1) = 1$, we get $\varphi'(1) = -1$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Verify that the equation
$$
e^{xy} + x^2 - y - 2 = 0
$$
implicitly defines a function $y = \varphi(x)$ near the point $(0, -1)$ and compute $\varphi'(0)$.

$$
F(x, y) = e^{xy} + x^2 - y - 2, \qquad F(0, -1) = 1 + 0 + 1 - 2 = 0
$$
$$
F_y(x, y) = x\,e^{xy} - 1, \qquad F_y(0, -1) = 0 - 1 = -1 \neq 0
$$
$$
F_x(x, y) = y\,e^{xy} + 2x, \qquad F_x(0, -1) = -1 + 0 = -1
$$
$$
\varphi'(0) = -\,\frac{F_x(0, -1)}{F_y(0, -1)} = -\,\frac{-1}{-1} = -1
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The point $(0, -1)$ belongs to the curve because, after substitution, the exponential equals $e^0 = 1$ and the sum $1 + 0 + 1 - 2$ is zero. To differentiate $e^{xy}$ with respect to $y$ we apply the **chain rule**: the derivative of the exponent $xy$ with respect to $y$ is $x$, hence $\dfrac{\partial}{\partial y} e^{xy} = x\,e^{xy}$; at the point this term is zero and what remains is $F_y(0, -1) = -1$, which is nonzero, therefore **Dini's theorem** applies. In the same way $\dfrac{\partial}{\partial x} e^{xy} = y\,e^{xy}$, which equals $-1$ at the point. The derivative formula gives $\varphi'(0) = -\dfrac{-1}{-1} = -1$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Verify that the equation
$$
x \ln y + y\, e^{x-1} - 1 = 0
$$
implicitly defines a function $y = \varphi(x)$ near the point $(1, 1)$ and compute $\varphi'(1)$.

$$
F(x, y) = x \ln y + y\, e^{x-1} - 1, \qquad F(1, 1) = 1 \cdot 0 + 1 \cdot 1 - 1 = 0
$$
$$
F_y(x, y) = \frac{x}{y} + e^{x-1}, \qquad F_y(1, 1) = 1 + 1 = 2 \neq 0
$$
$$
F_x(x, y) = \ln y + y\, e^{x-1}, \qquad F_x(1, 1) = 0 + 1 = 1
$$
$$
\varphi'(1) = -\,\frac{F_x(1, 1)}{F_y(1, 1)} = -\,\frac{1}{2}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The function $F$ is of class $C^1$ on the open set $\{ y > 0 \}$, which contains the point $(1, 1)$. At the point the logarithm vanishes and the exponential equals $e^0 = 1$, hence $F(1, 1) = 0$ and the point **belongs to the curve**. The derivative with respect to $y$ has two terms, $\dfrac{x}{y}$ from the term $x \ln y$ and $e^{x-1}$ from the term $y\,e^{x-1}$; at the point their sum equals $2$, which is nonzero, therefore **Dini's theorem** applies. In the derivative with respect to $x$ the first term is $\ln y$, which is zero at the point, and the second is $y\,e^{x-1}$, which equals $1$. From the formula of the theorem it follows that $\varphi'(1) = -\dfrac{1}{2}$.

</div>
