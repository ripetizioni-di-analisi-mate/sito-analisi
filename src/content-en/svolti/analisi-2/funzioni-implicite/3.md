## Exercise 1

Verify that the equation
$$
x^2 + y^3 + y = 3
$$
implicitly defines a function $y = \varphi(x)$ near the point $(1, 1)$ and compute $\varphi'(1)$ and $\varphi''(1)$.

$$
F(x, y) = x^2 + y^3 + y - 3, \qquad F(1, 1) = 1 + 1 + 1 - 3 = 0
$$
$$
F_y(1, 1) = 3y^2 + 1 \big|_{(1,1)} = 4 \neq 0
$$
$$
2x + 3y^2 y' + y' = 0 \qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad \varphi'(1) = -\,\frac{2}{4} = -\,\frac{1}{2}
$$
$$
2 + 6y\,(y')^2 + (3y^2 + 1)\,y'' = 0
$$
$$
2 + 6 \cdot \frac{1}{4} + 4\,y'' = 0 \qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad \varphi''(1) = -\,\frac{7}{8}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The point belongs to the curve and $F_y(1, 1) = 4$ is nonzero, therefore **Dini's theorem** applies; since $F$ is a polynomial, hence of class $C^2$, the implicit function is of class $C^2$. For the derivatives we **differentiate the identity** $x^2 + \varphi(x)^3 + \varphi(x) = 3$ with respect to $x$: we obtain $2x + (3y^2 + 1)\,y' = 0$, which at the point gives $2 + 4\,\varphi'(1) = 0$, therefore $\varphi'(1) = -\dfrac{1}{2}$. Differentiating a second time, the term $3y^2 y'$ produces $6y\,(y')^2 + 3y^2 y''$ by the **product rule**, hence the identity becomes $2 + 6y\,(y')^2 + (3y^2 + 1)\,y'' = 0$. Substituting $y = 1$ and $y' = -\dfrac{1}{2}$ we obtain $2 + \dfrac{3}{2} + 4\,y'' = 0$, from which $\varphi''(1) = -\dfrac{7}{8}$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Verify that the equation
$$
y^3 + y + x^2 - 2x - 1 = 0
$$
implicitly defines a function $y = \varphi(x)$ near the point $(1, 1)$, and determine whether $x_0 = 1$ is a local maximum or minimum point for $\varphi$.

$$
F(x, y) = y^3 + y + x^2 - 2x - 1, \qquad F(1, 1) = 1 + 1 + 1 - 2 - 1 = 0
$$
$$
F_y(1, 1) = 3y^2 + 1 \big|_{(1,1)} = 4 \neq 0, \qquad F_x(1, 1) = 2x - 2 \big|_{(1,1)} = 0
$$
$$
\varphi'(1) = -\,\frac{0}{4} = 0
$$
$$
(3y^2 + 1)\,y'' + 6y\,(y')^2 + 2 = 0
\qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad
4\,\varphi''(1) + 0 + 2 = 0
$$
$$
\varphi''(1) = -\,\frac{1}{2} < 0
\qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad
x_0 = 1 \ \text{local maximum point}
$$

::grafico{f="cbrt((1+2*x-x^2)/2+sqrt((1+2*x-x^2)^2/4+1/27))+cbrt((1+2*x-x^2)/2-sqrt((1+2*x-x^2)^2/4+1/27))" segmenti="0,1,2,1" punti="1,1,pieno" xmin=-1.5 xmax=3.5 ymin=-2 ymax=2 titolo="The curve $y^3 + y + x^2 - 2x - 1 = 0$ is the graph of $\varphi$: at $x = 1$ the tangent is horizontal and $\varphi$ has a local maximum"}

<div class="spiegazione">

The hypotheses of Dini's theorem are satisfied at the point, therefore the implicit function $\varphi$ exists and is of class $C^2$, because $F$ is a polynomial. Since $F_x(1, 1) = 0$, the first derivative $\varphi'(1) = -\dfrac{F_x}{F_y}$ is zero, therefore $x_0 = 1$ is a **stationary point** of $\varphi$. For the second derivative we differentiate the identity $\varphi(x)^3 + \varphi(x) + x^2 - 2x - 1 = 0$ twice: the first differentiation gives $(3y^2 + 1)\,y' + 2x - 2 = 0$ and the second gives $6y\,(y')^2 + (3y^2 + 1)\,y'' + 2 = 0$. At the point $y' = 0$, hence the first term is zero and what remains is $4\,y'' + 2 = 0$, that is $\varphi''(1) = -\dfrac{1}{2}$. Since the second derivative is *negative* at a stationary point, $x_0 = 1$ is a **local maximum point** for the implicit function.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Verify that the equation
$$
e^{y} + y - x^2 - 1 = 0
$$
implicitly defines a function $y = \varphi(x)$ near the point $(0, 0)$, study its monotonicity and determine the nature of the point $x_0 = 0$.

$$
F(x, y) = e^{y} + y - x^2 - 1, \qquad F(0, 0) = 1 + 0 - 0 - 1 = 0
$$
$$
F_y(x, y) = e^{y} + 1 > 0 \quad \text{for every } (x, y)
$$
$$
\varphi'(x) = -\,\frac{-2x}{e^{y} + 1} = \frac{2x}{e^{\varphi(x)} + 1}
$$
$$
\varphi'(x) < 0 \ \text{ for } x < 0,
\qquad
\varphi'(x) > 0 \ \text{ for } x > 0
$$
$$
e^{y} (y')^2 + e^{y} y'' + y'' - 2 = 0
\qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad
2\,\varphi''(0) - 2 = 0
$$
$$
\varphi''(0) = 1 > 0
\qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad
x_0 = 0 \ \text{minimum point}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The point $(0, 0)$ belongs to the curve and the derivative $F_y = e^y + 1$ is positive at *every* point of the plane, therefore **Dini's theorem** applies and the implicit function is defined and of class $C^2$ near $0$. The formula for the first derivative gives $\varphi'(x) = \dfrac{2x}{e^{\varphi(x)} + 1}$: the denominator is always positive, therefore the sign of $\varphi'$ coincides with the sign of $x$. Hence $\varphi$ is **decreasing** for $x < 0$ and **increasing** for $x > 0$, and from this alone $x_0 = 0$ turns out to be a minimum point. The second derivative confirms the result: differentiating the identity $e^{\varphi} + \varphi - x^2 - 1 = 0$ twice we obtain $e^{y}(y')^2 + (e^{y} + 1)\,y'' - 2 = 0$, and at the point, where $y = 0$ and $y' = 0$, what remains is $2\,y'' - 2 = 0$, that is $\varphi''(0) = 1 > 0$.

</div>
