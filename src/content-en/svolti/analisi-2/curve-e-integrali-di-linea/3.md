## Exercise 1

Compute the length of the semicircle
$$
\gamma(t) = (2\cos t,\; 2\sin t), \qquad t \in [0, \pi].
$$

$$
\gamma'(t) = (-2\sin t,\; 2\cos t)
$$
$$
\lVert \gamma'(t) \rVert = \sqrt{4\sin^2 t + 4\cos^2 t} = 2
$$
$$
L(\gamma) = \int_0^\pi 2 \, dt = 2\pi
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The curve is **regular**, since the norm of the derivative vector equals $2$ for *every* $t$ and in particular never vanishes. In computing the norm one factors out $4$ under the square root and applies the identity $\sin^2 t + \cos^2 t = 1$. The integral of a constant over the interval $[0, \pi]$ is the product of the constant and the length of the interval, hence $L(\gamma) = 2\pi$. The result is **half the length** $2\pi r = 4\pi$ of the circle of radius $2$, in agreement with the fact that the parameter interval covers half a turn.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Compute the length of the curve
$$
\gamma(t) = \left( t,\; t^{3/2} \right), \qquad t \in [0, 4].
$$

$$
\gamma'(t) = \left( 1,\; \frac{3}{2}\, t^{1/2} \right), \qquad
\lVert \gamma'(t) \rVert = \sqrt{1 + \frac{9}{4}\, t}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
L(\gamma) &= \int_0^4 \sqrt{1 + \frac{9}{4}\, t} \; dt \\[6pt]
&= \left[ \frac{4}{9} \cdot \frac{2}{3} \left( 1 + \frac{9}{4}\, t \right)^{\!3/2} \right]_0^4 \\[6pt]
&= \frac{8}{27} \left( 10^{3/2} - 1 \right) = \frac{8}{27} \left( 10\sqrt{10} - 1 \right)
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The curve is **Cartesian**, that is, of the form $(t, f(t))$ with $f(t) = t^{3/2}$, hence the first component of the derivative vector equals $1$ and the norm is $\sqrt{1 + f'(t)^2}$ with $f'(t) = \dfrac{3}{2} t^{1/2}$. The square of $\dfrac{3}{2} t^{1/2}$ is $\dfrac{9}{4} t$, therefore the integrand is $\left(1 + \dfrac{9}{4} t\right)^{1/2}$. An antiderivative is obtained with the **power rule**: one raises the exponent to $\dfrac{3}{2}$, divides by the new exponent and divides by the inner derivative $\dfrac{9}{4}$, which gives the factor $\dfrac{4}{9} \cdot \dfrac{2}{3} = \dfrac{8}{27}$. At $t = 4$ the argument equals $1 + 9 = 10$ and at $t = 0$ it equals $1$, hence the **length** is $\dfrac{8}{27}\left(10\sqrt{10} - 1\right)$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Compute the length of the helix
$$
\gamma(t) = (3\cos t,\; 3\sin t,\; 4t), \qquad t \in [0, 2\pi].
$$

$$
\gamma'(t) = (-3\sin t,\; 3\cos t,\; 4)
$$
$$
\lVert \gamma'(t) \rVert = \sqrt{9\sin^2 t + 9\cos^2 t + 16} = \sqrt{25} = 5
$$
$$
L(\gamma) = \int_0^{2\pi} 5 \, dt = 10\pi
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The derivative vector has the first two components of trigonometric type and the third one constant. In computing the norm, the contribution of the first two components is $9(\sin^2 t + \cos^2 t) = 9$ by the fundamental identity, while the third adds $16$; the sum $25$ has square root $5$. The speed is therefore **constant**, so the length is the **product** $5 \cdot 2\pi = 10\pi$. One turn of the helix is longer than one turn of the base circle alone, which measures $6\pi$, since the rise in height *is added* to the rotation.

</div>
