## Exercise 1

Compute the directional derivative of
$$
f(x, y) = x^2 y + y^3
$$
at the point $(1, 2)$ along the unit vector $\mathbf{v} = \left( \dfrac{3}{5},\, \dfrac{4}{5} \right)$.

$$
f_x = 2xy, \qquad f_y = x^2 + 3y^2
$$
$$
\nabla f(1, 2) = (2 \cdot 1 \cdot 2,\; 1 + 3 \cdot 4) = (4,\, 13)
$$
$$
D_{\mathbf{v}} f(1, 2) = 4 \cdot \frac{3}{5} + 13 \cdot \frac{4}{5} = \frac{12 + 52}{5} = \frac{64}{5}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The function is a polynomial, so the partial derivatives are continuous on all of $\mathbb{R}^2$, and by the **total differential theorem** $f$ is differentiable at every point. The **gradient formula** applies, therefore the directional derivative is the dot product of the gradient at the point and the unit vector. The given vector has norm $\sqrt{\dfrac{9}{25} + \dfrac{16}{25}} = 1$, so it is *already* a **unit vector** and the dot product directly gives $\dfrac{64}{5}$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Compute the directional derivative of
$$
f(x, y) = e^{x} \cos y
$$
at the point $\left( 0, \dfrac{\pi}{2} \right)$ in the direction of the vector $\mathbf{w} = (1, -1)$.

$$
\mathbf{v} = \frac{\mathbf{w}}{|\mathbf{w}|} = \left( \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}},\, -\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \right)
$$
$$
f_x = e^x \cos y, \qquad f_y = -e^x \sin y
$$
$$
\nabla f\!\left( 0, \frac{\pi}{2} \right) = \left( e^0 \cos\frac{\pi}{2},\; -e^0 \sin\frac{\pi}{2} \right) = (0,\, -1)
$$
$$
D_{\mathbf{v}} f\!\left( 0, \frac{\pi}{2} \right) = 0 \cdot \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} + (-1) \left( -\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \right) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} = \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The vector $\mathbf{w} = (1, -1)$ has norm $\sqrt{2}$, so one first **normalizes** it by dividing by its norm: the directional derivative is defined along a unit vector. The function is the product of an exponential and a cosine, with continuous partial derivatives, therefore it is differentiable and the **gradient formula** applies. At the given point the cosine vanishes and the sine equals $1$, so the gradient is $(0, -1)$. The dot product with the unit vector gives $\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}$, which after rationalizing is written $\dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Determine the direction of steepest ascent of
$$
f(x, y) = \ln\!\left( 1 + x^2 + 2y^2 \right)
$$
at the point $(1, 1)$ and the maximum value of the directional derivative at that point.

$$
f_x = \frac{2x}{1 + x^2 + 2y^2}, \qquad f_y = \frac{4y}{1 + x^2 + 2y^2}
$$
$$
\nabla f(1, 1) = \left( \frac{2}{4},\; \frac{4}{4} \right) = \left( \frac{1}{2},\, 1 \right)
$$
$$
\bigl| \nabla f(1,1) \bigr| = \sqrt{\frac{1}{4} + 1} = \frac{\sqrt{5}}{2}
$$
$$
\mathbf{v} = \frac{\nabla f(1,1)}{|\nabla f(1,1)|} = \left( \frac{1}{\sqrt{5}},\; \frac{2}{\sqrt{5}} \right),
\qquad
\max_{\mathbf{v}} D_{\mathbf{v}} f(1,1) = \frac{\sqrt{5}}{2}
$$

::grafico{curve="sqrt(3)*cos(t),sqrt(1.5)*sin(t),0,6.29;sqrt(6)*cos(t),sqrt(3)*sin(t),0,6.29" segmenti="-0.6,1.8,3,0" vettori="1,1,1.5,2,∇f" punti="1,1,pieno" xmin=-3 xmax=3.3 ymin=-2 ymax=2.4 uguale=1 titolo="The level curves $x^2 + 2y^2 = 3$ and $x^2 + 2y^2 = 6$ (dashed), where $f$ equals $\ln 4$ and $\ln 7$: at $(1, 1)$ the gradient $\left(\dfrac{1}{2}, 1\right)$ is orthogonal to the tangent and points toward higher levels"}

<div class="spiegazione">

The function is differentiable because its partial derivatives are continuous, since the denominator $1 + x^2 + 2y^2$ is always greater than or equal to $1$. At the point $(1,1)$ the denominator equals $4$, so the gradient is $\left( \dfrac{1}{2}, 1 \right)$. By the **theorem on the direction of steepest ascent**, the directional derivative is maximal along the **unit vector of the gradient** and its maximum value is the **norm of the gradient**. Normalizing $\left( \dfrac{1}{2}, 1 \right)$, which has norm $\dfrac{\sqrt{5}}{2}$, gives the unit vector $\left( \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{5}}, \dfrac{2}{\sqrt{5}} \right)$; the maximum rate of increase equals $\dfrac{\sqrt{5}}{2}$.

</div>
