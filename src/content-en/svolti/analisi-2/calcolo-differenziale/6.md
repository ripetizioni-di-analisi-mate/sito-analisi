## Exercise 1

Compute the Hessian matrix of
$$
f(x, y) = x^3 y - x y^3
$$
at the point $(1, 2)$.

$$
f_x = 3x^2 y - y^3, \qquad f_y = x^3 - 3x y^2
$$
$$
f_{xx} = 6xy, \qquad f_{xy} = 3x^2 - 3y^2, \qquad f_{yy} = -6xy
$$
$$
f_{xx}(1,2) = 12, \qquad f_{xy}(1,2) = 3 - 12 = -9, \qquad f_{yy}(1,2) = -12
$$
$$
H_f(1, 2) =
\begin{pmatrix}
12 & -9 \\[4pt]
-9 & -12
\end{pmatrix}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The first derivatives are computed by differentiating with respect to one variable with the other held constant. For the second derivatives one differentiates again: $f_{xx}$ is the derivative of $f_x$ with respect to $x$, $f_{xy}$ is the derivative of $f_x$ with respect to $y$, and $f_{yy}$ is the derivative of $f_y$ with respect to $y$. The function is a polynomial, so it is of class $C^2$, and by the **Schwarz theorem** the mixed derivative $f_{yx}$ **coincides** with $f_{xy}$; the direct computation confirms it, since differentiating $f_y = x^3 - 3xy^2$ with respect to $x$ gives $3x^2 - 3y^2$. The Hessian matrix at the point is built with the three values found and is **symmetric**.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Compute the Hessian matrix of
$$
f(x, y) = \ln\!\left( x + y^2 \right)
$$
at the point $(1, 1)$, on the set $x + y^2 > 0$.

$$
f_x = \frac{1}{x + y^2}, \qquad f_y = \frac{2y}{x + y^2}
$$
$$
f_{xx} = -\frac{1}{(x + y^2)^2}, \qquad f_{xy} = -\frac{2y}{(x + y^2)^2}
$$
$$
f_{yy} = \frac{2 (x + y^2) - 2y \cdot 2y}{(x + y^2)^2} = \frac{2x - 2y^2}{(x + y^2)^2}
$$
$$
f_{xx}(1,1) = -\frac{1}{4}, \qquad f_{xy}(1,1) = -\frac{1}{2}, \qquad f_{yy}(1,1) = \frac{2 - 2}{4} = 0
$$
$$
H_f(1, 1) =
\begin{pmatrix}
-\dfrac{1}{4} & -\dfrac{1}{2} \\[10pt]
-\dfrac{1}{2} & 0
\end{pmatrix}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The first derivatives follow from the differentiation rule for a **composite logarithm**: the reciprocal of the argument times the derivative of the argument, which is $1$ with respect to $x$ and $2y$ with respect to $y$. For $f_{xx}$ one differentiates $\left( x + y^2 \right)^{-1}$ with respect to $x$, and by the rule for a composite power one obtains $-\left( x + y^2 \right)^{-2}$. For $f_{xy}$ one differentiates the same expression with respect to $y$, so the factor $2y$ appears. For $f_{yy}$ one applies the **quotient rule** to $\dfrac{2y}{x + y^2}$, and the numerator becomes $2(x + y^2) - 4y^2 = 2x - 2y^2$. At the point $(1,1)$ the argument equals $2$ and its square equals $4$; the numerator of $f_{yy}$ **vanishes**, therefore the bottom-right entry of the matrix is $0$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Verify the Schwarz theorem for
$$
f(x, y) = e^{xy},
$$
by computing $f_{xy}$ and $f_{yx}$ separately, and write the Hessian matrix at a generic point.

$$
f_x = y\, e^{xy}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
f_{xy} = e^{xy} + y \cdot x\, e^{xy} = (1 + xy)\, e^{xy}
$$
$$
f_y = x\, e^{xy}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
f_{yx} = e^{xy} + x \cdot y\, e^{xy} = (1 + xy)\, e^{xy}
$$
$$
f_{xy} = f_{yx}
$$
$$
f_{xx} = y^2 e^{xy}, \qquad f_{yy} = x^2 e^{xy}
$$
$$
H_f(x, y) =
\begin{pmatrix}
y^2 e^{xy} & (1 + xy)\, e^{xy} \\[4pt]
(1 + xy)\, e^{xy} & x^2 e^{xy}
\end{pmatrix}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

For $f_{xy}$ one differentiates $f_x = y\,e^{xy}$ with respect to $y$: the product has derivative $e^{xy} + y \cdot x\, e^{xy}$, because the first factor has derivative $1$ and the exponential brings the factor $x$. For $f_{yx}$ one differentiates $f_y = x\, e^{xy}$ with respect to $x$ and, by the symmetry of the roles, obtains $e^{xy} + xy\, e^{xy}$. The two mixed derivatives **coincide**, as guaranteed by the **Schwarz theorem**, since $f$ is a composition of functions of class $C^2$. The **pure derivatives** are obtained by differentiating twice in the *same* variable: each differentiation with respect to $x$ brings a factor $y$, so $f_{xx} = y^2 e^{xy}$, and analogously $f_{yy} = x^2 e^{xy}$.

</div>
