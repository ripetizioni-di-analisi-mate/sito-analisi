## Exercise 1

Given $F(t) = f\bigl(x(t), y(t)\bigr)$ with
$$
f(x, y) = x^2 y, \qquad x(t) = \cos t, \qquad y(t) = \sin t,
$$
compute $F'(t)$ and the value $F'(0)$.

$$
f_x = 2xy, \qquad f_y = x^2, \qquad x'(t) = -\sin t, \qquad y'(t) = \cos t
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
F'(t) &= 2xy \cdot (-\sin t) + x^2 \cdot \cos t \\[6pt]
&= -2 \cos t \sin^2 t + \cos^3 t
\end{aligned}
$$
$$
F'(0) = -2 \cdot 1 \cdot 0 + 1 = 1
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The function $f$ is a polynomial, so it is differentiable everywhere, and $x(t)$, $y(t)$ are differentiable; the **chain rule** applies. The derivative of $F$ is the **sum** of the two contributions $f_x \, x'$ and $f_y \, y'$, in which the partial derivatives are evaluated at $\bigl(x(t), y(t)\bigr) = (\cos t, \sin t)$. Substituting gives $F'(t) = -2\cos t \sin^2 t + \cos^3 t$. The result is checked **directly**, since $F(t) = \cos^2 t \, \sin t$: differentiating with the product rule gives $-2\cos t \sin t \cdot \sin t + \cos^2 t \cdot \cos t$, which coincides with the expression found. At $t = 0$ the sine vanishes and the cosine equals $1$, so $F'(0) = 1$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Given $F(s, t) = f\bigl(x(s,t), y(s,t)\bigr)$ with
$$
f(x, y) = e^{\,x - y}, \qquad x = s\,t^2, \qquad y = s^2 + t,
$$
compute $\dfrac{\partial F}{\partial s}$ and $\dfrac{\partial F}{\partial t}$ at the point $(s, t) = (1, 1)$.

$$
f_x = e^{\,x - y}, \qquad f_y = -e^{\,x - y}
$$
$$
\frac{\partial x}{\partial s} = t^2, \qquad \frac{\partial x}{\partial t} = 2st, \qquad \frac{\partial y}{\partial s} = 2s, \qquad \frac{\partial y}{\partial t} = 1
$$
$$
\frac{\partial F}{\partial s} = e^{\,x - y}\, t^2 - e^{\,x - y} \, 2s = e^{\,x - y} \left( t^2 - 2s \right)
$$
$$
\frac{\partial F}{\partial t} = e^{\,x - y}\, 2st - e^{\,x - y} = e^{\,x - y} \left( 2st - 1 \right)
$$
$$
(s, t) = (1, 1): \quad x = 1, \quad y = 2, \quad
\frac{\partial F}{\partial s} = -e^{-1}, \quad
\frac{\partial F}{\partial t} = e^{-1}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The intermediate variables depend on two parameters, so for *each* parameter the **chain rule** adds two contributions: the derivative of $f$ with respect to $x$ times the derivative of $x$ with respect to the parameter, plus the analogous term for $y$. The derivatives of $f$ are $f_x = e^{\,x-y}$ and $f_y = -e^{\,x-y}$, because the exponent $x - y$ has derivatives $1$ and $-1$. **Factoring out** $e^{\,x-y}$ gives the two compact expressions. At the point $(s,t) = (1,1)$ the intermediate variables equal $x = 1$ and $y = 2$, so $e^{\,x-y} = e^{-1}$; the factors $t^2 - 2s$ and $2st - 1$ equal $1 - 2 = -1$ and $2 - 1 = 1$, therefore the required derivatives are $-e^{-1}$ and $e^{-1}$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Let $g(x, y) = f\bigl(x^2 - y^2,\; 2xy\bigr)$, with $f = f(u, v)$ differentiable. Given that
$$
\nabla f(3, 4) = (2, -1),
$$
compute $\nabla g(2, 1)$.

$$
u = x^2 - y^2, \qquad v = 2xy
$$
$$
(x, y) = (2, 1) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad u = 4 - 1 = 3, \quad v = 4
$$
$$
g_x = f_u \cdot 2x + f_v \cdot 2y, \qquad g_y = f_u \cdot (-2y) + f_v \cdot 2x
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
g_x(2, 1) &= 2 \cdot 4 + (-1) \cdot 2 = 6 \\[6pt]
g_y(2, 1) &= 2 \cdot (-2) + (-1) \cdot 4 = -8
\end{aligned}
$$
$$
\nabla g(2, 1) = (6,\, -8)
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The outer function $f$ is *not* given by a formula, so the derivatives of $g$ remain expressed in terms of $f_u$ and $f_v$ through the **chain rule**: for $g_x$ the intermediate variables have derivatives $2x$ and $2y$, for $g_y$ they have derivatives $-2y$ and $2x$. The point $(x, y) = (2, 1)$ corresponds to the **intermediate point** $(u, v) = (3, 4)$, which is *exactly* the one at which the gradient of $f$ is known, so the data are **sufficient**. Substituting $f_u(3,4) = 2$, $f_v(3,4) = -1$ and the derivatives of the intermediate variables, $2x = 4$ and $2y = 2$, gives $g_x = 6$ and $g_y = -8$.

</div>
