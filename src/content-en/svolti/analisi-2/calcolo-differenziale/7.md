## Exercise 1

Write the second-order Taylor polynomial of
$$
f(x, y) = e^{\,x + 2y}
$$
centered at $(0, 0)$.

$$
f(0,0) = 1
$$
$$
f_x = e^{\,x + 2y}, \qquad f_y = 2\,e^{\,x + 2y}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
f_x(0,0) = 1, \quad f_y(0,0) = 2
$$
$$
f_{xx} = e^{\,x + 2y}, \qquad f_{xy} = 2\,e^{\,x + 2y}, \qquad f_{yy} = 4\,e^{\,x + 2y}
$$
$$
f_{xx}(0,0) = 1, \qquad f_{xy}(0,0) = 2, \qquad f_{yy}(0,0) = 4
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
T_2(x, y) &= 1 + x + 2y + \frac{1}{2} \left( x^2 + 2 \cdot 2\, xy + 4 y^2 \right) \\[6pt]
&= 1 + x + 2y + \frac{x^2}{2} + 2xy + 2y^2
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Each differentiation with respect to $x$ multiplies the exponential by $1$ and each differentiation with respect to $y$ multiplies it by $2$, so *all* the derivatives are **multiples** of $e^{\,x+2y}$ and at the origin they equal $1$, $2$, $1$, $2$, $4$. In the Taylor polynomial the first-degree term is $f_x\,x + f_y\,y$, and the second-degree term is the **quadratic form** of the Hessian matrix with the factor $\dfrac{1}{2}$: the coefficient of $xy$ is $\dfrac{1}{2} \cdot 2 f_{xy} = f_{xy} = 2$. The result is checked by composing the **one-variable expansion** $e^u = 1 + u + \dfrac{u^2}{2} + o(u^2)$ with $u = x + 2y$, since the square $(x + 2y)^2 = x^2 + 4xy + 4y^2$ divided by $2$ gives back the same quadratic terms.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Write the second-order Taylor polynomial of
$$
f(x, y) = \ln\!\left( 1 + x + y^2 \right)
$$
centered at $(0, 0)$.

$$
f(0,0) = \ln 1 = 0
$$
$$
f_x = \frac{1}{1 + x + y^2}, \qquad f_y = \frac{2y}{1 + x + y^2}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
f_x(0,0) = 1, \quad f_y(0,0) = 0
$$
$$
f_{xx} = -\frac{1}{(1 + x + y^2)^2}, \qquad f_{xy} = -\frac{2y}{(1 + x + y^2)^2}
$$
$$
f_{yy} = \frac{2(1 + x + y^2) - 2y \cdot 2y}{(1 + x + y^2)^2} = \frac{2 + 2x - 2y^2}{(1 + x + y^2)^2}
$$
$$
f_{xx}(0,0) = -1, \qquad f_{xy}(0,0) = 0, \qquad f_{yy}(0,0) = 2
$$
$$
T_2(x, y) = x + \frac{1}{2} \left( -x^2 + 2y^2 \right) = x - \frac{x^2}{2} + y^2
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

At the origin the argument of the logarithm equals $1$, so $f(0,0) = 0$. The first derivatives follow from the rule for a **composite logarithm**; at the origin they equal $1$ and $0$, because the factor $2y$ vanishes. For the second derivatives, $f_{xx}$ and $f_{xy}$ are obtained by differentiating $\left( 1 + x + y^2 \right)^{-1}$, while $f_{yy}$ requires the quotient rule. At the origin the mixed derivative **vanishes** and $f_{xx} = -1$ and $f_{yy} = 2$ remain, so the quadratic term is $-\dfrac{x^2}{2} + y^2$. The check with the one-variable expansion $\ln(1 + u) = u - \dfrac{u^2}{2} + o(u^2)$, with $u = x + y^2$, **confirms the result**: neglecting the terms of degree greater than $2$ leaves $x + y^2 - \dfrac{x^2}{2}$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Write the second-order Taylor polynomial of
$$
f(x, y) = e^{x} \sin y
$$
centered at the point $\left( 0, \dfrac{\pi}{2} \right)$.

$$
f\!\left( 0, \frac{\pi}{2} \right) = e^0 \sin\frac{\pi}{2} = 1
$$
$$
f_x = e^x \sin y, \qquad f_y = e^x \cos y
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
f_x\!\left( 0, \frac{\pi}{2} \right) = 1, \quad f_y\!\left( 0, \frac{\pi}{2} \right) = 0
$$
$$
f_{xx} = e^x \sin y, \qquad f_{xy} = e^x \cos y, \qquad f_{yy} = -e^x \sin y
$$
$$
f_{xx}\!\left( 0, \frac{\pi}{2} \right) = 1, \qquad f_{xy}\!\left( 0, \frac{\pi}{2} \right) = 0, \qquad f_{yy}\!\left( 0, \frac{\pi}{2} \right) = -1
$$
$$
T_2(x, y) = 1 + x + \frac{x^2}{2} - \frac{1}{2} \left( y - \frac{\pi}{2} \right)^{\!2}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The center of the expansion is *not* the origin, so the **increments** are $x - 0 = x$ and $y - \dfrac{\pi}{2}$, and the polynomial must be written in these differences. The derivatives of $f$ are obtained from the product: each differentiation with respect to $x$ leaves the expression unchanged, while differentiation with respect to $y$ turns the sine into the cosine and the cosine into minus the sine. At the center the sine equals $1$ and the cosine equals $0$, so the values are $f = 1$, $f_x = 1$, $f_y = 0$, $f_{xx} = 1$, $f_{xy} = 0$ and $f_{yy} = -1$. The **mixed term is missing**, because $f_{xy}$ vanishes at the center, and the quadratic term collects $\dfrac{x^2}{2}$ and $-\dfrac{1}{2}\left( y - \dfrac{\pi}{2} \right)^{\!2}$.

</div>
