## Exercise 1

Applying Parseval's identity to the Fourier series of the sawtooth wave
$$
x \sim \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{2\,(-1)^{n+1}}{n} \sin(nx),
\qquad x \in [-\pi, \pi),
$$
compute the sum of the series $\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{n^2}$.

$$
\frac{1}{\pi} \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} x^2\, dx
= \frac{1}{\pi} \cdot \frac{2\pi^3}{3}
= \frac{2\pi^2}{3}
$$
$$
\frac{a_0^2}{2} + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \bigl( a_n^2 + b_n^2 \bigr)
= \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \left( \frac{2\,(-1)^{n+1}}{n} \right)^{\!2}
= 4 \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{n^2}
$$
$$
\frac{2\pi^2}{3} = 4 \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{n^2}
\implies
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{n^2} = \frac{\pi^2}{6}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The left-hand side of **Parseval's identity** is the integral of $f(x)^2 = x^2$ over one period, divided by $\pi$, and equals $\dfrac{2\pi^2}{3}$. On the right-hand side the $a_n$ are *all* zero, because the function is odd, and the square of $b_n$ equals $\dfrac{4}{n^2}$: the sign $(-1)^{n+1}$ disappears on squaring. Equating the two sides and dividing by $4$ we obtain $\sum \dfrac{1}{n^2} = \dfrac{\pi^2}{6}$, the same value derived in the theory from the pointwise convergence of the series of $x^2$: the two approaches are **independent** and lead to the **same result**.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Applying Parseval's identity to the Fourier series of the square wave
$$
f(x) =
\begin{cases}
-1 & \text{if } -\pi \leq x < 0 \\
1 & \text{if } 0 \leq x < \pi
\end{cases}
\qquad
f(x) \sim \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \frac{4}{(2k+1)\pi} \sin\bigl( (2k+1)x \bigr),
$$
compute the sum of the series $\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \frac{1}{(2k+1)^2}$.

$$
\frac{1}{\pi} \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} f(x)^2\, dx
= \frac{1}{\pi} \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} 1\, dx = 2
$$
$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} b_n^2
= \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \frac{16}{(2k+1)^2 \pi^2}
$$
$$
2 = \frac{16}{\pi^2} \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \frac{1}{(2k+1)^2}
\implies
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \frac{1}{(2k+1)^2} = \frac{\pi^2}{8}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The square of the square wave equals $1$ at every point, therefore the integral over one period is $2\pi$ and the left-hand side equals $2$. On the right-hand side the $a_n$ are zero, the $b_n$ are zero for even $n$ and equal $\dfrac{4}{n\pi}$ for odd $n$, as computed in the worksheet on square waves; therefore the sum of the squares runs over the odd indices $n = 2k+1$ *only*. Solving for the series we obtain $\dfrac{2\pi^2}{16} = \dfrac{\pi^2}{8}$. **Parseval's identity** produces the series of the reciprocals of the squares of the odd numbers only, because only the odd harmonics appear in the expansion.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Applying Parseval's identity to the Fourier series of $f(x) = |x|$, $x \in [-\pi, \pi]$,
$$
|x| \sim \frac{\pi}{2} - \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \frac{4}{\pi (2k+1)^2} \cos\bigl( (2k+1)x \bigr),
$$
compute $\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \frac{1}{(2k+1)^4}$ and deduce $\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{n^4}$.

$$
\frac{1}{\pi} \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} x^2\, dx = \frac{2\pi^2}{3},
\qquad
a_0 = \pi, \quad a_{2k+1} = -\frac{4}{\pi (2k+1)^2}
$$
$$
\frac{2\pi^2}{3} = \frac{\pi^2}{2} + \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \frac{16}{\pi^2 (2k+1)^4}
$$
$$
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \frac{1}{(2k+1)^4}
= \frac{\pi^2}{16} \left( \frac{2\pi^2}{3} - \frac{\pi^2}{2} \right)
= \frac{\pi^2}{16} \cdot \frac{\pi^2}{6}
= \frac{\pi^4}{96}
$$
$$
S = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{n^4}:
\qquad
S = \frac{\pi^4}{96} + \sum_{m=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{(2m)^4}
= \frac{\pi^4}{96} + \frac{S}{16}
\implies
S = \frac{16}{15} \cdot \frac{\pi^4}{96} = \frac{\pi^4}{90}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The square of $|x|$ is $x^2$, therefore the left-hand side is the same as in exercise 1 and equals $\dfrac{2\pi^2}{3}$. On the right-hand side the term $\dfrac{a_0^2}{2}$ equals $\dfrac{\pi^2}{2}$, and the sum of the squares of the $a_n$ runs over the odd indices *only*, with $a_n^2 = \dfrac{16}{\pi^2 n^4}$. Solving for the series we obtain $\dfrac{\pi^4}{96}$. To pass to the series over all integers, the sum $S$ is split into the terms with **odd index** and with **even index**: the even ones are written as $n = 2m$, and since $(2m)^4 = 16\, m^4$, their sum is $\dfrac{S}{16}$. From the equation $S = \dfrac{\pi^4}{96} + \dfrac{S}{16}$ it follows that $\dfrac{15}{16} S = \dfrac{\pi^4}{96}$, therefore $S = \dfrac{\pi^4}{90}$, in agreement with the value obtained in the theory by applying Parseval to $x^2$.

</div>
