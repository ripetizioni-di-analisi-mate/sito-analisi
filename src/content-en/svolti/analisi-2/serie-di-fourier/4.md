## Exercise 1

Let $f$ be the $2\pi$-periodic extension of
$$
f(x) =
\begin{cases}
x + 1 & \text{if } -\pi \leq x < 0 \\
x - 1 & \text{if } 0 \leq x < \pi
\end{cases}
$$
Find, without computing the coefficients, the sum of the Fourier series of $f$ at $x = 0$ and at $x = \pi$.

$$
f(0^-) = 0 + 1 = 1, \qquad f(0^+) = 0 - 1 = -1
$$
$$
\text{sum at } x = 0: \quad \frac{f(0^+) + f(0^-)}{2} = \frac{-1 + 1}{2} = 0
$$
$$
f(\pi^-) = \pi - 1, \qquad f(\pi^+) = f\bigl( (-\pi)^+ \bigr) = -\pi + 1
$$
$$
\text{sum at } x = \pi: \quad \frac{(\pi - 1) + (-\pi + 1)}{2} = 0
$$

::grafico{f="((x-2*pi*floor((x+pi)/(2*pi)))-sign((x-2*pi*floor((x+pi)/(2*pi)))))*(1+0*log(abs(sin(x))-0.02))" xmin=-7 xmax=7.3 ymin=-3 ymax=3 punti="-3.1416,0,pieno;0,0,pieno;3.1416,0,pieno" titolo="Periodic extension of $f$: at the jumps $x = 0$ and $x = \pm\pi$ the series converges to $0$, the mean of the one-sided limits (filled dots)"}

<div class="spiegazione">

The function is **piecewise smooth**, because on each of the two subintervals it is a first-degree polynomial and the one-sided limits of $f$ and of $f'$ exist and are finite; therefore the **pointwise convergence theorem** applies at every point. At $x = 0$ the function jumps: the left limit is computed with the expression $x + 1$ and equals $1$, the right limit with the expression $x - 1$ and equals $-1$, hence the series converges to the **mean of the two values**, which is $0$. At $x = \pi$ the left limit equals $\pi - 1$; the right limit, by periodicity, coincides with the right limit at $-\pi$, where the function equals $x + 1$, therefore it is $-\pi + 1$. At this point too the mean equals $0$. The value $f(0) = -1$ assigned to the function at the jump point plays *no* role, since the coefficients are integrals and the sum of the series depends *only* on the one-sided limits.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Using the Fourier series of the sawtooth wave
$$
x \sim \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{2\,(-1)^{n+1}}{n} \sin(nx),
\qquad x \in [-\pi, \pi),
$$
compute the sum of the numerical series $\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^k}{2k+1}$.

$$
x = \frac{\pi}{2}: \qquad
\frac{\pi}{2} = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{2\,(-1)^{n+1}}{n} \sin\frac{n\pi}{2}
$$
$$
\sin\frac{n\pi}{2} =
\begin{cases}
0 & \text{if } n \text{ is even} \\
(-1)^k & \text{if } n = 2k + 1
\end{cases}
$$
$$
\frac{\pi}{2} = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \frac{2\,(-1)^{2k+2}}{2k+1} \, (-1)^k
= 2 \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^k}{2k+1}
$$
$$
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^k}{2k+1} = \frac{\pi}{4}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The sawtooth wave is piecewise smooth and continuous at $x = \dfrac{\pi}{2}$, which is interior to the interval $[-\pi, \pi)$; therefore, by the **pointwise convergence theorem**, the series converges *exactly* to the value $f\!\left(\dfrac{\pi}{2}\right) = \dfrac{\pi}{2}$ and the symbol $\sim$ becomes an equality. The factor $\sin\dfrac{n\pi}{2}$ is zero for even $n$, therefore only the odd indices $n = 2k+1$ remain in the sum, for which $\sin\dfrac{(2k+1)\pi}{2} = (-1)^k$. The sign $(-1)^{n+1} = (-1)^{2k+2}$ always equals $1$, hence the equality reduces to $\dfrac{\pi}{2} = 2 \sum \dfrac{(-1)^k}{2k+1}$. Dividing by $2$ we obtain the sum of the **Leibniz series**, $\dfrac{\pi}{4}$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Using the Fourier series of $f(x) = x^2$, $x \in [-\pi, \pi]$,
$$
x^2 \sim \frac{\pi^2}{3} + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{4\,(-1)^n}{n^2} \cos(nx),
$$
compute the sum of the numerical series $\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^{n+1}}{n^2}$.

$$
x = 0: \qquad
0 = \frac{\pi^2}{3} + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{4\,(-1)^n}{n^2}
$$
$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^n}{n^2} = -\frac{\pi^2}{12}
$$
$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^{n+1}}{n^2} = \frac{\pi^2}{12}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The periodic extension of $x^2$ is **continuous** on all of $\mathbb{R}$, because the values at the endpoints $\pm\pi$ coincide, and it is piecewise smooth; therefore the series converges to $f(x)$ at every point and in particular at $x = 0$, where $f(0) = 0$ and every cosine equals $1$. The equality $0 = \dfrac{\pi^2}{3} + 4\sum \dfrac{(-1)^n}{n^2}$ is solved for the series, and we obtain $\sum \dfrac{(-1)^n}{n^2} = -\dfrac{\pi^2}{12}$. The required series has the *opposite* signs, therefore its sum is $\dfrac{\pi^2}{12}$. The same expansion evaluated at $x = \pi$ gives the sum $\sum \dfrac{1}{n^2} = \dfrac{\pi^2}{6}$, as shown in the theory: different evaluation points of the same expansion produce **different numerical series**.

</div>
