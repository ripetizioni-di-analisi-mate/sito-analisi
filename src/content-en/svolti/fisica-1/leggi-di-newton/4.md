## Exercise 1

A crate of mass $m = 40\,\mathrm{kg}$ is at rest on a horizontal floor, with friction coefficients $\mu_s = 0.50$ and $\mu_d = 0.35$. Determine whether a horizontal push of $150\,\mathrm{N}$ sets the crate in motion. Compute the minimum horizontal push that sets it in motion and the acceleration of the crate if, once it has started, the push remains equal to that value.

$$
N = m g = 40\,\mathrm{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2} = 392.4\,\mathrm{N}
$$
$$
f_{s,\max} = \mu_s N = 0.50 \cdot 392.4\,\mathrm{N} = 196\,\mathrm{N} > 150\,\mathrm{N}
$$
$$
F_{\min} = \mu_s N = 196\,\mathrm{N},
\qquad
f_d = \mu_d N = 0.35 \cdot 392.4\,\mathrm{N} = 137\,\mathrm{N}
$$
$$
a = \frac{F_{\min} - \mu_d N}{m} = \frac{(\mu_s - \mu_d)\,m g}{m} = (\mu_s - \mu_d)\,g = 0.15 \cdot 9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2} = 1.47\,\mathrm{m/s^2}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Along the vertical the crate does not accelerate, therefore the normal force equals the weight, $392\,\mathrm{N}$. Static friction can take any value up to $\mu_s N = 196\,\mathrm{N}$. With a push of $150\,\mathrm{N}$ equilibrium requires a static friction of $150\,\mathrm{N}$, which is less than the maximum; so the crate **remains at rest** and the friction is exactly $150\,\mathrm{N}$.

The crate starts when the push exceeds the maximum static friction, therefore the minimum push is $196\,\mathrm{N}$. As soon as the crate moves, friction becomes kinetic and drops to $137\,\mathrm{N}$, while the push remains $196\,\mathrm{N}$, therefore the net force is no longer zero and the crate accelerates at $1.47\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$. The result $(\mu_s - \mu_d)\,g$ does not depend on the mass and shows that a crate, once moving, is pushed with less force than was needed to start it.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A hockey puck is launched on the ice with initial velocity $v_0 = 12\,\mathrm{m/s}$ and stops after traveling $d = 40\,\mathrm{m}$. Assuming that the only horizontal force is kinetic friction, compute the coefficient of kinetic friction between puck and ice and the duration of the motion.

$$
\begin{cases}
-\mu_d N = m\,a \\[4pt]
N - m g = 0
\end{cases}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
a = -\mu_d\,g
$$
$$
0 = v_0^2 - 2\,\mu_d\,g\,d
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\mu_d = \frac{v_0^2}{2\,g\,d},
\qquad
t = \frac{v_0}{\mu_d\,g} = \frac{2\,d}{v_0}
$$
$$
\mu_d = \frac{(12\,\mathrm{m/s})^2}{2 \cdot 9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2} \cdot 40\,\mathrm{m}} = \frac{144}{785} = 0.18,
\qquad
t = \frac{2 \cdot 40\,\mathrm{m}}{12\,\mathrm{m/s}} = 6.7\,\mathrm{s}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The puck slides, therefore friction is kinetic, has magnitude $\mu_d N$ and points opposite to the velocity. Along the vertical $N = mg$, so the horizontal acceleration is $a = -\mu_d g$, which is constant and does not depend on the mass of the puck, which indeed is not among the data of the problem. The motion is uniformly decelerated.

Requiring the velocity to vanish after the distance $d$, the relation between velocity and distance gives $\mu_d = 0.18$. The duration of the motion is obtained by dividing the distance by the average velocity, which in a uniformly decelerated motion until stopping equals $\dfrac{v_0}{2}$, therefore $t = 6.7\,\mathrm{s}$. The coefficient found is dimensionless, as it must be, since $\dfrac{v_0^2}{g\,d}$ is the ratio of two quantities both measured in $\mathrm{m^2/s^2}$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A sled of mass $m = 30\,\mathrm{kg}$ is pulled at constant velocity over a level snow-covered surface, with coefficient of kinetic friction $\mu_d = 0.15$, by means of a rope inclined at $\theta = 30^\circ$ above the horizontal. Compute the force exerted by the rope and the normal force. Compare the result with the force needed if the rope were horizontal.

$$
\begin{cases}
F \cos\theta - \mu_d N = 0 \\[4pt]
N + F \sin\theta - m g = 0
\end{cases}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
F \cos\theta - \mu_d\,(m g - F \sin\theta) = 0
$$
$$
F = \frac{\mu_d\,m g}{\cos\theta + \mu_d \sin\theta},
\qquad
N = m g - F \sin\theta
$$
$$
F = \frac{0.15 \cdot 30\,\mathrm{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2}}{0.866 + 0.15 \cdot 0.500} = \frac{44.1\,\mathrm{N}}{0.941} = 46.9\,\mathrm{N}
$$
$$
N = 294.3\,\mathrm{N} - 46.9\,\mathrm{N} \cdot 0.500 = 271\,\mathrm{N}
$$
$$
\theta = 0:
\quad
F_0 = \mu_d\,m g = 44.1\,\mathrm{N}
$$

::grafico{assi=0 uguale=1 punti="0,0,pieno" vettori="0,0,0.406,0.235;0,0,0,-2.943,P;0,0,0,2.708,N;0,0,-0.406,0" segmenti="0.406,0.235,1.3,0.751;0,0,1.4,0" etichette="0.85,0.12,30°;0.3,0.42,F;-0.45,0.15,f" xmin=-3.4 xmax=3.4 ymin=-3.2 ymax=3.0 titolo="Free-body diagram of the sled, with the arrows to scale: the horizontal component of $\vec{F}$ balances the friction $\vec{f}$, the vertical component slightly reduces $N$ compared with the weight"}

<div class="spiegazione">

The sled moves at constant velocity, therefore the net force is zero. The **free-body diagram** shows the weight, the normal force, the force of the rope and kinetic friction, directed backward. The normal force is not equal to the weight, because the inclined rope also pulls the sled upward; for this reason the two equations are coupled. $N$ is obtained from the second equation, substituted into the first, and the result is solved for $F$.

The force of the rope is $46.9\,\mathrm{N}$ and the normal force $271\,\mathrm{N}$, less than the weight $294\,\mathrm{N}$. With the rope horizontal the force would be $\mu_d mg = 44.1\,\mathrm{N}$, therefore at $30^\circ$ a **greater** force is needed, because the reduction of friction does not compensate for the loss due to the factor $\cos\theta$. The function $\cos\theta + \mu_d\sin\theta$ has its maximum for $\tan\theta = \mu_d$, that is, for $\theta = 8.5^\circ$, where the force equals $\dfrac{\mu_d\,mg}{\sqrt{1 + \mu_d^2}} = 43.7\,\mathrm{N}$; for angles between $0$ and $2 \cdot 8.5^\circ$ the inclined rope is advantageous, at $30^\circ$ it is not.

</div>

## Exercise 4

A block $A$ of mass $m_A = 2.0\,\mathrm{kg}$ rests on top of a block $B$ of mass $m_B = 6.0\,\mathrm{kg}$, which in turn rests on a smooth horizontal floor. The coefficient of static friction between $A$ and $B$ is $\mu_s = 0.30$. A horizontal force $F$ is applied to block $B$. Compute the maximum value of $F$ for which the two blocks move together without $A$ sliding on $B$. For $F = 16\,\mathrm{N}$ compute the common acceleration and the friction force on $A$.

$$
\text{block } A:\quad f_s = m_A\,a,
\qquad
f_s \leq \mu_s\,m_A\,g
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
a \leq \mu_s\,g
$$
$$
\text{system } A + B:\quad F = (m_A + m_B)\,a
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
F_{\max} = (m_A + m_B)\,\mu_s\,g
$$
$$
F_{\max} = 8.0\,\mathrm{kg} \cdot 0.30 \cdot 9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2} = 23.5\,\mathrm{N}
$$
$$
F = 16\,\mathrm{N}:
\quad
a = \frac{16\,\mathrm{N}}{8.0\,\mathrm{kg}} = 2.0\,\mathrm{m/s^2},
\qquad
f_s = 2.0\,\mathrm{kg} \cdot 2.0\,\mathrm{m/s^2} = 4.0\,\mathrm{N}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The force $F$ is applied only to $B$, therefore the only horizontal force on $A$ is the static friction exerted by $B$, directed in the direction of motion. This is a case of **friction as a driving force**. By the second law applied to $A$ one has $f_s = m_A a$, and the law of static friction requires $f_s \leq \mu_s N_A = \mu_s m_A g$; so the blocks can move together only if the acceleration does not exceed $\mu_s g = 2.94\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$.

As long as the blocks move together they can be treated as a **single body** of mass $8.0\,\mathrm{kg}$, on which the friction between $A$ and $B$ is an internal force and the only external horizontal force is $F$, since the floor is smooth. The maximum acceleration therefore corresponds to $F_{\max} = 23.5\,\mathrm{N}$. With $F = 16\,\mathrm{N}$ the common acceleration is $2.0\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$, below the limit, and the friction on $A$ equals $4.0\,\mathrm{N}$, less than the maximum value $\mu_s m_A g = 5.89\,\mathrm{N}$; this confirms that $A$ does not slide.

</div>
