## Exercise 1

The rails of a railway line are made of steel ($\alpha = 1.2 \cdot 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{K^{-1}}$), are $12.0\,\mathrm{m}$ long, and are laid at a temperature of $10\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$. Compute the minimum gap to leave between two consecutive rails so that they do not come into contact up to a temperature of $50\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$.

$$
\Delta L = \alpha\, L_0\, \Delta T
$$
$$
\Delta L = 1.2 \cdot 10^{-5} \cdot 12.0 \cdot 40\,\mathrm{m} = 5.76 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m} \approx 5.8\,\mathrm{mm}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Each rail lengthens according to the law of **linear expansion**, with $\Delta T = 50\,^\circ\mathrm{C} - 10\,^\circ\mathrm{C} = 40\,\mathrm{K}$; the temperature difference has the same value in degrees Celsius and in kelvins. If each rail is fixed at its center, it lengthens by $\dfrac{\Delta L}{2}$ on each side, and the two rails facing the same joint advance together by $\dfrac{\Delta L}{2} + \dfrac{\Delta L}{2} = \Delta L$. Therefore the minimum gap between two rails equals the elongation of one rail, **about $5.8\,\mathrm{mm}$**.

The result has two significant figures, like the coefficient $\alpha$. The order of magnitude is that of the joints visible on railway tracks, a few millimeters for rails about ten meters long.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A steel ring ($\alpha = 1.2 \cdot 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{K^{-1}}$) has inner diameter $d_0 = 49.95\,\mathrm{mm}$ at a temperature of $20.0\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$, and must be fitted onto a shaft of diameter $50.00\,\mathrm{mm}$ that stays at $20.0\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$. Compute the minimum temperature to which the ring must be heated.

$$
d = d_0\,(1 + \alpha\,\Delta T) \geq d_{\text{shaft}}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\Delta T \geq \frac{d_{\text{shaft}} - d_0}{\alpha\, d_0}
$$
$$
\Delta T \geq \frac{50.00 - 49.95}{1.2 \cdot 10^{-5} \cdot 49.95}\,\mathrm{K} = \frac{0.05}{5.994 \cdot 10^{-4}}\,\mathrm{K} \approx 83\,\mathrm{K}
$$
$$
T_{\min} = 20.0\,^\circ\mathrm{C} + 83\,\mathrm{K} \approx 103\,^\circ\mathrm{C}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The hole of the ring expands as if it were filled with steel, therefore the inner diameter follows the law of **linear expansion** with the coefficient of steel. The ring slides onto the shaft when the inner diameter reaches that of the shaft; the condition is a linear inequality in $\Delta T$, with coefficient $\alpha\,d_0 > 0$, and it is solved by dividing by it.

In the difference $d_{\text{shaft}} - d_0$ the diameters can be left in millimeters, because the ratio with $d_0$ is dimensionless. An increase of about $83\,\mathrm{K}$ is needed, therefore the ring must be brought **to at least $103\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$**. On cooling, the ring tends to return to its initial diameter, smaller than that of the shaft, and stays locked by interference fit.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A glass container ($\alpha_r = 9.0 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\mathrm{K^{-1}}$) of volume $1.000\,\mathrm{L}$ is filled to the brim with glycerin ($\beta_{\ell} = 5.0 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{K^{-1}}$) at a temperature of $20\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$. The container and the glycerin are heated together up to $60\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$. Compute the volume of glycerin that overflows.

$$
\Delta V_{\ell} = \beta_{\ell}\, V_0\, \Delta T, \qquad \Delta V_r = 3\alpha_r\, V_0\, \Delta T
$$
$$
V_{\text{over}} = \Delta V_{\ell} - \Delta V_r = (\beta_{\ell} - 3\alpha_r)\, V_0\, \Delta T
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
V_{\text{over}} &= (5.0 \cdot 10^{-4} - 2.7 \cdot 10^{-5}) \cdot 1.000 \cdot 10^{-3} \cdot 40\,\mathrm{m^3} \\[6pt]
&= 4.73 \cdot 10^{-4} \cdot 4.0 \cdot 10^{-2} \cdot 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m^3} \approx 1.9 \cdot 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{m^3}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Both the glycerin and the container expand. For the liquid, **volume expansion** is used with the coefficient $\beta_{\ell}$; for the glass, which is a solid, the coefficient of volume expansion is $3\alpha_r$. The internal capacity of the container increases as if the cavity were filled with glass, therefore only the difference between the increase in volume of the liquid and that of the cavity overflows.

The overflowing volume is $1.9 \cdot 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{m^3}$, that is, **about $19\,\mathrm{cm^3}$**. Neglecting the expansion of the glass would give $20\,\mathrm{cm^3}$; the correction is small because the volume coefficient of glass is about one twentieth of that of glycerin.

</div>
