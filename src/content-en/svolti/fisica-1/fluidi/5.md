## Exercise 1

In a hydraulic lift the pistons, at the same height, have diameters $d_1 = 2.0\,\mathrm{cm}$ and $d_2 = 30\,\mathrm{cm}$. A car of mass $1200\,\mathrm{kg}$ rests on the large piston. Neglecting the masses of the pistons, compute the force to be applied to the small piston to support the car, how far the small piston must move down to raise the car by $10\,\mathrm{cm}$, and the work done on the two pistons.

$$
\frac{F_1}{A_1} = \frac{F_2}{A_2} ,
\qquad
A_1 s_1 = A_2 s_2 ,
\qquad
\frac{A_2}{A_1} = \left( \frac{d_2}{d_1} \right)^2
$$
$$
F_1 = m g \left( \frac{d_1}{d_2} \right)^2 ,
\qquad
s_1 = \left( \frac{d_2}{d_1} \right)^2 s_2
$$
$$
\left( \frac{d_2}{d_1} \right)^2 = \left( \frac{30}{2.0} \right)^2 = 225 ,
\qquad
F_1 = \frac{1200 \cdot 9.81}{225}\,\mathrm{N} = 52\,\mathrm{N}
$$
$$
s_1 = 225 \cdot 0.10\,\mathrm{m} = 22.5\,\mathrm{m} ,
\qquad
L = F_2 s_2 = 1200 \cdot 9.81 \cdot 0.10\,\mathrm{J} = 1.2 \cdot 10^3\,\mathrm{J}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The liquid of the lift is incompressible and at rest, and the pistons are at the same height. By **Pascal's principle** the pressure produced by the force on the small piston is the same that acts on the large piston, where it must balance the weight of the car. The areas of the pistons are proportional to the squares of the diameters, so the ratio of the areas is $225$ and the force on the small piston is $225$ times smaller than the weight, about $52\,\mathrm{N}$.

The volume of liquid that leaves the small cylinder enters the large cylinder, therefore the displacements are in the inverse ratio of the areas, so to raise the car by $10\,\mathrm{cm}$ the small piston must travel $22.5\,\mathrm{m}$, and in real lifts this is done with many successive strokes. The work is the same on the two pistons, since $F_1 s_1 = 52.3 \cdot 22.5 \approx 1.18 \cdot 10^3\,\mathrm{J}$; therefore the press **multiplies the force** and leaves the work unchanged.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A hydraulic jack has a pump piston of area $4.0\,\mathrm{cm^2}$, which moves down by $5.0\,\mathrm{cm}$ at each stroke, and a lifting piston of area $200\,\mathrm{cm^2}$, at the same height. The jack must support and raise by $15\,\mathrm{cm}$ a load of mass $800\,\mathrm{kg}$. Compute the force on the pump piston, the number of strokes needed and the total work done on the pump.

$$
F_1 = \frac{A_1}{A_2}\, m g ,
\qquad
N = \frac{A_2\, h}{A_1\, s} ,
\qquad
L = N F_1 s = m g h
$$
$$
F_1 = \frac{4.0}{200} \cdot 800 \cdot 9.81\,\mathrm{N} = 1.6 \cdot 10^2\,\mathrm{N}
$$
$$
N = \frac{200 \cdot 15}{4.0 \cdot 5.0} = \frac{3000}{20} = 150
$$
$$
L = 800 \cdot 9.81 \cdot 0.15\,\mathrm{J} = 1.2 \cdot 10^3\,\mathrm{J}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

By **Pascal's principle** the pressure under the two pistons is the same, so the force on the pump is the weight of the load reduced by the ratio of the areas, $\dfrac{4.0}{200} = \dfrac{1}{50}$. Each stroke pushes the volume $A_1 s = 20\,\mathrm{cm^3}$ into the large cylinder, while raising the load by $15\,\mathrm{cm}$ requires $A_2 h = 3000\,\mathrm{cm^3}$; the liquid is incompressible, therefore the number of strokes is the ratio of the two volumes. In the ratio the areas and lengths can be left in centimeters, because the units cancel.

The work of each stroke is $F_1 s = 157 \cdot 0.050 \approx 7.85\,\mathrm{J}$ and multiplied by $150$ it gives $1.18 \cdot 10^3\,\mathrm{J}$, which coincides with the increase in potential energy of the load $m g h$. The jack requires **one hundred fifty strokes**, each with a modest force.

</div>

## Exercise 3

In a hydraulic press full of oil ($\rho = 900\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}$) the small piston has radius $1.5\,\mathrm{cm}$ and the large piston has radius $15\,\mathrm{cm}$; the large piston is $1.50\,\mathrm{m}$ higher than the small piston and supports a load of mass $2000\,\mathrm{kg}$. Neglecting the masses of the pistons, compute the force to be applied to the small piston and compare it with the force needed if the pistons were at the same height.

$$
p_1 = p_2 + \rho g h ,
\qquad
p_2 - p_0 = \frac{m g}{A_2} ,
\qquad
F_1 = (p_1 - p_0)\, A_1
$$
$$
F_1 = \left( \frac{m g}{A_2} + \rho g h \right) A_1
$$
$$
\frac{m g}{A_2} = \frac{2000 \cdot 9.81}{\pi \cdot (0.15)^2}\,\mathrm{Pa} = 2.776 \cdot 10^5\,\mathrm{Pa} ,
\qquad
\rho g h = 900 \cdot 9.81 \cdot 1.50\,\mathrm{Pa} = 0.132 \cdot 10^5\,\mathrm{Pa}
$$
$$
F_1 = 2.908 \cdot 10^5\,\mathrm{Pa} \cdot \pi \cdot (0.015)^2\,\mathrm{m^2} = 2.1 \cdot 10^2\,\mathrm{N}
$$
$$
F_1' = m g\, \frac{A_1}{A_2} = \frac{2000 \cdot 9.81}{100}\,\mathrm{N} = 1.96 \cdot 10^2\,\mathrm{N}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Atmospheric pressure acts on both faces of each piston, so we work with gauge pressures. Under the large piston the gauge pressure must balance the load, and it equals $\dfrac{m g}{A_2}$. The small piston is lower by $h = 1.50\,\mathrm{m}$, so by **Stevin's law** the pressure of the oil under it is greater by $\rho g h$. The force on the small piston must balance this pressure, and it turns out to be about $206\,\mathrm{N}$.

With the pistons at the same height there would only be the ratio of the areas, $\left( \dfrac{15}{1.5} \right)^2 = 100$, and the force would be $196\,\mathrm{N}$. The difference in height therefore requires about $5\,\%$ **more** force, because the small piston must also support the weight of the $1.50\,\mathrm{m}$ oil column that separates it from the height of the large piston.

</div>
