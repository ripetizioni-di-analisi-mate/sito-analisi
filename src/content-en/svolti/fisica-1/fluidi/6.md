## Exercise 1

Water flows at a speed of $1.5\,\mathrm{m/s}$ in a horizontal pipe of diameter $4.0\,\mathrm{cm}$. The pipe has a constriction of diameter $2.0\,\mathrm{cm}$. Treating water as an ideal fluid, compute the flow rate, the velocity in the constriction and the pressure difference between the wide section and the constriction.

$$
Q = A_1 v_1 ,
\qquad
A_1 v_1 = A_2 v_2 ,
\qquad
p_1 + \frac{1}{2}\rho v_1^2 = p_2 + \frac{1}{2}\rho v_2^2
$$
$$
v_2 = \left( \frac{d_1}{d_2} \right)^2 v_1 ,
\qquad
p_1 - p_2 = \frac{1}{2}\rho\,(v_2^2 - v_1^2)
$$
$$
Q = \pi \cdot (0.020)^2 \cdot 1.5\,\mathrm{m^3/s} = 1.9 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m^3/s} ,
\qquad
v_2 = \left( \frac{4.0}{2.0} \right)^2 \cdot 1.5\,\mathrm{m/s} = 6.0\,\mathrm{m/s}
$$
$$
p_1 - p_2 = \frac{1}{2} \cdot 1000 \cdot (6.0^2 - 1.5^2)\,\mathrm{Pa} = 500 \cdot 33.75\,\mathrm{Pa} = 1.7 \cdot 10^4\,\mathrm{Pa}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The flow rate is the product of the area of the cross section and the velocity; the area is computed with the radius, $2.0\,\mathrm{cm} = 0.020\,\mathrm{m}$, and the flow rate turns out to be about $1.9\,\mathrm{L/s}$. Water is incompressible and the flow is steady, so by the **continuity equation** the flow rate is the same in the constriction. The areas of circular sections are proportional to the squares of the diameters, therefore halving the diameter makes the area one quarter and the velocity quadruples.

The pipe is horizontal, so in **Bernoulli's theorem** the terms $\rho g y$ are equal and cancel. What remains is a balance between pressure and kinetic energy per unit volume. In the constriction the velocity is greater, therefore the pressure is **lower** than in the wide section, by about $1.7 \cdot 10^4\,\mathrm{Pa}$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A horizontal Venturi tube has cross section $A_1 = 20\,\mathrm{cm^2}$ in the wide section and $A_2 = 5.0\,\mathrm{cm^2}$ in the constriction, and water flows through it. Two open vertical tubes, connected to the two sections, show a difference in level of $15.0\,\mathrm{cm}$ between the water columns. Compute the velocity in the wide section, the velocity in the constriction and the flow rate.

$$
p_1 - p_2 = \rho g\, \Delta h ,
\qquad
v_2 = \frac{A_1}{A_2}\, v_1 ,
\qquad
p_1 - p_2 = \frac{1}{2}\rho\,(v_2^2 - v_1^2)
$$
$$
\rho g\, \Delta h = \frac{1}{2}\rho\, v_1^2 \left( \frac{A_1^2}{A_2^2} - 1 \right)
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
v_1 = \sqrt{\frac{2 g\, \Delta h}{\dfrac{A_1^2}{A_2^2} - 1}}
$$
$$
v_1 = \sqrt{\frac{2 \cdot 9.81 \cdot 0.150}{16 - 1}}\,\mathrm{m/s} = \sqrt{0.196}\,\mathrm{m/s} = 0.443\,\mathrm{m/s} ,
\qquad
v_2 = 4 \cdot 0.443\,\mathrm{m/s} = 1.77\,\mathrm{m/s}
$$
$$
Q = A_1 v_1 = 20 \cdot 10^{-4} \cdot 0.443\,\mathrm{m^3/s} = 8.9 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m^3/s}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

In the vertical tubes the water is at rest, so by **Stevin's law** the pressure difference between the bases of the two tubes, that is, between the two sections of the duct, is $\rho g\, \Delta h$. By the **continuity equation** the velocity in the constriction is four times that in the wide section, because the ratio of the areas is $4$.

Substituting $v_2$ into Bernoulli's theorem for the horizontal duct we obtain an equation in the single unknown $v_1$; the density of water appears on both sides and cancels, so the result does not depend on the liquid, provided that the vertical tubes contain the same liquid as the duct. This is the formula of the **Venturi tube** with $\Delta p = \rho g\, \Delta h$. The flow rate is about $0.89\,\mathrm{L/s}$, and a difference in level of a few centimeters is enough to measure it.

</div>

## Exercise 3

In a building, water enters on the ground floor in a pipe of diameter $2.5\,\mathrm{cm}$, with speed $1.2\,\mathrm{m/s}$ and absolute pressure $3.00 \cdot 10^5\,\mathrm{Pa}$. The pipe rises to a faucet $6.0\,\mathrm{m}$ higher, where the diameter is $1.5\,\mathrm{cm}$. Treating water as an ideal fluid, compute the velocity and the pressure of the water in the pipe at the height of the faucet, while the water is flowing.

$$
A_1 v_1 = A_2 v_2 ,
\qquad
p_1 + \frac{1}{2}\rho v_1^2 + \rho g y_1 = p_2 + \frac{1}{2}\rho v_2^2 + \rho g y_2
$$
$$
v_2 = \left( \frac{d_1}{d_2} \right)^2 v_1 ,
\qquad
p_2 = p_1 - \frac{1}{2}\rho\,(v_2^2 - v_1^2) - \rho g\,(y_2 - y_1)
$$
$$
v_2 = \left( \frac{2.5}{1.5} \right)^2 \cdot 1.2\,\mathrm{m/s} = 3.33\,\mathrm{m/s}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
p_2
&= 3.00 \cdot 10^5\,\mathrm{Pa} - \frac{1}{2} \cdot 1000 \cdot (3.33^2 - 1.2^2)\,\mathrm{Pa} - 1000 \cdot 9.81 \cdot 6.0\,\mathrm{Pa} \\[6pt]
&= 3.00 \cdot 10^5\,\mathrm{Pa} - 0.048 \cdot 10^5\,\mathrm{Pa} - 0.589 \cdot 10^5\,\mathrm{Pa} \\[6pt]
&= 2.36 \cdot 10^5\,\mathrm{Pa}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The pipe narrows, so by the **continuity equation** the velocity increases in the ratio of the squares of the diameters, $\left( \dfrac{2.5}{1.5} \right)^2 = \dfrac{25}{9}$. In **Bernoulli's theorem** between the ground floor, at height $y_1 = 0$, and the faucet, at height $y_2 = 6.0\,\mathrm{m}$, all quantities are known except $p_2$, which is obtained by isolating it.

The pressure at the faucet decreases for two reasons: one part is used to accelerate the water, about $4.8 \cdot 10^3\,\mathrm{Pa}$, and a much larger part to lift it by $6.0\,\mathrm{m}$, about $5.9 \cdot 10^4\,\mathrm{Pa}$. At the height of the faucet the absolute pressure remains **greater than atmospheric pressure**, so the water reaches the faucet with enough pressure to flow out. The calculation used the unrounded value $v_2 = \dfrac{10}{3}\,\mathrm{m/s}$.

</div>
