## Exercise 1

An object hanging from a spring scale weighs $5.00\,\mathrm{N}$ in air; completely immersed in water, the spring scale reads $4.40\,\mathrm{N}$. Compute the volume and the density of the object, neglecting the buoyancy of the air.

$$
P - P_{\mathrm{app}} = S = \rho_a V g ,
\qquad
\frac{P}{P - P_{\mathrm{app}}} = \frac{\rho_c}{\rho_a}
$$
$$
V = \frac{P - P_{\mathrm{app}}}{\rho_a g} ,
\qquad
\rho_c = \frac{P}{P - P_{\mathrm{app}}}\, \rho_a
$$
$$
V = \frac{0.60}{1000 \cdot 9.81}\,\mathrm{m^3} = 6.1 \cdot 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{m^3} = 61\,\mathrm{cm^3}
$$
$$
\rho_c = \frac{5.00}{0.60} \cdot 1000\,\mathrm{kg/m^3} = 8.3 \cdot 10^3\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The immersed object is in equilibrium under three vertical forces: the weight $P$ downward, the buoyant force $S$ and the force of the spring scale upward. The spring scale therefore reads the **apparent weight** $P - S$, and the difference between the two readings is the buoyant force, $0.60\,\mathrm{N}$. By Archimedes' principle the buoyant force is the weight of the displaced water, which has the volume of the object; from this the volume is obtained.

The weight is $\rho_c V g$ and the buoyant force $\rho_a V g$, therefore their ratio is the ratio of the densities, and the volume and $g$ are not needed. The difference $5.00 - 4.40 = 0.60$ has only two significant figures, so the results are written with two figures. The object has a density about $8.3$ times that of water, a value compatible with a **metal alloy** such as brass.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A solid aluminum sphere ($\rho = 2.70 \cdot 10^3\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}$), of radius $3.0\,\mathrm{cm}$, hangs from an inextensible string of negligible mass and is held completely immersed in water, at rest, without touching the bottom. Compute the weight of the sphere, the buoyant force and the tension in the string.

::grafico{assi=0 uguale=1 curve="3+cos(t),2+sin(t),0,6.29" linee="0.5,4.5 0.5,0 5.5,0 5.5,4.5" poligoni="0.5,0 5.5,0 5.5,4 3,4 3,3 3.259,2.966 3.500,2.866 3.707,2.707 3.866,2.500 3.966,2.259 4,2 3.966,1.741 3.866,1.500 3.707,1.293 3.500,1.134 3.259,1.034 3,1 2.741,1.034 2.500,1.134 2.293,1.293 2.134,1.500 2.034,1.741 2,2 2.034,2.259 2.134,2.500 2.293,2.707 2.500,2.866 2.741,2.966 3,3 3,4 0.5,4" segmenti="3,3,3,4.8" vettori="3,3,3,3.75,T;3,2,3,2.44,S;3,2,3,0.8,P" xmin=0 xmax=6 ymin=-0.2 ymax=4.8 titolo="The forces acting on the sphere are the weight $P$, the buoyant force $S$ and the tension $T$ in the string, with $T + S = P$"}

$$
T + S - P = 0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
T = P - S = (\rho_c - \rho_a)\, V g ,
\qquad
V = \frac{4}{3}\pi r^3
$$
$$
V = \frac{4}{3}\pi\,(0.030)^3\,\mathrm{m^3} = 1.13 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m^3}
$$
$$
P = 2.70 \cdot 10^3 \cdot 1.13 \cdot 10^{-4} \cdot 9.81\,\mathrm{N} = 3.00\,\mathrm{N} ,
\qquad
S = 1000 \cdot 1.13 \cdot 10^{-4} \cdot 9.81\,\mathrm{N} = 1.11\,\mathrm{N}
$$
$$
T = 3.00\,\mathrm{N} - 1.11\,\mathrm{N} = 1.89\,\mathrm{N}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The free-body diagram contains the weight $P$ downward, the buoyant force $S$ and the tension $T$ upward. The sphere is at rest, so by Newton's first law the resultant is zero and the tension is the difference between the weight and the buoyant force. The sphere is completely immersed, therefore the immersed volume is the whole volume of the sphere, which must be computed in cubic meters.

The weight and the buoyant force differ only in the density, so the tension can also be computed in a single step, $T = (2700 - 1000) \cdot 1.13 \cdot 10^{-4} \cdot 9.81\,\mathrm{N} \approx 1.89\,\mathrm{N}$. The string supports $63\,\%$ of the weight, because the buoyant force of the water balances the remaining part; therefore the tension is **less than the weight** of the sphere in air.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A balloon has an envelope of mass $3.0\,\mathrm{g}$ and, inflated with helium, has volume $1.00 \cdot 10^{-2}\,\mathrm{m^3}$. The densities of air and helium are $1.20\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}$ and $0.166\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}$; the volume of the envelope is negligible. Compute the resultant force on the free balloon and the maximum mass that the balloon can lift hanging from a string of negligible mass.

$$
F = S - P_{\mathrm{He}} - P_{\mathrm{inv}} = (\rho_{\mathrm{air}} - \rho_{\mathrm{He}})\, V g - m_{\mathrm{inv}}\, g
$$
$$
F - m_{\max}\, g = 0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
m_{\max} = (\rho_{\mathrm{air}} - \rho_{\mathrm{He}})\, V - m_{\mathrm{inv}}
$$
$$
F = (1.20 - 0.166) \cdot 1.00 \cdot 10^{-2} \cdot 9.81\,\mathrm{N} - 3.0 \cdot 10^{-3} \cdot 9.81\,\mathrm{N} = 7.2 \cdot 10^{-2}\,\mathrm{N}
$$
$$
m_{\max} = 1.034 \cdot 10^{-2}\,\mathrm{kg} - 3.0 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{kg} = 7.3 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{kg} = 7.3\,\mathrm{g}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

**Archimedes' principle** holds for any fluid, hence also for air: the balloon receives a buoyant force equal to the weight of the air that occupies its volume. Acting downward on the balloon are the weight of the helium and the weight of the envelope. The buoyant force and the weight of the helium concern the same volume, therefore they are collected in the term $(\rho_{\mathrm{air}} - \rho_{\mathrm{He}})\, V g$, and the resultant is positive, that is, directed upward.

Hanging a mass $m$ adds $m g$ to the weight; the balloon stays aloft as long as the resultant is positive, and the maximum mass is the one that makes it zero. Dividing by $g$ the condition becomes a balance of masses. The balloon lifts **at most** about $7\,\mathrm{g}$; the result has two significant figures because the mass of the envelope has two.

</div>
