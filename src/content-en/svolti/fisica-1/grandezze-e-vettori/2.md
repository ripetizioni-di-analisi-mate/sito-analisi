## Exercise 1

Determine which of the following relations are dimensionally correct, where $x$ and $\Delta x$ are lengths, $t$ is a time, $v$ and $v_0$ are velocities and $a$ is an acceleration:
$$
\text{(i)}\quad v^2 = v_0^2 + 2 a\,\Delta x,
\qquad\qquad
\text{(ii)}\quad x = v_0 t + a t .
$$

$$
[v] = [L\,T^{-1}],
\qquad
[a] = [L\,T^{-2}],
\qquad
[\Delta x] = [L],
\qquad
[t] = [T]
$$
$$
\text{(i)}\quad [v^2] = [v_0^2] = [L^2\,T^{-2}],
\qquad
[a\,\Delta x] = [L\,T^{-2}]\,[L] = [L^2\,T^{-2}]
$$
$$
\text{(ii)}\quad [x] = [L],
\qquad
[v_0 t] = [L\,T^{-1}]\,[T] = [L],
\qquad
[a t] = [L\,T^{-2}]\,[T] = [L\,T^{-1}]
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

By the **principle of dimensional homogeneity**, all the terms added in a relation and the two sides of the equality must have the same dimensions. The factor $2$ is a pure number and does not change the dimensions. In relation (i) the three terms all have dimension $[L^2\,T^{-2}]$, so the relation is **dimensionally correct**. In relation (ii) the term $a t$ has the dimension of a velocity, whereas $x$ and $v_0 t$ are lengths; therefore relation (ii) is **wrong**. Relation (i) is a formula of uniformly accelerated motion; its dimensional correctness is a *necessary* condition, and establishing that it is exact requires its derivation, carried out in the topic on one-dimensional kinematics.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Determine the dimensions and the SI unit of the constants $k$, $b$ and $G$ that appear in the laws
$$
F = k x,
\qquad
F = b v,
\qquad
F = G\,\frac{m_1 m_2}{r^2} ,
$$
where $F$ is a force, $x$ and $r$ are lengths, $v$ is a velocity, $m_1$ and $m_2$ are masses.

$$
[F] = [M\,L\,T^{-2}]
$$
$$
[k] = \frac{[F]}{[x]} = \frac{[M\,L\,T^{-2}]}{[L]} = [M\,T^{-2}]
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\mathrm{kg/s^2} = \mathrm{N/m}
$$
$$
[b] = \frac{[F]}{[v]} = \frac{[M\,L\,T^{-2}]}{[L\,T^{-1}]} = [M\,T^{-1}]
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\mathrm{kg/s}
$$
$$
[G] = \frac{[F]\,[r^2]}{[m_1 m_2]} = \frac{[M\,L\,T^{-2}]\,[L^2]}{[M^2]} = [M^{-1}\,L^{3}\,T^{-2}]
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\mathrm{m^3/(kg\cdot s^2)} = \mathrm{N\cdot m^2/kg^2}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Each constant is isolated in its law, and its dimensions are those of the resulting ratio, because the two sides of a physical relation have the same dimensions. Force has the dimensions of mass times acceleration, that is, $[M\,L\,T^{-2}]$. The spring constant $k$ is a force divided by a length, so it is measured in $\mathrm{N/m}$, which in base units is $\mathrm{kg/s^2}$. The coefficient $b$ is a force divided by a velocity and is measured in $\mathrm{kg/s}$. For the gravitational constant the force is multiplied by the square of the distance and divided by the product of the masses; the unit is $\mathrm{N\cdot m^2/kg^2}$, which in **base units** becomes $\mathrm{m^3/(kg\cdot s^2)}$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

The speed $v$ of waves on a stretched string depends on the tension $F$ of the string, which is a force, and on its linear density $\mu$, that is, on the mass per unit length. Assuming $v = C\,F^{\alpha}\mu^{\beta}$ with $C$ dimensionless, determine $\alpha$ and $\beta$.

$$
[v] = [L\,T^{-1}],
\qquad
[F] = [M\,L\,T^{-2}],
\qquad
[\mu] = [M\,L^{-1}]
$$
$$
[L\,T^{-1}] = [M\,L\,T^{-2}]^{\alpha}\,[M\,L^{-1}]^{\beta} = [M^{\alpha + \beta}\,L^{\alpha - \beta}\,T^{-2\alpha}]
$$
$$
\begin{cases}
\alpha + \beta = 0 \\[4pt]
\alpha - \beta = 1 \\[4pt]
-2\alpha = -1
\end{cases}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\alpha = \frac{1}{2},
\qquad
\beta = -\frac{1}{2}
$$
$$
v = C\,\sqrt{\frac{F}{\mu}}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The dimensions of the two sides are written down: the linear density is a mass divided by a length, so it has dimensions $[M\,L^{-1}]$. The powers of a product distribute over the factors, therefore the right-hand side has exponent $\alpha + \beta$ for mass, $\alpha - \beta$ for length and $-2\alpha$ for time. By **dimensional homogeneity** these exponents must coincide with those of the left-hand side, namely $0$, $1$ and $-1$, and the three conditions must hold simultaneously. The third equation gives $\alpha = \dfrac{1}{2}$, the first $\beta = -\dfrac{1}{2}$, and the second is satisfied, since $\dfrac{1}{2} + \dfrac{1}{2} = 1$. Hence the speed is proportional to the **square root of the tension** and inversely proportional to $\sqrt{\mu}$, and quadrupling the tension doubles the speed.

</div>

## Exercise 4

The drag force $F$ that the air exerts on a fast car depends on the density $\rho$ of the air, on the speed $v$ of the car and on the area $A$ of its frontal cross section. Assuming $F = C\,\rho^{a} v^{b} A^{c}$ with $C$ dimensionless, determine the exponents and establish by how much the drag increases if the speed goes from $90\,\mathrm{km/h}$ to $126\,\mathrm{km/h}$.

$$
[F] = [M\,L\,T^{-2}],
\qquad
[\rho] = [M\,L^{-3}],
\qquad
[v] = [L\,T^{-1}],
\qquad
[A] = [L^2]
$$
$$
[M\,L\,T^{-2}] = [M^{a}\,L^{-3a + b + 2c}\,T^{-b}]
$$
$$
\begin{cases}
a = 1 \\[4pt]
-3a + b + 2c = 1 \\[4pt]
-b = -2
\end{cases}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
a = 1,
\qquad
b = 2,
\qquad
c = 1
$$
$$
F = C\,\rho\,v^2 A,
\qquad
\frac{F_2}{F_1} = \left( \frac{v_2}{v_1} \right)^2 = \left( \frac{126}{90} \right)^2 = 1.4^2 = 1.96
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The dimensions of the right-hand side are obtained by multiplying those of the three factors raised to their respective exponents: mass appears only in the density, time only in the speed, length in all three. Equating the exponents of $[M]$, $[L]$ and $[T]$ to those of the force gives a system of three equations in three unknowns. The first and the third immediately give $a = 1$ and $b = 2$; substituting into the second gives $-3 + 2 + 2c = 1$, hence $c = 1$. The drag is therefore proportional to the **square of the speed**. The ratio of the two drag forces does not depend on the units, since the speeds appear as a ratio, and a $40\%$ increase in speed almost doubles the drag, which becomes $1.96$ times the initial one.

</div>
