## Exercise 1

The position of a point in the $xy$-plane is
$$
\vec{r}(t) = b t\,\hat{\imath} + \left( c t - d t^2 \right)\hat{\jmath},
\qquad
b = 2.0\,\mathrm{m/s},
\quad
c = 6.0\,\mathrm{m/s},
\quad
d = 1.0\,\mathrm{m/s^2} .
$$
Compute the velocity and the acceleration, the magnitude of the velocity at the instant $t = 1.0\,\mathrm{s}$, and the instant at which the velocity is perpendicular to the acceleration.

::grafico{f="3*x - x^2/4" vettori="2,5,4,9,v(1 s);2,5,2,3,a;6,9,8,9,v(3 s)" punti="2,5,pieno;6,9,pieno" xmin=-0.6 xmax=12.6 ymin=-0.6 ymax=10.6 uguale=1 titolo="Trajectory $y = 3x - \dfrac{x^2}{4}$ with velocity and acceleration at $t = 1.0\,\mathrm{s}$ and velocity at $t = 3.0\,\mathrm{s}$ (lengths in meters)"}

$$
\vec{v} = \frac{d\vec{r}}{dt} = b\,\hat{\imath} + (c - 2 d t)\,\hat{\jmath},
\qquad
\vec{a} = \frac{d\vec{v}}{dt} = -2d\,\hat{\jmath}
$$
$$
\vec{v}(1.0\,\mathrm{s}) = \big( 2.0\,\hat{\imath} + (6.0 - 2.0)\,\hat{\jmath} \big)\,\mathrm{m/s} = (2.0\,\hat{\imath} + 4.0\,\hat{\jmath})\,\mathrm{m/s}
$$
$$
v(1.0\,\mathrm{s}) = \sqrt{2.0^2 + 4.0^2}\,\mathrm{m/s} = \sqrt{20}\,\mathrm{m/s} \approx 4.5\,\mathrm{m/s},
\qquad
\vec{a} = -2.0\,\hat{\jmath}\,\mathrm{m/s^2}
$$
$$
\vec{v} \cdot \vec{a} = -2d\,(c - 2dt) = 0
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
t = \frac{c}{2d} = \frac{6.0\,\mathrm{m/s}}{2.0\,\mathrm{m/s^2}} = 3.0\,\mathrm{s}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The **derivative of a vector** is computed by differentiating the components separately, because the unit vectors $\hat{\imath}$ and $\hat{\jmath}$ are constant. The $x$ component of the position grows linearly, so $v_x = b$ is constant; the $y$ component is a polynomial of degree two, and its derivative is $c - 2dt$. Differentiating again gives a constant acceleration, directed downward. The magnitude of the velocity at $t = 1.0\,\mathrm{s}$ is about $4.5\,\mathrm{m/s}$, with two significant figures like the data. Two nonzero vectors are perpendicular when their dot product is zero; the acceleration has only the $y$ component, therefore the condition requires $v_y = 0$, and this happens at $t = 3.0\,\mathrm{s}$. At that instant the point is at $(6.0,\ 9.0)\,\mathrm{m}$, that is, at the **highest point** of the trajectory, where the velocity is horizontal and tangent to the curve, as the figure shows.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A point moves on a circle centered at the origin according to the law
$$
\vec{r}(t) = R\cos(\omega t)\,\hat{\imath} + R\sin(\omega t)\,\hat{\jmath},
\qquad
R = 0.50\,\mathrm{m},
\quad
\omega = 4.0\,\mathrm{rad/s} .
$$
Compute the velocity and the acceleration, verify that the velocity is perpendicular to the position vector and that $\vec{a} = -\omega^2\,\vec{r}$, and compute the magnitudes of the velocity and of the acceleration.

$$
\vec{v} = \frac{d\vec{r}}{dt} = -R\omega\sin(\omega t)\,\hat{\imath} + R\omega\cos(\omega t)\,\hat{\jmath}
$$
$$
\vec{a} = \frac{d\vec{v}}{dt} = -R\omega^2\cos(\omega t)\,\hat{\imath} - R\omega^2\sin(\omega t)\,\hat{\jmath} = -\omega^2\,\vec{r}
$$
$$
\vec{v} \cdot \vec{r} = -R^2\omega\sin(\omega t)\cos(\omega t) + R^2\omega\cos(\omega t)\sin(\omega t) = 0
$$
$$
v = R\omega\sqrt{\sin^2(\omega t) + \cos^2(\omega t)} = R\omega = 0.50\,\mathrm{m} \cdot 4.0\,\mathrm{rad/s} = 2.0\,\mathrm{m/s}
$$
$$
a = \omega^2 R = (4.0\,\mathrm{rad/s})^2 \cdot 0.50\,\mathrm{m} = 8.0\,\mathrm{m/s^2}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The components are differentiated with the chain rule: the derivative of $\cos(\omega t)$ is $-\omega\sin(\omega t)$ and that of $\sin(\omega t)$ is $\omega\cos(\omega t)$. Differentiating each component twice multiplies it by $-\omega^2$, so the acceleration is $-\omega^2\,\vec{r}$, directed toward the center. The position vector has constant magnitude $R$, therefore by the **derivative of a vector of constant magnitude** the velocity is perpendicular to $\vec{r}$, and the calculation of the dot product confirms it. The magnitudes are obtained with the identity $\sin^2 + \cos^2 = 1$. The radian is dimensionless, so $\mathrm{rad/s} \cdot \mathrm{m} = \mathrm{m/s}$ and the units of the results are correct. The velocity also has constant magnitude, and indeed the acceleration, directed toward the center, is perpendicular to the velocity.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A point of mass $m = 0.50\,\mathrm{kg}$ moves in the $xy$-plane according to the law
$$
\vec{r}(t) = b t\,\hat{\imath} + \left( c t - d t^2 \right)\hat{\jmath},
\qquad
b = 2.0\,\mathrm{m/s},
\quad
c = 6.0\,\mathrm{m/s},
\quad
d = 1.0\,\mathrm{m/s^2} .
$$
Compute the rate of change of its kinetic energy $K = \dfrac{1}{2} m\,\vec{v} \cdot \vec{v}$ at the instant $t = 1.0\,\mathrm{s}$, first with the differentiation rule for the dot product and then by differentiating $K(t)$ directly.

$$
\vec{v} = b\,\hat{\imath} + (c - 2dt)\,\hat{\jmath},
\qquad
\vec{a} = -2d\,\hat{\jmath}
$$
$$
\frac{dK}{dt} = \frac{1}{2} m \left( \frac{d\vec{v}}{dt} \cdot \vec{v} + \vec{v} \cdot \frac{d\vec{v}}{dt} \right) = m\,\vec{v} \cdot \vec{a}
$$
$$
\frac{dK}{dt}(1.0\,\mathrm{s}) = 0.50\,\mathrm{kg} \cdot \big( 2.0 \cdot 0 + 4.0 \cdot (-2.0) \big)\,\mathrm{m^2/s^3} = -4.0\,\mathrm{W}
$$
$$
K(t) = \frac{1}{2} m \left[ b^2 + (c - 2dt)^2 \right],
\qquad
\frac{dK}{dt} = \frac{1}{2} m \cdot 2\,(c - 2dt)\,(-2d) = -2 m d\,(c - 2dt)
$$
$$
\frac{dK}{dt}(1.0\,\mathrm{s}) = -2 \cdot 0.50\,\mathrm{kg} \cdot 1.0\,\mathrm{m/s^2} \cdot 4.0\,\mathrm{m/s} = -4.0\,\mathrm{W}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The kinetic energy is defined through the dot product $\vec{v} \cdot \vec{v} = v^2$, so it is differentiated with the **differentiation rule for the dot product**. The two terms $\dfrac{d\vec{v}}{dt} \cdot \vec{v}$ and $\vec{v} \cdot \dfrac{d\vec{v}}{dt}$ are equal by the symmetry of the dot product, therefore the derivative reduces to $m\,\vec{v} \cdot \vec{a}$. The velocity and the acceleration are obtained by differentiating the components of the position, and at $t = 1.0\,\mathrm{s}$ they are $(2.0,\ 4.0)\,\mathrm{m/s}$ and $(0,\ -2.0)\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$. The result is $-4.0\,\mathrm{W}$, since $\mathrm{kg\cdot m^2/s^3} = \mathrm{J/s} = \mathrm{W}$. The direct calculation, which differentiates $K(t)$ with the chain rule, gives the same value. The negative sign indicates that the kinetic energy **decreases**: at $t = 1.0\,\mathrm{s}$ the point is rising and the acceleration has a component opposite to the velocity. The connection between $\dfrac{dK}{dt}$ and the power of the resultant force is seen in the topic on work and energy.

</div>

## Exercise 4

A point moves along a helix according to the law
$$
\vec{r}(t) = R\cos(\omega t)\,\hat{\imath} + R\sin(\omega t)\,\hat{\jmath} + u t\,\hat{k},
\qquad
R = 0.20\,\mathrm{m},
\quad
\omega = 3.0\,\mathrm{rad/s},
\quad
u = 0.80\,\mathrm{m/s} .
$$
Prove that the magnitude of the velocity is constant and compute it; deduce that the acceleration is perpendicular to the velocity, verify it by calculation and determine the magnitude of the acceleration.

$$
\vec{v} = -R\omega\sin(\omega t)\,\hat{\imath} + R\omega\cos(\omega t)\,\hat{\jmath} + u\,\hat{k}
$$
$$
v = \sqrt{R^2\omega^2 \left( \sin^2(\omega t) + \cos^2(\omega t) \right) + u^2} = \sqrt{R^2\omega^2 + u^2}
$$
$$
v = \sqrt{(0.20 \cdot 3.0)^2 + 0.80^2}\,\mathrm{m/s} = \sqrt{0.36 + 0.64}\,\mathrm{m/s} = 1.0\,\mathrm{m/s}
$$
$$
\vec{a} = \frac{d\vec{v}}{dt} = -R\omega^2\cos(\omega t)\,\hat{\imath} - R\omega^2\sin(\omega t)\,\hat{\jmath}
$$
$$
\vec{v} \cdot \vec{a} = R^2\omega^3\sin(\omega t)\cos(\omega t) - R^2\omega^3\cos(\omega t)\sin(\omega t) + u \cdot 0 = 0
$$
$$
a = R\omega^2 = 0.20\,\mathrm{m} \cdot (3.0\,\mathrm{rad/s})^2 = 1.8\,\mathrm{m/s^2}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Differentiating the components gives the velocity; the third component is constant, because along the $z$-axis the point advances in uniform motion. In the square of the magnitude the terms with sine and cosine are collected and simplified with $\sin^2 + \cos^2 = 1$, so the magnitude of the velocity **does not depend on time** and equals $1.0\,\mathrm{m/s}$. By the **derivative of a vector of constant magnitude**, applied to $\vec{v}$, the derivative $\vec{a}$ is perpendicular to $\vec{v}$ at every instant; the dot product computed in components is indeed zero. The acceleration is horizontal and directed toward the $z$-axis, and its magnitude is $1.8\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$. As a check, for $u = 0$ the helix becomes a circle of radius $R$ traversed with angular velocity $\omega$, and the formulas reduce to $v = R\omega$ and $a = R\omega^2$.

</div>
