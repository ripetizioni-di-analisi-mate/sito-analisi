## Exercise 1

A rocket in space, far from any celestial body, has initial mass $M_i = 3.0 \cdot 10^{3}\,\mathrm{kg}$, of which $2.0 \cdot 10^{3}\,\mathrm{kg}$ is fuel, and starts from rest. The gases are expelled backward with velocity $u = 2.0\,\mathrm{km/s}$ relative to the rocket. Compute the velocity of the rocket when it has burned all the fuel. Then compute what fraction of the initial mass would have to be fuel to reach $3.0\,\mathrm{km/s}$ with the same engine.

$$
M\,\frac{dv}{dt} = -u\,\frac{dM}{dt}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
v_f - v_i = u \ln \frac{M_i}{M_f}
$$
$$
M_f = 3.0 \cdot 10^{3}\,\mathrm{kg} - 2.0 \cdot 10^{3}\,\mathrm{kg} = 1.0 \cdot 10^{3}\,\mathrm{kg}
$$
$$
v_f = 2.0\,\mathrm{km/s} \cdot \ln 3 \approx 2.0 \cdot 1.099\,\mathrm{km/s} \approx 2.2\,\mathrm{km/s}
$$
$$
\frac{M_i}{M_f} = \exp\left( \frac{v_f - v_i}{u} \right) = \exp\left( \frac{3.0}{2.0} \right) \approx 4.48,
\qquad
\frac{M_i - M_f}{M_i} = 1 - \frac{M_f}{M_i} \approx 1 - 0.223 \approx 0.78
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The rocket is far from any celestial body, so the external forces are negligible and the **equation of variable-mass systems** reduces to $M\,\dfrac{dv}{dt} = -u\,\dfrac{dM}{dt}$, with the minus sign because the gases leave backward. Separating the variables and integrating we obtain the **rocket equation**, in which only the exhaust velocity and the ratio between initial and final mass appear. When the fuel is exhausted $\dfrac{1}{3}$ of the mass remains, so the rocket reaches $u \ln 3 \approx 2.2\,\mathrm{km/s}$.

For the second question we solve the equation for the mass ratio, which equals the exponential of $\dfrac{v_f - v_i}{u} = 1.5$. The fuel must be about $78\,\%$ of the initial mass, compared with $67\,\%$ in the first case. To go from about $2.2$ to $3.0\,\mathrm{km/s}$ the mass ratio must go from $3.0$ to about $4.5$, because it grows exponentially with the final velocity.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A rocket of mass $M_0 = 2.0 \cdot 10^{4}\,\mathrm{kg}$ stands vertically at rest on the launch pad. At ignition the engines expel gas downward with velocity $u = 2.5\,\mathrm{km/s}$ relative to the rocket, with a mass flow rate $q = 120\,\mathrm{kg/s}$. Compute the thrust, the acceleration of the rocket immediately after ignition and the minimum flow rate with which the rocket can lift off.

$$
M\,\frac{dv}{dt} = -M g + u\,q,
\qquad
q = -\frac{dM}{dt}
$$
$$
S = u\,q = 2.5 \cdot 10^{3}\,\mathrm{m/s} \cdot 120\,\mathrm{kg/s} = 3.0 \cdot 10^{5}\,\mathrm{N}
$$
$$
a_0 = \frac{u\,q - M_0 g}{M_0} = \frac{3.0 \cdot 10^{5}\,\mathrm{N} - 2.0 \cdot 10^{4} \cdot 9.81\,\mathrm{N}}{2.0 \cdot 10^{4}\,\mathrm{kg}} = \frac{1.04 \cdot 10^{5}\,\mathrm{N}}{2.0 \cdot 10^{4}\,\mathrm{kg}} \approx 5.2\,\mathrm{m/s^2}
$$
$$
u\,q_{\min} = M_0 g
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
q_{\min} = \frac{M_0 g}{u} = \frac{2.0 \cdot 10^{4} \cdot 9.81}{2.5 \cdot 10^{3}}\,\mathrm{kg/s} \approx 78\,\mathrm{kg/s}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

With the vertical axis pointing upward, the external force is the weight $-M g$ and the velocity of the gases relative to the rocket is $-u$. The mass decreases at rate $q$, that is $\dfrac{dM}{dt} = -q$, so the term $\vec{u}\,\dfrac{dM}{dt}$ of the **equation of variable-mass systems** equals $(-u)(-q) = u\,q$ and points upward; this term is the **thrust**, which equals $3.0 \cdot 10^{5}\,\mathrm{N}$.

Immediately after ignition the mass is still $M_0$, and dividing the resultant of thrust and weight by $M_0$ we obtain an initial acceleration of about $5.2\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$ upward. During the flight the mass decreases, so for the same thrust the acceleration increases.

The rocket lifts off the pad only if the thrust exceeds the weight; the condition $u\,q > M_0 g$ gives the minimum flow rate, about $78\,\mathrm{kg/s}$. Below this value the pad supports the difference and the rocket stays at rest while consuming fuel.

</div>

## Exercise 3

An open wagon moves on a straight horizontal track, with negligible friction, at constant velocity $v = 2.0\,\mathrm{m/s}$. From a stationary hopper, sand falls vertically into the wagon with flow rate $q = 50\,\mathrm{kg/s}$; the sand comes to rest relative to the wagon immediately. Compute the horizontal force needed to keep the velocity constant, the power of this force and the rate at which the kinetic energy of the system increases.

$$
M\,\frac{dv}{dt} = F + u_x\,\frac{dM}{dt},
\qquad
u_x = 0 - v = -v,
\qquad
\frac{dM}{dt} = q,
\qquad
\frac{dv}{dt} = 0
$$
$$
F = v\,q = 2.0\,\mathrm{m/s} \cdot 50\,\mathrm{kg/s} = 1.0 \cdot 10^{2}\,\mathrm{N}
$$
$$
P = F\,v = v^2 q = 1.0 \cdot 10^{2}\,\mathrm{N} \cdot 2.0\,\mathrm{m/s} = 2.0 \cdot 10^{2}\,\mathrm{W}
$$
$$
\frac{dK}{dt} = \frac{d}{dt}\left( \frac{1}{2}\,M v^2 \right) = \frac{1}{2}\,v^2\,\frac{dM}{dt} = \frac{1}{2}\,v^2 q = 1.0 \cdot 10^{2}\,\mathrm{W}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The sand falls vertically, so before entering the wagon it has zero horizontal velocity, and its velocity relative to the wagon, along the horizontal, is $-v$. The mass of the wagon increases at rate $q$, therefore in the **equation of variable-mass systems** the term $u_x\,\dfrac{dM}{dt} = -v\,q$ slows the wagon down. To keep $\dfrac{dv}{dt} = 0$ an external force $F = v\,q = 1.0 \cdot 10^{2}\,\mathrm{N}$ in the direction of motion is needed. The same result is obtained with the **impulse-momentum theorem** applied to the sand, since every second $50\,\mathrm{kg}$ of sand go from horizontal velocity $0$ to $2.0\,\mathrm{m/s}$, so they receive a momentum of $100\,\mathrm{kg\cdot m/s}$ per second.

The force acts on a wagon moving at $2.0\,\mathrm{m/s}$, so its power is $F v = 2.0 \cdot 10^{2}\,\mathrm{W}$. The kinetic energy increases only because the mass increases, and its derivative equals $\dfrac{1}{2}\,v^2 q = 1.0 \cdot 10^{2}\,\mathrm{W}$, half of the power supplied. The other half is dissipated in the perfectly inelastic collision between the sand and the wagon, during which the sand slides on the floor before coming to rest relative to the wagon.

</div>
