## Exercise 1

A cannon of mass $M = 900\,\mathrm{kg}$, free to slide on horizontal ground with negligible friction, fires horizontally a shell of mass $m = 6.0\,\mathrm{kg}$ that leaves the muzzle with velocity $v = 300\,\mathrm{m/s}$ relative to the ground. Compute the recoil velocity of the cannon and compare the kinetic energies of the shell and of the cannon.

$$
0 = m v + M V
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
V = -\frac{m}{M}\,v
$$
$$
V = -\frac{6.0}{900} \cdot 300\,\mathrm{m/s} = -2.0\,\mathrm{m/s}
$$
$$
K_p = \frac{1}{2} \cdot 6.0 \cdot 300^2\,\mathrm{J} = 2.7 \cdot 10^{5}\,\mathrm{J},
\qquad
K_c = \frac{1}{2} \cdot 900 \cdot 2.0^2\,\mathrm{J} = 1.8 \cdot 10^{3}\,\mathrm{J}
$$
$$
\frac{K_c}{K_p} = \frac{\dfrac{1}{2}\,M V^2}{\dfrac{1}{2}\,m v^2} = \frac{m}{M} = \frac{1}{150}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The system consists of the cannon and the shell, and the forces of the propellant gases are **internal**. The external forces, weights and the reaction of the ground, are vertical, because friction is negligible; therefore the horizontal component of momentum is conserved, and before firing it is zero. After firing the sum $m v + M V$ must remain zero, therefore the cannon **recoils** in the sense opposite to the shell at $2.0\,\mathrm{m/s}$.

The two momenta have the same magnitude, $1.8 \cdot 10^{3}\,\mathrm{kg\cdot m/s}$, while the kinetic energies are very different. Since $K = \dfrac{p^2}{2m}$, for equal $p$ the energy is inversely proportional to the mass, and the shell receives $150$ times the energy of the cannon. The total kinetic energy, about $2.7 \cdot 10^{5}\,\mathrm{J}$, comes from the chemical energy of the charge.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Two skaters, $A$ of mass $60\,\mathrm{kg}$ and $B$ of mass $40\,\mathrm{kg}$, stand at rest facing each other on a horizontal ice rink. They push each other with their hands and $B$ moves away with speed $1.5\,\mathrm{m/s}$. Neglecting friction with the ice, compute the velocity of $A$ and the distance between the skaters $4.0\,\mathrm{s}$ after the push.

$$
m_A v_A + m_B v_B = 0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
v_A = -\frac{m_B}{m_A}\,v_B
$$
$$
v_A = -\frac{40}{60} \cdot 1.5\,\mathrm{m/s} = -1.0\,\mathrm{m/s}
$$
$$
d = (v_B - v_A)\,t = (1.5 + 1.0)\,\mathrm{m/s} \cdot 4.0\,\mathrm{s} = 10\,\mathrm{m}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The forces with which the skaters push each other are internal to the system formed by the two of them; the ice is smooth, so the external forces are vertical and the horizontal momentum remains equal to the initial one, that is zero. With the axis in the direction of motion of $B$ we obtain $v_A = -1.0\,\mathrm{m/s}$, so $A$, which has the larger mass, moves away more slowly in the opposite sense.

After the push no horizontal force acts on the skaters, so each one moves with uniform rectilinear motion. The distance grows with the relative velocity, $2.5\,\mathrm{m/s}$, and after $4.0\,\mathrm{s}$ it **is $10\,\mathrm{m}$** (neglecting the initial distance between the centers of gravity). The center of mass stays at rest; indeed $A$ has moved by $4.0\,\mathrm{m}$ and $B$ by $6.0\,\mathrm{m}$, and $60 \cdot 4.0 = 40 \cdot 6.0$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A projectile is launched from horizontal ground with velocity $v_0 = 20\,\mathrm{m/s}$ inclined at $45^\circ$ above the horizontal. At the highest point of the trajectory it explodes into two fragments: the first, with mass equal to one third of the total mass, is at rest immediately after the explosion and falls vertically. Neglecting air resistance, determine the velocity of the second fragment immediately after the explosion and the point where it touches the ground.

::grafico{curve="t,t-t^2/40.77,0,20.39;t,t-t^2/40.77,20.39,40.77;t,10.19-0.0109*(t-20.39)^2,20.39,50.97" vettori="20.39,10.19,20.39,0.3" punti="20.39,10.19,pieno;20.39,0,vuoto,A;40.77,0,vuoto,CM;50.97,0,vuoto,B" xmin=-0.5 xmax=56 ymin=-0.8 ymax=13 nomi="x,y" titolo="Trajectory before the explosion, vertical fall of fragment $A$, parabola of fragment $B$ and parabola of the center of mass (dashed), which touches the ground at $R \approx 40.8\,\mathrm{m}$; fragment $B$ touches the ground at $\dfrac{5R}{4} \approx 51\,\mathrm{m}$ (distances in meters)"}

$$
v_{0x} = v_{0y} = v_0 \cos 45^\circ = 20 \cdot \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\,\mathrm{m/s} \approx 14.1\,\mathrm{m/s},
\qquad
R = \frac{2\,v_{0x}\,v_{0y}}{g} = \frac{400}{9.81}\,\mathrm{m} \approx 40.8\,\mathrm{m}
$$
$$
M\,v_{0x} = \frac{M}{3} \cdot 0 + \frac{2M}{3}\,v_2
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
v_2 = \frac{3}{2}\,v_{0x} \approx 21\,\mathrm{m/s}\ \text{ horizontal}
$$
$$
x_{cm} = \frac{\dfrac{M}{3}\,x_1 + \dfrac{2M}{3}\,x_2}{M} = R,
\qquad
x_1 = \frac{R}{2}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
x_2 = \frac{3R - x_1}{2} = \frac{5R}{4}
$$
$$
x_2 = \frac{5}{4} \cdot 40.8\,\mathrm{m} \approx 51\,\mathrm{m}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

At the highest point the velocity of the projectile is horizontal and equals $v_{0x}$. The explosion lasts a very short time and its forces are internal, while the weight is not impulsive; therefore the momentum immediately after the explosion is equal to that immediately before. The vertical component is zero before and the first fragment is at rest, so the second fragment also has zero vertical velocity; from the horizontal component we obtain $v_2 = \dfrac{3}{2}\,v_{0x} \approx 21\,\mathrm{m/s}$.

To find the landing point we use the **center-of-mass theorem**. The only external forces are the weights, so the center of mass continues along the original parabola and reaches the ground at distance $R$, the range. The two fragments start from the same height with zero vertical velocity, so they touch the ground at the same instant, when the center of mass is also on the ground. At that instant the first fragment is below the highest point, at $\dfrac{R}{2}$, and the definition of the center of mass gives $x_2 = \dfrac{5R}{4} \approx 51\,\mathrm{m}$.

As a check, the second fragment takes the same time to fall as the second half of the flight, $\dfrac{v_{0y}}{g} \approx 1.44\,\mathrm{s}$, and in this time it covers horizontally $21.2 \cdot 1.44\,\mathrm{m} \approx 30.6\,\mathrm{m}$; added to $\dfrac{R}{2} \approx 20.4\,\mathrm{m}$ this gives $51\,\mathrm{m}$.

</div>

## Exercise 4

A person of mass $m = 75\,\mathrm{kg}$ stands at one end of a boat of mass $M = 125\,\mathrm{kg}$ and length $L = 4.0\,\mathrm{m}$, at rest in calm water. The person walks to the other end and stops. Neglecting the horizontal resistance of the water, compute how far the boat moves and how far the person moves relative to the water.

$$
\Delta x_{cm} = 0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
m\,\Delta x_p + M\,\Delta x_b = 0,
\qquad
\Delta x_p - \Delta x_b = L
$$
$$
\Delta x_b = -\frac{m\,L}{m + M},
\qquad
\Delta x_p = \frac{M\,L}{m + M}
$$
$$
|\Delta x_b| = \frac{75 \cdot 4.0}{200}\,\mathrm{m} = 1.5\,\mathrm{m},
\qquad
\Delta x_p = \frac{125 \cdot 4.0}{200}\,\mathrm{m} = 2.5\,\mathrm{m}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

We consider the system formed by the person and the boat. The forces between the feet and the bottom of the boat are internal, and the external forces, weights and buoyancy, are vertical, because the horizontal resistance of the water is negligible. The system starts from rest, so by the **conservation of horizontal momentum** the center of mass stays at rest horizontally the whole time, even while the person accelerates and slows down.

The zero displacement of the center of mass gives the first equation; the second expresses that the person moves by $L$ relative to the boat. The system of the two linear equations gives the displacement of the boat, $1.5\,\mathrm{m}$ in the sense opposite to the person, and that of the person relative to the water, $2.5\,\mathrm{m}$. As a check, $75 \cdot 2.5 = 125 \cdot 1.5 = 187.5$, so the two contributions to the position of the center of mass cancel.

</div>
