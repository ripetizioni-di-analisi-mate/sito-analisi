## Exercise 1

A crane lifts a load of mass $m = 500\,\mathrm{kg}$ by $h = 12\,\mathrm{m}$ in $\Delta t = 20\,\mathrm{s}$, at constant speed. Compute the power developed by the crane.

$$
F = m g , \qquad L = m g h , \qquad P = \frac{L}{\Delta t} = \frac{m g h}{\Delta t}
$$
$$
P = \frac{500 \cdot 9.81 \cdot 12}{20}\,\mathrm{W} = 2943\,\mathrm{W} \approx 2.9\,\mathrm{kW}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The load rises at constant speed, so its acceleration is zero and the force of the crane balances the weight, $F = mg$. This force is vertical and in the direction of the displacement, therefore it does the work $mgh$, opposite to the work of the weight. Since the speed is constant, the work is distributed uniformly in time and the **power** is the ratio of the work to the duration of the lift. Equivalently, the lifting speed is $\dfrac{12}{20}\,\mathrm{m/s} = 0.60\,\mathrm{m/s}$ and $P = F\,v = 4905 \cdot 0.60\,\mathrm{W}$, with the same result.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A car of mass $m = 1000\,\mathrm{kg}$ climbs at constant speed $v = 72\,\mathrm{km/h}$ along a straight road inclined at $\alpha = 5.0^\circ$. The resistive forces (rolling friction and air resistance) have a total magnitude $F_r = 400\,\mathrm{N}$. Compute the power delivered by the engine.

$$
F_m - m g \sin\alpha - F_r = 0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
F_m = m g \sin\alpha + F_r
$$
$$
P = F_m\, v = (m g \sin\alpha + F_r)\, v
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
m g \sin\alpha &= 1000 \cdot 9.81 \cdot 0.0872\,\mathrm{N} \approx 855\,\mathrm{N} \\[6pt]
P &= (855 + 400) \cdot 20\,\mathrm{W} \approx 2.5 \cdot 10^4\,\mathrm{W} = 25\,\mathrm{kW}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The speed is constant, so the net force along the road is zero. The driving force, pointing forward, balances the component of the weight along the road, $mg\sin\alpha$, and the resistive forces. By the theorem on **power and velocity**, the power of the driving force is the product of the force and the speed, because the two are parallel and in the same direction. The speed must be expressed in meters per second, $\dfrac{72}{3.6}\,\mathrm{m/s} = 20\,\mathrm{m/s}$. About two thirds of the power is used to make the car climb, the rest to overcome the resistive forces; on level ground $400 \cdot 20\,\mathrm{W} = 8.0\,\mathrm{kW}$ would suffice.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A pump with efficiency $\eta = 0.75$ lifts $300\,\mathrm{L}$ of water per minute from a well to a tank located $h = 15\,\mathrm{m}$ higher. Compute the useful power and the power taken in by the pump, given that $1\,\mathrm{L}$ of water has mass $1.0\,\mathrm{kg}$ and neglecting the kinetic energy of the outflowing water.

$$
P_{\text{u}} = \frac{m g h}{\Delta t} ,
\qquad
P_{\text{in}} = \frac{P_{\text{u}}}{\eta}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
P_{\text{u}} &= \frac{300 \cdot 9.81 \cdot 15}{60}\,\mathrm{W} \approx 736\,\mathrm{W} \\[6pt]
P_{\text{in}} &= \frac{735.75}{0.75}\,\mathrm{W} \approx 981\,\mathrm{W}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

In one minute the pump lifts $300\,\mathrm{kg}$ of water by $15\,\mathrm{m}$. Neglecting the kinetic energy of the outflowing water, the useful work is the increase in potential energy $mgh$, and the useful power is this work divided by $\Delta t = 60\,\mathrm{s}$. By the definition of **efficiency**, the power taken in is the useful power divided by $\eta$; the difference, about $245\,\mathrm{W}$, is lost to friction and heat. The power taken in is therefore about $0.98\,\mathrm{kW}$.

</div>

## Exercise 4

A car of mass $m = 1200\,\mathrm{kg}$ accelerates from rest to $100\,\mathrm{km/h}$ in $\Delta t = 8.0\,\mathrm{s}$ on a horizontal road. Neglecting the resistive forces, compute the average power delivered to the wheels and, assuming constant acceleration, the instantaneous power at the end of the acceleration.

$$
L = K_f - 0 = \frac{1}{2} m v_f^2 , \qquad P_m = \frac{L}{\Delta t} = \frac{m v_f^2}{2\,\Delta t}
$$
$$
a = \frac{v_f}{\Delta t} , \qquad F = m a , \qquad P(t) = F\, v(t) = m a^2 t , \qquad P(\Delta t) = \frac{m v_f^2}{\Delta t} = 2\, P_m
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
v_f &= \frac{100}{3.6}\,\mathrm{m/s} \approx 27.8\,\mathrm{m/s} \\[6pt]
L &= \frac{1}{2} \cdot 1200 \cdot 27.78^2\,\mathrm{J} \approx 4.63 \cdot 10^5\,\mathrm{J} \\[6pt]
P_m &= \frac{4.63 \cdot 10^5}{8.0}\,\mathrm{W} \approx 58\,\mathrm{kW} ,
\qquad
P(\Delta t) \approx 116\,\mathrm{kW}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The resistive forces are neglected and the weight and the normal force do no work, so the total work is that of the driving force. By the **work-energy theorem** this work equals the final kinetic energy, and the average power is the work divided by the duration.

With constant acceleration the driving force is constant, $F = ma$, while the speed increases linearly, $v(t) = at$. By the theorem on power and velocity the instantaneous power is $F\,v = ma^2 t$, which grows in proportion to time; its average over the interval is half the final value. Therefore at the end of the acceleration the power is **twice** the average power, about $116\,\mathrm{kW}$.

</div>
