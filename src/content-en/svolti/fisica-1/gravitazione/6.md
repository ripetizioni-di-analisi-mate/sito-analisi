## Exercise 1

Compute the escape velocity from the surface of the Moon, which has mass $M_L = 7.35 \cdot 10^{22}\,\mathrm{kg}$ and radius $R_L = 1.74 \cdot 10^6\,\mathrm{m}$, and compare it with that of the Earth, $11.2\,\mathrm{km/s}$. Use $G = 6.67 \cdot 10^{-11}\,\mathrm{N\cdot m^2/kg^2}$.

$$
\frac{1}{2} m v_f^2 - \frac{G M_L m}{R_L} = 0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
v_f = \sqrt{\frac{2 G M_L}{R_L}}
$$
$$
v_f = \sqrt{\frac{2 \cdot 6.67 \cdot 10^{-11} \cdot 7.35 \cdot 10^{22}}{1.74 \cdot 10^6}}\,\mathrm{m/s} = \sqrt{5.64 \cdot 10^6}\,\mathrm{m/s} = 2.37 \cdot 10^3\,\mathrm{m/s}
$$
$$
\frac{v_{f,T}}{v_{f,L}} = \frac{11.2}{2.37} = 4.7
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The Moon has no atmosphere, so the only force on the launched body is lunar gravity, which is conservative. The **escape velocity** is the minimum initial speed for which the mechanical energy is zero, because with zero or positive energy the body moves away indefinitely. Setting $E = 0$, the mass of the body cancels.

The escape velocity from the Moon is $2.37\,\mathrm{km/s}$, about one fifth of that of the Earth. The small value explains why the modules of the lunar missions could take off again from the Moon with rockets much smaller than those used to leave the Earth.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A projectile is launched vertically from the Earth's surface with speed $v_0 = 5.0\,\mathrm{km/s}$. Neglecting the atmosphere and the Earth's rotation, compute the maximum altitude reached and compare it with the one predicted by uniformly accelerated motion. Data: $G M_T = 3.98 \cdot 10^{14}\,\mathrm{m^3/s^2}$, $R_T = 6.37 \cdot 10^6\,\mathrm{m}$, $g_0 = 9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$.

$$
\frac{1}{2} m v_0^2 - \frac{G M_T m}{R_T} = -\,\frac{G M_T m}{r_{\max}}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
r_{\max} = \frac{R_T}{1 - \dfrac{v_0^2 R_T}{2 G M_T}}
$$
$$
\frac{v_0^2 R_T}{2 G M_T} = \frac{(5.0 \cdot 10^3)^2 \cdot 6.37 \cdot 10^6}{2 \cdot 3.98 \cdot 10^{14}} = 0.200
$$
$$
r_{\max} = \frac{R_T}{0.800} = 1.25\,R_T = 7.96 \cdot 10^6\,\mathrm{m},
\qquad
h_{\max} = r_{\max} - R_T = 0.25\,R_T = 1.59 \cdot 10^6\,\mathrm{m}
$$
$$
g \ \text{constant} : \qquad h' = \frac{v_0^2}{2 g_0} = \frac{2.5 \cdot 10^7}{19.62}\,\mathrm{m} = 1.27 \cdot 10^6\,\mathrm{m}
$$

::grafico{f="x>=1 ? -1/x : NaN" segmenti="1,-0.8,1.25,-0.8;1.25,-0.8,1.25,0" punti="1,-0.8,pieno;1.25,-0.8,pieno,r max" etichette="1.6,-0.95,E = −0.8;2.3,-0.3,U(r)" xmin=0 xmax=3 ymin=-1.2 ymax=0.3 nomi="r,U" titolo="Energies in units of $\dfrac{G M_T m}{R_T}$ and distances in units of $R_T$: with $E = -0.8$ the projectile rises up to $r_{\max} = 1.25\,R_T$"}

<div class="spiegazione">

The initial speed is less than the escape velocity, so the mechanical energy is negative and the motion is bound: the projectile rises up to the distance $r_{\max}$ at which the kinetic energy vanishes and then falls back. At the highest point the velocity is zero, and conservation of mechanical energy between the launch and that point gives an equation in $r_{\max}$. Dividing by $\dfrac{G M_T m}{R_T}$ we obtain the form that contains the pure number $\dfrac{v_0^2 R_T}{2 G M_T}$.

The projectile reaches the altitude of $1.59 \cdot 10^6\,\mathrm{m}$, one quarter of the Earth's radius. Uniformly accelerated motion predicts $1.27 \cdot 10^6\,\mathrm{m}$, that is, it **underestimates** the altitude by about $20\%$: as the projectile rises the gravitational acceleration decreases, so the projectile slows down less than the constant-$g$ model assumes.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A probe is launched from the Earth's surface with speed $v_0 = 15\,\mathrm{km/s}$. Neglecting the atmosphere, the Earth's rotation and the attraction of other bodies, compute the speed of the probe when it is very far from the Earth. Data: $G M_T = 3.98 \cdot 10^{14}\,\mathrm{m^3/s^2}$, $R_T = 6.37 \cdot 10^6\,\mathrm{m}$.

$$
\frac{1}{2} m v_0^2 - \frac{G M_T m}{R_T} = \frac{1}{2} m v_{\infty}^2 + 0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
v_{\infty} = \sqrt{v_0^2 - \frac{2 G M_T}{R_T}} = \sqrt{v_0^2 - v_f^2}
$$
$$
v_f^2 = \frac{2 \cdot 3.98 \cdot 10^{14}}{6.37 \cdot 10^6}\,\mathrm{m^2/s^2} = 1.25 \cdot 10^8\,\mathrm{m^2/s^2}
$$
$$
v_{\infty} = \sqrt{2.25 \cdot 10^8 - 1.25 \cdot 10^8}\,\mathrm{m/s} = \sqrt{1.00 \cdot 10^8}\,\mathrm{m/s} = 1.00 \cdot 10^4\,\mathrm{m/s}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The initial speed exceeds the escape velocity, $v_f = 11.2\,\mathrm{km/s}$, so the mechanical energy is positive and the motion is **unbound**. At large distance the potential energy tends to zero, therefore all the mechanical energy is kinetic and conservation of energy gives $v_{\infty}$.

The probe moves away with a residual speed of $10.0\,\mathrm{km/s}$. The loss of speed, from $15$ to $10\,\mathrm{km/s}$, is less than the escape velocity, because conservation of energy involves the squares of the speeds, $v_{\infty}^2 = v_0^2 - v_f^2$, that is, the kinetic energies per unit mass.

</div>

## Exercise 4

A satellite travels on a circular orbit at an altitude $h = 600\,\mathrm{km}$. Compute the minimum change in velocity that an engine must give the satellite, with a short thrust, for it to escape the Earth's attraction. Data: $G M_T = 3.98 \cdot 10^{14}\,\mathrm{m^3/s^2}$, $R_T = 6.37 \cdot 10^6\,\mathrm{m}$.

$$
r = R_T + h = 6.97 \cdot 10^6\,\mathrm{m},
\qquad
v_{\text{orb}} = \sqrt{\frac{G M_T}{r}},
\qquad
v_{\text{esc}}(r) = \sqrt{\frac{2 G M_T}{r}} = \sqrt{2}\,v_{\text{orb}}
$$
$$
\Delta v = v_{\text{esc}}(r) - v_{\text{orb}} = \left( \sqrt{2} - 1 \right) v_{\text{orb}}
$$
$$
v_{\text{orb}} = \sqrt{\frac{3.98 \cdot 10^{14}}{6.97 \cdot 10^6}}\,\mathrm{m/s} = 7.56 \cdot 10^3\,\mathrm{m/s},
\qquad
\Delta v = 0.414 \cdot 7.56 \cdot 10^3\,\mathrm{m/s} = 3.13 \cdot 10^3\,\mathrm{m/s}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The satellite escapes if, immediately after the thrust, its mechanical energy is zero or positive; since the thrust is short, it takes place at distance $r$ from the center, where the potential energy is $-\dfrac{G M_T m}{r}$. The condition is therefore that the magnitude of the velocity reaches the **escape velocity** at distance $r$, which is $\sqrt{2}$ times the speed of the circular orbit.

The energy depends only on the magnitude of the velocity, so the smallest change in velocity is obtained by thrusting in the direction of motion, because in that case the magnitude increases by exactly $\Delta v$. The satellite must go from $7.56$ to $10.7\,\mathrm{km/s}$, an increase of $3.13\,\mathrm{km/s}$, much less than the $11.2\,\mathrm{km/s}$ needed starting from rest at the surface.

</div>
