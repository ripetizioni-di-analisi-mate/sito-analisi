## Exercise 1

Determine the point of the segment joining the Sun and the Earth at which the total gravitational field of the two bodies is zero, and the magnitude of the field of each body at that point. Data: $M_S = 1.99 \cdot 10^{30}\,\mathrm{kg}$, $M_T = 5.97 \cdot 10^{24}\,\mathrm{kg}$, distance between the centers $d = 1.50 \cdot 10^{11}\,\mathrm{m}$; the Moon and the other planets are neglected.

$$
\vec{g} = \vec{g}_S + \vec{g}_T = \vec{0}
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\frac{G M_T}{x^2} = \frac{G M_S}{(d - x)^2}
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\frac{d - x}{x} = \sqrt{\frac{M_S}{M_T}}
$$
$$
x = \frac{d}{1 + \sqrt{\dfrac{M_S}{M_T}}}
$$
$$
\sqrt{\frac{M_S}{M_T}} = \sqrt{\frac{1.99 \cdot 10^{30}}{5.97 \cdot 10^{24}}} = \sqrt{3.33 \cdot 10^5} = 577
$$
$$
x = \frac{1.50 \cdot 10^{11}}{578}\,\mathrm{m} = 2.59 \cdot 10^8\,\mathrm{m}
$$
$$
g_T = \frac{G M_T}{x^2} = \frac{6.67 \cdot 10^{-11} \cdot 5.97 \cdot 10^{24}}{(2.59 \cdot 10^8)^2}\,\mathrm{m/s^2} = 5.92 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m/s^2} = g_S
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Let $x$ be the distance of the point from the center of the Earth. On the segment between the two bodies the field of the Earth points toward the Earth and that of the Sun points toward the Sun, so the two fields have opposite directions and the total field is zero when their magnitudes are equal. Each magnitude is given by the **field of a point mass**; equating them and taking the square root of two positive quantities we obtain a first-degree equation in $x$.

The point lies $2.59 \cdot 10^8\,\mathrm{m}$ from the Earth, that is, at less than two thousandths of the Earth-Sun distance, because the mass of the Sun is about $3.3 \cdot 10^5$ times that of the Earth. At that point each field equals $5.92 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$. The Moon is $3.84 \cdot 10^8\,\mathrm{m}$ from the Earth, a distance of the same order as that of the point found, so its field in that region is not small and the result, obtained by neglecting it, is an approximation.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Two stars of equal mass $M = 2.0 \cdot 10^{30}\,\mathrm{kg}$ are at the points $(-a, 0)$ and $(a, 0)$, with $a = 1.0 \cdot 10^{11}\,\mathrm{m}$. Compute the gravitational field at the point $(0, y)$ of the perpendicular bisector of the segment, its value for $y = a$ and the point of the bisector with $y > 0$ at which the magnitude of the field is maximum.

$$
g_1 = g_2 = \frac{GM}{a^2 + y^2},
\qquad
\sin\alpha = \frac{y}{\sqrt{a^2 + y^2}}
$$
$$
g_x = 0,
\qquad
g_y = -\,2\,\frac{GM}{a^2 + y^2} \cdot \frac{y}{\sqrt{a^2 + y^2}} = -\,\frac{2GM\,y}{(a^2 + y^2)\sqrt{a^2 + y^2}}
$$
$$
y = a : \qquad |g_y| = \frac{2GM\,a}{2a^2\sqrt{2a^2}} = \frac{GM}{\sqrt{2}\,a^2} = \frac{6.67 \cdot 10^{-11} \cdot 2.0 \cdot 10^{30}}{1.414 \cdot 10^{22}}\,\mathrm{m/s^2} = 9.4 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m/s^2}
$$
$$
\frac{d}{dy}\,\frac{y}{(a^2 + y^2)\sqrt{a^2 + y^2}} = \frac{(a^2 + y^2) - 3y^2}{(a^2 + y^2)^2\sqrt{a^2 + y^2}} = \frac{a^2 - 2y^2}{(a^2 + y^2)^2\sqrt{a^2 + y^2}}
$$
$$
a^2 - 2y^2 = 0
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
y = \frac{a}{\sqrt{2}} = 7.1 \cdot 10^{10}\,\mathrm{m},
\qquad
g_{\max} = \frac{4\,GM}{3\sqrt{3}\,a^2} = 1.0 \cdot 10^{-2}\,\mathrm{m/s^2}
$$

::grafico{f="2*x/(1+x^2)^1.5" punti="0.707,0.770,pieno,maximum;1,0.707,pieno" segmenti="0.707,0,0.707,0.770" xmin=0 xmax=4 ymin=0 ymax=1 nomi="y,g" titolo="Magnitude of the field on the bisector in units of $\dfrac{GM}{a^2}$, with $y$ in units of $a$: it is zero at the midpoint, maximum at $y = \dfrac{a}{\sqrt{2}}$ and tends to zero far from the stars"}

<div class="spiegazione">

The point $(0, y)$ is equidistant from the two stars, at distance $\sqrt{a^2 + y^2}$, so the two fields have the same magnitude and are each directed toward their own star. Let $\alpha$ be the angle that each field forms with the $x$ axis; the $x$ components are opposite and cancel, while the $y$ components are equal, equal to $g_1 \sin\alpha$, and point toward the segment. By the **superposition principle** the total field is directed along the $y$ axis, toward the midpoint of the stars.

To find the maximum we study the sign of the derivative of the function $\dfrac{y}{(a^2 + y^2)\sqrt{a^2 + y^2}}$: the numerator $a^2 - 2y^2$ is positive for $y < \dfrac{a}{\sqrt{2}}$ and negative afterwards, so the magnitude of the field increases up to $y = \dfrac{a}{\sqrt{2}}$ and then decreases. The field is zero at the midpoint by symmetry, and for large $y$ it behaves like that of a mass $2M$, that is, $\dfrac{2GM}{y^2}$. The **maximum** is about $1.0 \cdot 10^{-2}\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Three spheres of mass $m = 50\,\mathrm{kg}$ have their centers at three vertices $A$, $B$, $C$ of a square of side $\ell = 2.0\,\mathrm{m}$; the fourth vertex $D$, opposite to $B$, is empty. Compute the magnitude, direction and sense of the gravitational field at the center of the square.

::grafico{vettori="1,1,0.4,0.4,g(A);1,1,1.6,1.6,g(C);1,1,1.6,0.4,g(B)" segmenti="0,0,2,0;2,0,2,2;2,2,0,2;0,2,0,0" punti="0,0,pieno,A;2,0,pieno,B;2,2,pieno,C;0,2,vuoto,D" xmin=-0.6 xmax=2.8 ymin=-0.6 ymax=2.8 uguale=1 titolo="At the center the fields of $A$ and $C$ cancel; what remains is the field of $B$, directed toward $B$"}

$$
\text{distance from the center} = \frac{\ell}{\sqrt{2}},
\qquad
g_A = g_B = g_C = \frac{Gm}{\left( \dfrac{\ell}{\sqrt{2}} \right)^2} = \frac{2Gm}{\ell^2}
$$
$$
\vec{g}_A + \vec{g}_C = \vec{0}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\vec{g} = \vec{g}_B,
\qquad
g = \frac{2Gm}{\ell^2}
$$
$$
g = \frac{2 \cdot 6.67 \cdot 10^{-11} \cdot 50}{2.0^2}\,\mathrm{N/kg} = 1.7 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\mathrm{N/kg}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The center of the square is equidistant from the four vertices, at a distance equal to half the diagonal, $\dfrac{\ell}{\sqrt{2}}$; therefore the three fields have the same magnitude $\dfrac{2Gm}{\ell^2}$. The vertices $A$ and $C$ are opposite, so their fields at the center have the same direction and opposite senses and their sum is zero. By the **superposition principle** the total field coincides with the field of $B$.

The field at the center has magnitude $1.7 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\mathrm{N/kg}$ and is directed along the diagonal $DB$, **toward** $B$. The same result is obtained by thinking of the system as four equal masses, which give zero field at the center, plus a negative mass $-m$ at $D$: the field of this fictitious mass points away from $D$, that is, toward $B$.

</div>
