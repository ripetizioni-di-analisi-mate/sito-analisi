## Exercise 1

Compute the minimum work needed to bring a satellite of mass $m = 1000\,\mathrm{kg}$, initially at rest on the Earth's surface, into a circular orbit at an altitude $h = 600\,\mathrm{km}$, and state which part of the work becomes kinetic energy and which part potential energy. The Earth's rotation and the atmosphere are neglected; data: $G M_T = 3.98 \cdot 10^{14}\,\mathrm{m^3/s^2}$, $R_T = 6.37 \cdot 10^6\,\mathrm{m}$.

$$
E_{\text{init}} = -\,\frac{G M_T m}{R_T},
\qquad
E_{\text{fin}} = -\,\frac{G M_T m}{2r},
\qquad
r = R_T + h = 6.97 \cdot 10^6\,\mathrm{m}
$$
$$
L = E_{\text{fin}} - E_{\text{init}} = G M_T m \left( \frac{1}{R_T} - \frac{1}{2r} \right)
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
L &= 3.98 \cdot 10^{17} \cdot \left( \frac{1}{6.37 \cdot 10^6} - \frac{1}{1.394 \cdot 10^7} \right)\mathrm{J} \\[6pt]
&= 3.98 \cdot 10^{17} \cdot \left( 1.570 - 0.717 \right) \cdot 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{J} \\[6pt]
&= 3.39 \cdot 10^{10}\,\mathrm{J}
\end{aligned}
$$
$$
K_{\text{fin}} = \frac{G M_T m}{2r} = 2.86 \cdot 10^{10}\,\mathrm{J},
\qquad
\Delta U = G M_T m \left( \frac{1}{R_T} - \frac{1}{r} \right) = 5.38 \cdot 10^9\,\mathrm{J}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The satellite starts from rest, so its initial mechanical energy is only the potential energy at the surface. In the final orbit the **energy of a satellite in a circular orbit** is $-\dfrac{G M_T m}{2r}$. Without dissipation, the work of the external forces, that is, of the engines, is the difference between the two mechanical energies, and it is the minimum work needed.

The work is $3.39 \cdot 10^{10}\,\mathrm{J}$, and its larger part, $2.86 \cdot 10^{10}\,\mathrm{J}$, becomes kinetic energy, while only $5.38 \cdot 10^9\,\mathrm{J}$ is used to lift the satellite. As a check, the sum $2.86 \cdot 10^{10} + 0.54 \cdot 10^{10} = 3.40 \cdot 10^{10}$ coincides with $L$ within rounding. For a low orbit, therefore, most of the energy goes into reaching the orbital speed.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A satellite of mass $m = 500\,\mathrm{kg}$ moves from a circular orbit of radius $r_1 = 7.00 \cdot 10^6\,\mathrm{m}$ to a circular orbit of radius $r_2 = 1.40 \cdot 10^7\,\mathrm{m}$. Compute the work done by the engines and the changes in kinetic energy and potential energy, with $G M_T = 3.98 \cdot 10^{14}\,\mathrm{m^3/s^2}$.

$$
E = -\,\frac{G M_T m}{2r},
\qquad
K = \frac{G M_T m}{2r},
\qquad
U = -\,\frac{G M_T m}{r}
$$
$$
L = E_2 - E_1 = \frac{G M_T m}{2} \left( \frac{1}{r_1} - \frac{1}{r_2} \right) = \frac{3.98 \cdot 10^{14} \cdot 500}{2} \cdot \frac{1}{1.40 \cdot 10^7}\,\mathrm{J} = 7.11 \cdot 10^9\,\mathrm{J}
$$
$$
K_1 = \frac{3.98 \cdot 10^{14} \cdot 500}{2 \cdot 7.00 \cdot 10^6}\,\mathrm{J} = 1.42 \cdot 10^{10}\,\mathrm{J},
\qquad
K_2 = \frac{K_1}{2} = 7.11 \cdot 10^9\,\mathrm{J}
$$
$$
\Delta K = K_2 - K_1 = -7.11 \cdot 10^9\,\mathrm{J},
\qquad
\Delta U = 2\,\Delta E = 1.42 \cdot 10^{10}\,\mathrm{J}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The work of the engines equals the change in mechanical energy, because gravity is conservative and its work is already included in the change in potential energy. Since $r_2 = 2r_1$, the difference $\dfrac{1}{r_1} - \dfrac{1}{r_2}$ equals $\dfrac{1}{2r_1} = \dfrac{1}{r_2}$, which simplifies the calculation. The work is positive: to move to a higher orbit, energy must be supplied.

In the new orbit the satellite is slower, so its kinetic energy **decreases** by $7.11 \cdot 10^9\,\mathrm{J}$. The relations $K = -E$ and $U = 2E$, valid for circular orbits, explain the result: the potential energy increases by twice the work supplied, and half of that increase comes from the work of the engines, the other half from the kinetic energy.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A satellite travels on an elliptical orbit around the Earth with minimum distance from the center (perigee) $r_p = 7.00 \cdot 10^6\,\mathrm{m}$ and maximum distance (apogee) $r_a = 1.40 \cdot 10^7\,\mathrm{m}$. Compute the speeds at perigee and at apogee and the period of the orbit, with $G M_T = 3.98 \cdot 10^{14}\,\mathrm{m^3/s^2}$.

::grafico{curve="10.5*cos(t)-3.5,9.9*sin(t),0,6.29;6.37*cos(t),6.37*sin(t),0,6.29" vettori="7,0,7,3.48,vₚ;-14,0,-14,-1.74,vₐ" punti="7,0,pieno,P;-14,0,pieno,A" etichette="-2.5,-2,Earth" xmin=-15 xmax=9 ymin=-12 ymax=12 uguale=1 titolo="Orbit of the satellite with the Earth at a focus, distances in units of $10^6\,\mathrm{m}$; at perigee $P$ the speed is twice that at apogee $A$, because $r_a = 2r_p$"}

$$
\begin{cases}
r_p v_p = r_a v_a \\[6pt]
\dfrac{1}{2} v_p^2 - \dfrac{G M_T}{r_p} = \dfrac{1}{2} v_a^2 - \dfrac{G M_T}{r_a}
\end{cases}
$$
$$
v_p = \sqrt{\frac{2 G M_T\,r_a}{r_p\,(r_p + r_a)}},
\qquad
v_a = \frac{r_p}{r_a}\,v_p
$$
$$
v_p = \sqrt{\frac{2 \cdot 3.98 \cdot 10^{14} \cdot 1.40 \cdot 10^7}{7.00 \cdot 10^6 \cdot 2.10 \cdot 10^7}}\,\mathrm{m/s} = \sqrt{7.58 \cdot 10^7}\,\mathrm{m/s} = 8.71 \cdot 10^3\,\mathrm{m/s},
\qquad
v_a = \frac{v_p}{2} = 4.35 \cdot 10^3\,\mathrm{m/s}
$$
$$
a = \frac{r_p + r_a}{2} = 1.05 \cdot 10^7\,\mathrm{m},
\qquad
T = 2\pi \sqrt{\frac{a^3}{G M_T}} = 2\pi \sqrt{\frac{1.158 \cdot 10^{21}}{3.98 \cdot 10^{14}}}\,\mathrm{s} = 1.07 \cdot 10^4\,\mathrm{s}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

At perigee and at apogee the velocity is perpendicular to the position vector, so the magnitude of the angular momentum is $m r v$. The gravitational force is central, therefore by **Kepler's second law** the angular momentum is conserved, and this gives the first equation. Gravity is conservative, so the mechanical energy is also conserved, which gives the second; the mass of the satellite cancels in both. The two equations must be satisfied together: substituting $v_a = \dfrac{r_p}{r_a} v_p$ into the second we obtain $v_p$.

The satellite travels at $8.71\,\mathrm{km/s}$ at perigee and at $4.35\,\mathrm{km/s}$ at apogee. As a check, at perigee the speed exceeds that of the circular orbit of radius $r_p$, $7.54\,\mathrm{km/s}$, and at apogee it is less than that of the circular orbit of radius $r_a$, $5.33\,\mathrm{km/s}$, as it must be for an orbit that rises from perigee and descends from apogee. For the period we apply **Kepler's third law** with the semi-major axis $a$ in place of the radius: $T = 1.07 \cdot 10^4\,\mathrm{s}$, about three hours.

</div>

## Exercise 4

The semi-major axis of the orbit of Mars is $a_M = 1.524$ astronomical units, where the astronomical unit (AU) is the semi-major axis of the Earth's orbit. Compute the period of revolution of Mars in years. Halley's comet has period $T_H = 76$ years: compute the semi-major axis of its orbit.

$$
\frac{T_M^2}{a_M^3} = \frac{T_T^2}{a_T^3}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
T_M = T_T \sqrt{\left( \frac{a_M}{a_T} \right)^3}
$$
$$
T_M = 1\,\text{year} \cdot \sqrt{1.524^3} = \sqrt{3.540}\,\text{years} = 1.88\,\text{years}
$$
$$
a_H = a_T \sqrt[3]{\left( \frac{T_H}{T_T} \right)^2} = \sqrt[3]{76^2}\,\mathrm{AU} = \sqrt[3]{5776}\,\mathrm{AU} = 17.9\,\mathrm{AU}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

By **Kepler's third law** the ratio $\dfrac{T^2}{a^3}$ is the same for all bodies orbiting the Sun. It is convenient to compare each body with the Earth, which has $a_T = 1\,\mathrm{AU}$ and $T_T = 1$ year: with these units the law becomes $T^2 = a^3$ and neither $G$ nor the mass of the Sun is needed.

Mars takes $1.88$ years to complete one revolution, since its orbit is larger than the Earth's. The orbit of Halley's comet has a semi-major axis of $17.9\,\mathrm{AU}$; the orbit is a very elongated ellipse, and the law applies with the semi-major axis, so the result does not tell how close the comet comes to the Sun. The mass of the comet, negligible compared with that of the Sun, does not appear in the calculation.

</div>
