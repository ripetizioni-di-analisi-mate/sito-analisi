## Exercise 1

Two homogeneous lead spheres, of masses $m_1 = 5.0\,\mathrm{kg}$ and $m_2 = 8.0\,\mathrm{kg}$, have their centers at a distance $r = 0.20\,\mathrm{m}$. Compute the magnitude of the gravitational force between the spheres, compare it with the weight of the first sphere and compute the acceleration that the force would give the first sphere if it were free to move. Use $G = 6.67 \cdot 10^{-11}\,\mathrm{N\cdot m^2/kg^2}$.

$$
F = G\,\frac{m_1 m_2}{r^2},
\qquad
P_1 = m_1 g,
\qquad
a_1 = \frac{F}{m_1} = G\,\frac{m_2}{r^2}
$$
$$
F = 6.67 \cdot 10^{-11} \cdot \frac{5.0 \cdot 8.0}{0.20^2}\,\mathrm{N} = 6.67 \cdot 10^{-11} \cdot 1000\,\mathrm{N} = 6.7 \cdot 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{N}
$$
$$
P_1 = 5.0 \cdot 9.81\,\mathrm{N} = 49\,\mathrm{N},
\qquad
\frac{F}{P_1} = \frac{6.67 \cdot 10^{-8}}{49.05} = 1.4 \cdot 10^{-9}
$$
$$
a_1 = \frac{6.67 \cdot 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{N}}{5.0\,\mathrm{kg}} = 1.3 \cdot 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{m/s^2}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The spheres are homogeneous, so by the **field of a homogeneous sphere** each one attracts external points as if its mass were concentrated at the center; for this reason the **law of universal gravitation** uses the distance between the centers, even though the spheres are not small compared with $0.20\,\mathrm{m}$. The force is about $6.7 \cdot 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{N}$, a little more than a billionth of the weight of the sphere. The acceleration is obtained from Newton's second law by dividing the force by $m_1$, and the mass $m_1$ cancels: the acceleration of a body depends only on the mass that attracts it.

As a dimensional check, $\mathrm{N\cdot m^2/kg^2} \cdot \mathrm{kg^2/m^2} = \mathrm{N}$. The result explains why the attraction between everyday objects is not observed: a torsion balance, like Cavendish's, is needed to measure it.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Three point masses lie on an $x$ axis: $m_1 = 4.0\,\mathrm{kg}$ at $x_1 = 0$, $m_2 = 1.0\,\mathrm{kg}$ at $x_2 = 0.50\,\mathrm{m}$ and $m_3 = 9.0\,\mathrm{kg}$ at $x_3 = 1.50\,\mathrm{m}$. Compute the total gravitational force on $m_2$.

$$
F_{12} = G\,\frac{m_1 m_2}{(x_2 - x_1)^2} \ \ \text{toward } -x,
\qquad
F_{32} = G\,\frac{m_3 m_2}{(x_3 - x_2)^2} \ \ \text{toward } +x
$$
$$
F_x = -\,F_{12} + F_{32} = G\,m_2 \left( \frac{m_3}{(x_3 - x_2)^2} - \frac{m_1}{(x_2 - x_1)^2} \right)
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
F_x &= 6.67 \cdot 10^{-11} \cdot 1.0 \cdot \left( \frac{9.0}{1.0^2} - \frac{4.0}{0.50^2} \right)\mathrm{N} \\[6pt]
&= 6.67 \cdot 10^{-11} \cdot (9.0 - 16)\,\mathrm{N} \\[6pt]
&= -4.7 \cdot 10^{-10}\,\mathrm{N}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

By the **superposition principle** the total force on $m_2$ is the sum of the forces due to $m_1$ and to $m_3$, each computed as if the other mass were not there. Each force is attractive, so it is directed toward the mass that exerts it: the force of $m_1$, which lies to the left of $m_2$, points toward $-x$, while that of $m_3$ points toward $+x$. The two forces have the same line of action, therefore the sum reduces to the sum of the $x$ components with their signs.

The mass $m_3$ is larger, but it is at twice the distance and the force decreases with the square of the distance. The contribution of $m_1$ prevails, so the total force has magnitude $4.7 \cdot 10^{-10}\,\mathrm{N}$ and is **directed toward** $m_1$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A point mass $m = 0.50\,\mathrm{kg}$ is at the origin of a frame $Oxy$. A mass $m_A = 6.0\,\mathrm{kg}$ is at the point $A = (0.30\,\mathrm{m},\ 0)$ and a mass $m_B = 8.0\,\mathrm{kg}$ at the point $B = (0,\ 0.40\,\mathrm{m})$. Compute the magnitude and direction of the total gravitational force on $m$.

::grafico{vettori="0,0,2,0,force of A;0,0,0,1.5,force of B;0,0,2,1.5,F" segmenti="2,0,2,1.5;0,1.5,2,1.5" punti="0,0,pieno,m;3,0,pieno,A;0,4,pieno,B" etichette="0.5,0.15,φ" xmin=-0.6 xmax=3.8 ymin=-0.6 ymax=4.6 uguale=1 titolo="Positions in decimeters; the arrows have length proportional to the magnitude of the forces, with $F_A : F_B = 4 : 3$"}

$$
\vec{F} = \vec{F}_A + \vec{F}_B,
\qquad
F_x = G\,\frac{m\,m_A}{x_A^2},
\qquad
F_y = G\,\frac{m\,m_B}{y_B^2}
$$
$$
F = \sqrt{F_x^2 + F_y^2},
\qquad
\tan\varphi = \frac{F_y}{F_x}
$$
$$
F_x = 6.67 \cdot 10^{-11} \cdot \frac{0.50 \cdot 6.0}{0.30^2}\,\mathrm{N} = 2.22 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\mathrm{N}
$$
$$
F_y = 6.67 \cdot 10^{-11} \cdot \frac{0.50 \cdot 8.0}{0.40^2}\,\mathrm{N} = 1.67 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\mathrm{N}
$$
$$
F = \sqrt{2.22^2 + 1.67^2} \cdot 10^{-9}\,\mathrm{N} = 2.78 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\mathrm{N},
\qquad
\tan\varphi = \frac{1.67}{2.22} = 0.75,
\qquad
\varphi = 36.9^\circ
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The two masses lie on the axes, so the force of $m_A$ is directed along $+x$ and that of $m_B$ along $+y$, toward the masses that exert them. By the **superposition principle** the total force is their vector sum, and since the two forces are perpendicular their magnitudes are directly the components $F_x$ and $F_y$ of the resultant.

The magnitude is obtained with the Pythagorean theorem and the angle $\varphi$ with the $x$ axis from the tangent. The numbers are in the ratio $F_x : F_y = \dfrac{100}{3} : 25 = 4 : 3$, so the triangle of forces has sides proportional to $3$, $4$, $5$ and $\tan\varphi = \dfrac{3}{4}$ exactly. The total force has magnitude $2.78 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\mathrm{N}$ and forms an angle of $36.9^\circ$ with the $x$ axis, toward the first quadrant.

</div>
