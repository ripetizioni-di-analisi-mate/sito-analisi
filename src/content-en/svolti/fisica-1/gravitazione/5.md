## Exercise 1

Compute the change in gravitational potential energy of a satellite of mass $m = 1000\,\mathrm{kg}$ brought from the Earth's surface to the altitude $h = 600\,\mathrm{km}$, and compare it with the value $m g_0 h$. Data: $G M_T = 3.98 \cdot 10^{14}\,\mathrm{m^3/s^2}$, $R_T = 6.37 \cdot 10^6\,\mathrm{m}$, $g_0 = 9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$.

$$
\Delta U = U(R_T + h) - U(R_T) = G M_T m \left( \frac{1}{R_T} - \frac{1}{R_T + h} \right) = \frac{G M_T m\,h}{R_T (R_T + h)}
$$
$$
\Delta U = \frac{3.98 \cdot 10^{14} \cdot 1000 \cdot 6.0 \cdot 10^5}{6.37 \cdot 10^6 \cdot 6.97 \cdot 10^6}\,\mathrm{J} = \frac{2.39 \cdot 10^{23}}{4.44 \cdot 10^{13}}\,\mathrm{J} = 5.38 \cdot 10^9\,\mathrm{J}
$$
$$
m g_0 h = 1000 \cdot 9.81 \cdot 6.0 \cdot 10^5\,\mathrm{J} = 5.89 \cdot 10^9\,\mathrm{J},
\qquad
\frac{m g_0 h}{\Delta U} = 1 + \frac{h}{R_T} = 1.094
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The **gravitational potential energy** of a mass at distance $r$ from the center of the Earth is $-\dfrac{G M_T m}{r}$, and the Earth is treated as a sphere that attracts as if its mass were at the center. The change between the surface, $r = R_T$, and the altitude $h$, $r = R_T + h$, is positive: moving away from the Earth, the potential energy increases. Bringing the two fractions to a common denominator gives the form with $h$ in the numerator, convenient for the calculation.

The value $m g_0 h$ overestimates the change by $9.4\%$, because assuming $g$ constant and equal to $g_0$ ignores the decrease of $g$ with altitude. The ratio between the two expressions is exactly $1 + \dfrac{h}{R_T}$, therefore the **approximation** $m g_0 h$ is used only for altitudes small compared with the Earth's radius.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A body is dropped from rest from an altitude $h = R_T$ above the Earth's surface. Neglecting the atmosphere and the rotation of the Earth, compute the speed with which it reaches the surface, with $g_0 = 9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$ and $R_T = 6.37 \cdot 10^6\,\mathrm{m}$. Compare the result with the one that would be obtained assuming $g$ constant.

$$
\frac{1}{2} m v^2 - \frac{G M_T m}{R_T} = 0 - \frac{G M_T m}{2R_T}
$$
$$
v^2 = 2 G M_T \left( \frac{1}{R_T} - \frac{1}{2R_T} \right) = \frac{G M_T}{R_T} = g_0 R_T
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
v = \sqrt{g_0 R_T}
$$
$$
v = \sqrt{9.81 \cdot 6.37 \cdot 10^6}\,\mathrm{m/s} = \sqrt{6.25 \cdot 10^7}\,\mathrm{m/s} = 7.91 \cdot 10^3\,\mathrm{m/s}
$$
$$
g \ \text{constant} : \qquad v' = \sqrt{2 g_0 h} = \sqrt{2 g_0 R_T} = \sqrt{2}\,v = 1.12 \cdot 10^4\,\mathrm{m/s}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The only force on the body is gravity, which is conservative, so the mechanical energy is conserved. At the initial instant the body is at rest at distance $2R_T$ from the center, on arrival it is on the surface at distance $R_T$; equating the mechanical energies, the mass $m$ cancels. The relation $G M_T = g_0 R_T^2$ makes it possible to express the result in terms of $g_0$ and $R_T$.

The body reaches the ground at $7.91\,\mathrm{km/s}$. With constant $g$ one would obtain a value larger by a factor $\sqrt{2}$, because the constant-$g$ model assigns to the body, along the whole fall, the acceleration $g_0$ that it actually has only near the ground; at altitude $R_T$ the acceleration is one quarter of $g_0$. For a fall from an altitude comparable with the Earth's radius, the **potential energy** $-\dfrac{G M_T m}{r}$ is therefore used.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Three homogeneous spheres of mass $m = 100\,\mathrm{kg}$ have their centers at the vertices of an equilateral triangle of side $\ell = 0.50\,\mathrm{m}$. Compute the gravitational potential energy of the system and the minimum work that an external force must do to move the spheres to infinite distance from one another. Also compute the work done by the gravitational force when the third sphere is brought from infinite distance to its position, with the other two already at rest at their vertices.

$$
U = -\left( \frac{G m_1 m_2}{r_{12}} + \frac{G m_1 m_3}{r_{13}} + \frac{G m_2 m_3}{r_{23}} \right) = -\,\frac{3 G m^2}{\ell}
$$
$$
U = -\,\frac{3 \cdot 6.67 \cdot 10^{-11} \cdot 100^2}{0.50}\,\mathrm{J} = -4.0 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\mathrm{J},
\qquad
L_{\text{ext}} = 0 - U = 4.0 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\mathrm{J}
$$
$$
L_{\text{grav}} = U_{\text{initial}} - U_{\text{final}} = 0 - \left( -\,\frac{2 G m^2}{\ell} \right) = \frac{2 G m^2}{\ell} = 2.7 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\mathrm{J}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The spheres are homogeneous, so they interact like point masses placed at their centers. The **potential energy of a system of masses** is the sum of the contributions of the pairs, each counted once; with three masses there are three pairs, all at distance $\ell$, so $U = -\dfrac{3Gm^2}{\ell}$. In the final configuration, with the spheres at infinite distance, the potential energy is zero. If the external force takes the spheres from rest to rest, the kinetic energy does not change and the external work equals the change in potential energy, $0 - U$; this is the minimum work, since any residual kinetic energy would require additional work.

When the third sphere arrives from infinite distance, only the energies of the two pairs that contain it change, each from $0$ to $-\dfrac{Gm^2}{\ell}$. The work of the gravitational force is the opposite of the change in potential energy, so it is **positive** and equals $2.7 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\mathrm{J}$: gravity attracts the third sphere toward the other two. The value is tiny, as expected for laboratory masses.

</div>
