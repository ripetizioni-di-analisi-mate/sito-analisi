## Exercise 1

A company has this rule: “All and only those employees who work more than $6$ hours a day receive a meal voucher.” Anna works $8$ hours a day, Bruno works $5$, Carlo receives a meal voucher, and Dario does not. Which statement must be true?

- A) Carlo works more than $6$ hours a day.
- B) Bruno receives a meal voucher.
- C) Dario works more than $6$ hours a day.
- D) Whether Anna receives a meal voucher cannot be determined.
- E) Anna does not receive a meal voucher.

| employee | more than $6$ hours | meal voucher |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Anna | yes (given) | yes |
| Bruno | no (given) | no |
| Carlo | yes | yes (given) |
| Dario | no | no (given) |

<div class="spiegazione">

“All and only” expresses a **necessary and sufficient condition**. Thus, for each employee, the statements “works more than $6$ hours” and “receives a voucher” are either both true or both false. Carlo receives a voucher, so he works more than $6$ hours. Similarly, Anna receives a voucher, Bruno does not, and Dario works at most $6$ hours. Choices B, C, and E contradict these conclusions. Choice D is false because working more than $6$ hours is sufficient for receiving a voucher, so the conclusion about Anna is certain. The correct answer is **A**.

</div>

## Exercise 2

For an integer $n$, what kind of condition is “$n^2$ is even” for “$n$ is even”?

- A) necessary but not sufficient
- B) sufficient but not necessary
- C) necessary and sufficient only for positive numbers
- D) neither necessary nor sufficient
- E) necessary and sufficient

| $n$ | $n^2$ | $n$ even | $n^2$ even |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $4$ | $16$ | T | T |
| $-6$ | $36$ | T | T |
| $3$ | $9$ | F | F |
| $-7$ | $49$ | F | F |

<div class="spiegazione">

If $n$ is even, that is, $n = 2k$, then $n^2 = 4k^2 = 2 \cdot 2k^2$ is even. If instead $n$ is odd, that is, $n = 2k + 1$, then $n^2 = 4k^2 + 4k + 1$ is odd. Therefore, $n$ and $n^2$ are always both even or both odd, so each statement is a **necessary and sufficient condition** for the other. The argument applies to every integer, including negative integers, so choice C is false. Choices A and B result from checking only one implication. The correct answer is **E**.

</div>

## Exercise 3

For an integer $n$, in which pair is the first statement a necessary and sufficient condition for the second?

- A) “$n$ is a multiple of $2$ and $4$” and “$n$ is a multiple of $8$”
- B) “$n$ is a multiple of $3$” and “$n$ is a multiple of $9$”
- C) “$n$ is a multiple of $2$ and $6$” and “$n$ is a multiple of $12$”
- D) “$n$ is a multiple of $3$ and $5$” and “$n$ is a multiple of $15$”
- E) “$n$ is a multiple of $4$ and $6$” and “$n$ is a multiple of $24$”

| pair | counterexample to the implication from the first statement to the second |
| --- | --- |
| A | $n = 4$ is a multiple of $2$ and $4$, but not of $8$ |
| B | $n = 3$ is a multiple of $3$, but not of $9$ |
| C | $n = 6$ is a multiple of $2$ and $6$, but not of $12$ |
| D | none, because $3$ and $5$ have no common factor greater than one |
| E | $n = 12$ is a multiple of $4$ and $6$, but not of $24$ |

<div class="spiegazione">

In each pair the second statement implies the first, because a multiple of $8$ is a multiple of $2$ and $4$, and similarly for the other pairs. It remains to check the implication from the first statement to the second. It fails if there is a **counterexample**. A number that is a multiple of both $a$ and $b$ is a multiple of their least common multiple. For $3$ and $5$, the least common multiple is $15$, so both implications hold in pair D. For $2$ and $4$ the least common multiple is $4$, for $2$ and $6$ it is $6$, and for $4$ and $6$ it is $12$. Thus in pairs A, C, and E the first statement is only necessary. A common mistake is to multiply the two numbers instead of finding their least common multiple. The correct answer is **D**.

</div>
