## Exercise 1

Consider the following argument:

“The evening course should finish before 22:15. In fact, many students live on the outskirts of town, and the last bus to the outskirts leaves at 22:15. Classes currently finish at 22:30.”

What is the conclusion of the argument?

- A) The evening course should finish before 22:15.
- B) Many students live on the outskirts of town.
- C) The last bus to the outskirts leaves at 22:15.
- D) Classes currently finish at 22:30.
- E) The last bus to the outskirts should leave later.

| statement | role | clue |
| --- | --- | --- |
| the course should finish before 22:15 | conclusion | the other statements give the reasons for it |
| many students live on the outskirts | premise | introduced by *in fact* |
| the last bus leaves at 22:15 | premise | joined to the preceding statement |
| classes currently finish at 22:30 | premise | information about the current situation |

<div class="spiegazione">

Statements B, C, and D are **premises** because they explain why the present schedule creates a problem. When classes finish at 22:30, students who rely on that bus to get home miss the last service. Statement A is the proposal that these reasons support, so it is the **conclusion**, even though it comes first. The *therefore* test confirms this, since the following statement is coherent: “Many students live on the outskirts, the last bus leaves at 22:15, and classes finish at 22:30. Therefore, the course should finish before 22:15.” Option E is a proposal that does not appear in the text. The correct answer is **A**.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Consider the following argument:

“The new station parking lot has $200$ spaces, so commuters will no longer have trouble finding a place to park.”

Which of the following is an unstated assumption of the argument?

- A) The new parking lot is free.
- B) All commuters arrive at the station by car.
- C) The $200$ spaces are enough for the cars of commuters who currently cannot find a space.
- D) The new station parking lot has $200$ spaces.
- E) Before the parking lot opened, many commuters arrived late for work.

| option | negation | does the conclusion still follow? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A | the parking lot charges a fee | yes |
| B | some commuters do not arrive by car | yes |
| C | there are not enough spaces | no |
| D | repeats the premise | it is not unstated |
| E | before the lot opened, it was not the case that many commuters arrived late for work | yes |

<div class="spiegazione">

Apply the **negation test** to each option. If the $200$ spaces are not enough for the cars of commuters who currently cannot find a space, some of them will continue to have trouble parking. The conclusion no longer follows, so option C is an assumption. Negating option A means there is a charge to park, but commuters can still park. Option B is too strong: the argument still holds if some commuters arrive by train or on foot. Option D repeats the stated premise, and option E concerns arrival times at work, which the argument does not mention. The correct answer is **C**.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Consider the following argument:

“Dr. Neri, an official in the office, attended Monday’s meeting, so he signed the minutes of that meeting.”

Which unstated premise, if added, is sufficient to justify the conclusion?

- A) Only officials who attended Monday’s meeting may sign its minutes.
- B) Dr. Neri attended Monday’s meeting.
- C) All the officials in the office attended Monday’s meeting.
- D) Every official who attended Monday’s meeting signed the minutes.
- E) Dr. Neri is the most senior official in the office.

$$
\text{premise: } P = \text{Neri attended},
\qquad
\text{conclusion: } F = \text{Neri signed}
$$

| option | implication | does the conclusion follow from the premise? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A | $F \Rightarrow P$ | no (affirming the consequent) |
| D | $P \Rightarrow F$ | yes (modus ponens) |

<div class="spiegazione">

The argument moves from “Neri attended” to “Neri signed,” and option D supplies a rule that makes attendance a **sufficient condition** for signing: “If an official attended, then that official signed.” Applied to Neri, that rule yields the conclusion by **modus ponens**. A rule specific to Neri would also suffice. Option A appeals to anyone who overlooks the direction of the arrow: it says only that attendance is *necessary* for signing, so inferring a signature from attendance would be affirming the consequent. Option B repeats the premise, and options C and E say nothing about signing. The correct answer is **D**.

</div>
