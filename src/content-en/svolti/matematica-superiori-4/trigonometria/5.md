## Exercise 1

Solve triangle $ABC$ given $a = 6$, $b = 4$, and $\alpha = 45^\circ$. Round the angles to the nearest hundredth of a degree.

$$
\sin\beta = \frac{b\sin\alpha}{a} = \frac{4 \cdot \dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}}{6} = \frac{\sqrt{2}}{3} \approx 0.4714
$$
$$
\beta = \arcsin\frac{\sqrt{2}}{3} \approx 28.13^\circ ,
\qquad
\gamma = 180^\circ - 45^\circ - \beta \approx 106.87^\circ
$$
$$
36 = 16 + c^2 - 2 \cdot 4 \cdot c \cdot \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}
\qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad
c^2 - 4\sqrt{2}\,c - 20 = 0
$$
$$
c = 2\sqrt{2} \pm \sqrt{8 + 20} = 2\sqrt{2} \pm 2\sqrt{7}
\qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad
c = 2\sqrt{2} + 2\sqrt{7} \approx 8.12
$$

Exactly one triangle exists, with $\beta \approx 28.13^\circ$, $\gamma \approx 106.87^\circ$, and $c = 2\sqrt{2} + 2\sqrt{7}$.

<div class="spiegazione">

Two sides and the angle opposite one of them are known, so this is the **ambiguous case**. Angle $\alpha$ is acute and its opposite side $a = 6$ is longer than the other known side $b = 4$, so the theorem on the ambiguous case gives **exactly one triangle**.

The **law of sines** gives $\sin\beta = \dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{3}$. In $(0^\circ, 180^\circ)$, there are two angles with this sine, approximately $28.13^\circ$ and its supplement $151.87^\circ$. The second must be rejected since adding it to $\alpha = 45^\circ$ gives more than $180^\circ$. The ordering of the sides gives the same conclusion, since $b < a$ implies $\beta < \alpha$, so $\beta$ is acute.

Find the third angle by subtraction. To find side $c$, apply the **law of cosines** to side $a$, with $c$ as the unknown and the cosine of the known angle $\alpha$. One-quarter of the discriminant of the quadratic equation equals $8 + 20 = 28$, and the solutions are $2\sqrt{2} \pm 2\sqrt{7}$. Since $2\sqrt{7} > 2\sqrt{2}$, the solution with the minus sign is negative and must be discarded. The single positive solution confirms that the triangle is unique.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Solve triangle $ABC$ given $a = 2\sqrt{10}$, $b = 6\sqrt{2}$, and $\alpha = 45^\circ$.

$$
h = b\sin\alpha = 6\sqrt{2} \cdot \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} = 6
$$
$$
6 < 2\sqrt{10} < 6\sqrt{2}
\qquad \text{because} \qquad
36 < 40 < 72
$$
$$
\sin\beta = \frac{h}{a} = \frac{6}{2\sqrt{10}} = \frac{3}{\sqrt{10}}
\qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad
\beta_1 \approx 71.57^\circ ,
\quad
\beta_2 \approx 108.43^\circ
$$
$$
40 = 72 + c^2 - 2 \cdot 6\sqrt{2} \cdot c \cdot \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}
\qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad
c^2 - 12c + 32 = 0
\qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad
c = 4 \quad \text{or} \quad c = 8
$$
$$
\gamma_1 = 180^\circ - 45^\circ - \beta_1 \approx 63.43^\circ ,
\qquad
\gamma_2 = 180^\circ - 45^\circ - \beta_2 \approx 26.57^\circ
$$

There are two triangles. The first has $\beta \approx 71.57^\circ$, $\gamma \approx 63.43^\circ$, and $c = 8$, and the second has $\beta \approx 108.43^\circ$, $\gamma \approx 26.57^\circ$, and $c = 4$.

<div class="spiegazione">

This is the **ambiguous case** with an acute angle $\alpha$, so the number of triangles depends on how $a$ compares with $h = b\sin\alpha$ and $b$. The distance from $C$ to line $AB$ is $h = 6$. To compare the three numbers, compare their squares, which are $36$, $40$, and $72$. Hence $h < a < b$ and there are **two** triangles.

The law of sines gives $\sin\beta = \dfrac{3}{\sqrt{10}}$. Both values of $\beta$ in $(0^\circ, 180^\circ)$ are admissible because even the larger one added to $45^\circ$ is less than $180^\circ$.

Find side $c$ using the law of cosines for side $a$. The product $2 \cdot 6\sqrt{2} \cdot \dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$ equals $12$, and the equation $c^2 - 12c + 32 = 0$ has solutions $4$ and $8$, both positive.

It remains to match each value of $c$ to its angle $\beta$. Side $b = 6\sqrt{2} \approx 8.49$ is opposite $\beta$. In the triangle with $c = 8$, we have $b^2 = 72 < a^2 + c^2 = 104$, so $\beta$ is acute. In the triangle with $c = 4$, we have $72 > 40 + 16$, so $\beta$ is obtuse.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Given $b = 10$ and $\alpha = 30^\circ$, let the length $a > 0$ of the side opposite $\alpha$ vary. Determine how many triangles $ABC$ exist. Then solve the case $a = 5\sqrt{2}$.

$$
h = b\sin\alpha = 10 \cdot \frac{1}{2} = 5
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
0 < a < 5 &: \quad \text{no triangles} \\[6pt]
a = 5 &: \quad \text{one triangle, right-angled at } B \\[6pt]
5 < a < 10 &: \quad \text{two triangles} \\[6pt]
a \geq 10 &: \quad \text{one triangle}
\end{aligned}
$$
$$
a = 5\sqrt{2} : \qquad \sin\beta = \frac{10 \cdot \dfrac{1}{2}}{5\sqrt{2}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} = \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}
\qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad
\beta = 45^\circ \quad \text{or} \quad \beta = 135^\circ
$$
$$
50 = 100 + c^2 - 2 \cdot 10 \cdot c \cdot \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}
\qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad
c^2 - 10\sqrt{3}\,c + 50 = 0
\qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad
c = 5\sqrt{3} \pm 5
$$

For $a = 5\sqrt{2}$, there are two triangles. One has $\beta = 45^\circ$, $\gamma = 105^\circ$, and $c = 5\sqrt{3} + 5$, and the other has $\beta = 135^\circ$, $\gamma = 15^\circ$, and $c = 5\sqrt{3} - 5$.

<div class="spiegazione">

This case analysis follows the **theorem on the ambiguous case** with acute $\alpha$. The distance from vertex $C$ to the line containing side $c$ is $h = b\sin\alpha = 5$. Vertex $B$ lies where the circle with center $C$ and radius $a$ intersects the ray from $A$. For $a < 5$, the circle does not reach the line. For $a = 5$, it touches the line at the foot of the perpendicular, and the triangle has a right angle at $B$. For $5 < a < 10$, it meets the line at two points on the ray. For $a \geq 10$, one of the two points lies on the extension of the ray beyond $A$ or coincides with $A$, leaving only one triangle.

The value $a = 5\sqrt{2} \approx 7.07$ lies between $5$ and $10$, so there are **two** triangles. The law of sines gives $\sin\beta = \dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$, yielding the two supplementary angles $45^\circ$ and $135^\circ$. The third angle is $180^\circ - 30^\circ - \beta$, that is, $105^\circ$ or $15^\circ$.

The law of cosines for side $a$ gives a quadratic equation in $c$. One-quarter of its discriminant is $75 - 50 = 25$, so $c = 5\sqrt{3} \pm 5$, with both values positive. The longer side, $c = 5\sqrt{3} + 5 \approx 13.66$, belongs to the triangle with the larger angle at $C$, namely $\gamma = 105^\circ$. The shorter side, $c \approx 3.66$, corresponds to $\gamma = 15^\circ$.

</div>
