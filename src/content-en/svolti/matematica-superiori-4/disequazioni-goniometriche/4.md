## Exercise 1

Solve the inequality $\sin x - \cos x < 1$ using the auxiliary-angle method and give the solutions in $[0, 2\pi)$.

$$
r = \sqrt{1^2 + (-1)^2} = \sqrt{2} ,
\qquad
\cos\varphi = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} , \quad \sin\varphi = -\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}
\qquad
\Longrightarrow
\qquad
\varphi = -\frac{\pi}{4}
$$
$$
\sqrt{2}\sin\left( x - \frac{\pi}{4} \right) < 1
\iff
\sin\left( x - \frac{\pi}{4} \right) < \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}
$$
$$
\frac{3\pi}{4} + 2k\pi < x - \frac{\pi}{4} < \frac{9\pi}{4} + 2k\pi
\iff
\pi + 2k\pi < x < \frac{5\pi}{2} + 2k\pi
$$
$$
\text{in } [0, 2\pi) : \qquad 0 \leq x < \frac{\pi}{2} \quad \text{or} \quad \pi < x < 2\pi
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The inequality is **linear** in sine and cosine, with $a = 1$ and $b = -1$. The auxiliary-angle identity expresses the left-hand side as $r\sin(x + \varphi)$, with $r = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2} = \sqrt{2}$ and with $\varphi$ chosen so that $\cos\varphi = \dfrac{a}{r}$ and $\sin\varphi = \dfrac{b}{r}$. The cosine of $\varphi$ is positive and its sine is negative, so $\varphi$ lies in the fourth quadrant, and since $\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}} = \dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$, we have $\varphi = -\dfrac{\pi}{4}$.

Divide by $\sqrt{2} > 0$, which preserves the direction, and rationalize to obtain $\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}} = \dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$. The sine equals $\dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$ at $\dfrac{\pi}{4}$ and $\dfrac{3\pi}{4}$, and it is less than $\dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$ along the arc from $\dfrac{3\pi}{4}$ to $\dfrac{\pi}{4} + 2\pi = \dfrac{9\pi}{4}$ through $\dfrac{3\pi}{2}$. Add one full turn to the right endpoint so that the left endpoint is less than the right endpoint.

Add $\dfrac{\pi}{4}$ to all three parts, giving $\dfrac{3\pi}{4} + \dfrac{\pi}{4} = \pi$ and $\dfrac{9\pi}{4} + \dfrac{\pi}{4} = \dfrac{5\pi}{2}$. In $[0, 2\pi)$, the interval for $k = 0$ gives $\pi < x < 2\pi$, and the interval for $k = -1$, that is, $-\pi < x < \dfrac{\pi}{2}$, gives the part $0 \leq x < \dfrac{\pi}{2}$. Checking $x = 0$ gives $0 - 1 = -1 < 1$, whereas $x = \dfrac{\pi}{2}$ gives $1 - 0 = 1$, which is not less than $1$, so the endpoint $\dfrac{\pi}{2}$ is **excluded**.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Solve the inequality $\sqrt{3}\sin x - \cos x \geq 1$ graphically.

$$
X = \cos x , \quad Y = \sin x
\qquad
\Longrightarrow
\qquad
\begin{cases}
\sqrt{3}\,Y - X \geq 1 \\[4pt]
X^2 + Y^2 = 1
\end{cases}
$$
$$
Y = \frac{1 + X}{\sqrt{3}}
\qquad
\Longrightarrow
\qquad
X^2 + \frac{(1 + X)^2}{3} = 1
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
3X^2 + 1 + 2X + X^2 &= 3 \\[6pt]
4X^2 + 2X - 2 &= 0 \\[6pt]
2X^2 + X - 1 &= 0
\end{aligned}
$$
$$
X_1 = \frac{1}{2} ,\ Y_1 = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}
\qquad
X_2 = -1 ,\ Y_2 = 0
$$
$$
\frac{\pi}{3} + 2k\pi \leq x \leq \pi + 2k\pi , \qquad k \in \mathbb{Z}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

In the **graphical method**, interpret $\cos x$ and $\sin x$ as the coordinates $X$ and $Y$ of the point on the unit circle corresponding to $x$. The inequality becomes $\sqrt{3}\,Y - X \geq 1$, which describes a half-plane, and the point must also lie on the circle $X^2 + Y^2 = 1$. Both conditions must hold simultaneously, so write them as a system.

To find where the line $\sqrt{3}\,Y - X = 1$ intersects the circle, solve for $Y = \dfrac{1 + X}{\sqrt{3}}$ and substitute. Multiplying by $3$ and expanding the square gives $2X^2 + X - 1 = 0$, with discriminant $1 + 8 = 9$ and roots $X = \dfrac{-1 \pm 3}{4}$, that is, $\dfrac{1}{2}$ and $-1$. The corresponding vertical coordinates are $\dfrac{1 + \frac{1}{2}}{\sqrt{3}} = \dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$ and $0$. The point $\left( \dfrac{1}{2}, \dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2} \right)$ corresponds to the angle $\dfrac{\pi}{3}$, and the point $(-1, 0)$ to the angle $\pi$.

To select the half-plane, test the origin: $\sqrt{3} \cdot 0 - 0 = 0$, which is not greater than or equal to $1$. The solution half-plane is therefore the one above the line, which does not contain the origin. The arc of the circle within it runs from $\dfrac{\pi}{3}$ to $\pi$ through $(0, 1)$, including both endpoints because equality holds on the line. The auxiliary-angle method gives the same result from $2\sin\left( x - \dfrac{\pi}{6} \right) \geq 1$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Solve the inequality $\cos x - \sqrt{3}\sin x < \sqrt{2}$.

$$
\cos x - \sqrt{3}\sin x
= 2\left( \frac{1}{2}\cos x - \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\sin x \right)
= 2\cos\left( x + \frac{\pi}{3} \right)
$$
$$
2\cos\left( x + \frac{\pi}{3} \right) < \sqrt{2}
\iff
\cos\left( x + \frac{\pi}{3} \right) < \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}
$$
$$
\frac{\pi}{4} + 2k\pi < x + \frac{\pi}{3} < \frac{7\pi}{4} + 2k\pi
$$
$$
-\frac{\pi}{12} + 2k\pi < x < \frac{17\pi}{12} + 2k\pi , \qquad k \in \mathbb{Z}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The inequality is linear with coefficients $1$ and $-\sqrt{3}$, and $\sqrt{1 + 3} = 2$. Factor out $2$, and the coefficients of $\cos x$ and $\sin x$ become $\dfrac{1}{2}$ and $-\dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$, with $\dfrac{1}{2} = \cos\dfrac{\pi}{3}$ and $\dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2} = \sin\dfrac{\pi}{3}$. By the cosine **addition formula**, $\cos x\cos\dfrac{\pi}{3} - \sin x\sin\dfrac{\pi}{3} = \cos\left( x + \dfrac{\pi}{3} \right)$. This is the cosine form of the auxiliary-angle identity. Using sine gives the equivalent form $2\sin\left( x + \dfrac{5\pi}{6} \right)$.

Dividing by $2 > 0$ gives a basic cosine inequality with argument $x + \dfrac{\pi}{3}$. The cosine equals $\dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$ at $\pm\dfrac{\pi}{4}$, and the points with horizontal coordinate less than $\dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$ form the arc from $\dfrac{\pi}{4}$ to $2\pi - \dfrac{\pi}{4} = \dfrac{7\pi}{4}$ through $\pi$, excluding both endpoints.

Subtract $\dfrac{\pi}{3}$ from all three parts, using the common denominator $12$:
$$
\frac{\pi}{4} - \frac{\pi}{3} = \frac{3\pi - 4\pi}{12} = -\frac{\pi}{12} ,
\qquad
\frac{7\pi}{4} - \frac{\pi}{3} = \frac{21\pi - 4\pi}{12} = \frac{17\pi}{12} .
$$
The inequality is therefore **satisfied** for $-\dfrac{\pi}{12} + 2k\pi < x < \dfrac{17\pi}{12} + 2k\pi$. For $x = \dfrac{\pi}{2}$, the result is $0 - \sqrt{3} < \sqrt{2}$, and indeed $\dfrac{\pi}{2}$ lies in the interval for $k = 0$.

</div>
