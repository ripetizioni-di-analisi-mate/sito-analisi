## Exercise 1

Solve the inequality $2\sin x - \sqrt{3} > 0$.

$$
2\sin x - \sqrt{3} > 0
\iff
\sin x > \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}
$$
$$
\sin x = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}
\iff
x = \frac{\pi}{3} + 2k\pi
\quad \text{or} \quad
x = \frac{2\pi}{3} + 2k\pi
$$
$$
\frac{\pi}{3} + 2k\pi < x < \frac{2\pi}{3} + 2k\pi , \qquad k \in \mathbb{Z}
$$

::grafico{curve="cos(t),sin(t),1.0472,2.0944;cos(t),sin(t),2.0944,7.3304;t,0.866,-1.4,1.4" vettori="0,0,0.5,0.866;0,0,-0.5,0.866" unicolore=1 punti="0.5,0.866,vuoto,π/3;-0.5,0.866,vuoto,2π/3" xmin=-1.6 xmax=1.6 ymin=-1.4 ymax=1.4 uguale=1 legenda=0 titolo="The angles with sine greater than $\dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$ form the solid arc from $\dfrac{\pi}{3}$ to $\dfrac{2\pi}{3}$, above the line $y = \dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$"}

<div class="spiegazione">

Isolate $\sin x$ by adding $\sqrt{3}$ to both sides and then dividing by $2$, which is positive, so the direction of the inequality is unchanged. This gives the **basic inequality** $\sin x > \dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$, with $\dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$ between $-1$ and $1$.

The first step is to solve the corresponding equation. The sine equals $\dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$ at the angle $\dfrac{\pi}{3}$ in the first quadrant and at its supplement $\pi - \dfrac{\pi}{3} = \dfrac{2\pi}{3}$ in the second quadrant. On the unit circle, the sine is the vertical coordinate of the point corresponding to the angle, so the solutions are the angles whose points lie above the horizontal line $y = \dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$. These points form the arc from $\dfrac{\pi}{3}$ to $\dfrac{2\pi}{3}$ through $(0, 1)$, as shown in the figure.

The endpoints of the arc are excluded because the sine equals $\dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$ there and the inequality is strict. Sine has period $2\pi$, so add $2k\pi$ to both endpoints. The inequality is therefore **satisfied** for $\dfrac{\pi}{3} + 2k\pi < x < \dfrac{2\pi}{3} + 2k\pi$. For example, $x = \dfrac{\pi}{2}$ belongs to the interval for $k = 0$, and indeed $2\sin\dfrac{\pi}{2} - \sqrt{3} = 2 - \sqrt{3} > 0$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Solve the inequality $2\cos x + \sqrt{2} \leq 0$ and give the solutions in the interval $[0, 2\pi)$.

$$
2\cos x + \sqrt{2} \leq 0
\iff
\cos x \leq -\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}
$$
$$
\cos x = -\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}
\iff
x = \frac{3\pi}{4} + 2k\pi
\quad \text{or} \quad
x = \frac{5\pi}{4} + 2k\pi
$$
$$
\frac{3\pi}{4} + 2k\pi \leq x \leq \frac{5\pi}{4} + 2k\pi , \qquad k \in \mathbb{Z}
$$
$$
\text{in } [0, 2\pi) : \qquad \frac{3\pi}{4} \leq x \leq \frac{5\pi}{4}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Subtract $\sqrt{2}$ from both sides and divide by $2$, which is positive, so the direction is unchanged. This gives $\cos x \leq -\dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$.

Since $\arccos\left( -\dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2} \right) = \dfrac{3\pi}{4}$, the solutions of the equation $\cos x = -\dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$ in $[0, 2\pi)$ are $\dfrac{3\pi}{4}$ and $2\pi - \dfrac{3\pi}{4} = \dfrac{5\pi}{4}$, in the second and third quadrants. The cosine is the horizontal coordinate of the point corresponding to the angle, so the inequality asks for points on the circle that lie to the left of the vertical line $x = -\dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$ or on the line itself. These points form the arc from $\dfrac{3\pi}{4}$ to $\dfrac{5\pi}{4}$ through $(-1, 0)$, that is, through the angle $\pi$.

The endpoints are included because the inequality allows equality. Adding integer multiples of the period $2\pi$ gives the solutions on $\mathbb{R}$. In the interval $[0, 2\pi)$, only $k = 0$ gives an overlapping interval, since for $k = 1$ the left endpoint is already $\dfrac{11\pi}{4} > 2\pi$. The solutions in $[0, 2\pi)$ are $\dfrac{3\pi}{4} \leq x \leq \dfrac{5\pi}{4}$. Checking $x = \pi$ gives $2 \cdot (-1) + \sqrt{2} < 0$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Solve the inequality $\sqrt{3}\tan x + 1 \geq 0$ and give the solutions in the interval $[0, 2\pi)$.

$$
\sqrt{3}\tan x + 1 \geq 0
\iff
\tan x \geq -\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}} = -\frac{\sqrt{3}}{3}
$$
$$
\arctan\left( -\frac{\sqrt{3}}{3} \right) = -\frac{\pi}{6}
$$
$$
-\frac{\pi}{6} + k\pi \leq x < \frac{\pi}{2} + k\pi , \qquad k \in \mathbb{Z}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
k = 0 &: \quad -\frac{\pi}{6} \leq x < \frac{\pi}{2} \\[6pt]
k = 1 &: \quad \frac{5\pi}{6} \leq x < \frac{3\pi}{2} \\[6pt]
k = 2 &: \quad \frac{11\pi}{6} \leq x < \frac{5\pi}{2}
\end{aligned}
$$
$$
\text{in } [0, 2\pi) : \qquad 0 \leq x < \frac{\pi}{2} \quad \text{or} \quad \frac{5\pi}{6} \leq x < \frac{3\pi}{2} \quad \text{or} \quad \frac{11\pi}{6} \leq x < 2\pi
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The inequality is defined for $x \neq \dfrac{\pi}{2} + k\pi$, where tangent is defined. Subtract $1$ and divide by $\sqrt{3} > 0$, so the direction is unchanged, then rationalize to obtain $-\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{3}} = -\dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{3}$.

Tangent is **increasing** on each interval $\left( -\dfrac{\pi}{2} + k\pi, \dfrac{\pi}{2} + k\pi \right)$ and equals $-\dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{3}$ at $-\dfrac{\pi}{6}$. Therefore, on the branch for $k = 0$, the tangent is greater than or equal to $-\dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{3}$ from $-\dfrac{\pi}{6}$, included, to the asymptote $\dfrac{\pi}{2}$, excluded. Tangent has period $\pi$, so add $k\pi$ to the endpoints.

To write the solutions in $[0, 2\pi)$, give $k$ the values $0$, $1$, $2$. For $k = 0$, the interval starts at $-\dfrac{\pi}{6} < 0$, and its part in $[0, 2\pi)$ is $0 \leq x < \dfrac{\pi}{2}$. For $k = 1$, add $\pi$ to the endpoints, and the interval lies within $[0, 2\pi)$. For $k = 2$, the interval starts at $\dfrac{11\pi}{6}$ and extends beyond $2\pi$, so the part that remains is $\dfrac{11\pi}{6} \leq x < 2\pi$. The solutions in $[0, 2\pi)$ therefore form **three intervals**. For example, $x = \pi$ gives $\sqrt{3} \cdot 0 + 1 = 1 \geq 0$, and indeed $\pi$ lies in the second interval.

</div>
