## Exercise 1

The side lengths of a right triangle are in arithmetic progression, and its perimeter is $36\ \mathrm{cm}$. Find the side lengths and the area of the triangle.

$$
\text{sides:} \qquad a - d, \quad a, \quad a + d \qquad \text{with } 0 < d < a
$$
$$
(a - d) + a + (a + d) = 3a = 36
\qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad
a = 12
$$
$$
(12 - d)^2 + 12^2 = (12 + d)^2
$$
$$
144 - 24d + d^2 + 144 = 144 + 24d + d^2
\qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad
48d = 144
\qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad
d = 3
$$
$$
\text{sides: } 9, \ 12, \ 15,
\qquad
\mathcal{A} = \frac{9 \cdot 12}{2} = 54
$$

::grafico{assi=0 uguale=1 poligoni="0,0 12,0 0,9" linee="0,0 12,0 0,9 0,0;0,0.8 0.8,0.8 0.8,0" etichette="6,-0.9,12;-1,4.5,9;6.6,5.2,15" xmin=-2 xmax=14 ymin=-2 ymax=11 titolo="A right triangle with legs $9$ and $12$ and hypotenuse $15$. Successive side lengths differ by $3$"}

The side lengths are $9\ \mathrm{cm}$, $12\ \mathrm{cm}$, and $15\ \mathrm{cm}$, and the area is $54\ \mathrm{cm^2}$.

<div class="spiegazione">

Three numbers in **arithmetic progression** can be written using the middle term $a$ and the common difference $d$ as $a - d$, $a$, $a + d$. We can assume $d > 0$ by listing the sides from shortest to longest, and $d < a$ because the shortest side must have positive length. With this choice the common difference cancels from the sum of the three side lengths, and the perimeter condition immediately gives $3a = 36$, that is, $a = 12$.

The longest side $12 + d$ is the hypotenuse, so by the **Pythagorean theorem** the sum of the squares of the legs $12 - d$ and $12$ equals the square of the hypotenuse. Expand the squares of the binomials. The terms $d^2$ and one of the two $144$ terms cancel from both sides, leaving $144 - 24d = 24d$, that is, $48d = 144$. Therefore $d = 3$, which satisfies the condition $0 < d < 12$.

The side lengths are $9$, $12$, and $15$, and the check $81 + 144 = 225 = 15^2$ confirms that the triangle is a right triangle. The area of a right triangle is half the product of its legs, so it equals $\dfrac{9 \cdot 12}{2} = 54$. The side lengths are proportional to $3$, $4$, $5$, as in every right triangle whose side lengths form an arithmetic progression.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A ball falls from a height of $2\ \mathrm{m}$ and, after each bounce, rises to $\dfrac{3}{4}$ of the height from which it fell. Calculate the height reached after the third bounce and the total distance the ball travels, assuming that it continues bouncing indefinitely.

$$
h_n = 2 \cdot \left( \frac{3}{4} \right)^n
\qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad
h_3 = 2 \cdot \frac{27}{64} = \frac{27}{32}\ \mathrm{m}
$$
$$
\frac{3}{2} + \frac{9}{8} + \frac{27}{32} + \dots = \frac{\frac{3}{2}}{1 - \frac{3}{4}} = \frac{\frac{3}{2}}{\frac{1}{4}} = 6
$$
$$
D = 2 + 2 \cdot 6 = 14\ \mathrm{m}
$$

After the third bounce the ball rises to $\dfrac{27}{32}\ \mathrm{m} \approx 0.84\ \mathrm{m}$, and the total distance traveled is $14\ \mathrm{m}$.

<div class="spiegazione">

Each bounce multiplies the height by $\dfrac{3}{4}$, so the heights reached after successive bounces form a **geometric progression** with common ratio $\dfrac{3}{4}$. The height after the first bounce is $2 \cdot \dfrac{3}{4} = \dfrac{3}{2}$, and the height after bounce number $n$ is $h_n = 2 \cdot \left( \dfrac{3}{4} \right)^n$. Setting $n = 3$ gives $2 \cdot \dfrac{27}{64} = \dfrac{27}{32}$ meters.

To find the total distance, observe that the ball travels $2\ \mathrm{m}$ on its first fall and then, after each bounce, rises and falls through the same height $h_n$. The total distance is therefore $2$ plus twice the sum of the heights $h_1 + h_2 + h_3 + \dots$

The heights form a geometric progression with first term $\dfrac{3}{2}$ and common ratio $\dfrac{3}{4}$. The common ratio satisfies $\left| \dfrac{3}{4} \right| < 1$, so we can apply the formula for the **sum of a geometric progression** whose common ratio has absolute value less than $1$, namely $S = \dfrac{a_1}{1 - q}$. The denominator is $\dfrac{1}{4}$, and dividing by $\dfrac{1}{4}$ is equivalent to multiplying by $4$, so $S = 6$. The total distance is $2 + 12 = 14$ meters. The model with infinitely many bounces is an idealization. The result shows that the distance traveled stays below $14\ \mathrm{m}$ for any finite number of bounces.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A square $Q_1$ has side length $8\ \mathrm{cm}$. Construct square $Q_2$ by joining the midpoints of the sides of $Q_1$, then square $Q_3$ by joining the midpoints of the sides of $Q_2$, and so on.

1. Prove that the areas of the squares form a geometric progression and find its common ratio.
2. Calculate the side length of square $Q_5$.
3. Calculate the sum of the areas of the first five squares.
4. Calculate the sum of the areas of all the squares and the sum of their perimeters.

**Part 1**

$$
\ell_{n+1} = \sqrt{\left( \frac{\ell_n}{2} \right)^2 + \left( \frac{\ell_n}{2} \right)^2} = \frac{\ell_n}{\sqrt{2}},
\qquad
\mathcal{A}_{n+1} = \ell_{n+1}^2 = \frac{\ell_n^2}{2} = \frac{\mathcal{A}_n}{2}
$$

**Part 2**

$$
\ell_5 = 8 \cdot \left( \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \right)^4 = 8 \cdot \frac{1}{4} = 2
$$

**Part 3**

$$
S_5 = 64 \cdot \frac{1 - \left( \frac{1}{2} \right)^5}{1 - \frac{1}{2}} = 64 \cdot \frac{31}{32} \cdot 2 = 124
$$

**Part 4**

$$
\mathcal{A}_{\text{tot}} = \frac{64}{1 - \frac{1}{2}} = 128
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
p_{\text{tot}} &= \frac{32}{1 - \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}} = \frac{32\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{2} - 1} \\[6pt]
&= \frac{32\sqrt{2}\left( \sqrt{2} + 1 \right)}{2 - 1} = 64 + 32\sqrt{2}
\end{aligned}
$$

::grafico{assi=0 uguale=1 poligoni="0,0 8,0 8,8 0,8" linee="0,0 8,0 8,8 0,8 0,0;4,0 8,4 4,8 0,4 4,0;2,2 6,2 6,6 2,6 2,2;4,2 6,4 4,6 2,4 4,2;3,3 5,3 5,5 3,5 3,3" etichette="4,-0.8,8" xmin=-1 xmax=9 ymin=-1.5 ymax=9 titolo="The first five squares. The vertices of each square are the midpoints of the sides of the preceding square, and $Q_5$ has side length $2$"}

The areas form a geometric progression with common ratio $\dfrac{1}{2}$, the side length of $Q_5$ is $2\ \mathrm{cm}$, and the sum of the first five areas is $124\ \mathrm{cm^2}$. The sum of all the areas is $128\ \mathrm{cm^2}$ and the sum of all the perimeters is $\left( 64 + 32\sqrt{2} \right)\ \mathrm{cm}$.

<div class="spiegazione">

**Part 1.** A side of $Q_{n+1}$ joins the midpoints of two adjacent sides of $Q_n$. These sides meet at a right angle, so the side of $Q_{n+1}$ is the hypotenuse of an isosceles right triangle whose legs have length $\dfrac{\ell_n}{2}$. The **Pythagorean theorem** gives $\ell_{n+1} = \dfrac{\ell_n}{\sqrt{2}}$. The area of a square is the square of its side length, so $\mathcal{A}_{n+1} = \dfrac{\mathcal{A}_n}{2}$. The ratio of each area to the preceding one is always $\dfrac{1}{2}$, so the areas form a **geometric progression** with common ratio $\dfrac{1}{2}$ and first term $64$.

**Part 2.** The side lengths form a geometric progression with common ratio $\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}$, and the general term gives $\ell_5 = \ell_1 \cdot \left( \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \right)^4$. The fourth power of $\sqrt{2}$ is $4$, so $\ell_5 = 2$.

**Part 3.** Apply the formula for the sum of the first $5$ terms with $a_1 = 64$ and $q = \dfrac{1}{2}$. The numerator is $1 - \dfrac{1}{32} = \dfrac{31}{32}$ and dividing by $\dfrac{1}{2}$ is equivalent to multiplying by $2$, so $S_5 = 124$. Direct addition gives $64 + 32 + 16 + 8 + 4 = 124$ and confirms the result.

**Part 4.** Both common ratios are less than $1$, so the formula for the **sum**, $\dfrac{a_1}{1 - q}$, applies. For the areas this gives $128$, twice the area of the first square. For the perimeters, the first term is $4 \cdot 8 = 32$ and the common ratio is $\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}$, the same as for the side lengths. Multiply the numerator and denominator by $\sqrt{2}$ and then rationalize by multiplying by $\sqrt{2} + 1$. The denominator becomes $\left( \sqrt{2} \right)^2 - 1 = 1$, and the sum is $64 + 32\sqrt{2} \approx 109.3$ centimeters.

</div>
