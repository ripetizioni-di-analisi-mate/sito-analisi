## Exercise 1

Prove by induction that, for every $n \geq 1$,
$$
1 + 3 + 5 + \dots + (2n - 1) = n^2 .
$$

$$
n = 1: \qquad 1 = 1^2
$$
$$
\text{inductive hypothesis:} \qquad 1 + 3 + \dots + (2n - 1) = n^2
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
1 + 3 + \dots + (2n - 1) + (2n + 1) &= n^2 + (2n + 1) \\[6pt]
&= (n + 1)^2
\end{aligned}
$$

The formula holds for every $n \geq 1$.

<div class="spiegazione">

To apply the **principle of induction**, verify the base case and the inductive step.

For the base case, set $n = 1$. The left-hand side has a single term, the odd number $2 \cdot 1 - 1 = 1$, and the right-hand side is $1^2 = 1$, so the formula holds for $n = 1$.

For the inductive step, assume that the formula holds for some $n$ and show that it holds for $n + 1$, that is, that the sum of the first $n + 1$ odd numbers is $(n + 1)^2$. The odd number in position $n + 1$ is $2(n + 1) - 1 = 2n + 1$. The sum of the first $n + 1$ odd numbers is therefore obtained by adding $2n + 1$ to the sum of the first $n$ odd numbers, which by the inductive hypothesis is $n^2$. The expression $n^2 + 2n + 1$ is the expansion of the square of the binomial $n + 1$, so the sum is $(n + 1)^2$, as the formula states for $n + 1$.

The base case and the inductive step have been verified, so by the principle of induction the formula holds for **every** $n \geq 1$. For example, the odd numbers from $1$ to $39$, of which there are $20$, have sum $400$. The same formula also follows by summing the terms of an arithmetic progression with common difference $2$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Prove by induction that, for every $n \geq 1$, the number $7^n - 1$ is divisible by $6$.

$$
n = 1: \qquad 7^1 - 1 = 6 = 6 \cdot 1
$$
$$
\text{inductive hypothesis:} \qquad 7^n - 1 = 6m \quad \text{with } m \text{ an integer}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
7^{n+1} - 1 &= 7 \cdot 7^n - 1 \\[6pt]
&= 7\left( 7^n - 1 \right) + 7 - 1 \\[6pt]
&= 7 \cdot 6m + 6 \\[6pt]
&= 6\,(7m + 1)
\end{aligned}
$$

The number $7^n - 1$ is divisible by $6$ for every $n \geq 1$.

<div class="spiegazione">

An integer is divisible by $6$ if it can be written as $6$ times an integer. The statement to prove is therefore “$7^n - 1 = 6m$ for some integer $m$.” We use the **principle of induction**.

For the base case, setting $n = 1$ gives $7 - 1 = 6$, which is divisible by $6$.

For the inductive step, assume that $7^n - 1$ is divisible by $6$, that is, that $7^n - 1 = 6m$ with $m$ an integer, and consider $7^{n+1} - 1$. The idea is to write the expression in terms of $7^n - 1$, whose divisibility is known. By the rule for powers, $7^{n+1} = 7 \cdot 7^n$. Now add and subtract $7$, so that $7 \cdot 7^n - 7 = 7\left( 7^n - 1 \right)$, with $+7 - 1 = 6$ left over. By the inductive hypothesis, $7\left( 7^n - 1 \right) = 7 \cdot 6m$. Factoring out $6$ gives $6\,(7m + 1)$. The number $7m + 1$ is an integer because $m$ is an integer, so $7^{n+1} - 1$ is divisible by $6$.

Both steps have been verified, so the statement holds for every $n \geq 1$. For example, $7^3 - 1 = 342 = 6 \cdot 57$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Prove by induction that $2^n > n^2$ for every $n \geq 5$.

$$
n = 5: \qquad 2^5 = 32 > 25 = 5^2
$$
$$
\text{inductive hypothesis:} \qquad 2^n > n^2 \quad \text{with } n \geq 5
$$
$$
2^{n+1} = 2 \cdot 2^n > 2n^2
$$
$$
2n^2 \geq (n + 1)^2 \iff n^2 - 2n - 1 \geq 0 \iff n \geq 1 + \sqrt{2}
$$
$$
2^{n+1} > 2n^2 \geq (n + 1)^2
$$

The inequality $2^n > n^2$ holds for every $n \geq 5$.

<div class="spiegazione">

For the first few values of $n$, the inequality does not always hold. For example, $2^3 = 8$ is less than $3^2 = 9$, and $2^4 = 16$ equals $4^2$. For this reason, take $n_0 = 5$ as the **base case**, and the principle of induction gives the result for every $n \geq 5$. For $n = 5$, we have $32 > 25$, so the base case holds.

For the inductive step, assume $2^n > n^2$, with $n \geq 5$, and aim to show $2^{n+1} > (n + 1)^2$. Write $2^{n+1} = 2 \cdot 2^n$ and multiply both sides of the inductive hypothesis by $2$. The direction of the inequality is preserved because $2 > 0$. This gives $2^{n+1} > 2n^2$.

It remains to compare $2n^2$ with $(n + 1)^2 = n^2 + 2n + 1$. The inequality $2n^2 \geq n^2 + 2n + 1$ is equivalent to $n^2 - 2n - 1 \geq 0$. The quadratic has roots $1 - \sqrt{2}$ and $1 + \sqrt{2} \approx 2.41$, and its parabola opens upward, so the quadratic is greater than or equal to $0$ for $n \geq 1 + \sqrt{2}$. This condition holds because $n \geq 5$. Combining the two inequalities gives $2^{n+1} > (n + 1)^2$ and verifies the inductive step. By the principle of induction, the inequality holds for **every** $n \geq 5$.

</div>
