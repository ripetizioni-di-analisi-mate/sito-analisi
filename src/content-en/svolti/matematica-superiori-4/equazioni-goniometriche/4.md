## Exercise 1

Solve the equation $2\sin^2 x + \sin x - 1 = 0$.

$$
t = \sin x , \qquad -1 \leq t \leq 1
$$
$$
2t^2 + t - 1 = 0 ,
\qquad
\Delta = 1^2 - 4 \cdot 2 \cdot (-1) = 9
$$
$$
t = \frac{-1 \pm 3}{4}
\qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad
t = \frac{1}{2}
\quad \text{or} \quad
t = -1
$$
$$
\sin x = \frac{1}{2} \implies x = \frac{\pi}{6} + 2k\pi \ \text{ or } \ x = \frac{5\pi}{6} + 2k\pi
$$
$$
\sin x = -1 \implies x = \frac{3\pi}{2} + 2k\pi
$$

::grafico{curve="cos(t),sin(t),0,6.29" segmenti="-1.4,0.5,1.4,0.5;-1.4,-1,1.4,-1" punti="0.866,0.5,pieno,P₁;-0.866,0.5,pieno,P₂;0,-1,pieno,P₃" xmin=-1.5 xmax=1.5 ymin=-1.5 ymax=1.5 uguale=1 legenda=0 titolo="The lines $y = \dfrac{1}{2}$ and $y = -1$ intersect the unit circle at $P_1$, $P_2$, and $P_3$, corresponding to the angles $\dfrac{\pi}{6}$, $\dfrac{5\pi}{6}$, and $\dfrac{3\pi}{2}$"}

<div class="spiegazione">

The equation contains only one trigonometric function, the sine, to the first and second powers. With the **substitution** $t = \sin x$, it becomes the algebraic equation $2t^2 + t - 1 = 0$. Since $t$ is a sine, only values satisfying $-1 \leq t \leq 1$ are admissible.

The discriminant is $\Delta = 1 + 8 = 9$, so the quadratic formula gives $t = \dfrac{-1 \pm 3}{4}$. The plus sign gives $\dfrac{2}{4} = \dfrac{1}{2}$, and the minus sign gives $\dfrac{-4}{4} = -1$. Both values lie between $-1$ and $1$, so both are admissible.

Return to the unknown $x$ by solving two basic equations. For the equation $\sin x = \dfrac{1}{2}$, we have $\arcsin\dfrac{1}{2} = \dfrac{\pi}{6}$, and the second family is $\pi - \dfrac{\pi}{6} = \dfrac{5\pi}{6}$. The equation $\sin x = -1$ is a special case because the line $y = -1$ is tangent to the circle at $(0, -1)$. Its solutions are $\dfrac{3\pi}{2} + 2k\pi$, which can also be written as $-\dfrac{\pi}{2} + 2k\pi$.

The general solution is the union of the **three families**. Each full turn around the circle therefore gives three solutions, one for each of the points $P_1$, $P_2$, $P_3$ in the figure.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Solve the equation $2\sin^2 x - 5\cos x + 1 = 0$.

$$
\sin^2 x = 1 - \cos^2 x
$$
$$
2\left( 1 - \cos^2 x \right) - 5\cos x + 1 = 0
\iff
-2\cos^2 x - 5\cos x + 3 = 0
\iff
2\cos^2 x + 5\cos x - 3 = 0
$$
$$
t = \cos x : \qquad 2t^2 + 5t - 3 = 0 ,
\qquad
\Delta = 25 + 24 = 49
$$
$$
t = \frac{-5 \pm 7}{4}
\qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad
t = \frac{1}{2}
\quad \text{or} \quad
t = -3
$$
$$
\cos x = \frac{1}{2} \implies x = \pm\frac{\pi}{3} + 2k\pi
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The equation contains both sine and cosine. The sine appears only as a square, so the **Pythagorean identity** $\sin^2 x + \cos^2 x = 1$ allows us to replace $\sin^2 x$ with $1 - \cos^2 x$, leaving only the cosine in the equation.

Expand the product $2\left(1 - \cos^2 x\right) = 2 - 2\cos^2 x$ and combine the constant terms, $2 + 1 = 3$. This gives $-2\cos^2 x - 5\cos x + 3 = 0$, and reversing the signs of all the terms gives an equation with a positive leading coefficient.

With $t = \cos x$, the equation $2t^2 + 5t - 3 = 0$ has discriminant $\Delta = 5^2 - 4 \cdot 2 \cdot (-3) = 25 + 24 = 49$, which is the square of $7$. The solutions are $t = \dfrac{-5 + 7}{4} = \dfrac{1}{2}$ and $t = \dfrac{-5 - 7}{4} = -3$.

The value $t = -3$ **is inadmissible** because the cosine of an angle always lies between $-1$ and $1$, and the equation $\cos x = -3$ has no solutions. This leaves $\cos x = \dfrac{1}{2}$. Since $\arccos\dfrac{1}{2} = \dfrac{\pi}{3}$, the theorem on the basic cosine equation gives the solutions $x = \pm\dfrac{\pi}{3} + 2k\pi$.

For $x = \dfrac{\pi}{3}$, we have $\sin^2 x = \dfrac{3}{4}$ and $\cos x = \dfrac{1}{2}$, so the left-hand side equals $\dfrac{3}{2} - \dfrac{5}{2} + 1 = 0$, in agreement with the result.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Solve the equation $\tan x + \cot x = \dfrac{4\sqrt{3}}{3}$.

$$
\text{Domain:} \qquad
\begin{cases}
\cos x \neq 0 \\[4pt]
\sin x \neq 0
\end{cases}
\iff
x \neq \frac{k\pi}{2}
$$
$$
t = \tan x , \qquad \cot x = \frac{1}{t}
$$
$$
t + \frac{1}{t} = \frac{4\sqrt{3}}{3}
\iff
3t^2 + 3 = 4\sqrt{3}\,t
\iff
3t^2 - 4\sqrt{3}\,t + 3 = 0
$$
$$
\frac{\Delta}{4} = \left( 2\sqrt{3} \right)^2 - 3 \cdot 3 = 12 - 9 = 3
$$
$$
t = \frac{2\sqrt{3} \pm \sqrt{3}}{3}
\qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad
t = \sqrt{3}
\quad \text{or} \quad
t = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{3}
$$
$$
\tan x = \sqrt{3} \implies x = \frac{\pi}{3} + k\pi ,
\qquad
\tan x = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{3} \implies x = \frac{\pi}{6} + k\pi
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Tangent is defined for $\cos x \neq 0$ and cotangent for $\sin x \neq 0$. Together, these two conditions exclude all multiples of $\dfrac{\pi}{2}$, that is, $x \neq \dfrac{k\pi}{2}$.

At the admissible points, cotangent is the reciprocal of tangent, so setting $t = \tan x$ gives $\cot x = \dfrac{1}{t}$, with $t \neq 0$. Multiplying both sides by $3t$, which differs from $0$, gives the quadratic equation $3t^2 - 4\sqrt{3}\,t + 3 = 0$.

The coefficient of $t$ contains an even integer factor, so we use the quadratic formula in its reduced form with $\dfrac{\Delta}{4} = \left(\dfrac{b}{2}\right)^2 - ac = 12 - 9 = 3$. The solutions are $t = \dfrac{2\sqrt{3} \pm \sqrt{3}}{3}$, that is, $t = \dfrac{3\sqrt{3}}{3} = \sqrt{3}$ and $t = \dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{3}$. Both differ from $0$, and every real value is admissible for the tangent.

The special-angle values give $\arctan\sqrt{3} = \dfrac{\pi}{3}$ and $\arctan\dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{3} = \dfrac{\pi}{6}$. By the theorem on the basic tangent equation, the solutions are $x = \dfrac{\pi}{3} + k\pi$ or $x = \dfrac{\pi}{6} + k\pi$. None of these values is a multiple of $\dfrac{\pi}{2}$, so the **domain restrictions** are satisfied and all the solutions are admissible.

</div>
