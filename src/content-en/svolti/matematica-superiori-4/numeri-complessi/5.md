## Exercise 1

Given $z = 2\left( \cos\dfrac{\pi}{3} + i\sin\dfrac{\pi}{3} \right)$ and $w = 4\left( \cos\dfrac{\pi}{6} + i\sin\dfrac{\pi}{6} \right)$, calculate $z w$ and $\dfrac{z}{w}$ and write them in algebraic form.

$$
z w = 2 \cdot 4 \left( \cos\left( \frac{\pi}{3} + \frac{\pi}{6} \right) + i\sin\left( \frac{\pi}{3} + \frac{\pi}{6} \right) \right) = 8\left( \cos\frac{\pi}{2} + i\sin\frac{\pi}{2} \right)
$$
$$
z w = 8\,(0 + i) = 8i
$$
$$
\frac{z}{w} = \frac{2}{4}\left( \cos\left( \frac{\pi}{3} - \frac{\pi}{6} \right) + i\sin\left( \frac{\pi}{3} - \frac{\pi}{6} \right) \right) = \frac{1}{2}\left( \cos\frac{\pi}{6} + i\sin\frac{\pi}{6} \right)
$$
$$
\frac{z}{w} = \frac{1}{2}\left( \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} + \frac{1}{2}\,i \right) = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{4} + \frac{1}{4}\,i
$$

::grafico{vettori="0,0,1,1.732,z;0,0,3.464,2,w;0,0,0,8,zw" xmin=-2 xmax=5 ymin=-1 ymax=9 uguale=1 nomi="Re,Im" titolo="The product of $z$, with argument $\dfrac{\pi}{3}$, and $w$, with argument $\dfrac{\pi}{6}$, has argument $\dfrac{\pi}{2}$ and modulus $8$, so it lies on the positive imaginary axis"}

Thus $z w = 8i$ and $\dfrac{z}{w} = \dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{4} + \dfrac{1}{4}\,i$.

<div class="spiegazione">

Both numbers are already in trigonometric form because the factors $2$ and $4$ are positive. The number $z$ has modulus $2$ and argument $\dfrac{\pi}{3}$. The number $w$ has modulus $4$ and argument $\dfrac{\pi}{6}$.

By the **product rule in trigonometric form**, the modulus of the product is the product of the moduli, $8$, and an argument is the sum of the arguments. Calculate the sum $\dfrac{\pi}{3} + \dfrac{\pi}{6}$ using the common denominator $6$ to obtain $\dfrac{3\pi}{6} = \dfrac{\pi}{2}$. Since $\cos\dfrac{\pi}{2} = 0$ and $\sin\dfrac{\pi}{2} = 1$, the product is the purely imaginary number $8i$. In the figure, the vector $z$ is rotated through the angle $\dfrac{\pi}{6}$ and stretched by a factor of $4$.

By the **quotient rule**, the modulus is the quotient of the moduli, $\dfrac{2}{4} = \dfrac{1}{2}$, and an argument is the difference between the arguments, $\dfrac{\pi}{3} - \dfrac{\pi}{6} = \dfrac{\pi}{6}$. The divisor $w$ is different from $0$, so the rule applies. To return to algebraic form, substitute $\cos\dfrac{\pi}{6} = \dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$ and $\sin\dfrac{\pi}{6} = \dfrac{1}{2}$ and multiply each term by $\dfrac{1}{2}$.

In algebraic form the same calculations would require first converting the numbers, $z = 1 + \sqrt{3}\,i$ and $w = 2\sqrt{3} + 2i$, then finding a product and a quotient using the conjugate.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Calculate $(1 + i)^{10}$ using De Moivre's formula.

$$
|1 + i| = \sqrt{2} ,
\qquad
\begin{cases}
\cos\theta = \dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2} \\[10pt]
\sin\theta = \dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}
\end{cases}
\quad
\Longrightarrow
\quad
\theta = \frac{\pi}{4}
$$
$$
(1 + i)^{10} = \left( \sqrt{2} \right)^{10} \left( \cos\frac{10\pi}{4} + i\sin\frac{10\pi}{4} \right)
$$
$$
\left( \sqrt{2} \right)^{10} = 2^5 = 32 ,
\qquad
\frac{10\pi}{4} = \frac{5\pi}{2} = \frac{\pi}{2} + 2\pi
$$
$$
(1 + i)^{10} = 32\left( \cos\frac{\pi}{2} + i\sin\frac{\pi}{2} \right) = 32i
$$

Thus $(1 + i)^{10} = 32i$.

<div class="spiegazione">

To apply **De Moivre's formula**, we need the number in trigonometric form. The modulus of $1 + i$ is $\sqrt{1 + 1} = \sqrt{2}$. The cosine and sine of its argument both equal $\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}} = \dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$, a positive number, so the argument is the special first-quadrant angle $\dfrac{\pi}{4}$.

By De Moivre's formula, the modulus of the tenth power is the tenth power of the modulus, and an argument is ten times the original argument. The modulus is $\left( \sqrt{2} \right)^{10} = \left( 2^{\frac{1}{2}} \right)^{10} = 2^5 = 32$. The argument is $\dfrac{10\pi}{4}$, which simplifies to $\dfrac{5\pi}{2}$.

The angle $\dfrac{5\pi}{2}$ is greater than $2\pi$. The sine and cosine have period $2\pi$, so subtracting $2\pi$ gives the angle $\dfrac{\pi}{2}$, whose cosine is $0$ and whose sine is $1$. The power is therefore the **purely imaginary number $32i$**.

The same result follows by observing that $(1 + i)^2 = 1 + 2i + i^2 = 2i$. Then $(1 + i)^{10} = (2i)^5 = 32\,i^5$, and $i^5 = i^4 \cdot i = i$. Computing with De Moivre's formula does not require finding a simple power first, and it works for any exponent.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Calculate $\left( \dfrac{1 + \sqrt{3}\,i}{1 - i} \right)^{12}$.

$$
1 + \sqrt{3}\,i = 2\left( \cos\frac{\pi}{3} + i\sin\frac{\pi}{3} \right)
$$
$$
1 - i = \sqrt{2}\left( \cos\frac{7\pi}{4} + i\sin\frac{7\pi}{4} \right) = \sqrt{2}\left( \cos\left( -\frac{\pi}{4} \right) + i\sin\left( -\frac{\pi}{4} \right) \right)
$$
$$
\frac{1 + \sqrt{3}\,i}{1 - i} = \frac{2}{\sqrt{2}}\left( \cos\left( \frac{\pi}{3} + \frac{\pi}{4} \right) + i\sin\left( \frac{\pi}{3} + \frac{\pi}{4} \right) \right) = \sqrt{2}\left( \cos\frac{7\pi}{12} + i\sin\frac{7\pi}{12} \right)
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
\left( \frac{1 + \sqrt{3}\,i}{1 - i} \right)^{12} &= \left( \sqrt{2} \right)^{12} \left( \cos\frac{12 \cdot 7\pi}{12} + i\sin\frac{12 \cdot 7\pi}{12} \right) \\[6pt]
&= 64\,(\cos 7\pi + i\sin 7\pi) \\[6pt]
&= 64\,(\cos\pi + i\sin\pi) = -64
\end{aligned}
$$

The result is the real number $-64$.

<div class="spiegazione">

Raising the algebraic form of the quotient to the twelfth power would take many calculations because the argument of the quotient is not a special angle. Therefore, write the numerator and denominator in **trigonometric form**, find the quotient using the quotient rule, and apply De Moivre's formula.

The numerator $1 + \sqrt{3}\,i$ has modulus $\sqrt{1 + 3} = 2$, and the cosine and sine of its argument are $\dfrac{1}{2}$ and $\dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$, so its argument is $\dfrac{\pi}{3}$. The denominator $1 - i$ has modulus $\sqrt{2}$, and the cosine and sine of its argument are $\dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$ and $-\dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$. It lies in the fourth quadrant, so its argument is $\dfrac{7\pi}{4}$ or, equivalently, $-\dfrac{\pi}{4}$. Using the argument $-\dfrac{\pi}{4}$ shortens the calculations.

By the **quotient rule**, the modulus is $\dfrac{2}{\sqrt{2}} = \sqrt{2}$ and the argument is $\dfrac{\pi}{3} - \left( -\dfrac{\pi}{4} \right) = \dfrac{4\pi + 3\pi}{12} = \dfrac{7\pi}{12}$. The denominator is different from $0$, so the rule applies.

By **De Moivre's formula**, the twelfth power has modulus $\left( \sqrt{2} \right)^{12} = 2^6 = 64$ and argument $12 \cdot \dfrac{7\pi}{12} = 7\pi$. Since $7\pi = \pi + 6\pi$ and $6\pi$ is a multiple of $2\pi$, we have $\cos 7\pi = -1$ and $\sin 7\pi = 0$. Thus the result is the real number **$-64$**.

</div>
