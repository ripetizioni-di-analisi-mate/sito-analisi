## Exercise 1

Find the square roots of the number $3 + 4i$ in algebraic form.

$$
w = x + yi ,
\qquad
w^2 = x^2 - y^2 + 2xy\,i = 3 + 4i
$$
$$
\begin{cases}
x^2 - y^2 = 3 \\[4pt]
2xy = 4
\end{cases}
\iff
\begin{cases}
x^2 - y^2 = 3 \\[4pt]
y = \dfrac{2}{x}
\end{cases}
$$
$$
x^2 - \frac{4}{x^2} = 3
\iff
x^4 - 3x^2 - 4 = 0
$$
$$
x^2 = \frac{3 \pm \sqrt{9 + 16}}{2} = \frac{3 \pm 5}{2}
\qquad
\Longrightarrow
\qquad
x^2 = 4 \quad \text{or} \quad x^2 = -1
$$
$$
x = 2 ,\ y = 1
\qquad
\text{or}
\qquad
x = -2 ,\ y = -1
$$

The square roots of $3 + 4i$ are $2 + i$ and $-2 - i$.

<div class="spiegazione">

The argument of $3 + 4i$ has cosine $\dfrac{3}{5}$ and sine $\dfrac{4}{5}$, so it is not a special angle, and the trigonometric formula for the roots would not give simple exact values. For this reason, we seek the square roots in **algebraic form**, setting $w = x + yi$ with $x$ and $y$ real.

Expand the square of $w$ as the square of a binomial and use $(yi)^2 = -y^2$. Equating the real and imaginary parts of $w^2$ and $3 + 4i$ gives a system. The second equation gives $y = \dfrac{2}{x}$. The division is valid because $x = 0$ would make the product $2xy$ zero, whereas its value is $4$.

Substituting into the first equation and multiplying by $x^2 \neq 0$ gives a **biquadratic** equation. With the auxiliary variable $t = x^2$, it becomes $t^2 - 3t - 4 = 0$, which has discriminant $25$ and solutions $t = 4$ and $t = -1$. Discard $t = -1$ because $t = x^2$ is the square of a real number and cannot be negative.

From $x^2 = 4$ we get $x = 2$ or $x = -2$, and the corresponding values of $y = \dfrac{2}{x}$ are $1$ and $-1$. The two roots, **$2 + i$ and $-2 - i$**, are negatives of each other, as are the two square roots of every nonzero number. Indeed, $(2 + i)^2 = 4 + 4i + i^2 = 3 + 4i$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Find the cube roots of $-8i$, write them in algebraic form, and plot them in the complex plane.

$$
-8i = 8\left( \cos\frac{3\pi}{2} + i\sin\frac{3\pi}{2} \right)
$$
$$
w_k = \sqrt[3]{8}\left( \cos\frac{\frac{3\pi}{2} + 2k\pi}{3} + i\sin\frac{\frac{3\pi}{2} + 2k\pi}{3} \right) = 2\left( \cos\left( \frac{\pi}{2} + \frac{2k\pi}{3} \right) + i\sin\left( \frac{\pi}{2} + \frac{2k\pi}{3} \right) \right)
$$
$$
k = 0 :
\qquad
w_0 = 2\left( \cos\frac{\pi}{2} + i\sin\frac{\pi}{2} \right) = 2i
$$
$$
k = 1 :
\qquad
w_1 = 2\left( \cos\frac{7\pi}{6} + i\sin\frac{7\pi}{6} \right) = -\sqrt{3} - i
$$
$$
k = 2 :
\qquad
w_2 = 2\left( \cos\frac{11\pi}{6} + i\sin\frac{11\pi}{6} \right) = \sqrt{3} - i
$$

::grafico{curve="2*cos(t),2*sin(t),0,6.29" linee="0,2 -1.732,-1 1.732,-1 0,2" punti="0,2,pieno,w₀;-1.732,-1,pieno,w₁;1.732,-1,pieno,w₂" xmin=-3 xmax=3 ymin=-3 ymax=3 uguale=1 nomi="Re,Im" legenda="|w| = 2" titolo="The cube roots of $-8i$ have modulus $2$ and arguments $\dfrac{\pi}{2}$, $\dfrac{7\pi}{6}$, and $\dfrac{11\pi}{6}$, and are the vertices of an equilateral triangle"}

The cube roots of $-8i$ are $2i$, $-\sqrt{3} - i$, and $\sqrt{3} - i$.

<div class="spiegazione">

To apply the **theorem on $n$-th roots**, we need the number in trigonometric form. The number $-8i$ is purely imaginary with negative imaginary part, so it lies on the negative imaginary axis and therefore has modulus $8$ and argument $\dfrac{3\pi}{2}$.

The cube roots all have modulus $\sqrt[3]{8} = 2$, the principal real cube root of the given modulus. Their arguments are obtained by dividing by $3$ the angle $\dfrac{3\pi}{2} + 2k\pi$, giving $\dfrac{\pi}{2} + \dfrac{2k\pi}{3}$ with $k = 0, 1, 2$. Other values of $k$ would give the same three roots again.

For $k = 0$ the argument is $\dfrac{\pi}{2}$ and the root is $2i$. For $k = 1$ the argument is $\dfrac{\pi}{2} + \dfrac{2\pi}{3} = \dfrac{7\pi}{6}$, whose cosine is $-\dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$ and whose sine is $-\dfrac{1}{2}$. Multiplying by $2$ gives $-\sqrt{3} - i$. For $k = 2$ the argument is $\dfrac{\pi}{2} + \dfrac{4\pi}{3} = \dfrac{11\pi}{6}$, with cosine $\dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$ and sine $-\dfrac{1}{2}$, and the root is $\sqrt{3} - i$.

The three roots lie on the circle of radius $2$ and their arguments are spaced $\dfrac{2\pi}{3}$ apart, so they are the vertices of an inscribed **equilateral triangle**. Cubing the first root gives the original number because $(2i)^3 = 8\,i^3 = -8i$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Over $\mathbb{C}$, solve the equation $z^4 = -8 + 8\sqrt{3}\,i$ and calculate the area of the polygon whose vertices are the solutions.

$$
\left| -8 + 8\sqrt{3}\,i \right| = \sqrt{64 + 192} = \sqrt{256} = 16
$$
$$
\begin{cases}
\cos\theta = -\dfrac{8}{16} = -\dfrac{1}{2} \\[10pt]
\sin\theta = \dfrac{8\sqrt{3}}{16} = \dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}
\end{cases}
\qquad
\Longrightarrow
\qquad
\theta = \frac{2\pi}{3}
$$
$$
z_k = \sqrt[4]{16}\left( \cos\frac{\frac{2\pi}{3} + 2k\pi}{4} + i\sin\frac{\frac{2\pi}{3} + 2k\pi}{4} \right) = 2\left( \cos\left( \frac{\pi}{6} + \frac{k\pi}{2} \right) + i\sin\left( \frac{\pi}{6} + \frac{k\pi}{2} \right) \right)
$$
$$
z_0 = \sqrt{3} + i ,
\qquad
z_1 = -1 + \sqrt{3}\,i ,
\qquad
z_2 = -\sqrt{3} - i ,
\qquad
z_3 = 1 - \sqrt{3}\,i
$$
$$
\ell = |z_1 - z_0| = 2\sqrt{2} ,
\qquad
A = \ell^2 = 8
$$

The solutions are $\sqrt{3} + i$, $-1 + \sqrt{3}\,i$, $-\sqrt{3} - i$, and $1 - \sqrt{3}\,i$, and are the vertices of a square of area $8$.

<div class="spiegazione">

The solutions of the equation are the **fourth roots** of $-8 + 8\sqrt{3}\,i$. Write the number in trigonometric form. Its modulus is $\sqrt{64 + 64 \cdot 3} = 16$, and the cosine and sine of its argument are $-\dfrac{1}{2}$ and $\dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$, so the argument is in the second quadrant and equals $\pi - \dfrac{\pi}{3} = \dfrac{2\pi}{3}$.

By the theorem on $n$-th roots with $n = 4$, the solutions have modulus $\sqrt[4]{16} = 2$ and arguments $\dfrac{1}{4}\left( \dfrac{2\pi}{3} + 2k\pi \right) = \dfrac{\pi}{6} + \dfrac{k\pi}{2}$, with $k = 0, 1, 2, 3$. The arguments are $\dfrac{\pi}{6}$, $\dfrac{2\pi}{3}$, $\dfrac{7\pi}{6}$, and $\dfrac{5\pi}{3}$. The sine and cosine values for these special angles give the four algebraic forms.

The four solutions lie on the circle of radius $2$, and consecutive arguments differ by $\dfrac{\pi}{2}$, so the solutions are the vertices of a **square**. The side length is the distance between two consecutive vertices, $|z_1 - z_0| = \left| \left( -1 - \sqrt{3} \right) + \left( \sqrt{3} - 1 \right) i \right|$. The sum of the squares of the two parts is $\left( 4 + 2\sqrt{3} \right) + \left( 4 - 2\sqrt{3} \right) = 8$, so $\ell = \sqrt{8} = 2\sqrt{2}$ and the area is $\ell^2 = 8$. The same side length follows from the Pythagorean theorem in the isosceles right triangle whose legs equal the radius $2$.

</div>
