## Exercise 1

Write the number $z = 1 - \sqrt{3}\,i$ in trigonometric form and plot it in the complex plane.

$$
\rho = |z| = \sqrt{1^2 + \left( -\sqrt{3} \right)^2} = \sqrt{1 + 3} = 2
$$
$$
\begin{cases}
\cos\theta = \dfrac{1}{2} \\[10pt]
\sin\theta = -\dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}
\end{cases}
\qquad
\Longrightarrow
\qquad
\theta = 2\pi - \frac{\pi}{3} = \frac{5\pi}{3}
$$
$$
z = 2\left( \cos\frac{5\pi}{3} + i\sin\frac{5\pi}{3} \right)
$$

::grafico{curve="2*cos(t),2*sin(t),0,6.29;0.6*cos(t),0.6*sin(t),0,5.236" vettori="0,0,1,-1.732,z" punti="1,-1.732,pieno" segmenti="1,-1.732,1,0;1,-1.732,0,-1.732" etichette="0.75,0.5,θ" xmin=-3 xmax=3 ymin=-3 ymax=3 uguale=1 nomi="Re,Im" legenda="|z| = 2;\theta = \frac{5\pi}{3}" titolo="The number $z = 1 - \sqrt{3}\,i$ is in the fourth quadrant and has modulus $2$ and argument $\dfrac{5\pi}{3}$"}

The trigonometric form is $z = 2\left( \cos\dfrac{5\pi}{3} + i\sin\dfrac{5\pi}{3} \right)$.

<div class="spiegazione">

To write a number in **trigonometric form**, we need its modulus $\rho$ and an argument $\theta$. The number has real part $a = 1$ and imaginary part $b = -\sqrt{3}$, so its modulus is $\sqrt{a^2 + b^2} = \sqrt{4} = 2$.

The argument is the angle satisfying both $\cos\theta = \dfrac{a}{\rho}$ and $\sin\theta = \dfrac{b}{\rho}$, so we write the two conditions as a system. This gives $\cos\theta = \dfrac{1}{2}$ and $\sin\theta = -\dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$. The cosine is positive and the sine is negative, so the angle is in the **fourth quadrant**, as the point $(1, -\sqrt{3})$ in the figure confirms.

The values $\dfrac{1}{2}$ and $\dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$ are the cosine and sine of the special angle $\dfrac{\pi}{3}$. In the fourth quadrant the angle with the same cosine and the opposite sine is $2\pi - \dfrac{\pi}{3} = \dfrac{5\pi}{3}$, which belongs to the interval $[0, 2\pi)$. Thus $\arg z = \dfrac{5\pi}{3}$. The angle $-\dfrac{\pi}{3}$ is also an argument of $z$ because it differs from $\dfrac{5\pi}{3}$ by $2\pi$ and gives the same trigonometric form.

Substituting the modulus and argument into $\rho(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta)$ gives the required form.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Write the numbers $z_1 = -3 + 3i$ and $z_2 = -2i$ in trigonometric form.

$$
|z_1| = \sqrt{9 + 9} = \sqrt{18} = 3\sqrt{2}
$$
$$
\begin{cases}
\cos\theta_1 = \dfrac{-3}{3\sqrt{2}} = -\dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2} \\[10pt]
\sin\theta_1 = \dfrac{3}{3\sqrt{2}} = \dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}
\end{cases}
\qquad
\Longrightarrow
\qquad
\theta_1 = \pi - \frac{\pi}{4} = \frac{3\pi}{4}
$$
$$
z_1 = 3\sqrt{2}\left( \cos\frac{3\pi}{4} + i\sin\frac{3\pi}{4} \right)
$$
$$
|z_2| = 2 ,
\qquad
\theta_2 = \frac{3\pi}{2} ,
\qquad
z_2 = 2\left( \cos\frac{3\pi}{2} + i\sin\frac{3\pi}{2} \right)
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

For $z_1$ the real part is $-3$ and the imaginary part is $3$. The modulus is $\sqrt{(-3)^2 + 3^2} = \sqrt{18}$, and since $18 = 9 \cdot 2$, we can take the factor $3$ out of the radical, obtaining $3\sqrt{2}$.

In the equations for the cosine and sine, cancel $3$ and rationalize $\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}} = \dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$. The cosine is negative and the sine is positive, so the argument is in the **second quadrant**. The special angle whose cosine and sine both equal $\dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$ is $\dfrac{\pi}{4}$, and its supplement $\pi - \dfrac{\pi}{4} = \dfrac{3\pi}{4}$ has the same sine and the opposite cosine. Thus $\arg z_1 = \dfrac{3\pi}{4}$.

Here, applying the arctangent to the ratio $\dfrac{b}{a} = -1$ would give the angle $-\dfrac{\pi}{4}$, which is in the fourth quadrant. This is why we find the argument from the equations for the cosine and sine.

The number $z_2 = -2i$ is purely imaginary, so it lies on the imaginary axis. Its modulus is the distance from the origin to the point $(0, -2)$, namely $2$. The point lies on the negative imaginary axis, which is obtained by rotating the positive real axis three quarters of a turn counterclockwise, so $\arg z_2 = \dfrac{3\pi}{2}$. The system confirms the result because $\cos\theta_2 = 0$ and $\sin\theta_2 = -1$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

The number $z = -2\left( \cos\dfrac{\pi}{5} + i\sin\dfrac{\pi}{5} \right)$ is not written in trigonometric form. Explain why and write it in trigonometric form. Then write its conjugate $\bar z$ in trigonometric form.

$$
-1 = \cos\pi + i\sin\pi
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
z &= 2\,(\cos\pi + i\sin\pi)\left( \cos\frac{\pi}{5} + i\sin\frac{\pi}{5} \right) \\[6pt]
&= 2\left( \cos\frac{6\pi}{5} + i\sin\frac{6\pi}{5} \right)
\end{aligned}
$$
$$
\bar z = 2\left( \cos\frac{6\pi}{5} - i\sin\frac{6\pi}{5} \right) = 2\left( \cos\left( -\frac{6\pi}{5} \right) + i\sin\left( -\frac{6\pi}{5} \right) \right)
$$
$$
-\frac{6\pi}{5} + 2\pi = \frac{4\pi}{5}
\qquad
\Longrightarrow
\qquad
\bar z = 2\left( \cos\frac{4\pi}{5} + i\sin\frac{4\pi}{5} \right)
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

In the trigonometric form $\rho(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta)$, the factor multiplying the expression $\cos\theta + i\sin\theta$ is the modulus, and the modulus is always positive. Here this factor is $-2$, so the expression **is not in trigonometric form**, even though it looks like it.

To put it in the correct form, write $-2 = 2 \cdot (-1)$ and express $-1$ in trigonometric form. The number $-1$ has modulus $1$ and argument $\pi$. The number $z$ is therefore the product of $2\,(\cos\pi + i\sin\pi)$ and $\cos\dfrac{\pi}{5} + i\sin\dfrac{\pi}{5}$. By the **product rule in trigonometric form**, we multiply the moduli, $2 \cdot 1 = 2$, and add the arguments, $\pi + \dfrac{\pi}{5} = \dfrac{6\pi}{5}$. In the complex plane the minus sign corresponds to reflection through the origin, that is, a rotation through $\pi$.

The conjugate has the same real part and the opposite imaginary part, so it is obtained by changing the sign of the sine term. Since the cosine is even and the sine is odd, $\cos\dfrac{6\pi}{5} - i\sin\dfrac{6\pi}{5}$ equals $\cos\left( -\dfrac{6\pi}{5} \right) + i\sin\left( -\dfrac{6\pi}{5} \right)$. The argument of $\bar z$ is therefore $-\dfrac{6\pi}{5}$. Adding $2\pi$ gives the argument $\dfrac{4\pi}{5}$ in the interval $[0, 2\pi)$. The modulus remains $2$ because $|\bar z| = |z|$.

</div>
