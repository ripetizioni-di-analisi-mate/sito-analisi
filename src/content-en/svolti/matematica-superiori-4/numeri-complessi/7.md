## Exercise 1

Over $\mathbb{C}$, solve the equation $z^2 + 6z + 13 = 0$ and plot the solutions in the complex plane.

$$
\Delta = 6^2 - 4 \cdot 1 \cdot 13 = 36 - 52 = -16
$$
$$
\sqrt{-\Delta} = \sqrt{16} = 4
$$
$$
z_{1,2} = \frac{-6 \pm 4i}{2} = -3 \pm 2i
$$
$$
z_1 + z_2 = -6 ,
\qquad
z_1 z_2 = (-3)^2 + 2^2 = 13
$$

::grafico{punti="-3,2,pieno,z₁ = −3 + 2i;-3,-2,pieno,z₂ = −3 − 2i" segmenti="-3,2,-3,-2" xmin=-5 xmax=2 ymin=-3 ymax=3 uguale=1 nomi="Re,Im" titolo="The solutions of $z^2 + 6z + 13 = 0$ are conjugates and therefore reflections of one another across the real axis"}

The solutions are $z_1 = -3 + 2i$ and $z_2 = -3 - 2i$.

<div class="spiegazione">

This is a quadratic equation with real coefficients $a = 1$, $b = 6$, and $c = 13$. First calculate the discriminant, which equals $-16$. Since the discriminant is negative, the equation has no real solutions, and its solutions in $\mathbb{C}$ are found using the formula for **equations with a negative discriminant**.

The number $-\Delta = 16$ is positive and its principal square root is $4$. The formula $z = \dfrac{-b \pm i\sqrt{-\Delta}}{2a}$ therefore gives $\dfrac{-6 \pm 4i}{2}$. Dividing the real and imaginary parts by $2$ gives the solutions $-3 + 2i$ and $-3 - 2i$.

The two solutions are **complex conjugates**, as always happens for a quadratic equation with real coefficients and $\Delta < 0$. In the complex plane they are reflections of one another across the real axis and lie on the vertical line $x = -3$, the axis of symmetry of the parabola $y = x^2 + 6x + 13$.

The relationships between coefficients and solutions also hold in $\mathbb{C}$ and provide a check on the calculations. The sum of the solutions is $-6 = -\dfrac{b}{a}$ because the imaginary parts cancel. The product of two conjugates is the sum of the squares of their real and imaginary parts, namely $9 + 4 = 13 = \dfrac{c}{a}$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Over $\mathbb{C}$, solve the equation $z^3 - 3z^2 + 4z - 2 = 0$.

$$
p(1) = 1 - 3 + 4 - 2 = 0
$$
$$
\begin{array}{c|ccc|c}
 & 1 & -3 & 4 & -2 \\
1 & & 1 & -2 & 2 \\
\hline
 & 1 & -2 & 2 & 0
\end{array}
$$
$$
z^3 - 3z^2 + 4z - 2 = (z - 1)\left( z^2 - 2z + 2 \right)
$$
$$
z^2 - 2z + 2 = 0 ,
\qquad
\Delta = 4 - 8 = -4 ,
\qquad
z = \frac{2 \pm 2i}{2} = 1 \pm i
$$

The solutions are $1$, $1 + i$, and $1 - i$.

<div class="spiegazione">

The equation is cubic, so by the **fundamental theorem of algebra** it has three solutions in $\mathbb{C}$, counted with multiplicity. To find them, factor the polynomial.

The coefficients are integers and the leading coefficient is $1$, so any integer root must divide the constant term $-2$. The possible values are $\pm 1$ and $\pm 2$. Try $z = 1$. The sum of the coefficients is $0$, so $p(1) = 0$. By the factor theorem, the polynomial is therefore divisible by $z - 1$.

**Synthetic division** gives the quotient $z^2 - 2z + 2$ with remainder $0$. By the zero-product property, which also holds in $\mathbb{C}$, the equation is equivalent to $z - 1 = 0$ or $z^2 - 2z + 2 = 0$. The first gives the real solution $z = 1$.

The quadratic factor has discriminant $-4$, which is negative, so its roots are not real. By the formula for equations with a negative discriminant, $\sqrt{-\Delta} = 2$ and $z = \dfrac{2 \pm 2i}{2} = 1 \pm i$.

The two nonreal solutions are **conjugates**, as expected for polynomials with real coefficients. Thus the cubic polynomial has one real root and one pair of conjugate roots. A polynomial with real coefficients and odd degree always has at least one real root because its nonreal roots occur in pairs.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Over $\mathbb{C}$, solve the equation $z^4 + 3z^2 - 4 = 0$ and write the polynomial as a product of linear factors.

$$
t = z^2 ,
\qquad
t^2 + 3t - 4 = 0
$$
$$
t = \frac{-3 \pm \sqrt{9 + 16}}{2} = \frac{-3 \pm 5}{2}
\qquad
\Longrightarrow
\qquad
t = 1 \quad \text{or} \quad t = -4
$$
$$
z^2 = 1
\iff
z = \pm 1
$$
$$
z^2 = -4
\iff
z^2 = (2i)^2
\iff
z = \pm 2i
$$
$$
z^4 + 3z^2 - 4 = (z - 1)(z + 1)(z - 2i)(z + 2i)
$$

The solutions are $1$, $-1$, $2i$, and $-2i$.

<div class="spiegazione">

The equation involves only the powers $z^4$ and $z^2$, so it is **biquadratic**. With the substitution $t = z^2$, it becomes a quadratic equation in $t$ because $z^4 = \left( z^2 \right)^2 = t^2$.

The equation $t^2 + 3t - 4 = 0$ has positive discriminant $9 + 16 = 25$ and solutions $t = 1$ and $t = -4$. Over the real numbers we would discard $t = -4$ because a real square is nonnegative. In $\mathbb{C}$, however, both values give solutions because every complex number has two square roots.

Return to the original unknown $z$. The equation $z^2 = 1$ has the real solutions $1$ and $-1$. The equation $z^2 = -4$ can be written as $z^2 = (2i)^2$ because $(2i)^2 = 4i^2 = -4$. The difference of squares $z^2 - (2i)^2 = (z - 2i)(z + 2i)$ vanishes at $z = 2i$ and $z = -2i$.

There are four solutions, as predicted by the **fundamental theorem of algebra** for a fourth-degree equation. Each solution $z_0$ corresponds to a factor $z - z_0$, so the polynomial can be written as a product of four linear factors. The product of the two factors containing $\pm 2i$ is $z^2 + 4$, which has real coefficients, and the product of the other two is $z^2 - 1$. Indeed, $\left( z^2 - 1 \right)\left( z^2 + 4 \right) = z^4 + 3z^2 - 4$.

</div>
