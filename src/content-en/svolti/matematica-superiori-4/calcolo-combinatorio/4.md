## Exercise 1

A coach must choose the $5$ starting players of a basketball team from a roster of $9$ players that includes the captain. Find the number of possible selections and how many include the captain.

$$
C_{9,5} = \binom{9}{5} = \frac{9 \cdot 8 \cdot 7 \cdot 6 \cdot 5}{5!} = \frac{15\,120}{120} = 126
$$
$$
\text{selections including the captain} = \binom{8}{4} = \frac{8 \cdot 7 \cdot 6 \cdot 5}{4!} = \frac{1680}{24} = 70
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The starting players form a group of $5$ distinct players. Their selection order does not matter, since the starters are the same in any order. The selections are therefore **combinations without repetition** from $9$ objects taken $5$ at a time. In the formula $C_{n,k} = \dfrac{D_{n,k}}{k!}$ the numerator is the product of $5$ consecutive decreasing numbers starting with $9$, namely $15\,120$, and the denominator is $5! = 120$. There are therefore $126$ selections. The symmetry of binomial coefficients gives the same result with less arithmetic, since $\dbinom{9}{5} = \dbinom{9}{4} = \dfrac{9 \cdot 8 \cdot 7 \cdot 6}{24} = 126$.

In selections that include the captain, one place is already filled. There are $4$ players to choose from the other $8$, still without regard to order and without repetition, so there are $\dbinom{8}{4} = 70$ such selections.

The number of selections without the captain is consequently $126 - 70 = 56$, which agrees with $\dbinom{8}{5} = 56$, the number of groups of $5$ players chosen from the $8$ players other than the captain.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Mark $8$ distinct points on a circle. Find the number of segments with two of these points as endpoints and the number of triangles with three of these points as vertices.

$$
\text{segments} = \binom{8}{2} = \frac{8 \cdot 7}{2} = 28
$$
$$
\text{triangles} = \binom{8}{3} = \frac{8 \cdot 7 \cdot 6}{3 \cdot 2 \cdot 1} = 56
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

A segment is determined by its two endpoints, and segment $P_1 P_2$ is the same as segment $P_2 P_1$. Segments therefore correspond to subsets of $2$ points, the **combinations without repetition** from $8$ points taken $2$ at a time, of which there are $\dbinom{8}{2} = 28$.

A triangle is determined by its set of three vertices, and changing the order in which the vertices are named leaves the triangle unchanged. Three distinct points on a circle cannot be collinear, since a line meets a circle in at most two points. Every triple of points therefore gives the vertices of a nondegenerate triangle, and the triangles correspond to the combinations from $8$ points taken $3$ at a time, numbering $\dbinom{8}{3} = 56$.

In the calculation of $\dbinom{8}{3}$ the numerator is $D_{8,3} = 8 \cdot 7 \cdot 6 = 336$, the number of ordered triples of vertices. Each triangle corresponds to $3! = 6$ ordered triples, one for each possible order of its vertices, and $\dfrac{336}{6} = 56$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A committee of $4$ people is chosen from $7$ teachers and $5$ students and must contain at least $2$ students. Find the number of possible committees.

$$
\text{2 students and 2 teachers} = \binom{5}{2} \binom{7}{2} = 10 \cdot 21 = 210
$$
$$
\text{3 students and 1 teacher} = \binom{5}{3} \binom{7}{1} = 10 \cdot 7 = 70
$$
$$
\text{4 students} = \binom{5}{4} \binom{7}{0} = 5 \cdot 1 = 5
$$
$$
\text{committees} = 210 + 70 + 5 = 285
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

A committee is a group of people with no assigned roles, so order does not matter and we use **combinations without repetition**. The restriction “at least $2$ students” allows committees with $2$, $3$, or $4$ students. Count each case separately.

In each case the committee is formed in two stages: choose students from the $5$ available, then choose teachers from the $7$ available so that there are $4$ members altogether. The number of choices of teachers does not depend on which students were chosen, so by the **fundamental counting principle** multiply the two binomial coefficients. With $2$ students we need $2$ teachers, giving $10 \cdot 21 = 210$ committees. With $3$ students we need $1$ teacher, giving $10 \cdot 7 = 70$ committees. With $4$ students no teachers are needed. The factor $\dbinom{7}{0} = 1$ counts the single choice of no teachers, and there are $5$ committees.

The three cases are disjoint since they have different numbers of students, so by the **addition principle** there are $285$ committees. The complementary case provides a check: there are $\dbinom{12}{4} = 495$ committees in total, $\dbinom{7}{4} = 35$ with no students, and $5 \cdot \dbinom{7}{3} = 175$ with one student, and $495 - 35 - 175 = 285$.

</div>
