## Exercise 1

Given $\sin\alpha = -\dfrac{5}{13}$ with $\dfrac{3\pi}{2} < \alpha < 2\pi$, calculate $\sin 2\alpha$, $\cos 2\alpha$, and $\tan 2\alpha$.

$$
\cos\alpha = +\sqrt{1 - \frac{25}{169}} = \sqrt{\frac{144}{169}} = \frac{12}{13}
$$
$$
\sin 2\alpha = 2 \cdot \left( -\frac{5}{13} \right) \cdot \frac{12}{13} = -\frac{120}{169}
$$
$$
\cos 2\alpha = 1 - 2 \cdot \frac{25}{169} = \frac{169 - 50}{169} = \frac{119}{169}
$$
$$
\tan 2\alpha = \frac{\sin 2\alpha}{\cos 2\alpha} = -\frac{120}{169} \cdot \frac{169}{119} = -\frac{120}{119}
$$

::grafico{curve="cos(t),sin(t),0,6.29" vettori="0,0,0.923,-0.385,α;0,0,0.704,-0.710,2α" xmin=-1.5 xmax=1.5 ymin=-1.3 ymax=1.3 uguale=1 legenda=0 titolo="The angle $\alpha$ lies in the fourth quadrant, and $2\alpha$ is also coterminal with a fourth-quadrant angle, with $\cos 2\alpha = \dfrac{119}{169}$ and $\sin 2\alpha = -\dfrac{120}{169}$"}

<div class="spiegazione">

The sine double-angle formula, $\sin 2\alpha = 2\sin\alpha\cos\alpha$, also requires the cosine of $\alpha$. The Pythagorean identity gives $\cos^2\alpha = 1 - \sin^2\alpha = \dfrac{144}{169}$. The angle $\alpha$ lies in the fourth quadrant, where cosine is positive, so $\cos\alpha = \dfrac{12}{13}$.

The sine of twice the angle is the product of $2$, a negative number, and a positive number, so it is negative and equals $-\dfrac{120}{169}$.

For the cosine of twice the angle, choose the form $\cos 2\alpha = 1 - 2\sin^2\alpha$, which uses only the given value $\sin\alpha$. The square of a negative number is positive, so $\sin^2\alpha = \dfrac{25}{169}$ and $\cos 2\alpha = \dfrac{119}{169}$. The form $2\cos^2\alpha - 1 = \dfrac{288}{169} - 1$ gives the same value.

The cosine of $2\alpha$ differs from $0$, so the tangent is defined and can be calculated as a ratio. The factor $169$ cancels, leaving $-\dfrac{120}{119}$.

The condition $\dfrac{3\pi}{2} < \alpha < 2\pi$ implies $3\pi < 2\alpha < 4\pi$. Subtracting the period $2\pi$ gives $\pi < 2\alpha - 2\pi < 2\pi$, so twice the angle is coterminal with an angle in either the third or fourth quadrant. The positive cosine indicates the **fourth quadrant**, consistent with the negative sine, as the figure shows.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Given $\tan\alpha = 2$ with $\pi < \alpha < \dfrac{3\pi}{2}$, calculate $\sin 2\alpha$, $\cos 2\alpha$, and $\tan 2\alpha$.

$$
\cos^2\alpha = \frac{1}{1 + \tan^2\alpha} = \frac{1}{5}
\qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad
\cos\alpha = -\frac{1}{\sqrt{5}}
$$
$$
\sin\alpha = \tan\alpha\cos\alpha = -\frac{2}{\sqrt{5}}
$$
$$
\sin 2\alpha = 2 \cdot \left( -\frac{2}{\sqrt{5}} \right) \cdot \left( -\frac{1}{\sqrt{5}} \right) = \frac{4}{5}
$$
$$
\cos 2\alpha = \cos^2\alpha - \sin^2\alpha = \frac{1}{5} - \frac{4}{5} = -\frac{3}{5}
$$
$$
\tan 2\alpha = \frac{2 \cdot 2}{1 - 4} = -\frac{4}{3}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The given value is the tangent, so find the sine and cosine using the identity $1 + \tan^2\alpha = \dfrac{1}{\cos^2\alpha}$ studied in the chapter on angles and trigonometric functions. This gives $\cos^2\alpha = \dfrac{1}{1 + 4} = \dfrac{1}{5}$. The angle $\alpha$ lies in the third quadrant, where cosine is negative, so $\cos\alpha = -\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{5}}$.

Find the sine from the definition of tangent, $\tan\alpha = \dfrac{\sin\alpha}{\cos\alpha}$, by multiplying both sides by $\cos\alpha$. The result $-\dfrac{2}{\sqrt{5}}$ is negative, as it must be in the third quadrant.

In the **sine double-angle** formula, the two negative factors give a positive product, and $\sqrt{5} \cdot \sqrt{5} = 5$, so $\sin 2\alpha = \dfrac{4}{5}$. For the cosine, use the form $\cos^2\alpha - \sin^2\alpha$ with $\sin^2\alpha = \dfrac{4}{5}$. There is no need to rationalize because only squares appear.

For the tangent, the conditions for the formula $\tan 2\alpha = \dfrac{2\tan\alpha}{1 - \tan^2\alpha}$ are satisfied because $\tan\alpha$ is defined and $\tan^2\alpha = 4 \neq 1$. The formula gives $-\dfrac{4}{3}$, which agrees with the ratio $\dfrac{4}{5} : \left( -\dfrac{3}{5} \right)$.

From $\pi < \alpha < \dfrac{3\pi}{2}$ it follows that $2\pi < 2\alpha < 3\pi$. The positive sine and negative cosine indicate that $2\alpha$ is coterminal with an angle in the **second quadrant**.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Given $\cos\alpha = \dfrac{1}{3}$ with $0 < \alpha < \dfrac{\pi}{2}$, calculate $\cos 4\alpha$ and $\sin 4\alpha$.

$$
\cos 2\alpha = 2 \cdot \frac{1}{9} - 1 = -\frac{7}{9}
$$
$$
\cos 4\alpha = 2\cos^2 2\alpha - 1 = 2 \cdot \frac{49}{81} - 1 = \frac{98 - 81}{81} = \frac{17}{81}
$$
$$
\sin\alpha = \sqrt{1 - \frac{1}{9}} = \frac{2\sqrt{2}}{3} ,
\qquad
\sin 2\alpha = 2 \cdot \frac{2\sqrt{2}}{3} \cdot \frac{1}{3} = \frac{4\sqrt{2}}{9}
$$
$$
\sin 4\alpha = 2\sin 2\alpha\cos 2\alpha = 2 \cdot \frac{4\sqrt{2}}{9} \cdot \left( -\frac{7}{9} \right) = -\frac{56\sqrt{2}}{81}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The angle $4\alpha$ is twice the angle $2\alpha$, so apply the **double-angle formulas** twice, first from $\alpha$ to $2\alpha$ and then from $2\alpha$ to $4\alpha$.

For the cosine, the given value is sufficient. The form $\cos 2\alpha = 2\cos^2\alpha - 1$ involves only $\cos\alpha$ and gives $-\dfrac{7}{9}$. Apply the same form to the angle $2\alpha$, that is, $\cos 4\alpha = 2\cos^2 2\alpha - 1$, to obtain $\dfrac{17}{81}$. The sine of $\alpha$ was not needed in this calculation.

For the sine of $4\alpha$, the formula $\sin 4\alpha = 2\sin 2\alpha\cos 2\alpha$ also requires $\sin 2\alpha$ and hence $\sin\alpha$. The angle $\alpha$ lies in the first quadrant, so its sine is positive and $\sin\alpha = \sqrt{\dfrac{8}{9}} = \dfrac{2\sqrt{2}}{3}$. It follows that $\sin 2\alpha = \dfrac{4\sqrt{2}}{9}$.

Multiplying by $2$ and by $\cos 2\alpha = -\dfrac{7}{9}$ gives $\sin 4\alpha = -\dfrac{56\sqrt{2}}{81}$. The negative sign agrees with the quadrant. Indeed, $\cos 2\alpha < 0$ and $\sin 2\alpha > 0$, so $2\alpha$ lies in the second quadrant and $\dfrac{\pi}{2} < 2\alpha < \pi$. Doubling gives $\pi < 4\alpha < 2\pi$, where sine is negative.

</div>
