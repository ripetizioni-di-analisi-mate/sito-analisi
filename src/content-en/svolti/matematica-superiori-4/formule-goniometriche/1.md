## Exercise 1

Find the exact values of $\sin\dfrac{7\pi}{12}$ and $\cos\dfrac{7\pi}{12}$.

$$
\frac{7\pi}{12} = \frac{4\pi}{12} + \frac{3\pi}{12} = \frac{\pi}{3} + \frac{\pi}{4}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
\sin\frac{7\pi}{12} &= \sin\frac{\pi}{3}\cos\frac{\pi}{4} + \cos\frac{\pi}{3}\sin\frac{\pi}{4} \\[6pt]
&= \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} \cdot \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} + \frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} \\[6pt]
&= \frac{\sqrt{6} + \sqrt{2}}{4}
\end{aligned}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
\cos\frac{7\pi}{12} &= \cos\frac{\pi}{3}\cos\frac{\pi}{4} - \sin\frac{\pi}{3}\sin\frac{\pi}{4} \\[6pt]
&= \frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} - \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} \cdot \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} \\[6pt]
&= \frac{\sqrt{2} - \sqrt{6}}{4}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The angle $\dfrac{7\pi}{12}$ is not one of the standard special angles, so its sine and cosine cannot be read from a table. The numerator $7$ can be written as $4 + 3$, so the angle becomes the sum of $\dfrac{4\pi}{12} = \dfrac{\pi}{3}$ and $\dfrac{3\pi}{12} = \dfrac{\pi}{4}$, two special angles whose sine and cosine are known.

For the sine, apply the **sine sum** formula, $\sin(\alpha + \beta) = \sin\alpha\cos\beta + \cos\alpha\sin\beta$, with $\alpha = \dfrac{\pi}{3}$ and $\beta = \dfrac{\pi}{4}$. Substitute $\sin\dfrac{\pi}{3} = \dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$, $\cos\dfrac{\pi}{3} = \dfrac{1}{2}$, and $\sin\dfrac{\pi}{4} = \cos\dfrac{\pi}{4} = \dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$. In the products, $\sqrt{3} \cdot \sqrt{2} = \sqrt{6}$ and the denominators become $4$, so the two fractions can be added directly.

For the cosine, apply the **cosine sum** formula, which has a minus sign before the product of the sines. The result is $\dfrac{\sqrt{2} - \sqrt{6}}{4}$, which is negative because $\sqrt{6} > \sqrt{2}$.

The sign agrees with the position of the angle. Indeed, $\dfrac{\pi}{2} = \dfrac{6\pi}{12} < \dfrac{7\pi}{12} < \pi$, so the angle lies in the second quadrant, where sine is positive and cosine is negative. Therefore $\sin\dfrac{7\pi}{12} = \dfrac{\sqrt{6} + \sqrt{2}}{4}$ and $\cos\dfrac{7\pi}{12} = \dfrac{\sqrt{2} - \sqrt{6}}{4}$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Calculate the exact values of $\tan\dfrac{5\pi}{12}$ and $\tan\dfrac{11\pi}{12}$ using the formulas for the tangent of a sum.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\tan\frac{5\pi}{12} &= \tan\left( \frac{\pi}{4} + \frac{\pi}{6} \right) = \frac{1 + \dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{3}}{1 - 1 \cdot \dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{3}} \\[6pt]
&= \frac{3 + \sqrt{3}}{3 - \sqrt{3}} = \frac{\left( 3 + \sqrt{3} \right)^2}{9 - 3} \\[6pt]
&= \frac{12 + 6\sqrt{3}}{6} = 2 + \sqrt{3}
\end{aligned}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
\tan\frac{11\pi}{12} &= \tan\left( \frac{2\pi}{3} + \frac{\pi}{4} \right) = \frac{-\sqrt{3} + 1}{1 - \left( -\sqrt{3} \right) \cdot 1} \\[6pt]
&= \frac{1 - \sqrt{3}}{1 + \sqrt{3}} = \frac{\left( 1 - \sqrt{3} \right)^2}{1 - 3} \\[6pt]
&= \frac{4 - 2\sqrt{3}}{-2} = \sqrt{3} - 2
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The first angle can be written as $\dfrac{5\pi}{12} = \dfrac{3\pi}{12} + \dfrac{2\pi}{12} = \dfrac{\pi}{4} + \dfrac{\pi}{6}$. The tangents of $\dfrac{\pi}{4}$, $\dfrac{\pi}{6}$, and $\dfrac{5\pi}{12}$ are defined because none of the three angles has the form $\dfrac{\pi}{2} + k\pi$, so the **tangent sum** formula applies. Substitute $\tan\dfrac{\pi}{4} = 1$ and $\tan\dfrac{\pi}{6} = \dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{3}$.

To clear the fractions within the quotient, multiply the numerator and denominator by $3$ to obtain $\dfrac{3 + \sqrt{3}}{3 - \sqrt{3}}$. To rationalize the denominator, multiply the numerator and denominator by $3 + \sqrt{3}$. The denominator becomes the difference of squares $9 - 3 = 6$, and the numerator is $\left( 3 + \sqrt{3} \right)^2 = 9 + 6\sqrt{3} + 3$. Dividing by $6$ gives $2 + \sqrt{3}$.

The second angle can be written as $\dfrac{11\pi}{12} = \dfrac{8\pi}{12} + \dfrac{3\pi}{12} = \dfrac{2\pi}{3} + \dfrac{\pi}{4}$. The angle $\dfrac{2\pi}{3}$ lies in the second quadrant and its tangent is $-\sqrt{3}$. This time, rationalize by multiplying by $1 - \sqrt{3}$, so the denominator becomes $1 - 3 = -2$. The numerator is $1 - 2\sqrt{3} + 3 = 4 - 2\sqrt{3}$, and dividing by $-2$ gives $\sqrt{3} - 2$.

The second result is negative, as expected, because $\dfrac{11\pi}{12}$ lies in the second quadrant. Therefore $\tan\dfrac{5\pi}{12} = 2 + \sqrt{3}$ and $\tan\dfrac{11\pi}{12} = \sqrt{3} - 2$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Given $\sin\alpha = \dfrac{4}{5}$ with $\dfrac{\pi}{2} < \alpha < \pi$ and $\cos\beta = -\dfrac{5}{13}$ with $\pi < \beta < \dfrac{3\pi}{2}$, calculate $\sin(\alpha + \beta)$, $\cos(\alpha + \beta)$, $\tan(\alpha + \beta)$, and $\cos(\alpha - \beta)$.

$$
\cos\alpha = -\sqrt{1 - \frac{16}{25}} = -\frac{3}{5} ,
\qquad
\sin\beta = -\sqrt{1 - \frac{25}{169}} = -\frac{12}{13}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
\sin(\alpha + \beta) &= \frac{4}{5} \cdot \left( -\frac{5}{13} \right) + \left( -\frac{3}{5} \right) \cdot \left( -\frac{12}{13} \right) = \frac{-20 + 36}{65} = \frac{16}{65} \\[6pt]
\cos(\alpha + \beta) &= \left( -\frac{3}{5} \right) \cdot \left( -\frac{5}{13} \right) - \frac{4}{5} \cdot \left( -\frac{12}{13} \right) = \frac{15 + 48}{65} = \frac{63}{65} \\[6pt]
\tan(\alpha + \beta) &= \frac{16}{65} \cdot \frac{65}{63} = \frac{16}{63} \\[6pt]
\cos(\alpha - \beta) &= \frac{15}{65} + \left( -\frac{48}{65} \right) = -\frac{33}{65}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The addition formulas require the sine and cosine of both angles, so first calculate $\cos\alpha$ and $\sin\beta$ using the Pythagorean identity $\sin^2 x + \cos^2 x = 1$. The square root gives the absolute value, and the quadrant determines the sign. The angle $\alpha$ is in the second quadrant, where cosine is negative, so $\cos\alpha = -\dfrac{3}{5}$. The angle $\beta$ is in the third quadrant, where sine is negative, so $\sin\beta = -\dfrac{12}{13}$.

Next apply the **sine sum** formula, noting that the product of two negative numbers is positive. Similarly, the **cosine sum** formula gives $\dfrac{63}{65}$. Here the second term is $-\dfrac{4}{5} \cdot \left( -\dfrac{12}{13} \right) = +\dfrac{48}{65}$.

The cosine of $\alpha + \beta$ differs from $0$, so the tangent is defined and equals the ratio of sine to cosine. The denominators $65$ cancel, leaving $\dfrac{16}{63}$.

For $\cos(\alpha - \beta)$ use the **cosine difference** formula, which adds the two products already calculated, $\cos\alpha\cos\beta = \dfrac{15}{65}$ and $\sin\alpha\sin\beta = -\dfrac{48}{65}$. Their sum is $-\dfrac{33}{65}$.

The sine and cosine of $\alpha + \beta$ are positive, so the angle $\alpha + \beta$ is coterminal with a first-quadrant angle. This agrees with the given information because $\dfrac{3\pi}{2} < \alpha + \beta < \dfrac{5\pi}{2}$.

</div>
