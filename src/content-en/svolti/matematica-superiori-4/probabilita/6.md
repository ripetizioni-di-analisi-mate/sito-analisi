## Exercise 1

A card is drawn from a deck of $40$ Neapolitan cards. Let $A$ be the event “drawing an ace,” $B$ the event “drawing a card from the suit of coins,” and $F$ the event “drawing a face card.” Determine whether $A$ and $B$ are independent and whether $A$ and $F$ are independent.

$$
P(A) = \frac{4}{40} = \frac{1}{10} ,
\qquad
P(B) = \frac{10}{40} = \frac{1}{4} ,
\qquad
P(A \cap B) = \frac{1}{40}
$$
$$
P(A) \cdot P(B) = \frac{1}{10} \cdot \frac{1}{4} = \frac{1}{40} = P(A \cap B)
$$
$$
P(F) = \frac{12}{40} = \frac{3}{10} ,
\qquad
P(A \cap F) = 0
$$
$$
P(A) \cdot P(F) = \frac{1}{10} \cdot \frac{3}{10} = \frac{3}{100} \neq 0 = P(A \cap F)
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

To determine whether two events are **independent**, compare the probability of their intersection with the product of their probabilities. The cards are equally likely, so each probability is a ratio of counts.

There are $4$ aces, one per suit, and $10$ cards in the suit of coins. The intersection $A \cap B$ contains only the ace of coins, so its probability is $\dfrac{1}{40}$. The product $P(A) \cdot P(B)$ also equals $\dfrac{1}{40}$, so $A$ and $B$ are **independent**. In other words, knowing that the card belongs to the suit of coins leaves the probability that it is an ace unchanged. Indeed, the $10$ cards of that suit include one ace, and the fraction $\dfrac{1}{10}$ is the same as for the whole deck.

An ace is not a face card, since the face cards are the jack, knight, and king. Events $A$ and $F$ are therefore mutually exclusive and $P(A \cap F) = 0$. The product of the probabilities, however, is $\dfrac{3}{100}$, which is positive. The two quantities differ, so $A$ and $F$ are **dependent**. Two mutually exclusive events with positive probabilities are always dependent, since knowing that one has occurred makes the other impossible.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A device contains three components $C_1$, $C_2$, and $C_3$ that work independently of one another with probabilities $0.9$, $0.8$, and $0.7$. Find the probability that the system works when the components are connected in series, so that the system works only when they all work, and when they are connected in parallel, so that the system works when at least one component works.

$$
P(\text{series}) = 0.9 \cdot 0.8 \cdot 0.7 = 0.504
$$
$$
P\left( \overline{C_1} \right) = 0.1 ,
\qquad
P\left( \overline{C_2} \right) = 0.2 ,
\qquad
P\left( \overline{C_3} \right) = 0.3
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
P(\text{parallel}) &= 1 - P\left( \overline{C_1} \cap \overline{C_2} \cap \overline{C_3} \right) \\[6pt]
&= 1 - 0.1 \cdot 0.2 \cdot 0.3 \\[6pt]
&= 1 - 0.006 = 0.994
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

When the components are connected **in series**, the system works if and only if all three components work, so the event is the intersection $C_1 \cap C_2 \cap C_3$. The components are independent, so the probability of their intersection is the product of the three probabilities and equals $0.504$.

When the components are connected **in parallel**, the system works if at least one component works. Its complement is “no component works,” that is, $\overline{C_1} \cap \overline{C_2} \cap \overline{C_3}$. By the complement rule, the probabilities of failure are $1 - 0.9 = 0.1$, $1 - 0.8 = 0.2$, and $1 - 0.7 = 0.3$. Since the components are independent, their failures are also independent, by the theorem on the independence of complementary events. The probability that they all fail is therefore the product $0.006$.

Applying the complement rule again gives $0.994$ for the probability that the parallel system works. A parallel system has a higher probability of working than any individual component, while a series system has a lower probability.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A fair die is rolled $n$ times. Find the smallest number of rolls $n$ for which the probability of rolling at least one $6$ is greater than $0.9$.

$$
P(\text{at least one } 6) = 1 - \left( \frac{5}{6} \right)^n
$$
$$
1 - \left( \frac{5}{6} \right)^n > 0.9
\iff
\left( \frac{5}{6} \right)^n < 0.1
$$
$$
n \ln \frac{5}{6} < \ln 0.1
\iff
n > \frac{\ln 0.1}{\ln \frac{5}{6}} = \frac{\ln 10}{\ln 1.2} \approx 12.63
$$
$$
\left( \frac{5}{6} \right)^{12} \approx 0.112 ,
\qquad
\left( \frac{5}{6} \right)^{13} \approx 0.093
\qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad
n = 13
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The complement of “at least one $6$” is “no $6$ in the $n$ rolls.” On each roll the probability of not rolling $6$ is $\dfrac{5}{6}$, and the rolls are **independent**, so the probability of no $6$ is $\left( \dfrac{5}{6} \right)^n$. The complement rule gives the first formula.

The required condition is an exponential inequality in the unknown $n$. Subtracting $1$ and changing signs gives $\left( \dfrac{5}{6} \right)^n < 0.1$. Both sides are positive, so we can take the natural logarithm, which is increasing and therefore preserves the direction of the inequality. The power rule for logarithms gives $n \ln \dfrac{5}{6} < \ln 0.1$.

The number $\ln \dfrac{5}{6}$ is **negative** because $\dfrac{5}{6} < 1$. Dividing by a negative number reverses the inequality. The quotient $\dfrac{\ln 0.1}{\ln \frac{5}{6}}$ equals $\dfrac{\ln 10}{\ln 1.2}$ because both the numerator and the denominator change sign, and its approximate value is $12.63$.

The number $n$ is an integer, so the smallest value satisfying the condition is $n = 13$. A direct calculation confirms this, since with $12$ rolls the probability of no $6$ is still greater than $0.1$, whereas with $13$ rolls it is smaller. The minimum number of rolls is therefore **thirteen**.

</div>
