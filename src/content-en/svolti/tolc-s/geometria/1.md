## Exercise 1

An isosceles triangle has equal sides of $13\,\mathrm{cm}$ and base $10\,\mathrm{cm}$. Find its altitude and area.

::struttura{nodi="A,-5,0;B,5,0;C,0,12;H,0,0" linee="-5,0 5,0 0,12 -5,0;0,0 0,12;0,0.7 0.7,0.7 0.7,0" quote="A,H,5 cm;H,B,5 cm;A,C,13 cm,-0.8" titolo="Altitude CH divides the base into two equal parts"}

$$
\begin{aligned}
AH&=\frac{10}{2}=5\,\mathrm{cm},\\[6pt]
h^2&=13^2-5^2=144\,\mathrm{cm^2},\\[6pt]
h&=12\,\mathrm{cm},\\[6pt]
A&=\frac{10\cdot12}{2}=60\,\mathrm{cm^2}.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The altitude from the main vertex of an isosceles triangle bisects the base. This gives two congruent right triangles: the $13\,\mathrm{cm}$ sloping side is the hypotenuse and the $5\,\mathrm{cm}$ half-base is one leg. The **Pythagorean theorem** gives the other leg, which is the altitude. Take the positive root because the requested measurement is a length. Once the altitude is known, the base and height are perpendicular and the area formula applies. Using the sloping side directly instead of $h$ would give $65\,\mathrm{cm^2}$ and would not satisfy this condition. The check $5^2+12^2=13^2$ confirms the measurements of the constructed right triangle.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A right trapezoid has bases $14\,\mathrm{cm}$ and $8\,\mathrm{cm}$ and height $8\,\mathrm{cm}$. Find its area and perimeter.

::struttura{nodi="A,0,0;B,14,0;C,8,8;D,0,8;H,8,0" linee="0,0 14,0 8,8 0,8 0,0;8,0 8,8" quote="A,B,14 cm;D,C,8 cm;D,A,8 cm;H,B,6 cm" titolo="The sloping side is the hypotenuse of triangle BHC"}

$$
\begin{aligned}
BH&=14-8=6\,\mathrm{cm},\\[6pt]
BC&=\sqrt{6^2+8^2}=10\,\mathrm{cm},\\[6pt]
A&=\frac{(14+8)\cdot8}{2}=88\,\mathrm{cm^2},\\[6pt]
P&=14+8+8+10=40\,\mathrm{cm}.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The right trapezoid splits into a rectangle $8\,\mathrm{cm}$ wide and a right triangle with base $6\,\mathrm{cm}$. The difference of the bases gives the triangle’s horizontal leg. The sloping side follows from the Pythagorean theorem, while the other nonparallel side is already the given height. For the area, use the base sum multiplied by half the height. For the perimeter, instead add the four boundary sides; the internal segment $CH$ does not belong to it. As an area check, the rectangle occupies $8\cdot8=64\,\mathrm{cm^2}$ and the triangle $\dfrac{6\cdot8}{2}=24\,\mathrm{cm^2}$, totaling $88\,\mathrm{cm^2}$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A route starts at the origin, goes $7\,\mathrm{km}$ east, then $4\,\mathrm{km}$ north, and finally $4\,\mathrm{km}$ west. Find the straight-line distance from the starting point and its difference from the route length.

$$
\begin{aligned}
P&=(7-4,4)=(3,4),\\[6pt]
OP&=\sqrt{3^2+4^2}=5\,\mathrm{km},\\[6pt]
L&=7+4+4=15\,\mathrm{km},\\[6pt]
L-OP&=10\,\mathrm{km}.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Eastward and westward displacements have opposite directions, so they are subtracted to locate the final point. The net horizontal displacement is $3\,\mathrm{km}$ and the vertical displacement is $4\,\mathrm{km}$. The distance between start and finish depends on these net displacements and follows from the Pythagorean theorem. The traveled distance instead adds all positive segment lengths, including the westward segment. This gives two different quantities, $5\,\mathrm{km}$ and $15\,\mathrm{km}$. The direct connection is $10\,\mathrm{km}$ shorter; the segment traveled in the opposite direction reduces the final displacement while increasing the total path length.

</div>
