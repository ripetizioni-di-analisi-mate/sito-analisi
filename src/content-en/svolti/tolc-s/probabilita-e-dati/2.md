## Exercise 1

The table records sixty journeys. One journey is selected uniformly. Calculate the probability that it is by bus given that it is late, and the probability that it is late given that it is by bus.

| Transport | On time | Late |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Bus | 18 | 6 |
| Train | 24 | 12 |

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(A\mid R)&=\frac6{6+12}=\frac13,\\[4pt]
P(R\mid A)&=\frac6{18+6}=\frac14.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Let A denote a bus journey and R a delay. In the first question, the condition selects the delay column: eighteen journeys are eligible and six are by bus. In the second, it selects the bus row: twenty-four journeys are eligible and six are late. The numerators agree because the intersection is the same cell, but the denominators change. The different results show why reversing the condition changes the meaning of the probability.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Two events satisfy $P(A)=\dfrac25$, $P(B)=\dfrac12$, and $P(A\cap B)=\dfrac15$. Check whether they are independent and calculate the probability that exactly one occurs.

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(A)P(B)&=\frac25\cdot\frac12=\frac15=P(A\cap B),\\[4pt]
P(A\cap B^c)&=\frac25-\frac15=\frac15,\\[4pt]
P(A^c\cap B)&=\frac12-\frac15=\frac3{10},\\[4pt]
P((A\cap B^c)\cup(A^c\cap B))&=\frac15+\frac3{10}=\frac12.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The product of the individual probabilities equals the intersection probability, so the events are independent. This does not make them disjoint: their common part has probability one fifth. To require exactly one event, separate A only and B only by removing the intersection from each initial probability. The remaining regions do not overlap and can be added. The ordinary union would give a larger result because it would also include both events occurring together.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A coin shows heads with probability $\dfrac23$. Two independent tosses are made. Calculate the probabilities of exactly one head and of at least one head.

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(HT)&=\frac23\cdot\frac13=\frac29,\qquad
P(TH)=\frac13\cdot\frac23=\frac29,\\[4pt]
P(\text{one head})&=\frac29+\frac29=\frac49,\\[4pt]
P(\text{at least one head})&=1-P(TT)=1-\frac13\cdot\frac13=\frac89.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The letter H denotes heads and T tails. The four tree paths are not equally likely: HH has weight four ninths, HT and TH two ninths each, and TT one ninth. For exactly one head, add the two mixed paths, which are disjoint. For at least one head, it is shorter to exclude only TT. The probability eight ninths includes HH, whereas four ninths excludes it. The coin was not assumed fair: its weights come from the problem statement.

</div>
