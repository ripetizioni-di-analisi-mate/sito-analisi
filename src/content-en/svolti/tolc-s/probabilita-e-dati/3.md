## Exercise 1

An urn contains four red and two blue balls. Calculate the probability of two red balls in two uniform draws without replacement. Compare it with two independent uniform draws with replacement.

$$
\begin{aligned}
P_{\mathrm{without}}&=\frac46\cdot\frac35=\frac25,\\[4pt]
P_{\mathrm{with}}&=\frac46\cdot\frac46=\frac49,\\[4pt]
P_{\mathrm{with}}-P_{\mathrm{without}}&=\frac49-\frac25=\frac2{45}>0.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Without replacement, the required path removes a red ball before the second draw, leaving three red balls among five. With replacement, the composition is restored, so each draw retains four red balls out of six. The products therefore share the first factor but have different second factors. The probability with replacement is greater by two forty-fifths. The difference comes from the change in composition, not from a different number of draws or a different meaning of the final event.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Urn U1 is chosen with probability $\dfrac13$ and urn U2 with probability $\dfrac23$. U1 contains four red balls and one blue; U2 contains one red and four blue. After a uniform draw, calculate the probability of red and, given red, the probability that U1 was chosen.

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(U_1\cap R)&=\frac13\cdot\frac45=\frac4{15},\\[4pt]
P(U_2\cap R)&=\frac23\cdot\frac15=\frac2{15},\\[4pt]
P(R)&=\frac4{15}+\frac2{15}=\frac25,\\[4pt]
P(U_1\mid R)&=\frac{\frac4{15}}{\frac25}=\frac23.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Red can arise along either of two paths, each consisting of the urn choice and the color drawn from that urn. The urns are not selected with equal weights: the factors one third and two thirds must be retained. Adding the paths gives the total probability of red. To reverse the information, compare the U1-and-red path with all red paths. The observation makes U1 more likely, increasing its initial weight from one third to two thirds.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A spinner gives red with probability $\dfrac14$ on each spin, independently of earlier spins. Spinning continues until red appears. Calculate the probability that exactly three spins are needed.

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(R^c)&=1-\frac14=\frac34,\\[4pt]
P(R^c,R^c,R)&=\frac34\cdot\frac34\cdot\frac14=\frac9{64}.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The number of spins required depends on the position of the first red. Stopping on the third spin means the first two are not red and the third is red. Thus a single category order is fixed: there is no multiplication by three, unlike counting one red in three spins without fixing its position. Independence allows the same weights at each stage. The result does not require describing what would happen after the third spin, because the experiment ends there.

</div>
