## Exercise 1

The table lists the scores of twelve trials. One trial is chosen uniformly: calculate the probability of a score of at least two, and the probability of the same event given that the score is positive. Also identify the mode.

| Score | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Frequency | 2 | 5 | 4 | 1 |

$$
N=2+5+4+1=12,\qquad n_{X\ge2}=4+1=5.
$$
$$
P(X\ge2)=\frac5{12},\qquad
P(X\ge2\mid X>0)=\frac5{5+4+1}=\frac12.
$$
$$
\text{mode}=1.
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Uniform choice among the trials gives each observation equal weight. The event “at least two” includes scores two and three, comprising five observations. In the first question, the denominator is the total of twelve; in the second, the two zero scores are removed because the additional information guarantees a positive value.

The numerator remains five: every observation meeting the threshold is already positive. The **reference population** changes, however, decreasing to ten observations. Conditional probability is not obtained by dividing by the number of remaining categories. The mode is score one because its frequency, five, exceeds the others; five is the mode’s frequency, not its value.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A survey invites one hundred people to support or oppose a proposal. Sixty respond: forty-five support it and fifteen oppose it. Calculate the favorable share among respondents and the smallest and largest possible shares among all invitees, without assumptions about the forty nonrespondents.

$$
f_{\mathrm{respondents}}=\frac{45}{60}=\frac34=75\%.
$$
$$
0\le k\le40,\qquad
f_{\mathrm{invitees}}=\frac{45+k}{100}.
$$
$$
f_{\min}=\frac{45}{100}=45\%,\qquad
f_{\max}=\frac{85}{100}=85\%.
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The respondent percentage uses only people who supplied an opinion, so forty-five is divided by sixty. For invitees, the denominator is instead one hundred. The number $k$ denotes how many nonrespondents would support the proposal: the only available information is that it is an integer between zero and forty.

The lower bound is attained if every nonrespondent is opposed, and the upper bound if every nonrespondent is favorable. Both configurations satisfy the observed data, so the bounds are actually attainable. The $75\%$ figure is not false, but it describes a **different group**. Without further information, it does not identify a unique invitee percentage or justify assigning nonrespondents the same opinions as respondents.

</div>

## Exercise 3

The table gives successes and attempts for two methods on easy and difficult trials. Compare success rates within each category and overall, explaining any reversal of the comparison.

| Method | Easy trials | Difficult trials |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| A | 9 successes out of 10 | 1 success out of 10 |
| B | 72 successes out of 90 | 0 successes out of 1 |

$$
r_{A,\mathrm{easy}}=\frac9{10}>\frac{72}{90}=\frac45=r_{B,\mathrm{easy}},
$$
$$
r_{A,\mathrm{difficult}}=\frac1{10}>0=r_{B,\mathrm{difficult}}.
$$
$$
r_A=\frac{9+1}{10+10}=\frac12,
\qquad r_B=\frac{72+0}{90+1}=\frac{72}{91}>\frac12.
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

A has the higher rate on both easy and difficult trials. Overall, however, B exceeds A: its rate is greater than one half because $2\cdot72>91$. Overall rates are calculated from total successes and total attempts; adding category percentages or averaging them without weights would discard the group sizes.

A attempts equal numbers of easy and difficult trials, whereas B attempts almost exclusively easy trials. The aggregate comparison therefore incorporates a **different mix of trials**. It does not contradict the within-category comparisons: it answers a different question concerning groups with different weights. The observed data alone do not prove that one method causes better results on every future trial.

</div>
