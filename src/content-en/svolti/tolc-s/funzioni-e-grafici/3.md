## Exercise 1

Solve $3^x-3^{x-1}=18$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
3\cdot3^{x-1}-3^{x-1}&=18,\\[6pt]
2\cdot3^{x-1}&=18,\\[6pt]
3^{x-1}&=9=3^2.
\end{aligned}
$$
$$
x-1=2\quad\Longrightarrow\quad x=3.
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The powers differ by one in their exponents, so the first is three times the second. Rewriting $3^x$ as $3\cdot3^{x-1}$ allows the common factor to be taken out. After division by $2$, the equation compares powers with the same base.

The base-$3$ exponential is **strictly increasing**, so two of its values agree only when their exponents agree. This property produces the linear equation $x-1=2$. An independent substitution check gives $3^3-3^2=27-9=18$. The domain is all of $\mathbb R$, so the value found requires no further exclusions.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Solve $4^x+2^x-6=0$.

$$
u=2^x>0,\qquad 4^x=(2^x)^2=u^2.
$$
$$
u^2+u-6=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad(u+3)(u-2)=0.
$$
$$
u=-3\ \text{or}\ u=2.
$$
$$
u>0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad u=2\quad\Longrightarrow\quad2^x=2\quad\Longrightarrow\quad x=1.
$$

::grafico{f="4^x+2^x-6" xmin=-2 xmax=2 ymin=-7 ymax=15 punti="1,0" titolo="The function is zero only at x = 1."}

<div class="spiegazione">

The **substitution** $u=2^x$ reduces the equation to a quadratic polynomial. The constraint $u>0$ follows from positivity of every power with positive base. It is not optional, because it determines which roots of the transformed equation correspond to values of $x$.

The polynomial has two roots, but $u=-3$ cannot be a value of $2^x$. The positive root $u=2$ instead gives $x=1$. Checking in the original problem yields $4+2-6=0$. Two roots in $u$ therefore do not imply two real solutions for the original unknown.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Solve $\log_2x+\log_2(x-2)=3$.

$$
\begin{cases}
x>0,\\
x-2>0
\end{cases}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad x>2.
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
\log_2[x(x-2)]&=3,\\[6pt]
x(x-2)&=8,\\[6pt]
x^2-2x-8&=0,\\[6pt]
(x-4)(x+2)&=0.
\end{aligned}
$$
$$
x=4\ \text{or}\ x=-2,\qquad x>2\quad\Longrightarrow\quad x=4.
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Both arguments must be positive before applying the logarithm sum property. Their intersection is the half-line $x>2$. On this domain, the sum equals the logarithm of the product, and the logarithm definition allows that product to be set equal to $2^3$.

Factoring gives two candidates. The value $-2$ makes both original arguments negative, so it is excluded even though their product is positive. The value $4$ is allowed, and substitution gives $\log_2 4+\log_2 2=2+1=3$. Checking the **original domain** therefore identifies the sole valid solution.

</div>
