## Exercise 1

A sequence that has not been overturned has A below B. Fault F cuts both but is covered by undeformed C. Order the depositions and faulting. If A is 120 million years old and C is 100 million years old, which interval contains the age of movement on F?

<div class="spiegazione">

A precedes B by superposition. F follows both because it cuts them. C covers the structure without being cut, so it was deposited after the represented movement. The sequence is **A, B, F, C**.

F is younger than A and older than C, so its age lies **between 100 and 120 million years**. A larger number indicates greater age when counting years before the present. We do not know the exact movement date or the interval between deposition of B and faulting.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A closed system initially contains $80$ units of parent isotope with a half-life of $10$ million years. Find the remaining amount after $20$ million years and the fraction converted to daughter product, assuming the daughter is stable and initially absent.

$$
n=\frac{20}{10}=2,\qquad N=80\left(\frac12\right)^2=20,
$$
$$
N_{\mathrm{daughter}}=80-20=60,\qquad
\frac{N_{\mathrm{daughter}}}{80}=\frac{60}{80}=\frac34=75\%.
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

After the first half-life, $40$ parent units remain; after the second, $20$. The $60$ converted units are **$75\%$ of the initial quantity**, while the remaining parent is **$25\%$**. The count concerns nuclei or quantity units consistent with the transformation, not a claim that isotope masses are exactly equal.

A closed system, stable daughter, and initially absent daughter permit calculation of the converted amount by subtraction. If fluids added or removed isotopes, this reconstruction would not automatically be valid.

</div>

## Exercise 3

An identified deposit was not economically exploitable; a new technique makes part of it extractable profitably and in compliance with applicable criteria. Total ore underground does not change. What can change in its classification?

<div class="spiegazione">

The newly exploitable portion can be classified as a **reserve**. The geological resource need not increase for the economically usable fraction to grow. The new technology changes the relationship among costs, recovery, and obtainable product.

This distinction avoids interpreting reserve growth as physical creation of new ore. A later deterioration in economic conditions could reduce the portion classed as reserves while leaving some material underground.

</div>

## Exercise 4

From $200\,\mathrm{t}$ of ore grading $3\%$ metal, processing recovers $50\%$ of the contained metal. Determine recovered and unrecovered metal, without assuming that waste consists only of metal.

$$
m_{\mathrm{contained}}=200\cdot0.03=6\,\mathrm{t},
$$
$$
m_{\mathrm{recovered}}=6\cdot0.50=3\,\mathrm{t},
\qquad m_{\mathrm{unrecovered}}=6-3=3\,\mathrm{t}.
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Grade applies to extracted mass; recovery efficiency then applies only to contained metal. **$3\,\mathrm{t}$ of metal** are obtained and **$3\,\mathrm{t}$ remain unrecovered**. The rest of the initial material includes unwanted components, so those three tonnes are not the total waste mass.

The two stages distinguish geological information, namely grade, from processing yield. Better recovery increases output without altering the deposit's initial grade.

</div>
