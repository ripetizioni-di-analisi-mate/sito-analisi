## Exercise 1

In a model, Earth completes a uniform $360^\circ$ rotation in $24\,\mathrm{h}$. Through what angle does it turn in $6\,\mathrm{h}$? Which daily phenomenon is connected with this motion?

$$
\omega=\frac{360^\circ}{24\,\mathrm{h}}=15^\circ/\mathrm{h},
\qquad \theta=15^\circ/\mathrm{h}\cdot6\,\mathrm{h}=90^\circ.
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Angular speed is angle travelled divided by time. A quarter of a day gives a quarter turn. Rotation carries a location from the illuminated side into shadow and explains the alternation of day and night. The calculation does not claim that daylight always lasts six or twelve hours: illumination duration also depends on latitude and season. **Result: $90^\circ$; alternation of day and night.**

</div>

## Exercise 2

In June, a temperate Northern Hemisphere location has long daylight, while a temperate Southern Hemisphere location has short daylight. Explain why variation in Earth-Sun distance alone cannot account for this contrast.

<div class="spiegazione">

Distance from the Sun is essentially the same for both hemispheres at the same moment. A difference in illumination is therefore needed: the tilted axis points the Northern Hemisphere towards the Sun in June. The Sun stays above the horizon longer and its rays are generally less oblique. The other hemisphere experiences opposite conditions.

Revolution changes Earth's orbital position while the axis keeps almost the same direction. About six months later, the hemispheres exchange roles. **Axial tilt combined with revolution explains opposite seasons.** Rotation continues to produce the daily alternation between light and shadow.

</div>

## Exercise 3

In the model considered, a star is $4$ light-years away and remains at a constant distance. How long does its light take to reach us? Then distinguish a lunar eclipse configuration from a solar eclipse configuration.

$$
d=4c\cdot1\,\mathrm{year},\qquad t=\frac dc=4\,\mathrm{years}.
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

A light-year is a distance defined using a propagation time. We therefore observe light emitted **four years earlier**. Dividing length units by speed gives time; four light-years must not be interpreted as a speed.

In a lunar eclipse, Earth intercepts sunlight directed towards the Moon: the order is Sun, Earth, Moon. In a solar eclipse the Moon lies between the others, giving Sun, Moon, Earth. The figure shows the first case and only represents alignment, with radii and distances not to scale. An eclipse requires sufficiently precise alignment of the three bodies: the Moon must be near a node, where its orbit crosses Earth's orbital plane.
