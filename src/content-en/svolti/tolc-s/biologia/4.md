## Exercise 1

Net oxygen exchange is measured in an aquatic plant: a positive value means release, a negative one consumption. Respiration constantly consumes $2$ oxygen units per hour over the interval studied. At light intensities $L=0,1,3$, observed net exchanges are respectively $-2,1,5$. Calculate gross photosynthetic production for each measurement. Assuming net exchange is linear only between $L=0$ and $L=1$, find the compensation point.

$$
\begin{aligned}
P_{\text{gross}}&=P_{\text{net}}+R,\\
P_{\text{gross}}(0)&=-2+2=0,\\
P_{\text{gross}}(1)&=1+2=3,\\
P_{\text{gross}}(3)&=5+2=7,\\
P_{\text{net}}(L)&=-2+3L=0\quad\Rightarrow\quad L=\frac23.
\end{aligned}
$$

::grafico{xmin=0 xmax=3.5 ymin=-3 ymax=6 nomi="L,N" linee="0,-2 1,1;1,1 3,5" punti="0,-2,pieno;1,1,pieno;3,5,pieno;0.6666667,0,pieno" titolo="Light in arbitrary units and net oxygen exchange in units per hour; the segment after L=1 only joins measurements"}

<div class="spiegazione">

Net release is the difference between photosynthetic production and respiratory consumption. Adding consumption reconstructs gross production, giving **0, 3 and 7 units per hour**. In darkness light-driven production is zero but consumption continues.

Between the first two points, net increase is $1-(-2)=3$ per light unit. The assigned linear model gives compensation at **$L=\dfrac23$**. Net flow is zero here because production and consumption balance; both can be active. Connecting the other measurements graphically does not by itself authorize another linear law outside the stated interval.

</div>

## Exercise 2

In a model ecosystem, gross primary production is $5000$ energy units and producer respiration is $2000$. Herbivores ingest $20\%$ of net primary production, assimilate half the ingested energy and spend $200$ units on their own respiration. The remainder becomes new biomass. Calculate net primary production and herbivore secondary production.

$$
\begin{aligned}
P_{\text{net primary}}&=5000-2000=3000,\\
E_{\text{ingested}}&=0.20\cdot3000=600,\\
E_{\text{assimilated}}&=\frac12\cdot600=300,\\
P_{\text{secondary}}&=300-200=100.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

**Net primary production is 3000 units and secondary production 100 units.** The two respiration terms belong to different organisms and are subtracted at different steps. The unassimilated portion, $600-300=300$, cannot be counted as new herbivore biomass because it has not entered their usable metabolic balance.

The $20\%$ value is a model condition, not a general law. Matter in residues can support decomposers, whereas energy dissipated through respiration is not fully recycled as available energy in the chain. The balance sequence prevents applying every percentage to initial gross production.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A population starts the year with $200$ individuals. There are $50$ births, $30$ deaths, $10$ immigrants and $5$ emigrants. Calculate final size and percentage change. Is it correct to use the same percentage automatically to predict every future year?

$$
\begin{aligned}
N_f&=200+50-30+10-5=225,\\
\Delta N&=225-200=25,\\
\frac{\Delta N}{N_i}\cdot100\%&=\frac{25}{200}\cdot100\%=12.5\%.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Inputs are births and immigration; outputs are deaths and emigration. **Final population size is 225 individuals, a $12.5\%$ increase.** The percentage uses initial size as its base, not final size.

A balance observed in one year does not prove that the rate will remain constant. Resources, density, predation and migration can change. Extrapolation would require an additional model, such as constant-rate growth within a specified interval. The calculation describes the given year; it does not automatically estimate carrying capacity or indefinite exponential growth.

</div>

## Exercise 4

An insect population has two inherited colors, light and dark, initially with $100$ individuals each. After predation in a particular environment, $20$ light and $80$ dark individuals remain. Every survivor leaves the same number of viable offspring. Calculate dark frequency among survivors and explain why the result is consistent with natural selection. Compare it with a storm killing individuals randomly with respect to color.

$$
f_{\text{initial dark}}=\frac{100}{200}=\frac12,\qquad
f_{\text{surviving dark}}=\frac{80}{20+80}=\frac45=80\%.
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Dark frequency increases from $50\%$ to **$80\%$** among survivors. If color affects vulnerability to predators and the difference is inherited, the groups contribute differently to the next generation. Equal offspring numbers per survivor preserve the proportions of the two groups’ contributions, without requiring each insect to change color.

The data are consistent with selection, but counts alone do not establish the cause: a link between color and predation is needed. A storm random with respect to genotype can instead change frequencies through drift, especially with few survivors. In neither case does a changed frequency automatically demonstrate a new species.

</div>
