## Exercise 1

For three months, a municipal library extended its opening hours until ten in the evening. The final report records an increase in evening entries, while those in the morning remained stable. The counters record every entry: a person entering twice is therefore counted twice. The report measures neither time spent reading nor the distinction between new and established users.

The management considers the experiment useful for finding out whether the evening hours are used. Before deciding on a permanent extension, it wants to compare the cost of the additional hours with visits and collect information about the activities undertaken. Some visitors used the tables for group work, while others consulted books. Increased entry numbers are therefore an observed result; increased individual reading remains a question to investigate.

What does “those in the morning” refer to?

- Entries
- Counters
- New visitors
- Books consulted
- Additional costs

| Expression | Referent | Check within the sentence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Evening entries | Recorded evening entries | They increased |
| Those in the morning | Morning entries | They remained stable |
| Counters | Measuring instruments | They are introduced later |

<div class="spiegazione">

The sentence compares entries in the two time periods. “Those” therefore refers to entries, with “in the morning” specifying the period. Counters are the measuring instruments and are introduced only in the following sentence; they are not what remained stable.

The check consists in replacing the reference with the candidate noun: “morning entries remained stable” preserves the comparison between time periods. “Morning counters” would instead introduce a category of instruments absent from the passage. Semantic coherence also matters: the report is comparing entry counts. Visitors being countable more than once concerns data interpretation and does not change the pronoun’s referent. A grammatical question is therefore resolved within the sentence while preserving the text’s meaning.

**Answer.** Entries.

</div>

## Exercise 2

During a visit to neighborhood fountains, the guide reads a municipal register and then recounts a resident’s memory. The register dates one fountain’s closure to 1978; the resident recalls using it during a summer associated with 1980. The guide presents the discrepancy instead of immediately choosing one date.

The guide explains that the register documents an administrative decision, whereas the memory concerns a personal experience. An approved closure might not coincide with the water actually stopping; the remembered date might also be imprecise. To clarify the sequence, the guide proposes looking for work orders and dated photographs. The account does not put every source on the same footing for every question: it asks what each can establish and what checks are needed before turning a discrepancy into an accusation of falsehood.

What would the mentioned work orders help establish?

- The timing of physical work on the fountain
- The absolute reliability of every personal memory
- The administrative date already recorded in the register
- Why every other source should be discarded
- The guide’s aesthetic preference among fountains

| Source | Directly relevant information |
| --- | --- |
| Administrative register | Date of the closure decision |
| Resident’s memory | Recalled personal use in 1980 |
| Work orders and photographs | Evidence for reconstructing physical implementation |

<div class="spiegazione">

The uncertainty concerns the time between approved closure and actual closure. Work orders might document the physical intervention and contribute to the timeline. The other information would not clarify the relationship between the two accounts discussed in the passage.

Before treating the dates as conflicting, identify the event dated by each source. A decision can precede its implementation: that possibility makes a 1978 resolution and 1980 use compatible in principle. It does not prove that events occurred that way. Work orders and photographs would help distinguish this reconstruction from others, including an imprecise memory. The new evidence sought must concern the uncertain step rather than simply repeat a date already known.

**Answer.** The timing of physical work on the fountain.

</div>

## Exercise 3

In the novel, a ship’s voyage is told by two voices. A sailor describes maneuvers on deck as they occur. Many years later, a passenger remembers the same voyage, experienced as a child. Recalling the stormy night, she remembers a closed door and the sound of water; the sailor instead recounts repairs made outside.

The accounts do not agree in every detail. The commentator observes that this difference does not justify immediately declaring either narrator unreliable: their viewpoints and temporal distances differ. The reader thus reconstructs the event partly through what each voice could not see. The novel leaves the comparison of perspectives to the reader without introducing a third voice to settle every discrepancy definitively.

What is the function of the closed door and exterior repairs?

- They make the different observational limits concrete
- They prove the accounts concern different voyages
- They make every remembered detail certainly accurate
- They show both voices could observe the same facts
- They definitively resolve every disagreement in the novel

| Voice | Position during the voyage | Time of narration |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sailor | On deck, describes the exterior repairs | Contemporary with the events |
| Passenger | A child, recalls a closed door and the sound of water | Recollection many years later |
| Commentator | Compares the accounts | Distinct from both narrators |

<div class="spiegazione">

The details place the child and sailor in different situations during the same episode. They show why their information may differ without necessarily intentional falsehood. The passage concerns the same voyage, but does not give the two voices the same information or resolve every discrepancy.

The question concerns the details’ function, so merely repeating them is insufficient. Deck and door explain what each character could perceive; temporal distance explains another difference between accounts. These conditions allow different narratives without establishing that both are completely accurate. Declaring intentional lying would require additional evidence. The table also separates the voices: the cautious judgment belongs to the commentator and should not automatically be assigned to the sailor or passenger.

**Answer.** They make the different observational limits concrete.

</div>
