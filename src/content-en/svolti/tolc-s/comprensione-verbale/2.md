## Exercise 1

The same street appears on two neighborhood maps. The pedestrian map marks sidewalks, crossings and sections with steps. The maintenance map instead shows pipes, technical access points and paving materials. Neither reproduces every feature of the place: each selects what matters for its intended use.

A resident observes that the pedestrian map omits valve locations. The curator replies that this omission does not make it inaccurate for planning a walk, provided it does not hide obstacles relevant to pedestrians. She adds that a more detailed map can become less readable if necessary details fail to stand out. She therefore distinguishes the accuracy of the information shown from the total amount of information: good selection reduces content without distorting what must guide route choice.

Which two properties are distinguished in the final part?

- Accuracy of information and amount of information
- Completeness of information and map price
- Pipe precision and maintenance safety
- Number of pedestrians and number of symbols
- Neighborhood size and name legibility

| Aspect | Relevant question |
| --- | --- |
| Amount of detail | How much information is displayed? |
| Accuracy | Is the selected information correct? |
| Fitness for purpose | Does the information help the intended user? |

<div class="spiegazione">

The curator separates the precision of what is represented from the total number of details. A selection can be limited and accurate for its purpose. Adding details alone does not guarantee a better guide and may make necessary ones harder to identify.

A pedestrian map can omit valve locations while still representing crossings and obstacles correctly. Omission therefore does not automatically make the displayed information false. Conversely, a richly detailed map could place a crossing incorrectly: quantity and accuracy do not coincide in the other direction either. The answer preserves the curator’s distinction and relates it to use. It does not attribute to the text the idea that every omission is harmless: hiding an obstacle relevant to pedestrians would violate the stated criterion.

**Answer.** Accuracy of information and amount of information.

</div>

## Exercise 2

During restoration of a mosaic, some tiles are missing along its border. The team could reconstruct the design by perfectly imitating ancient materials, but chooses a slightly different shade for the additions. From a distance the pattern remains readable; close up, visitors can distinguish what has been added.

A note explains that the work should support understanding of the piece without presenting recent material as ancient. The different color does not signal a temporary repair: it makes the intervention recognizable. Where the original design cannot be reconstructed with sufficient confidence, the team leaves a neutral surface. It therefore does not treat every gap as an invitation to invent the missing pattern. The result combines visual continuity with transparency about the intervention, accepting that some parts of the work’s history remain unknown.

Which goals are combined?

- Readability of the pattern and recognizability of additions
- Material uniformity and invisible additions
- Readability at close range and an unrecognizable distant pattern
- Complete reconstruction and certainty about every detail
- Preservation of every gap and abandonment of visual continuity

| Restoration choice | Intended effect |
| --- | --- |
| Reconstruct the missing border | Make the whole readable |
| Use a slightly different shade | Identify the addition at close range |
| Remain neutral in uncertain areas | Avoid invented details |

<div class="spiegazione">

The first paragraph distinguishes viewing from a distance and close inspection. The conclusion restates these as visual continuity and transparency. The correct summary preserves both, whereas the alternatives remove one aim or attribute a completeness the text does not promise.

The summary must include two aims the design seeks to satisfy together. Image continuity concerns viewing from a distance; identifying the intervention concerns close observation. There is no contradiction because the viewing conditions differ. A title addressing color alone would lose the reconstruction’s function. A summary describing indistinguishable imitation would erase transparency about the intervention. Finally, limited knowledge explains why reconstruction does not freely extend to every missing detail.

**Answer.** Readability of the pattern and recognizability of additions.

</div>

## Exercise 3

To renovate a museum entrance, an initial design places a ramp along a secondary route. A group of visitors observes that the solution overcomes the steps but forces some people to separate from their companions and use a less recognizable entrance. The designers therefore revise the plan, incorporating the step-free route into the main entrance.

The final report distinguishes the physical possibility of entering from the quality of the access experience. It does not argue that every difference in route is always unacceptable: different buildings may have different constraints. In this case, however, consultation with users revealed a problem that checking gradients alone had not identified. The ramp remains necessary, but its position changes. The example illustrates that a measurable requirement can be indispensable without covering every aspect of a good solution.

Which general claim does the example illustrate?

- Meeting a measurable requirement may be insufficient to solve a problem well
- Every measurable requirement should be ignored
- User opinions always make projects impossible
- Quality is identical in every building
- A secondary entrance is always technically impossible

| Reasoning level | Content |
| --- | --- |
| Case | Ramp placed on a secondary route |
| Identified limitation | Separation from companions and an unclear entrance |
| Revision | Step-free route incorporated into the main entrance |
| Claim | A measurable requirement may not exhaust a good solution |

<div class="spiegazione">

The final sentence explicitly states the claim: a requirement can be indispensable yet not exhaust the problem. The ramp case makes this concrete. The alternatives turn this combination of criteria into absolute rejections or absent universal laws.

The claim is not identical to the detail “the ramp was moved”: that detail is the concrete application. The general step concerns the relationship between a partial check and a broader assessment. The passage retains the usefulness of measuring gradient while also considering visitors’ experience. An answer rejecting all technical measurements is therefore as excessive as one treating them as always sufficient. The qualification about different buildings limits generalizing the design without erasing the example’s value.

**Answer.** Meeting a measurable requirement may be insufficient to solve a problem well.

</div>
