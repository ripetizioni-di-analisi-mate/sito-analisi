## Exercise 1

A laboratory offers two optional courses. Its report distinguishes enrollment numbers from the number of students completing every required activity. Each enrolled student appears in only one row.

| Course | Enrolled | Completions |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Short | 40 | 30 |
| Extended | 100 | 60 |

The director observes that the extended course produces more completions in absolute terms, while the short course has a higher proportion relative to its enrollment. She adds that students chose their course independently and that the programs demand different commitments. The table describes observed outcomes but does not prove that moving the same students between courses would improve results. Assessing that effect would require a comparison designed to distinguish the course’s influence from the characteristics of those choosing it.

Which fraction represents the overall completion proportion?

- $\dfrac{90}{140}$
- $\dfrac{135}{200}$
- $\dfrac{30}{100}$
- $\dfrac{60}{40}$
- $\dfrac{90}{100}$

$$
\begin{aligned}
N&=40+100=140,\\[6pt]
C&=30+60=90,\\[6pt]
\text{overall proportion}&=\frac{C}{N}=\frac{90}{140}=\frac{9}{14}.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Total completions are 30 plus 60, or 90; total enrollment is 40 plus 100, or 140. The overall proportion is therefore $\dfrac{90}{140}$. A simple average of 75% and 60% would give equal weight to differently sized groups.

Here N denotes all enrolled students and C all completions. Every student belongs to one row only, so the sums do not duplicate people. A simple average of the two percentages would give equal weight to forty and one hundred students; the ratio of totals instead gives each student equal weight. A separate check adds non-completions: ten in the short course and forty in the extended course, totaling fifty. Since ninety plus fifty equals one hundred forty, the count accounts for the entire group.

**Answer.** $\dfrac{90}{140}$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A transport service offers two monthly fare options. Under Flex there is no fixed charge and each journey costs two euros. Under Plan there is a twenty-euro monthly charge plus one euro per journey. There are no other charges or discounts; every journey considered is eligible under both options.

| Fare | Monthly charge | Cost per journey |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Flex | 0 euros | 2 euros |
| Plan | 20 euros | 1 euro |

An advertising brochure calls Plan “the cheapest choice for everyone.” A consultant observes that value depends on the number of journeys made that month: the lower price per journey is insufficient to compare total spending. For someone whose travel varies, she also recommends comparing several plausible scenarios rather than treating one forecast as certain.

Which case is enough to refute “Plan is cheaper for everyone”?

- Someone making five journeys: Flex costs 10 euros and Plan 25
- Someone making thirty journeys: Flex costs 60 euros and Plan 50
- Someone making forty journeys: Flex costs 80 euros and Plan 60
- Someone making fifty journeys: Flex costs 100 euros and Plan 70
- Someone making one hundred journeys: Flex costs 200 euros and Plan 120

$$
\begin{aligned}
L(5)&=2\cdot5=10\,\mathrm{euros},\\[6pt]
P(5)&=20+1\cdot5=25\,\mathrm{euros},\\[6pt]
25&>10.
\end{aligned}
$$

::grafico{f="2*x;20+x" xmin="0" xmax="40" ymin="0" ymax="85" nomi="n,C" punti="5,10;5,25;20,40" legenda="L=2n;P=20+n" titolo="Journeys n and cost C in euros: at five journeys Plan costs more; at twenty the costs coincide."}

<div class="spiegazione">

Refuting “for everyone” requires only one user for whom Plan is not cheaper. With five journeys, Plan costs twenty-five euros against ten. In the other proposed cases Plan is cheaper, so those cases alone do not refute the advertisement.

L and P denote the costs of Flex and Plan, while n is the number of journeys. The advertisement makes a universal claim: it asserts an advantage for every user. Refuting it does not require showing that Plan is always worse; one user for whom it is not cheaper suffices. Five journeys provide that case. Cases involving many journeys instead show that Plan can be advantageous: they are compatible with the counterexample and explain why the correct conclusion must be limited to the advertising claim.

**Answer.** Someone making five journeys: Flex costs 10 euros and Plan 25.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A research group uses a model to estimate three buildings’ water consumption in the following month. The table gives a central estimate and a plausible interval under the conditions considered. The endpoints are not presented as absolutely impossible to exceed.

| Building | Central estimate, cubic meters | Plausible interval, cubic meters |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| A | 100 | 90–110 |
| B | 105 | 80–130 |
| C | 95 | 92–98 |

The report explains that a higher central value does not imply that actual consumption will certainly be higher. The intervals describe model uncertainty, not extra quantities to add to the estimates. If building usage hours change substantially, the forecasts must be updated. After the observed month, comparison with measured consumption will help assess the model; one error alone does not prove that all its predictions are useless.

What can be stated when comparing B and A?

- B has a higher central estimate, but this does not make its actual consumption certainly higher
- B will certainly consume more than A
- A will certainly consume more than B
- Actual consumption will necessarily be equal
- B has both a lower central estimate and a smaller interval width

| Comparison | Result |
| --- | --- |
| Central estimates | B: 105; A: 100 |
| One outcome compatible with the intervals | B: 90; A: 105 |
| Ordering in the proposed outcome | B consumes less than A |
| Conclusion | Estimate rankings do not guarantee actual rankings |

<div class="spiegazione">

B’s central estimate is 105 against 100, but the intervals overlap and the report rules out a certain ordering of actual values. Confusing estimate rankings with certainty about outcomes erases the stated uncertainty.

The value 90 lies within B’s interval, from 80 to 130; 105 lies within A’s, from 90 to 110. This pair concretely illustrates an ordering opposite to the central estimates. It is not a new prediction and does not say the pair is most likely. It checks the word “certainly”: the passage provides no ordering guarantee. Moreover, the intervals are plausible ranges, not absolute barriers; the answer must also respect that limitation of the representation.

**Answer.** B has a higher central estimate, but this does not make its actual consumption certainly higher.

</div>
