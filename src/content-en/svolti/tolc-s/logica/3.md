## Exercise 1

In a group of $40$ people, $22$ study English, $18$ French and $7$ both languages. How many study neither?

- $7$
- $0$
- $14$
- $33$
- $15$

$$
\begin{aligned}
|I\cup F|&=22+18-7=33,\\[6pt]
N-|I\cup F|&=40-33=7.
\end{aligned}
$$

::venn{tipo="due" nomi="I,F" universo="1" elementi="a:15;ab:7;b:11;u:7" titolo="The four regions of the group of 40 people"}

<div class="spiegazione">

**Answer.** $7$.

The sum $22+18$ counts the seven people shared by the two courses twice. The union therefore contains $22+18-7=33$ people. The whole group contains $40$, so the complement of the union contains $40-33=7$ people. This counts those studying neither language, rather than those studying exactly one.

The division into four regions provides another check. English only contains $22-7=15$ people, and French only $18-7=11$. Adding the seven shared and seven outside gives $15+11+7+7=40$, matching the total. Labels I and F in the diagram identify the two languages and introduce no new groups.

</div>

## Exercise 2

For three courses, the A-B, A-C and B-C intersections contain $6$, $5$ and $7$ people, including $2$ enrolled in all three. How many people attend exactly two courses?

- $12$
- $18$
- $16$
- $14$
- $6$

$$
\begin{aligned}
|AB\text{ only}|&=6-2=4,\\
|AC\text{ only}|&=5-2=3,\\
|BC\text{ only}|&=7-2=5,\\[6pt]
N_2&=4+3+5=12.
\end{aligned}
$$

::venn{tipo="tre" nomi="A,B,C" zone="ab,ac,bc" elementi="ab:4;ac:3;bc:5;abc:2" titolo="Only membership in exactly two courses is shaded"}

<div class="spiegazione">

**Answer.** $12$.

Remove the two people shared by all three courses from each pairwise intersection. The remaining regions contain $6-2=4$, $5-2=3$ and $7-2=5$ people. These regions are disjoint, so their counts add: $4+3+5=12$. Subtracting the two people only once would leave unwanted copies in the counts.

In the formula, $N_2$ counts only those attending two courses. The diagram separates the three double regions from the central triple region; the outer-region counts are unspecified and unnecessary. The sum of pairwise intersections is $18$ and contains three copies of each of the two central people. Removing every such copy also gives $18-3\cdot2=12$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

In a class of $30$ students, $19$ study French and $16$ German. What is the smallest possible number studying both languages?

- $5$
- $0$
- $3$
- $16$
- $19$

$$
\begin{aligned}
|F\cap T|&=|F|+|T|-|F\cup T|,\\
|F\cup T|&\le30,\\[6pt]
|F\cap T|&\ge19+16-30=5.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

**Answer.** $5$.

The two counts total $35$ enrollments among only $30$ people, so at least $5$ must be counted twice. The bound is attainable with $14$ French-only students, $11$ German-only students and $5$ studying both, totaling $30$. This construction shows that $5$ is an actual minimum.

The union’s bound comes from the class size. Proving only that the intersection cannot be smaller than five would give a lower bound without yet proving that five is attainable. The distribution in the explanation completes this second step. All counts are nonnegative and reconstruct both groups and the total, so the configuration is valid.

</div>
