## Exercise 1

Three-digit numbers with distinct digits are formed from $0,1,2,3,4$. How many are there? How many have digits in strictly increasing order from left to right?

$$
N=4\cdot4\cdot3=48.
$$
$$
N_{\mathrm{increasing}}=\binom43=4.
$$
$$
\text{Increasing numbers: }123,124,134,234.
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The first digit cannot be zero, leaving four possibilities. The second still has four choices: it cannot repeat the first but may be zero. Three choices remain for the third digit. The positions have different roles, so the **product rule** applies without division by a factorial.

In the increasing case, zero cannot appear: it would necessarily be the first digit, and the result would not be a three-digit number. Therefore choose three digits from $1,2,3,4$. Each choice has exactly one increasing order, so a combination is sufficient. The explicit list confirms the count and shows why ordering the digits again would multiply cases unnecessarily.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Determine how many distinct arrangements can be formed from SASSO and how many begin and end with S. Meaningless strings are allowed.

$$
N=\frac{5!}{3!}=20.
$$
$$
N_{\mathrm{S\ldots S}}=3!=6.
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Five positions would allow $5!$ arrangements if all letters were distinct. However, the three S letters are **indistinguishable**: permuting them always produces the same string. Every arrangement is therefore counted $3!$ times, and division gives the correct number. A and O each occur only once and introduce no further divisors.

With one S fixed at the beginning and one at the end, the three internal positions contain S, A and O, all different. These three letters can be arranged in $3!$ ways. There is no multiplication for choosing which S occupies the endpoints, because the S letters have no distinguishable identities. The six allowed cases form a subset of the twenty total arrangements.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Three people are chosen from four women and three men. How many groups contain at least one woman and at least one man? How many remain if a specified woman and a specified man cannot both be selected?

$$
N=\binom73-\binom43-\binom33=35-4-1=30.
$$
$$
N_{\mathrm{forbidden}}=5,\qquad N_{\mathrm{allowed}}=30-5=25.
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

A group has no internal roles, so the order of its members does not matter. From all groups of three, exclude those consisting only of women and those consisting only of men. These excluded types are disjoint: no group of three can belong to both. **Counting by complement** therefore gives thirty mixed groups.

For the second restriction, start from these thirty. A forbidden group already contains the two specified people; its third member can be any of the other five. All such groups are mixed, so they already occur in the starting total and can each be subtracted once. Twenty-five groups remain. The condition permits selecting only one of the specified people or neither of them.

</div>
