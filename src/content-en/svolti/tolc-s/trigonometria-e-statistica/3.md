## Exercise 1

The values $1,2,5$ have frequencies $2,4,4$. Calculate the mean, median, mode and descriptive variance using divisor $N$.

$$
N=2+4+4=10,\qquad
\bar x=\frac{2\cdot1+4\cdot2+4\cdot5}{10}=3.
$$
$$
x_{(5)}=x_{(6)}=2,\qquad
\text{median}=2,\qquad \text{modes}=2\ \text{and}\ 5.
$$
$$
\sigma^2=\frac{2(1-3)^2+4(2-3)^2+4(5-3)^2}{10}
=\frac{8+4+16}{10}=\frac{14}{5}.
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The mean must include every repetition: multiply values by their frequencies, whose sum is the total number of observations. In the sorted list, positions one and two contain $1$, positions three through six contain $2$, and the last four contain $5$. Thus both central positions contain $2$.

The highest frequency is $4$, shared by two values: the distribution has **two modes**. For the variance, calculate deviations from the mean $3$, square them and again weight them by their frequencies. The divisor is $10$, as required by the descriptive convention. The mean, median and modes need not coincide: they measure different features of the data.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A first group of $12$ observations has mean $15$. A second group contains $8$ observations. Their combined mean is $17$. Determine the second group’s mean and the new combined mean after adding $3$ to all twenty observations.

$$
12\cdot15+8m=20\cdot17
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad180+8m=340.
$$
$$
8m=160\quad\Longrightarrow\quad m=20.
$$
$$
\bar y=\frac{340+20\cdot3}{20}=20.
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

A mean multiplied by the group size recovers the **group sum**. The combined sum is $20\cdot17$, and the first group’s sum is known. Their difference must be distributed across the eight observations in the second group. Its mean of $20$ exceeds $17$, balancing the first group’s mean of $15$.

Adding $3$ to every observation adds $3$ twenty times to the sum. Dividing the new sum by the unchanged size increases the mean by exactly $3$. The new combined mean is $20$; it numerically equals the old second-group mean but concerns different data and operations. Deviations from the respective mean remain unchanged, so this translation does not change the variance.

</div>

## Exercise 3

An absolute-frequency histogram has classes $[0,2)$, $[2,7)$, $[7,11)$ with densities $8,4,3$. Determine the total, the fraction of observations below $7$ and an estimate of the mean using class midpoints.

$$
n_1=2\cdot8=16,\qquad n_2=5\cdot4=20,\qquad n_3=4\cdot3=12.
$$
$$
N=16+20+12=48,\qquad f_{x<7}=\frac{16+20}{48}=\frac34.
$$
$$
\bar x_{\mathrm{estimated}}=
\frac{16\cdot1+20\cdot\frac92+12\cdot9}{48}
=\frac{214}{48}=\frac{107}{24}.
$$

::grafico{poligoni="0,0 2,0 2,8 0,8;2,0 7,0 7,4 2,4;7,0 11,0 11,3 7,3" xmin=-1 xmax=12 ymin=-1 ymax=9 nomi="x,d" titolo="Area, rather than height alone, recovers the frequency of each class."}

<div class="spiegazione">

The classes have unequal widths, so their frequencies come from the rectangle **areas**. The total is the sum of the three areas. Values below $7$ lie in the first two classes; the endpoint $7$ already belongs to the third and is excluded from the numerator. Thus the required fraction is exact even though individual observations are unavailable.

To estimate the mean, replace every observation in a class by its midpoint: $1$, $\dfrac92$ or $9$. Then apply a weighted mean using the recovered frequencies. The result is an **estimate**, because two different datasets can produce the same histogram yet have different means. The display preserves counts within classes, not each observation’s position.

</div>
