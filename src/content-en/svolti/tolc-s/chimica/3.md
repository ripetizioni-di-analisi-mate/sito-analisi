## Exercise 1: Diluting a solution

A $100\,\mathrm{mL}$ portion of a $1.0\,\mathrm{mol/L}$ solution is taken to a final volume of $500\,\mathrm{mL}$ without losing solute. What is the final concentration?

- $0.20\,\mathrm{mol/L}$
- $5.0\,\mathrm{mol/L}$
- $1.0\,\mathrm{mol/L}$
- $0.50\,\mathrm{mol/L}$
- $0.020\,\mathrm{mol/L}$

<div class="spiegazione">

**Answer.** $0.20\,\mathrm{mol/L}$

The initial amount is $1.0\cdot0.100=0.100\,\mathrm{mol}$ and remains constant. The final volume is $0.500\,\mathrm L$, so $c_f=\dfrac{0.100}{0.500}=0.20\,\mathrm{mol/L}$. Volume increases fivefold and concentration falls to one fifth. Five hundred milliliters is the final volume, not the volume of added solvent.

$$
\begin{aligned}n&=1.0\cdot0.100=0.100\,\mathrm{mol},\\[6pt]c_f&=\frac{0.100}{0.500}=0.20\,\mathrm{mol/L}.\end{aligned}
$$

Dilution increases the volume available to the same amount of solute. The first step determines how many moles are in the portion taken; the second distributes them over the final volume. To use liters and moles in the formula, milliliters are divided by one thousand. The curve shows the inverse relationship between concentration and volume at fixed amount: initial and final points lie on the same curve because concentration times volume is constant.

</div>

## Exercise 2: Comparing osmotic pressures

At equal temperature and molarity, which dilute ideal solution has the greatest osmotic pressure, assuming complete salt dissociation?

- $\mathrm{CaCl_2}$
- Glucose
- Urea
- $\mathrm{NaCl}$
- Sucrose

<div class="spiegazione">

**Answer.** $\mathrm{CaCl_2}$

In the ideal model, $\pi=icRT$. Calcium chloride produces three ions per formula unit, NaCl produces two, and the listed molecular solutes remain one particle per molecule. Since $c$ and $T$ match, the largest factor $i=3$ gives the greatest osmotic pressure. The comparison concerns effective particles, not molar masses.

$$
i_{\mathrm{glucose}}=1,\quad i_{\mathrm{NaCl}}=2,\quad i_{\mathrm{CaCl_2}}=3,\qquad\pi=icRT.
$$

Initial molar concentrations count the introduced solute units, but osmotic pressure depends on the particles actually present. One mole of fully dissociated calcium chloride gives one mole of cations and two of anions. The three moles of particles explain the larger factor. Ideal solution and complete dissociation assumptions are necessary: at high concentrations, ion interactions can make this simple count insufficient.

</div>

## Exercise 3: Buffer composition

Which pair, present in appreciable amounts, can form an aqueous buffer?

- Acetic acid and sodium acetate
- HCl and NaCl
- NaOH and NaCl
- Water and glucose
- Ethanol and water

<div class="spiegazione">

**Answer.** Acetic acid and sodium acetate

Acetic acid is weak and acetate is its conjugate base. Together they provide one species able to consume small base additions and one able to consume small acid additions. HCl with NaCl does not provide an effective weak-acid/conjugate-base pair; the proposed neutral solutes do not perform this function.

$$
\mathrm{CH_3COOH\rightleftharpoons H^++CH_3COO^-}.
$$

The pair must contain species related by one proton transfer and retain usable amounts of both. With a small acid addition, acetate can become acetic acid; with a small base addition, acetic acid can donate protons and become acetate. These changes limit variation in free hydronium. The salt supplies the conjugate base: simply combining any acid with any salt does not guarantee a buffer.

</div>
