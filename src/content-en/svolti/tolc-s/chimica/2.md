## Exercise 1: From mass to moles

How many moles of water molecules are present in $36\,\mathrm g$? Take $M(\mathrm{H_2O})=18\,\mathrm{g/mol}$.

- $2\,\mathrm{mol}$
- $18\,\mathrm{mol}$
- $0.5\,\mathrm{mol}$
- $648\,\mathrm{mol}$
- $36\,\mathrm{mol}$

<div class="spiegazione">

**Answer.** $2\,\mathrm{mol}$

Molar mass states that each mole of molecules has a mass of $18\,\mathrm g$. Dividing the available mass by the mass of one mole gives $n=\dfrac{36}{18}=2\,\mathrm{mol}$. The result counts water molecules; the total amount of atoms would be six moles because each molecule contains three atoms.

$$
n(\mathrm{H_2O})=\frac{36\,\mathrm g}{18\,\mathrm{g/mol}}=2\,\mathrm{mol}.
$$

The conversion requires division because molar mass states how many grams correspond to one mole. Gram units cancel and moles remain, as required. After calculation, the identity of the counted entity must be retained: here it is water molecules. Counting oxygen atoms would still give two moles; counting hydrogen atoms would give twice as many. The chemical formula enters only when the counted entity changes.

</div>

## Exercise 2: Product mass and reactant consumed

The reaction $2\mathrm{Mg}+\mathrm{O_2}\to2\mathrm{MgO}$ produces $10\,\mathrm g$ of MgO. With $M(\mathrm{MgO})=40\,\mathrm{g/mol}$, how many moles of oxygen were consumed?

- $0.125\,\mathrm{mol}$
- $0.25\,\mathrm{mol}$
- $0.50\,\mathrm{mol}$
- $2\,\mathrm{mol}$
- $5\,\mathrm{mol}$

<div class="spiegazione">

**Answer.** $0.125\,\mathrm{mol}$

The product mass corresponds to $n(\mathrm{MgO})=\dfrac{10}{40}=0.25\,\mathrm{mol}$. The equation requires one mole of $\mathrm{O_2}$ for every two moles of MgO, so $n(\mathrm{O_2})=\dfrac{0.25}{2}=0.125\,\mathrm{mol}$. The factor of two arises from diatomic oxygen and is already represented by the balanced coefficients.

$$
\begin{aligned}n(\mathrm{MgO})&=\frac{10}{40}=0.25\,\mathrm{mol},\\[6pt]n(\mathrm{O_2})&=\frac12n(\mathrm{MgO})=0.125\,\mathrm{mol}.\end{aligned}
$$

The initial datum is product mass, whereas reaction coefficients connect amounts in moles. The first conversion therefore puts MgO mass into the quantity to which the stoichiometric ratio applies. Each oxygen molecule supplies two oxygen atoms, which enter two MgO units. The result is therefore half the product amount. Treating coefficients as ratios between grams would skip the necessary conversion.

</div>

## Exercise 3: Identifying the limiting reactant

For $\mathrm{N_2+3H_2\to2NH_3}$, $2\,\mathrm{mol}$ of nitrogen and $3\,\mathrm{mol}$ of hydrogen are introduced. Which reactant limits the maximum amount of ammonia?

- Hydrogen, because its initial amount divided by its coefficient is smaller
- Nitrogen, because it initially has fewer moles
- Both, because there are two reactants
- Nitrogen, because its molar mass is greater
- Neither, because all reactants are gases

<div class="spiegazione">

**Answer.** Hydrogen, because its initial amount divided by its coefficient is smaller

Compare the amounts divided by their coefficients: $\dfrac21=2$ for nitrogen and $\dfrac33=1$ for hydrogen. The smaller ratio belongs to hydrogen, which is consumed first. One mole of nitrogen reacts with the three moles of hydrogen; the other mole of nitrogen remains. Comparing initial amounts alone would be insufficient.

$$
\begin{aligned}\frac{n(\mathrm{N_2})}{1}&=2,\qquad\frac{n(\mathrm{H_2})}{3}=1,\\[6pt]n(\mathrm{NH_3})&=2\cdot1=2\,\mathrm{mol},\\[6pt]n_{\mathrm{N_2,remaining}}&=2-1=1\,\mathrm{mol}.\end{aligned}
$$

The comparison is made after normalizing amounts by coefficients. A larger amount in moles can still be insufficient if the reaction requires it in a greater proportion. Here hydrogen is enough to consume only one of the two nitrogen moles. The graph fixes two initial moles of nitrogen and shows the maximum ammonia amount as hydrogen varies. Beyond six hydrogen moles, nitrogen would become the limiting factor.

</div>
