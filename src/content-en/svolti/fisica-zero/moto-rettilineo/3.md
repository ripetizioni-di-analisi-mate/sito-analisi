## Exercise 1

Convert $54\,\mathrm{km/h}$ to meters per second and $7.5\,\mathrm{m/s}$ to kilometers per hour.

$$
\begin{aligned}54\,\mathrm{km/h}&=54\cdot\frac{1000\,\mathrm{m}}{3600\,\mathrm{s}}\\[6pt]&=15\,\mathrm{m/s}\\[6pt]7.5\,\mathrm{m/s}&=7.5\cdot3.6\,\mathrm{km/h}\\[6pt]&=27\,\mathrm{km/h}\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The first conversion changes kilometers to meters and hours to seconds at the same time. The numerical factor is $\dfrac{1000}{3600}$, which is equivalent to dividing by $3.6$ and gives $15\,\mathrm{m/s}$. The reverse conversion uses multiplication by $3.6$. Since $7.5\cdot3.6=27$, the second speed is $27\,\mathrm{km/h}$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A vehicle travels along a straight road at a constant speed of $90\,\mathrm{km/h}$. Calculate the distance it travels in $6\,\mathrm{s}$, neglecting the stages when the vehicle starts and stops.

$$
\begin{aligned}v&=\frac{90}{3.6}\,\mathrm{m/s}=25\,\mathrm{m/s}\\[6pt]s&=v\Delta t\\[6pt]&=25\,\mathrm{m/s}\cdot6\,\mathrm{s}\\[6pt]&=150\,\mathrm{m}\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The time is given in seconds, so we convert the speed to meters per second. The velocity is constant and the direction stays the same, so the distance is the speed multiplied by the elapsed time. In one second the vehicle travels $25\,\mathrm{m}$, and in six seconds it travels $150\,\mathrm{m}$. The model in the problem excludes the periods when the velocity changes.

</div>
