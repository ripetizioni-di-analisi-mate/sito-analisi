## Exercise 1

Calculate the heat needed to turn $0.050\,\mathrm{kg}$ of ice at $0\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ into water at $20\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ at standard atmospheric pressure. Assume $L_f=334\,000\,\mathrm{J/kg}$, $c_a=4180\,\mathrm{J/(kg\cdot K)}$, and no heat losses.

$$
\begin{aligned}
Q_{\mathrm{tot}}&=mL_f+mc_a\Delta T\\[6pt]
Q_{\mathrm{fus}}&=0.050\cdot334\,000\,\mathrm{J}=16\,700\,\mathrm{J}\\[6pt]
Q_{\mathrm{risc}}&=0.050\cdot4180\cdot20\,\mathrm{J}=4180\,\mathrm{J}\\[6pt]
Q_{\mathrm{tot}}&=(16\,700+4180)\,\mathrm{J}\\[6pt]
&=20\,880\,\mathrm{J}.
\end{aligned}
$$
::grafico{linee="0,0 16.7,0 20.88,20" xmin=0 xmax=25 ymin=-5 ymax=25 nomi="Q,t" titolo="Melting requires $16.7\,\mathrm{kJ}$ at constant temperature. Heating the water requires an additional $4.18\,\mathrm{kJ}$. The $Q$ axis is in kilojoules and the temperature is in degrees Celsius"}

<div class="spiegazione">

The process has two stages, so calculate the energy required for each separately. During **melting**, the temperature stays constant and $Q=mL_f$ applies. Once melting is complete, the entire sample is liquid, so $Q=mc_a\Delta T$ applies with a temperature rise of $20\,\mathrm{K}$.

The mass is the same in both stages. Both contributions are positive because the sample absorbs energy, so they are added. The horizontal segment of the graph represents melting. The rising segment represents heating the liquid water.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A sample of $0.020\,\mathrm{kg}$ of water at $20\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ is converted entirely into vapor at $100\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ at standard atmospheric pressure. Calculate the heat absorbed using $c_a=4180\,\mathrm{J/(kg\cdot K)}$ and $L_v=2\,260\,000\,\mathrm{J/kg}$, assuming no heat losses.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta T&=(100-20)\,\mathrm{K}=80\,\mathrm{K}\\[6pt]
Q_{\mathrm{tot}}&=mc_a\Delta T+mL_v\\[6pt]
Q_{\mathrm{risc}}&=0.020\cdot4180\cdot80\,\mathrm{J}=6688\,\mathrm{J}\\[6pt]
Q_{\mathrm{vap}}&=0.020\cdot2\,260\,000\,\mathrm{J}=45\,200\,\mathrm{J}\\[6pt]
Q_{\mathrm{tot}}&=(6688+45\,200)\,\mathrm{J}\\[6pt]
&=51\,888\,\mathrm{J}\approx51.9\,\mathrm{kJ}.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The water must first reach its boiling point. It remains liquid during this stage, so the **specific heat equation** applies with a temperature change of $80\,\mathrm{K}$. It then changes from liquid to vapor at a constant temperature of $100\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$, so the latent heat of vaporization applies.

The total heat is the sum of $6688\,\mathrm{J}$ and $45\,200\,\mathrm{J}$. The vapor is already at the required final temperature, so no additional term for heating the vapor is needed. Rounded to three significant figures, the result is $51.9\,\mathrm{kJ}$.

</div>
