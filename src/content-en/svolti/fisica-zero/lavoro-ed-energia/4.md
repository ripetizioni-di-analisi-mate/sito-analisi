## Exercise 1

A body of mass $2\,\mathrm{kg}$ initially moves at $2\,\mathrm{m/s}$. The forces acting on it do a total of $32\,\mathrm{J}$ of work. Calculate the final speed.

$$
\begin{aligned}K_i&=\frac{1}{2}mv_i^2=4\,\mathrm{J},\\[6pt]K_f&=K_i+W_{\mathrm{tot}}\\[6pt]&=4+32=36\,\mathrm{J},\\[6pt]v_f&=\sqrt{\frac{2K_f}{m}}\\[6pt]&=\sqrt{\frac{2\cdot36}{2}}\,\mathrm{m/s}=6\,\mathrm{m/s}.\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The **work–energy theorem** gives the net work as the increase in kinetic energy. The initial energy is $4\,\mathrm{J}$, so after $32\,\mathrm{J}$ of positive work it becomes $36\,\mathrm{J}$. The kinetic energy formula gives the final speed. It is greater than the initial speed, as expected for positive work.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A body of mass $4\,\mathrm{kg}$ slides on a horizontal surface with initial speed $6\,\mathrm{m/s}$. A constant resistive force of $9\,\mathrm{N}$ is the only force doing work. Calculate the stopping distance.

$$
\begin{aligned}-fs&=K_f-K_i,\\[6pt]-fs&=-\frac{1}{2}mv_i^2,\\[6pt]s&=\frac{mv_i^2}{2f}\\[6pt]&=\frac{4\cdot6^2}{2\cdot9}\,\mathrm{m}\\[6pt]&=\frac{144}{18}\,\mathrm{m}=8\,\mathrm{m}.\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

When the body stops, its final kinetic energy is zero. The resistive force acts opposite to the displacement, so it does work $-fs$. Setting this work equal to the change in kinetic energy gives the formula for the distance. The negative signs on the two sides cancel, and the resulting distance is positive. The formula applies to the part of the motion up to the first stop.

</div>
