## Exercise 1

A liquid sample has a mass of $0.90\,\mathrm{kg}$ and a volume of $1.2\,\mathrm{L}$. Calculate its density in $\mathrm{kg/m^3}$.

$$
\begin{aligned}V&=1.2\cdot0.001\,\mathrm{m^3}\\[6pt]&=0.0012\,\mathrm{m^3},\\[6pt]\rho&=\frac{m}{V}\\[6pt]&=\frac{0.90}{0.0012}\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}\\[6pt]&=750\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}.\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

One liter equals $0.001\,\mathrm{m^3}$, so $1.2\,\mathrm{L}$ equals $0.0012\,\mathrm{m^3}$. Dividing mass by volume gives the **density**. The result means that one cubic meter of the same liquid under the same conditions would have a mass of $750\,\mathrm{kg}$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A bottle contains $0.75\,\mathrm{L}$ of oil with a density of $800\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}$. Calculate the mass of the oil, excluding the bottle.

$$
\begin{aligned}V&=0.00075\,\mathrm{m^3},\\[6pt]m&=\rho V\\[6pt]&=800\cdot0.00075\,\mathrm{kg}\\[6pt]&=0.60\,\mathrm{kg}.\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Starting from the definition $\rho=m/V$, multiplying by $V$ gives $m=\rho V$. The volume must be in cubic meters because the density is expressed in kilograms per cubic meter. The calculated mass is that of the oil alone. The given volume and density exclude the container.

</div>
