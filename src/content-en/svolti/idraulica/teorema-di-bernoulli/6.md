## Exercise 1

The two constant-level reservoirs shown are connected by a pipe of diameter $D = 200\,\mathrm{mm}$. Their surface elevations differ by $\Delta z = 12.00\,\mathrm{m}$. The total head loss, including friction and local losses, is $\Delta H = 40\,\dfrac{v^2}{2g}$. Find the speed and discharge.

::schema{acqua="0.2,7 2.4,7 2.4,13 0.2,13;15,0.6 18.4,0.6 18.4,4.4 15,4.4" solidi="0,6.4 2.6,6.4 2.6,7 0,7;0,6.4 0.2,6.4 0.2,13.6 0,13.6;2.4,7 2.6,7 2.6,8.65 2.4,8.65;2.4,9.35 2.6,9.35 2.6,13.6 2.4,13.6;14.8,0 18.6,0 18.6,0.6 14.8,0.6;14.8,0.6 15,0.6 15,2.65 14.8,2.65;14.8,3.35 15,3.35 15,5 14.8,5;18.4,0 18.6,0 18.6,5 18.4,5" pelo="0.2,2.4,13;15,18.4,4.4" condotte="2.6 9-9 9-15 3,0.7" quote="17 4.4,17 13,Δz = 12.00 m,0.9" etichette="1.3,13.5,A;16.3,4.9,B;5.8,9.9,D = 200 mm" riferimento=0 titolo="Pipe connecting two reservoirs with a difference in water level of $\Delta z = 12.00\,\mathrm{m}$"}

$$
\Delta z = \Delta H = K\,\frac{v^2}{2g}
\qquad\text{with}\qquad
K = 40
$$
$$
\frac{v^2}{2g} = \frac{\Delta z}{K} = \frac{12.00}{40} = 0.300\,\mathrm{m}
$$
$$
v = \sqrt{2 g \cdot 0.300} = \sqrt{19.62 \cdot 0.300} = \sqrt{5.886} = 2.43\,\mathrm{m/s}
$$
$$
A = \frac{\pi \cdot (0.200)^2}{4} = 0.03142\,\mathrm{m^2}
$$
$$
Q = A v = 0.03142 \cdot 2.43 = 0.0762\,\mathrm{m^3/s} = 76.2\,\mathrm{L/s}
$$

Check the head loss and flow regime:
$$
\Delta H = 40 \cdot 0.300 = 12.00\,\mathrm{m} = \Delta z
$$
$$
Re = \frac{v D}{\nu} = \frac{2.43 \cdot 0.200}{10^{-6}} = 4.86 \cdot 10^5
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Apply the energy equation with head loss between the free surfaces of the upstream and downstream reservoirs. Both are at zero gauge pressure. Their velocities are negligible because the reservoirs have much larger cross-sectional areas than the pipe. Total head at each surface therefore equals its elevation.

The balance reduces to $\Delta z = \Delta H$. The entire elevation difference is dissipated as head loss. There is no residual velocity-head term because the water comes to rest in the downstream reservoir.

The loss is expressed in terms of the velocity head, so the equation determines $\dfrac{v^2}{2g} = 0.300\,\mathrm{m}$. This gives the speed, **$2.43\,\mathrm{m/s}$**, and the discharge, $76.2\,\mathrm{L/s}$. The speed falls within the usual range for a pressurized pipe.

The Reynolds number is $4.86 \cdot 10^5$, indicating turbulent flow, consistent with the assumed quadratic dependence of head loss on speed.

</div>

## Exercise 2

The pipe shown has diameter $D = 150\,\mathrm{mm}$, starts at a constant-level reservoir, and discharges to the atmosphere $7.00\,\mathrm{m}$ below its free surface. The total head loss $\Delta H = 20\,\dfrac{v^2}{2g}$ is distributed in proportion to distance along the length $800\,\mathrm{m}$. Find the speed, discharge, and pressure at section $B$, located $300\,\mathrm{m}$ from the inlet and $3.00\,\mathrm{m}$ above the outlet axis.

::schema{acqua="0.2,6.5 2.4,6.5 2.4,11 0.2,11" solidi="0,6 2.6,6 2.6,6.5 0,6.5;0,6 0.2,6 0.2,11.6 0,11.6;2.4,6.5 2.6,6.5 2.6,8.65 2.4,8.65;2.4,9.35 2.6,9.35 2.6,11.6 2.4,11.6" pelo="0.2,2.4,11" condotte="2.6 9-7 9-13 4,0.6" punti="9.4,7,B,pieno" quote="15.2 4,15.2 11,7.00 m,0.9;9.4 4,9.4 7,3.00 m,-1" frecce="13.4,4,14.6,4,v" etichette="4.6,9.9,D = 150 mm;11.4,2.8,outlet to atmosphere" riferimento=0 titolo="Pipe discharging freely under a head $7.00\,\mathrm{m}$, with section $B$ at a height $3.00\,\mathrm{m}$ above the outlet"}

$$
7.00 = \frac{v^2}{2g} + 20\,\frac{v^2}{2g} = 21\,\frac{v^2}{2g}
$$
$$
\frac{v^2}{2g} = \frac{7.00}{21} = 0.3333\,\mathrm{m}
$$
$$
v = \sqrt{19.62 \cdot 0.3333} = \sqrt{6.540} = 2.56\,\mathrm{m/s}
$$
$$
A = \frac{\pi \cdot (0.150)^2}{4} = 0.01767\,\mathrm{m^2},
\qquad
Q = 0.01767 \cdot 2.56 = 0.0452\,\mathrm{m^3/s} = 45.2\,\mathrm{L/s}
$$
$$
\Delta H_{A,B} = \frac{300}{800} \cdot 20\,\frac{v^2}{2g} = 7.5 \cdot 0.3333 = 2.50\,\mathrm{m}
$$
$$
H_B = 7.00 - 2.50 = 4.50\,\mathrm{m}
$$
$$
\frac{p_B}{\gamma} = H_B - z_B - \frac{v^2}{2g} = 4.50 - 3.00 - 0.333 = 1.167\,\mathrm{m}
$$
$$
p_B = 9.81 \cdot 1.167 = 11.4\,\mathrm{kPa}
$$

Check total head at the outlet:
$$
H_U = 0 + 0 + \frac{v^2}{2g} = 0.333\,\mathrm{m}
$$
$$
7.00 - 20 \cdot 0.3333 = 7.00 - 6.667 = 0.333\,\mathrm{m}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Choose the centerline at the outlet as the datum. At the reservoir surface the total head is $7.00\,\mathrm{m}$. At the outlet the elevation is zero and the jet is open to the atmosphere, so only velocity head remains.

The balance therefore contains three terms. The initial head supplies the final velocity head and the head loss. The water leaves the pipe as a moving jet, so the outlet retains its velocity head. Water discharging into a large reservoir would eventually come to rest.

Solving gives $\dfrac{v^2}{2g} = 0.333\,\mathrm{m}$, then $v = 2.56\,\mathrm{m/s}$ and $Q = 45.2\,\mathrm{L/s}$.

To find the pressure at $B$, first calculate the head lost between the entrance and this section. It is proportional to the distance traveled, which is $\dfrac{300}{800}$ of the total length. Subtracting that loss from the initial total head gives $H_B = 4.50\,\mathrm{m}$. Subtract the elevation and velocity head to obtain the pressure head, $1.167\,\mathrm{m}$, corresponding to a gauge pressure of **$11.4\,\mathrm{kPa}$**.

As a check, subtract the total loss from the initial head. The result must equal the velocity head of the jet at the outlet.

</div>

## Exercise 3

The reservoirs shown are connected by a pipe of diameter $D = 250\,\mathrm{mm}$. The difference in surface elevations is $\Delta z = 6.00\,\mathrm{m}$ and the total loss is $\Delta H = 30\,\dfrac{v^2}{2g}$. At crest $S$ the pipe centerline is $2.00\,\mathrm{m}$ above the upstream surface, and the loss to the crest is $8\,\dfrac{v^2}{2g}$. Find the discharge and crest pressure.

::schema{acqua="0.2,3.4 2.4,3.4 2.4,7 0.2,7;16,-2.4 19.4,-2.4 19.4,1 16,1" solidi="0,2.8 2.6,2.8 2.6,3.4 0,3.4;0,2.8 0.2,2.8 0.2,7.6 0,7.6;2.4,3.4 2.6,3.4 2.6,4.65 2.4,4.65;2.4,5.35 2.6,5.35 2.6,7.6 2.4,7.6;15.8,-3 19.6,-3 19.6,-2.4 15.8,-2.4;15.8,-2.4 16,-2.4 16,-0.35 15.8,-0.35;15.8,0.35 16,0.35 16,1.6 15.8,1.6;19.4,-3 19.6,-3 19.6,1.6 19.4,1.6" pelo="0.2,2.4,7;16,19.4,1" tratteggi="2.4,7 10.4,7" condotte="2.6 5-8 9-12 9-16 0,0.7" punti="10,9,S,pieno" quote="11 7,11 9,2.00 m,-1.3;17.6 1,17.6 7,Δz = 6.00 m,0.9" etichette="1.3,7.5,A;16.2,1.9,B;9.8,10.3,D = 250 mm" riferimento=0 titolo="Pipe crossing a rise between two reservoirs. The high point $S$ lies $2.00\,\mathrm{m}$ above the upstream free surface"}

$$
\frac{v^2}{2g} = \frac{\Delta z}{K} = \frac{6.00}{30} = 0.200\,\mathrm{m}
$$
$$
v = \sqrt{19.62 \cdot 0.200} = \sqrt{3.924} = 1.98\,\mathrm{m/s}
$$
$$
A = \frac{\pi \cdot (0.250)^2}{4} = 0.04909\,\mathrm{m^2},
\qquad
Q = 0.04909 \cdot 1.98 = 0.0972\,\mathrm{m^3/s} = 97.2\,\mathrm{L/s}
$$
$$
H_S = 0 - 8 \cdot 0.200 = -1.60\,\mathrm{m}
$$
$$
\frac{p_S}{\gamma} = H_S - z_S - \frac{v^2}{2g} = -1.60 - 2.00 - 0.200 = -3.80\,\mathrm{m}
$$
$$
p_S = 9.81 \cdot (-3.80) = -37.3\,\mathrm{kPa}
$$

Check absolute pressure at the summit:
$$
\frac{p_{\mathrm{abs}}}{\gamma} = \frac{p_{\mathrm{atm}}}{\gamma} + \frac{p_S}{\gamma} = 10.33 - 3.80 = 6.53\,\mathrm{m} > \frac{p_v}{\gamma} = 0.24\,\mathrm{m}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Use the same discharge calculation as in the first exercise. The energy balance between the free surfaces depends only on their elevation difference, all of which is dissipated by losses along the pipe. The results are $v = 1.98\,\mathrm{m/s}$ and $Q = 97.2\,\mathrm{L/s}$.

To find the pressure at the summit, choose the upstream free surface as the datum. The initial total head is then zero. The flow has lost $8 \cdot 0.200 = 1.60\,\mathrm{m}$ upon reaching the summit, so its total head there is $-1.60\,\mathrm{m}$.

Subtract the summit elevation $+2.00\,\mathrm{m}$ and the velocity head. The remaining pressure head is $-3.80\,\mathrm{m}$. The summit gauge pressure is **negative**, $-37.3\,\mathrm{kPa}$. The hydraulic grade line passes below the pipe centerline, so the pipe operates below atmospheric pressure there.

Add atmospheric pressure, $10.33\,\mathrm{m}$, to obtain an absolute pressure of $6.53\,\mathrm{m}$. This is well above the vapor pressure of water at $20\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$, so the pipe can operate without cavitation.

</div>
