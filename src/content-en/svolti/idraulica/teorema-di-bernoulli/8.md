## Exercise 1

The pump shown draws discharge $Q = 40\,\mathrm{L/s}$ from a constant-level reservoir through a pipe of diameter $D = 150\,\mathrm{mm}$. Its inlet is $4.50\,\mathrm{m}$ above the free surface and the suction-line head loss is $\Delta H = 1.20\,\mathrm{m}$. Find the absolute and gauge inlet pressures and check for cavitation. Use atmospheric pressure $p_{\mathrm{atm}} = 101.3\,\mathrm{kPa}$, equivalent to $\dfrac{p_{\mathrm{atm}}}{\gamma} = 10.33\,\mathrm{m}$ of water, and water at $20\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ with vapor pressure $p_v = 2.34\,\mathrm{kPa}$, equivalent to $\dfrac{p_v}{\gamma} = 0.24\,\mathrm{m}$.

::schema{acqua="0.2,0.5 4.6,0.5 4.6,3 0.2,3" solidi="0,0 4.8,0 4.8,0.5 0,0.5;0,0 0.2,0 0.2,3.4 0,3.4;4.6,0 4.8,0 4.8,3.4 4.6,3.4" pelo="0.2,4.6,3" condotte="2.4 1.2-2.4 7.5-6.8 7.5,0.55" pompa="7.5,7.5,P" punti="6.6,7.5,M,pieno" quote="1.2 3,1.2 7.5,4.50 m,0" etichette="4.4,8.2,D = 150 mm;8.4,7.5,pump" riferimento=0 titolo="Suction pipe with the pump inlet at section $M$, a distance $4.50\,\mathrm{m}$ above the free surface"}

$$
A = \frac{\pi \cdot (0.150)^2}{4} = 0.01767\,\mathrm{m^2},
\qquad
v = \frac{0.040}{0.01767} = 2.264\,\mathrm{m/s}
$$
$$
\frac{v^2}{2g} = \frac{5.126}{19.62} = 0.261\,\mathrm{m}
$$
$$
\frac{p_M^{\mathrm{abs}}}{\gamma} = \frac{p_{\mathrm{atm}}}{\gamma} - z_M - \frac{v^2}{2g} - \Delta H
$$
$$
\frac{p_M^{\mathrm{abs}}}{\gamma} = 10.33 - 4.50 - 0.261 - 1.20 = 4.37\,\mathrm{m}
$$
$$
p_M^{\mathrm{abs}} = p_{\mathrm{atm}} - \gamma\left(z_M + \frac{v^2}{2g} + \Delta H\right)
= 101.3 - 9.81 \cdot 5.96 = 42.8\,\mathrm{kPa}
$$
$$
\frac{p_M}{\gamma} = 4.37 - 10.33 = -5.96\,\mathrm{m}
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
p_M = -58.5\,\mathrm{kPa}
$$
$$
\frac{p_M^{\mathrm{abs}}}{\gamma} = 4.37\,\mathrm{m} > \frac{p_v}{\gamma} = 0.24\,\mathrm{m}
$$

Check the balance between the free surface and section $M$ using gauge pressures:
$$
0 = z_M + \frac{p_M}{\gamma} + \frac{v^2}{2g} + \Delta H = 4.50 - 5.96 + 0.261 + 1.20 = 0.00
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Apply the energy equation with head loss between the reservoir surface and the pump inlet. Use absolute pressures because the comparison is with vapor pressure, which is an absolute pressure.

At the free surface the elevation is zero, the speed is negligible, and the absolute pressure is atmospheric, equivalent to $10.33\,\mathrm{m}$ of water. To find the pressure head at section $M$, deduct the suction-line loss from the total head, then subtract the rise in elevation and the velocity head.

The absolute pressure at the inlet is $42.8\,\mathrm{kPa}$, equivalent to $4.37\,\mathrm{m}$ of water. The gauge pressure is $-58.5\,\mathrm{kPa}$ because the inlet operates below atmospheric pressure.

The absolute pressure exceeds vapor pressure by $4.13\,\mathrm{m}$, so **cavitation does not occur** under the stated criterion. Compare the available margin with the pump manufacturer's requirement.

As a check, repeat the balance using gauge pressures. The total head at the free surface is then zero, and the four terms sum to zero as required.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Under the conditions of the preceding exercise, with the same pipe shown, discharge, and head loss $\Delta H = 1.20\,\mathrm{m}$, the pump is to be installed as high as possible. Find the maximum inlet elevation $z_{\max}$ above the reservoir surface while maintaining a margin equivalent to $0.50\,\mathrm{m}$ of water above the vapor pressure.

::schema{acqua="0.2,0.5 4.6,0.5 4.6,3 0.2,3" solidi="0,0 4.8,0 4.8,0.5 0,0.5;0,0 0.2,0 0.2,3.4 0,3.4;4.6,0 4.8,0 4.8,3.4 4.6,3.4" pelo="0.2,4.6,3" condotte="2.4 1.2-2.4 8.5-6.8 8.5,0.55" pompa="7.5,8.5,P" punti="6.6,8.5,M,pieno" quote="1.2 3,1.2 8.5,z_max,0" etichette="4.4,9.2,D = 150 mm;8.4,8.5,pump" riferimento=0 titolo="The same suction pipe, with the pump elevation $z_{\max}$ to be determined"}

$$
\frac{p_M^{\mathrm{abs}}}{\gamma} = \frac{p_{\mathrm{atm}}}{\gamma} - z_M - \frac{v^2}{2g} - \Delta H
\geq \frac{p_v}{\gamma} + 0.50
$$
$$
z_M \leq \frac{p_{\mathrm{atm}}}{\gamma} - \frac{p_v}{\gamma} - 0.50 - \frac{v^2}{2g} - \Delta H
$$
$$
z_M \leq 10.33 - 0.24 - 0.50 - 0.261 - 1.20 = 8.13\,\mathrm{m}
$$

Calculate the absolute pressure directly at the elevation just found:
$$
\frac{p_M^{\mathrm{abs}}}{\gamma} = 10.33 - 8.13 - 0.261 - 1.20 = 0.74\,\mathrm{m}
= \frac{p_v}{\gamma} + 0.50
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Require the absolute inlet pressure to exceed the vapor pressure by at least the stated margin. Write the expression for absolute pressure and solve the inequality for inlet elevation.

The term $\dfrac{p_{\mathrm{atm}}}{\gamma} - \dfrac{p_v}{\gamma} = 10.09\,\mathrm{m}$ is the theoretical limit on suction lift for water at $20\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ at sea level. Increasing pump power cannot raise this limit. Subtract the required pressure margin, the velocity head, and the suction-line head loss.

The maximum elevation is **$8.13\,\mathrm{m}$** under the conditions given. Greater head loss, higher discharge, warmer water, or a higher site elevation above sea level would reduce the allowable lift.

Check the absolute pressure with the pump at the calculated elevation. It is $0.74\,\mathrm{m}$, exactly the vapor pressure plus the required margin of $0.50\,\mathrm{m}$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

The siphon shown, of diameter $D = 100\,\mathrm{mm}$, discharges to the atmosphere $3.00\,\mathrm{m}$ below the constant reservoir surface. Crest $S$ is $5.00\,\mathrm{m}$ above that surface. The total loss is $9\,\dfrac{v^2}{2g}$ and the loss from the reservoir to the crest is $2.5\,\dfrac{v^2}{2g}$. Find the discharge and crest pressure, and check that the siphon can operate. Use atmospheric pressure $p_{\mathrm{atm}} = 101.3\,\mathrm{kPa}$, equivalent to $\dfrac{p_{\mathrm{atm}}}{\gamma} = 10.33\,\mathrm{m}$ of water, and water at $20\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ with vapor pressure $p_v = 2.34\,\mathrm{kPa}$, equivalent to $\dfrac{p_v}{\gamma} = 0.24\,\mathrm{m}$.

::schema{acqua="0.2,3 5.6,3 5.6,8 0.2,8" solidi="0,2.4 5.8,2.4 5.8,3 0,3;0,2.4 0.2,2.4 0.2,8.6 0,8.6;5.6,2.4 5.8,2.4 5.8,8.6 5.6,8.6" pelo="0.2,5.6,8" condotte="3 4-3 13-8.6 13-8.6 5,0.5" punti="5.8,13,S,pieno" quote="4.4 8,4.4 13,5.00 m,0;10 5,10 8,3.00 m,0.9" frecce="8.6,4.6,8.6,3.4,v" etichette="6.6,13.8,D = 100 mm;9.6,4,outlet" riferimento=0 titolo="Siphon with its crest $S$ a distance $5.00\,\mathrm{m}$ above the free surface and its outlet $3.00\,\mathrm{m}$ below it"}

$$
3.00 = \frac{v^2}{2g} + 9\,\frac{v^2}{2g} = 10\,\frac{v^2}{2g}
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
\frac{v^2}{2g} = 0.300\,\mathrm{m}
$$
$$
v = \sqrt{19.62 \cdot 0.300} = 2.43\,\mathrm{m/s}
$$
$$
A = \frac{\pi \cdot (0.100)^2}{4} = 0.007854\,\mathrm{m^2},
\qquad
Q = 0.007854 \cdot 2.43 = 0.0191\,\mathrm{m^3/s} = 19.1\,\mathrm{L/s}
$$
$$
H_S = 3.00 - 2.5 \cdot 0.300 = 3.00 - 0.75 = 2.25\,\mathrm{m}
$$
$$
\frac{p_S}{\gamma} = H_S - z_S - \frac{v^2}{2g} = 2.25 - 8.00 - 0.300 = -6.05\,\mathrm{m}
$$
$$
p_S = 9.81 \cdot (-6.05) = -59.4\,\mathrm{kPa}
$$
$$
\frac{p_S^{\mathrm{abs}}}{\gamma} = 10.33 - 6.05 = 4.28\,\mathrm{m} > \frac{p_v}{\gamma} = 0.24\,\mathrm{m}
$$

Check the head balance between the summit and the outlet:
$$
H_S - H_U = 2.25 - 0.300 = 1.95\,\mathrm{m}
= (9 - 2.5) \cdot 0.300 = 1.95\,\mathrm{m}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Take the outlet centerline as the datum. The reservoir free surface then lies at $3.00\,\mathrm{m}$ and the siphon crest at $8.00\,\mathrm{m}$. Since the siphon issues a free jet, the energy balance between the surface and the outlet includes initial head, outlet velocity head, and total head loss.

This gives $\dfrac{v^2}{2g} = 0.300\,\mathrm{m}$, hence $v = 2.43\,\mathrm{m/s}$, and a discharge of **$19.1\,\mathrm{L/s}$**.

Subtract only the losses accumulated between the upstream free surface and the crest from the initial total head, giving $H_S = 2.25\,\mathrm{m}$. Next subtract crest elevation and velocity head. The remaining pressure head is $-6.05\,\mathrm{m}$.

The gauge pressure at the crest is $-59.4\,\mathrm{kPa}$. The absolute pressure head is $4.28\,\mathrm{m}$ of water, well above vapor pressure, so the **siphon operates**. The crest could theoretically be raised a further $4\,\mathrm{m}$ before the pressure reached vapor pressure. In practice dissolved air can come out of solution at higher pressures and break the continuous water column.

Check the energy balance between the crest and the outlet. Their total-head difference must equal the loss in this reach, obtained by subtracting the upstream-reach loss from the overall loss.

</div>
