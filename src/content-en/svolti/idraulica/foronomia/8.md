## Exercise 1

A contoured nozzle at the end of a pipe has exit diameter $d = 40\,\mathrm{mm}$ and velocity coefficient $C_v = 0.97$. The shape suppresses contraction, so $C_c = 1$. Immediately upstream of the nozzle the total head relative to the outlet axis is $H = 42.0\,\mathrm{m}$, as shown. Determine the jet speed, discharge, and power.

::schema{nodi="A,0,1.5;B,4,1.5" condotte="A-B,0.6,D" acqua="4.7,1.37 8,1.37 8,1.63 4.7,1.63" linee="4,1.8 4.7,1.63;4,1.2 4.7,1.37" energia="0,3.4 4,3.4 4.7,3.22 8,3.22" frecce="5.3,1.5,6.5,1.5,v" tratteggi="7.4,1.5 7.4,3.22" quote="4 1.5,4 3.4,42.0 m,-0.5" etichette="2,3.65,energy grade line;6.1,2.6,velocity head;6.4,1.1,d = 40 mm" riferimento=0 titolo="Nozzle with outlet diameter $d = 40\,\mathrm{mm}$ operating under a total head $H = 42.0\,\mathrm{m}$"}

$$
v = C_v\sqrt{2 g H} = 0.97 \cdot \sqrt{2 \cdot 9.81 \cdot 42.0} = 0.97 \cdot \sqrt{824.0} = 0.97 \cdot 28.71 = 27.8\,\mathrm{m/s}
$$
$$
A = \frac{\pi \cdot 0.040^2}{4} = 1.257 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m^2},
\qquad
Q = A\,v = 1.257 \cdot 10^{-3} \cdot 27.8 = 0.0350\,\mathrm{m^3/s} = 35.0\,\mathrm{L/s}
$$
$$
\frac{v^2}{2g} = C_v^2\,H = 0.9409 \cdot 42.0 = 39.5\,\mathrm{m}
$$
$$
P = \gamma\,Q\,\frac{v^2}{2g} = 9810 \cdot 0.0350 \cdot 39.5 = 13.6 \cdot 10^3\,\mathrm{W} = 13.6\,\mathrm{kW}
$$

Check with an alternative expression for jet power and compare with the available power.
$$
P = \frac{1}{2}\,\rho\,Q\,v^2 = \frac{1}{2} \cdot 1000 \cdot 0.0350 \cdot 27.8^2 = 13.6 \cdot 10^3\,\mathrm{W}
$$
$$
\gamma\,Q\,H = 9810 \cdot 0.0350 \cdot 42.0 = 14.4 \cdot 10^3\,\mathrm{W},
\qquad
\frac{P}{\gamma Q H} = \frac{13.6}{14.4} = 0.94 = C_v^2
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Flow remains attached to the nozzle wall up to the outlet, giving $C_c = 1$. The discharge coefficient therefore equals the velocity coefficient. Apply Bernoulli’s equation from the upstream section, where the total head is $H$, to the outlet, where the total head equals the velocity head.

This gives a jet speed of $v = 27.8\,\mathrm{m/s}$. Multiplying by the exit area gives the discharge $Q = 35.0\,\mathrm{L/s}$ by continuity.

The jet velocity head is $C_v^2 H = 39.5\,\mathrm{m}$, which is $2.5\,\mathrm{m}$ below the upstream head. The difference is the nozzle head loss. Jet power is the liquid weight flow rate multiplied by the jet velocity head, giving $P = 13.6\,\mathrm{kW}$.

Recalculate power using $\dfrac{1}{2}\rho Q v^2$, obtained from the first expression by substituting $\gamma = \rho g$, and compare the jet power with the available power $\gamma Q H$. Their ratio is $0.94$, or $C_v^2$, as expected. The nozzle converts $94\%$ of the incoming energy to jet kinetic energy.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A fire hose ends in a nozzle of diameter $d = 25\,\mathrm{mm}$ and velocity coefficient $C_v = 0.96$, with $C_c = 1$. A gauge on the hose centerline immediately upstream of the nozzle reads $p = 500\,\mathrm{kPa}$. The approach velocity head is negligible compared with the pressure head, as shown. Find the discharge, the jet speed, the maximum rise of the jet when directed vertically upward, and the jet power.

::schema{nodi="A,0,1.5;B,4,1.5" condotte="A-B,0.5,nozzle" acqua="4.7,1.39 8,1.39 8,1.61 4.7,1.61" linee="4,1.75 4.7,1.61;4,1.25 4.7,1.39;3,1.75 3,2.6" cerchi="3,2.85,0.25" frecce="5.3,1.5,6.5,1.5,v" etichette="3,3.4,p = 500 kPa;6.4,1.1,d = 25 mm" riferimento=0 titolo="Fire hose nozzle of diameter $d = 25\,\mathrm{mm}$, with an upstream pressure gauge reading $p = 500\,\mathrm{kPa}$"}

$$
H = \frac{p}{\gamma} = \frac{500}{9.81} = 51.0\,\mathrm{m}
$$
$$
v = C_v\sqrt{2 g H} = 0.96 \cdot \sqrt{2 \cdot 9.81 \cdot 51.0} = 0.96 \cdot 31.62 = 30.4\,\mathrm{m/s}
$$
$$
A = \frac{\pi \cdot 0.025^2}{4} = 4.909 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m^2},
\qquad
Q = A\,v = 4.909 \cdot 10^{-4} \cdot 30.4 = 0.0149\,\mathrm{m^3/s} = 14.9\,\mathrm{L/s}
$$
$$
h_{\max} = \frac{v^2}{2g} = C_v^2\,H = 0.9216 \cdot 51.0 = 47.0\,\mathrm{m}
$$
$$
P = \gamma\,Q\,h_{\max} = 9810 \cdot 0.0149 \cdot 47.0 = 6.87 \cdot 10^3\,\mathrm{W} = 6.87\,\mathrm{kW}
$$

Check using the alternative jet power equation.
$$
P = \frac{1}{2}\,\rho\,Q\,v^2 = \frac{1}{2} \cdot 1000 \cdot 0.0149 \cdot 30.4^2 = \frac{1}{2} \cdot 1000 \cdot 0.0149 \cdot 924 = 6.87 \cdot 10^3\,\mathrm{W}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The pressure tap lies at the outlet-axis elevation, so its elevation head is zero relative to the outlet. Neglecting the approach velocity head, the upstream total head is the pressure head $\dfrac{p}{\gamma}$. The gauge reading $500\,\mathrm{kPa}$ corresponds to a water head $51.0\,\mathrm{m}$.

The jet speed is $v = 30.4\,\mathrm{m/s}$ and the discharge is $Q = 14.9\,\mathrm{L/s}$.

When the nozzle points vertically upward and air resistance is neglected, gravity is the only force on the jet after it leaves the nozzle. All its velocity head can convert to elevation, giving a maximum rise of $\dfrac{v^2}{2g} = 47.0\,\mathrm{m}$. Air drag and breakup reduce the height of a real jet, so the computed rise is an upper bound.

The jet power is $P = 6.87\,\mathrm{kW}$.

The alternative expression $\dfrac{1}{2}\rho Q v^2$ uses mass flow rate in place of weight flow rate and gives the same power.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A constant-level reservoir supplies a pipe of length $L = 300\,\mathrm{m}$, diameter $D = 150\,\mathrm{mm}$, and Darcy friction factor $\lambda = 0.025$. The pipe ends in a nozzle of diameter $d = 50\,\mathrm{mm}$ and velocity coefficient $C_v = 0.97$, with $C_c = 1$. The reservoir free surface is $H_0 = 60.0\,\mathrm{m}$ above the nozzle axis, as shown. Neglect local losses and calculate the discharge and jet power.

::schema{nodi="A,0.4,4.5;B,0.4,0.6;C,7,0.6" condotte="A-B-C,0.25,D = 150 mm" solidi="-2.4,4.2 1.5,4.2 1.5,4.5 -2.4,4.5;-2.4,4.5 -2.1,4.5 -2.1,6.7 -2.4,6.7;1.2,4.5 1.5,4.5 1.5,6.7 1.2,6.7" acqua="-2.1,4.5 1.2,4.5 1.2,6.4 -2.1,6.4;7.6,0.54 10,0.54 10,0.66 7.6,0.66" linee="7,0.73 7.6,0.66;7,0.47 7.6,0.54" pelo="-2.1,1.2,6.4," energia="-2.1,6.4 0.4,6.4 7,4.27 7.6,4.05 10,4.05" tratteggi="0.4,6.4 10.9,6.4;7.6,0.6 10.9,0.6" frecce="8.2,0.6,9.4,0.6,v" quote="10.9 0.6,10.9 6.4,60.0 m,0.6" etichette="4.2,1.25,L = 300 m;8.6,1.15,d = 50 mm;5.5,5.4,energy grade line" riferimento=0 titolo="Reservoir $60.0\,\mathrm{m}$ above the nozzle, connected by a pipe of length $L = 300\,\mathrm{m}$ and diameter $D = 150\,\mathrm{mm}$ to a nozzle of diameter $d = 50\,\mathrm{mm}$"}

$$
A_t = \frac{\pi \cdot 0.150^2}{4} = 0.01767\,\mathrm{m^2},
\qquad
A = \frac{\pi \cdot 0.050^2}{4} = 1.963 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m^2},
\qquad
\frac{A_t}{A} = 9.00
$$
$$
\begin{cases}
H = H_0 - \lambda\,\dfrac{L}{D}\,\dfrac{v_t^2}{2g} \\[8pt]
v = C_v\sqrt{2 g H}
\end{cases}
\qquad
v_t = \frac{A}{A_t}\,v = \frac{v}{9.00}
$$
$$
\lambda\,\frac{L}{D} = 0.025 \cdot \frac{300}{0.150} = 50.0
$$
$$
H_0 = \lambda\,\frac{L}{D}\,\frac{v^2}{81.0 \cdot 2g} + \frac{v^2}{2 g\,C_v^2}
= \frac{v^2}{2g}\left(\frac{50.0}{81.0} + \frac{1}{0.9409}\right)
= \frac{v^2}{2g} \cdot 1.6801
$$
$$
\frac{v^2}{2g} = \frac{60.0}{1.6801} = 35.71\,\mathrm{m},
\qquad
v = \sqrt{2 \cdot 9.81 \cdot 35.71} = 26.5\,\mathrm{m/s}
$$
$$
Q = A\,v = 1.963 \cdot 10^{-3} \cdot 26.5 = 0.0520\,\mathrm{m^3/s} = 52.0\,\mathrm{L/s}
$$
$$
P = \gamma\,Q\,\frac{v^2}{2g} = 9810 \cdot 0.0520 \cdot 35.71 = 18.2 \cdot 10^3\,\mathrm{W} = 18.2\,\mathrm{kW}
$$

Check the power balance.
$$
v_t = \frac{26.5}{9.00} = 2.94\,\mathrm{m/s},
\qquad
\Delta H_t = 50.0 \cdot \frac{2.94^2}{19.62} = 22.0\,\mathrm{m},
\qquad
H = 60.0 - 22.0 = 38.0\,\mathrm{m}
$$
$$
\gamma Q H_0 = 30.6\,\mathrm{kW},
\qquad
\gamma Q\,\Delta H_t = 11.2\,\mathrm{kW},
\qquad
\gamma Q\left(H - \frac{v^2}{2g}\right) = 9810 \cdot 0.0520 \cdot 2.29 = 1.2\,\mathrm{kW}
$$
$$
18.2 + 11.2 + 1.2 = 30.6\,\mathrm{kW}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The total head supplying the nozzle is less than the elevation difference $H_0$ because the pipe dissipates head. This loss depends on the discharge, which itself depends on the head remaining at the nozzle. Determine $H$ and $v$ together using the pipe head balance and the nozzle discharge equation.

Continuity relates the two speeds. The area ratio $\left(\dfrac{D}{d}\right)^2 = 9.00$ makes the pipe speed one-ninth of the jet speed. Substitution into the first equation makes both loss terms proportional to $\dfrac{v^2}{2g}$, leaving a single equation for the jet velocity head.

The sum of the two coefficients is $1.6801$. The first term, $0.617$, accounts for pipe friction, and the second, $1.063$, accounts for the jet kinetic energy and nozzle loss. We obtain $\dfrac{v^2}{2g} = 35.71\,\mathrm{m}$, giving $v = 26.5\,\mathrm{m/s}$ and $Q = 52.0\,\mathrm{L/s}$. The pipe velocity $2.94\,\mathrm{m/s}$ is reasonable for a pressurized pipe, and the Reynolds number $\dfrac{2.94 \cdot 0.150}{10^{-6}} = 4.4 \cdot 10^5$ is consistent with the turbulent flow assumed for $\lambda = 0.025$.

The jet power is $P = 18.2\,\mathrm{kW}$.

Check conservation of power. The available power $\gamma Q H_0 = 30.6\,\mathrm{kW}$ is distributed among pipe friction $11.2\,\mathrm{kW}$, nozzle dissipation $1.2\,\mathrm{kW}$, and jet power. These three contributions sum to the available power. **More than one-third of the available energy is dissipated in the pipe** because its length is large relative to its diameter.

</div>
