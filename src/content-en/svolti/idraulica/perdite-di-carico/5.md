## Exercise 1

On a cast-iron pipe of diameter $D = 300\,\mathrm{mm}$ with $\varepsilon = 0.5\,\mathrm{mm}$, two piezometers $L = 200\,\mathrm{m}$ apart show a head difference of $\Delta h = 3.00\,\mathrm{m}$, as illustrated. Find the mean velocity and discharge.

::schema{nodi="A,0,1,-;B,12,1,-" condotte="A-B,0.5" linee="2,1.25 2,5.0;10,1.25 10,4.2" piezometrica="2,5.0 10,4.2" tratteggi="2,5.0 10.8,5.0" etichette="0.6,5.2,piezometers;5.4,0.3,D = 300 mm · ε = 0.5 mm" quote="2 0,10 0,L = 200 m,1.4;10.8 4.2,10.8 5.0,Δh = 3.00 m,0.9" riferimento=0 titolo="Piezometric head difference measured over $200\,\mathrm{m}$"}

$$
J = \frac{\Delta h}{L} = \frac{3.00}{200} = 0.0150 ,
\qquad
\frac{\varepsilon}{D} = \frac{0.5}{300} = 1.67 \cdot 10^{-3}
$$

Solving the Darcy–Weisbach equation for velocity in terms of the Darcy friction factor gives
$$
v = \sqrt{\frac{2\,g\,D\,J}{\lambda}} = \sqrt{\frac{2 \cdot 9.81 \cdot 0.300 \cdot 0.0150}{\lambda}} = \sqrt{\frac{0.0883}{\lambda}} .
$$

The friction factor depends on velocity through the Reynolds number. Start the iteration with $\lambda_0 = 0.025$.

| Trial | Assumed $\lambda$ | $v$ in m/s | $Re$ | Calculated $\lambda$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | $0.02500$ | $1.879$ | $5.64 \cdot 10^5$ | $0.02267$ |
| 2 | $0.02267$ | $1.974$ | $5.92 \cdot 10^5$ | $0.02267$ |

Stop when the difference between successive values is less than $10^{-5}$.
$$
v = 1.97\,\mathrm{m/s} ,
\qquad
A = \frac{\pi \cdot 0.300^2}{4}\,\mathrm{m^2} = 0.0707\,\mathrm{m^2}
$$
$$
Q = v\,A = 1.97 \cdot 0.0707\,\mathrm{m^3/s} = 0.140\,\mathrm{m^3/s} = 140\,\mathrm{L/s}
$$

The known energy gradient also permits a direct check without iteration because the product $Re\sqrt{\lambda}$ can be calculated from the data. Thus
$$
Re\,\sqrt{\lambda} = \frac{v\,D}{\nu}\sqrt{\frac{2\,g\,D\,J}{v^2}} = \frac{D}{\nu}\sqrt{2\,g\,D\,J} = \frac{0.300}{10^{-6}}\sqrt{0.0883} = 8.91 \cdot 10^4 .
$$
Substitution into the Colebrook–White equation gives
$$
\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda}} = -2\,\log_{10}\!\left( \frac{2.51}{8.91 \cdot 10^4} + \frac{0.5}{3.71 \cdot 300} \right) = -2\,\log_{10}\!\left( 4.77 \cdot 10^{-4} \right) = 6.642 ,
$$
so $\lambda = 0.0227$, the value obtained by iteration.

<div class="spiegazione">

The difference between the piezometer levels is the drop in piezometric head. Since the diameter is constant, the velocity heads are equal and this difference equals the head loss. The energy gradient is the head loss divided by the distance between the taps.

Solving the Darcy–Weisbach equation for velocity relates $v$ to $\lambda$. The Colebrook–White equation relates $\lambda$ to $Re$ and hence to $v$. Solve this implicit system by iteration. Assume $\lambda$, calculate $v$, determine $Re$, and update $\lambda$. Convergence is rapid because velocity varies with $\lambda$ as $\lambda^{-1/2}$, and $\lambda$ depends on velocity only through the logarithm. The **Moody diagram** gives the same value at $Re = 5.9 \cdot 10^5$ on the curve for $\dfrac{\varepsilon}{D} = 1.67 \cdot 10^{-3}$.

A direct check uses the product $Re\sqrt{\lambda}$, in which the velocity cancels. The Reynolds number $Re$ is proportional to $v$, whereas $\sqrt{\lambda}$ is inversely proportional to $v$ at a fixed energy gradient. The product can therefore be calculated from the given pipe and fluid data and the energy gradient, making the Colebrook–White equation explicit. Agreement with the iterative result **confirms** the calculation.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A steel pipe with $\varepsilon = 0.1\,\mathrm{mm}$ must carry $Q = 100\,\mathrm{L/s}$ of water with an energy gradient no greater than $J_{\max} = 0.005$, as shown. Calculate the theoretical diameter and select a standard diameter from $100$, $150$, $200$, $250$, $300$, $400$ and $500\,\mathrm{mm}$.

::schema{nodi="A,0,1,-;B,12,1,-" condotte="A-B,0.5" frecce="4.6,1,6.2,1,Q = 100 L/s" etichette="2.4,0.05,D = ?;8.2,0.05,J ≤ 0.005" quote="A,B,L,1.7" riferimento=0 titolo="Sizing a steel pipe"}

Substituting $v = \dfrac{4Q}{\pi D^2}$ into the Darcy–Weisbach equation gives
$$
J = \lambda\,\frac{1}{D}\,\frac{1}{2g}\left( \frac{4Q}{\pi D^2} \right)^2 = \frac{8\,\lambda\,Q^2}{g\,\pi^2\,D^5}
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
D = \left( \frac{8\,\lambda\,Q^2}{g\,\pi^2\,J_{\max}} \right)^{\frac{1}{5}} .
$$

Here too, $\lambda$ depends on diameter. Start the iteration with $D_0 = 250\,\mathrm{mm}$.

| Trial | $D$ in m | $v$ in m/s | $Re$ | $\lambda$ | Calculated $D$ in m |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | $0.2500$ | $2.037$ | $5.09 \cdot 10^5$ | $0.01698$ | $0.3088$ |
| 2 | $0.3088$ | $1.336$ | $4.12 \cdot 10^5$ | $0.01668$ | $0.3077$ |
| 3 | $0.3077$ | $1.345$ | $4.14 \cdot 10^5$ | $0.01668$ | $0.3077$ |

The calculated diameter is $D = 308\,\mathrm{mm}$. The adjacent standard pipe diameters are $300$ and $400\,\mathrm{mm}$.
$$
D = 300\,\mathrm{mm} : \quad v = 1.41\,\mathrm{m/s} , \quad Re = 4.24 \cdot 10^5 , \quad \lambda = 0.0167 , \quad J = 0.00568 ,
$$
$$
D = 400\,\mathrm{mm} : \quad v = 0.796\,\mathrm{m/s} , \quad Re = 3.18 \cdot 10^5 , \quad \lambda = 0.0165 , \quad J = 0.00133 .
$$

With $D = 300\,\mathrm{mm}$, the calculation gives $J = 0.00568 > 0.005$, exceeding the limit. With $D = 400\,\mathrm{mm}$, it gives $J = 0.00133 \leq 0.005$, which meets the limit. Select the $400\,\mathrm{mm}$ diameter.

<div class="spiegazione">

This is a design calculation with specified discharge and maximum energy gradient. Expressing velocity in terms of discharge in the Darcy–Weisbach equation shows that the gradient varies as $D^{-5}$. Solving for diameter therefore requires a fifth root.

The exponent $\dfrac{1}{5}$ makes the iteration stable. An initial error of $30\,\%$ produces an error of less than $6\,\%$ in the calculated diameter, and the value settles by the second iteration.

The calculated diameter, $308\,\mathrm{mm}$, falls between the standard sizes $300$ and $400\,\mathrm{mm}$. At $300\,\mathrm{mm}$, the energy gradient exceeds the limit by $14\,\%$. At $400\,\mathrm{mm}$, it falls to about one quarter of the limit. Select the **larger** diameter to meet the maximum permitted gradient. The corresponding mean velocity, $0.796\,\mathrm{m/s}$, remains above the minimum of $0.5\,\mathrm{m/s}$ below which solids tend to settle.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Identify the flow regimes in the two pipes shown. Calculate their Darcy friction factors using the Colebrook–White equation and compare them with the values given by the limiting formulas.

- Pipe $a$: encrusted cast iron, $D = 400\,\mathrm{mm}$, $\varepsilon = 1.2\,\mathrm{mm}$ and $Q = 300\,\mathrm{L/s}$.
- Pipe $b$: plastic, $D = 200\,\mathrm{mm}$, $\varepsilon = 0.02\,\mathrm{mm}$ and $Q = 50\,\mathrm{L/s}$.

Finally, for each pipe, calculate the head loss over $1000\,\mathrm{m}$ and the error that would result from using the limiting formula.

::schema{nodi="A,0,3,-;B,10,3,-;C,0,0.6,-;E,10,0.6,-" condotte="A-B,0.7;C-E,0.45" frecce="4.0,3,5.4,3,Q = 300 L/s;4.0,0.6,5.4,0.6,Q = 50 L/s" etichette="1.6,2.2,a: D = 400 mm · ε = 1.2 mm;1.6,-0.1,b: D = 200 mm · ε = 0.02 mm" riferimento=0 titolo="Two pipes of markedly different roughness"}

For pipe $a$, the calculation gives
$$
A = 0.1257\,\mathrm{m^2} ,
\qquad
v = \frac{0.300}{0.1257}\,\mathrm{m/s} = 2.39\,\mathrm{m/s} ,
\qquad
Re = 9.55 \cdot 10^5 ,
\qquad
\frac{\varepsilon}{D} = 3.00 \cdot 10^{-3}
$$
$$
\lambda = 0.0263 ,
\qquad
Re\,\sqrt{\lambda}\;\frac{\varepsilon}{D} = 9.55 \cdot 10^5 \cdot 0.162 \cdot 3.00 \cdot 10^{-3} = 464 > 200
$$

For pipe $b$, the calculation gives
$$
A = 0.0314\,\mathrm{m^2} ,
\qquad
v = \frac{0.050}{0.0314}\,\mathrm{m/s} = 1.59\,\mathrm{m/s} ,
\qquad
Re = 3.18 \cdot 10^5 ,
\qquad
\frac{\varepsilon}{D} = 1.00 \cdot 10^{-4}
$$
$$
\lambda = 0.0153 ,
\qquad
Re\,\sqrt{\lambda}\;\frac{\varepsilon}{D} = 3.18 \cdot 10^5 \cdot 0.124 \cdot 1.00 \cdot 10^{-4} = 3.94
$$

Compare these results with the limiting formulas.
$$
\lambda_{\mathrm{a.t.}}^{(a)} = \left[ 2\,\log_{10}\!\left( \frac{3.71}{3.00 \cdot 10^{-3}} \right) \right]^{-2} = 0.0261 ,
\qquad
\lambda_{\mathrm{smooth}}^{(b)} = 0.0143 ,
\qquad
\lambda_{\mathrm{a.t.}}^{(b)} = 0.0120
$$

The head losses over $1000\,\mathrm{m}$ are
$$
\Delta H_a = 0.0263 \cdot \frac{1000}{0.400} \cdot \frac{2.39^2}{19.62}\,\mathrm{m} = 0.0263 \cdot 2500 \cdot 0.290\,\mathrm{m} = 19.1\,\mathrm{m}
$$
$$
\Delta H_b = 0.0153 \cdot \frac{1000}{0.200} \cdot \frac{1.59^2}{19.62}\,\mathrm{m} = 0.0153 \cdot 5000 \cdot 0.129\,\mathrm{m} = 9.88\,\mathrm{m}
$$

As a check, using the limiting formulas gives
$$
\Delta H_a^{\mathrm{a.t.}} = 19.1 \cdot \frac{0.0261}{0.0263}\,\mathrm{m} = 19.0\,\mathrm{m} ,
\qquad
\Delta H_b^{\mathrm{smooth}} = 9.88 \cdot \frac{0.0143}{0.0153}\,\mathrm{m} = 9.23\,\mathrm{m} ,
$$
with differences of $0.6\,\%$ for pipe $a$ and $6.6\,\%$ for pipe $b$.

<div class="spiegazione">

The dimensionless group $Re\sqrt{\lambda}\,\dfrac{\varepsilon}{D}$ identifies the roughness regime. It is proportional to the ratio of the roughness height to the thickness of the viscous layer next to the wall.

For pipe $a$ the group is $464$, greater than $200$, so the flow is **fully rough turbulent**. The roughness elements extend well beyond the viscous layer. Viscosity no longer affects the resistance, and $\lambda$ depends only on relative roughness. The Prandtl–Nikuradse relation gives $0.0261$ compared with $0.0263$, a difference of $0.6\,\%$ in head loss.

For pipe $b$ the group is $3.94$, so the wall is close to hydraulically smooth. The Colebrook–White equation gives $\lambda = 0.0153$, while Prandtl’s smooth-pipe law gives $0.0143$. The two terms inside the logarithm explain the difference. The viscous term is $6.38 \cdot 10^{-5}$ and the roughness term is $2.70 \cdot 10^{-5}$, or $30\,\%$ of their sum. The pipe is therefore **transitionally rough**, and both contributions matter.

In the transitionally rough regime, neither limiting formula is accurate. The smooth-pipe law gives an error of $6.6\,\%$. The fully rough law, with $\lambda = 0.0120$, gives an error of $22\,\%$. Use the full **Colebrook–White** equation or the Moody diagram in this regime.

</div>
