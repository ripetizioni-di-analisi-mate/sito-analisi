## Exercise 1

A steel pipe of diameter $D = 150\,\mathrm{mm}$ and length $L = 60\,\mathrm{m}$, with $\varepsilon = 0.1\,\mathrm{mm}$, connects two reservoirs and carries $Q = 30\,\mathrm{L/s}$ of water. The diagram shows a sharp-edged entrance ($\xi = 0.5$), two $90^\circ$ bends ($\xi = 0.25$ each), a fully open gate valve ($\xi = 0.15$) and the exit into the downstream reservoir ($\xi = 1$). Find each minor loss, the friction loss and the fraction of the total loss attributable to minor losses.

::schema{nodi="A,1,4.6,-;B,1,0.8,-;C,11,0.8,-;D,11,2.6,-" condotte="A-B-C-D,0.35" acqua="-1.4,4.1 1.6,4.1 1.6,6.2 -1.4,6.2;9.4,2.2 12.4,2.2 12.4,3.6 9.4,3.6" pelo="-1.4,1.6,6.2,;9.4,12.4,3.6," linee="-1.4,6.6 -1.4,4.1 1.6,4.1 1.6,6.6;9.4,4.0 9.4,2.2 12.4,2.2 12.4,4.0" etichette="3.0,4.3,inlet;2.1,1.25,bend;8.0,1.25,bend;5.0,1.25,gate valve;8.2,2.9,outlet;5.6,0.05,D = 150 mm · L = 60 m" riferimento=0 titolo="Pipe between two reservoirs with an entrance, two bends, a gate valve and an exit"}

$$
A = \frac{\pi \cdot 0.150^2}{4}\,\mathrm{m^2} = 0.01767\,\mathrm{m^2} ,
\qquad
v = \frac{0.030}{0.01767}\,\mathrm{m/s} = 1.70\,\mathrm{m/s}
$$
$$
\frac{v^2}{2g} = \frac{1.70^2}{19.62}\,\mathrm{m} = 0.147\,\mathrm{m}
$$

| Fitting | $\xi$ | $\Delta H_{\mathrm{loc}}$ in m |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sharp-edged entrance | $0.50$ | $0.0735$ |
| First bend | $0.25$ | $0.0368$ |
| Second bend | $0.25$ | $0.0368$ |
| Open gate valve | $0.15$ | $0.0221$ |
| Discharge into reservoir | $1.00$ | $0.147$ |
| Total | $2.15$ | $0.316$ |

$$
Re = \frac{1.70 \cdot 0.150}{10^{-6}} = 2.55 \cdot 10^5 ,
\qquad
\frac{\varepsilon}{D} = 6.67 \cdot 10^{-4}
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
\lambda = 0.0193
$$
$$
\Delta H_{\mathrm{cont}} = \lambda\,\frac{L}{D}\,\frac{v^2}{2g} = 0.0193 \cdot \frac{60}{0.150} \cdot 0.147\,\mathrm{m} = 1.13\,\mathrm{m}
$$
$$
\Delta H = 1.13 + 0.316\,\mathrm{m} = 1.45\,\mathrm{m} ,
\qquad
\frac{\Delta H_{\mathrm{loc}}}{\Delta H} = \frac{0.316}{1.45} = 0.218
$$

Check the result by replacing the minor losses with their equivalent length of straight pipe, giving
$$
L_{\mathrm{eq}} = \sum_i \xi_i\,\frac{D}{\lambda} = 2.15 \cdot \frac{0.150}{0.0193}\,\mathrm{m} = 16.7\,\mathrm{m} ,
$$
$$
\Delta H = \lambda\,\frac{L + L_{\mathrm{eq}}}{D}\,\frac{v^2}{2g} = 0.0193 \cdot \frac{76.7}{0.150} \cdot 0.147\,\mathrm{m} = 1.45\,\mathrm{m} ,
$$
which agrees with the earlier result.

<div class="spiegazione">

All the fittings have the same diameter, so the velocity is constant throughout the pipe and the same velocity head $\dfrac{v^2}{2g} = 0.147\,\mathrm{m}$ enters every loss. Each minor loss equals the velocity head multiplied by the fitting's loss coefficient. To obtain the total, first add the loss coefficients.

The largest loss occurs at the **outlet**, with $\xi = 1$. The flow enters the downstream reservoir and comes to rest, dissipating its entire velocity head in eddies. The second-largest loss occurs at the sharp-edged entrance, where the flow separates and contracts before filling the pipe.

First find $\lambda$ from the Colebrook–White equation using $Re = 2.55 \cdot 10^5$ and $\dfrac{\varepsilon}{D} = 6.67 \cdot 10^{-4}$. The Darcy–Weisbach equation then gives the friction head loss.

Minor losses account for $21.8\,\%$ of the total. This pipe is only four hundred diameters long, so omitting the fittings would underestimate the loss by more than one fifth.

For a check, replace the fittings by their **equivalent length** of straight pipe, which produces the same head loss. This length is $16.7\,\mathrm{m}$, just over one quarter of the actual pipe length. Applying the Darcy–Weisbach equation to the combined length gives the same result.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Two open reservoirs whose surface elevations differ by $\Delta z = 8.00\,\mathrm{m}$ are connected by a cast-iron pipe with $D = 200\,\mathrm{mm}$, $L = 250\,\mathrm{m}$ and $\varepsilon = 0.25\,\mathrm{mm}$, as shown. The pipe has a sharp-edged entrance ($\xi = 0.5$), two bends ($\xi = 0.3$ each), an open butterfly valve ($\xi = 0.5$) and an exit ($\xi = 1$). Find the discharge.

::schema{nodi="A,1,4.6,-;B,1,0.8,-;C,11,0.8,-;D,11,2.6,-" condotte="A-B-C-D,0.4" acqua="-1.4,4.1 1.6,4.1 1.6,6.2 -1.4,6.2;9.4,2.2 12.4,2.2 12.4,3.6 9.4,3.6" pelo="-1.4,1.6,6.2,;9.4,12.4,3.6," linee="-1.4,6.6 -1.4,4.1 1.6,4.1 1.6,6.6;9.4,4.0 9.4,2.2 12.4,2.2 12.4,4.0" tratteggi="1.6,6.2 13.4,6.2" quote="13.4 3.6,13.4 6.2,Δz = 8.00 m,0.9" etichette="3.0,4.3,inlet;2.1,1.25,bend;8.0,1.25,bend;5.0,1.25,valve;8.2,2.9,outlet;5.6,0.05,D = 200 mm · L = 250 m" riferimento=0 titolo="Pipe between reservoirs with a specified difference in water level"}

Applying Bernoulli's equation between the two free surfaces, where both velocity and gauge pressure are zero, gives
$$
\Delta z = \left( \lambda\,\frac{L}{D} + \sum_i \xi_i \right)\frac{v^2}{2g} ,
\qquad
\sum_i \xi_i = 0.5 + 0.3 + 0.3 + 0.5 + 1 = 2.60 .
$$
$$
v = \sqrt{\frac{2\,g\,\Delta z}{\lambda\,\dfrac{L}{D} + \sum_i \xi_i}}
$$

The mean velocity and Darcy friction factor depend on one another. Start the iteration with $\lambda_0 = 0.025$ and use the relative roughness $\dfrac{\varepsilon}{D} = 1.25 \cdot 10^{-3}$.

| Trial | Assumed $\lambda$ | $v$ in m/s | $Re$ | Calculated $\lambda$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | $0.02500$ | $2.153$ | $4.31 \cdot 10^5$ | $0.02128$ |
| 2 | $0.02128$ | $2.318$ | $4.64 \cdot 10^5$ | $0.02129$ |
| 3 | $0.02129$ | $2.318$ | $4.64 \cdot 10^5$ | $0.02129$ |

Stop when successive values differ by less than $10^{-5}$. The third iteration meets this criterion.
$$
v = 2.32\,\mathrm{m/s} ,
\qquad
A = \frac{\pi \cdot 0.200^2}{4}\,\mathrm{m^2} = 0.0314\,\mathrm{m^2}
$$
$$
Q = v\,A = 2.32 \cdot 0.0314\,\mathrm{m^3/s} = 0.0728\,\mathrm{m^3/s} = 72.8\,\mathrm{L/s}
$$

As a check, calculate the two head losses separately at the computed velocity. The velocity head is $\dfrac{v^2}{2g} = 0.274\,\mathrm{m}$, giving
$$
\Delta H_{\mathrm{cont}} = 0.0213 \cdot \frac{250}{0.200} \cdot 0.274\,\mathrm{m} = 7.29\,\mathrm{m} ,
\qquad
\Delta H_{\mathrm{loc}} = 2.60 \cdot 0.274\,\mathrm{m} = 0.712\,\mathrm{m} .
$$
The sum is $8.00\,\mathrm{m}$, equal to the available elevation difference, so the energy balance is satisfied.

<div class="spiegazione">

The total head available between the two free surfaces equals their elevation difference. The velocities in the reservoirs are negligible and the gauge pressure is zero at each surface, so the entire elevation difference is dissipated as head loss.

Factoring out the velocity head gives one equation for the velocity. However, $\lambda$ depends on velocity through the Reynolds number. Solve the resulting implicit equation by iteration. Assume $\lambda$, calculate $v$, obtain $Re$ and update $\lambda$ using the Colebrook–White equation. The first trial, $\lambda_0 = 0.025$, already gives a velocity within $8\,\%$ of the final value because $\lambda$ appears under a square root.

The operating velocity is $2.32\,\mathrm{m/s}$, typical of a cast-iron pipe, and the discharge is $72.8\,\mathrm{L/s}$.

As a check, calculate the two losses separately. The friction loss is $7.29\,\mathrm{m}$ and the minor loss is $0.712\,\mathrm{m}$. Their sum equals the elevation difference exactly, **confirming the energy balance** between the reservoirs. Minor losses account for less than $9\,\%$ of the total because the pipe is $1250$ diameters long.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Two reservoirs whose surface elevations differ by $\Delta z = 3.00\,\mathrm{m}$ are connected by a cast-iron pipe with $D = 200\,\mathrm{mm}$, $L = 40\,\mathrm{m}$ and $\varepsilon = 0.25\,\mathrm{mm}$. The diagram shows a sharp-edged entrance ($\xi = 0.5$), two long-radius $90^\circ$ bends ($\xi = 0.15$ each), a gate valve and an exit ($\xi = 1$). Find the discharges with the gate valve fully open ($\xi = 0.2$) and half open ($\xi = 5.6$), and the reduction in discharge caused by partly closing it.

::schema{nodi="A,1,3.6,-;B,1,0.8,-;C,8,0.8,-;D,8,2.0,-" condotte="A-B-C-D,0.4" acqua="-1.4,3.1 1.6,3.1 1.6,4.8 -1.4,4.8;6.4,1.6 9.4,1.6 9.4,2.8 6.4,2.8" pelo="-1.4,1.6,4.8,;6.4,9.4,2.8," linee="-1.4,5.2 -1.4,3.1 1.6,3.1 1.6,5.2;6.4,3.2 6.4,1.6 9.4,1.6 9.4,3.2" tratteggi="1.6,4.8 10.4,4.8" quote="10.4 2.8,10.4 4.8,Δz = 3.00 m,0.9" etichette="2.8,3.35,inlet;2.1,1.25,bend;6.9,1.25,bend;4.4,1.25,gate valve;5.6,2.3,outlet;4.4,0.05,D = 200 mm · L = 40 m" riferimento=0 titolo="Short pipe between two reservoirs with a regulating gate valve"}

With the gate valve fully open, $\sum \xi_i = 0.5 + 0.15 + 0.15 + 0.2 + 1 = 2.00$, giving

| Trial | Assumed $\lambda$ | $v$ in m/s | $Re$ | Calculated $\lambda$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | $0.02500$ | $2.900$ | $5.80 \cdot 10^5$ | $0.02114$ |
| 2 | $0.02114$ | $3.074$ | $6.15 \cdot 10^5$ | $0.02115$ |
| 3 | $0.02115$ | $3.074$ | $6.15 \cdot 10^5$ | $0.02115$ |

$$
v = 3.07\,\mathrm{m/s} ,
\qquad
Q = 3.07 \cdot 0.0314\,\mathrm{m^3/s} = 0.0966\,\mathrm{m^3/s} = 96.6\,\mathrm{L/s}
$$

With the gate valve half open, $\sum \xi_i = 0.5 + 0.15 + 0.15 + 5.6 + 1 = 7.40$, giving

| Trial | Assumed $\lambda$ | $v$ in m/s | $Re$ | Calculated $\lambda$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | $0.02500$ | $2.179$ | $4.36 \cdot 10^5$ | $0.02128$ |
| 2 | $0.02128$ | $2.247$ | $4.49 \cdot 10^5$ | $0.02130$ |
| 3 | $0.02130$ | $2.247$ | $4.49 \cdot 10^5$ | $0.02130$ |

$$
v = 2.25\,\mathrm{m/s} ,
\qquad
Q = 2.25 \cdot 0.0314\,\mathrm{m^3/s} = 0.0706\,\mathrm{m^3/s} = 70.6\,\mathrm{L/s}
$$
$$
\frac{96.6 - 70.6}{96.6} = 0.269
$$

To check the second case, use $\dfrac{v^2}{2g} = 0.257\,\mathrm{m}$ to obtain
$$
\Delta H_{\mathrm{cont}} = 0.0213 \cdot \frac{40}{0.200} \cdot 0.257\,\mathrm{m} = 1.10\,\mathrm{m} ,
\qquad
\Delta H_{\mathrm{loc}} = 7.40 \cdot 0.257\,\mathrm{m} = 1.90\,\mathrm{m} ,
$$
and the sum is $3.00\,\mathrm{m}$, the available elevation difference.

<div class="spiegazione">

Apply the method of the preceding exercise to the two gate-valve loss coefficients. The pipe is $200$ diameters long, giving $\lambda\dfrac{L}{D} = 4.23$. The coefficients $\lambda\dfrac{L}{D}$ and $\sum_i \xi_i$ are therefore comparable, so the fittings appreciably affect the flow.

Closing the gate valve halfway increases its loss coefficient from $0.2$ to $5.6$. The sum of the loss coefficients rises from $2.00$ to $7.40$, and the denominator under the square root increases from $6.23$ to $11.66$. The velocity is multiplied by $\sqrt{\dfrac{6.23}{11.66}}$ and the discharge falls from $96.6$ to $70.6\,\mathrm{L/s}$, a decrease of $26.9\,\%$.

Throttling the gate valve increases the minor loss, leaving less head available for pipe friction and thus reducing the velocity. In both cases the total head dissipated is $3.00\,\mathrm{m}$. Its distribution between the pipe and valve changes.

As a check, calculate the two losses in the second case separately. Their sum equals the elevation difference, **confirming the energy balance**. With the gate valve half closed, minor losses account for $63\,\%$ of the total.

</div>
