## Exercise 1

A straight pipe of diameter $D = 60\,\mathrm{mm}$ and length $L = 150\,\mathrm{m}$ carries $Q = 2\,\mathrm{L/s}$ of oil of density $\rho = 900\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}$ and kinematic viscosity $\nu = 4 \cdot 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{m^2/s}$, as shown. Determine the flow regime and find the Darcy friction factor, energy gradient, head loss, centerline velocity and wall shear stress.

::schema{nodi="A,0,1,-;B,12,1,-" condotte="A-B,0.4" frecce="4.6,1,6.2,1,Q" etichette="2.4,0.4,D = 60 mm;0.3,1.8,1;11.4,1.8,2;7.4,0.4,oil ν = 4·10⁻⁵ m²/s" quote="A,B,L = 150 m,1.3" riferimento=0 titolo="Oil flow through a pipe between sections $1$ and $2$"}

The fluid is incompressible, the flow is steady and uniform, and all pressures are gauge pressures. The oil has specific weight $\gamma = \rho g = 900 \cdot 9.81 = 8829\,\mathrm{N/m^3}$ and dynamic viscosity $\mu = \rho\,\nu = 900 \cdot 4 \cdot 10^{-5} = 0.0360\,\mathrm{Pa\,s}$.

$$
A = \frac{\pi \cdot 0.060^2}{4}\,\mathrm{m^2} = 0.00283\,\mathrm{m^2} ,
\qquad
v = \frac{0.002}{0.00283}\,\mathrm{m/s} = 0.707\,\mathrm{m/s}
$$
$$
Re = \frac{v\,D}{\nu} = \frac{0.707 \cdot 0.060}{4 \cdot 10^{-5}} = 1061
$$

The Reynolds number is $Re < 2000$, so the flow is laminar and
$$
\lambda = \frac{64}{Re} = \frac{64}{1061} = 0.0603 .
$$
$$
\frac{v^2}{2g} = \frac{0.707^2}{19.62}\,\mathrm{m} = 0.0255\,\mathrm{m}
$$
$$
J = \lambda\,\frac{1}{D}\,\frac{v^2}{2g} = 0.0603 \cdot \frac{0.0255}{0.060} = 0.0256
$$
$$
\Delta H = J\,L = 0.0256 \cdot 150\,\mathrm{m} = 3.85\,\mathrm{m}
$$

The velocity profile is parabolic in laminar flow, so
$$
v_{\max} = 2\,v = 2 \cdot 0.707\,\mathrm{m/s} = 1.41\,\mathrm{m/s} .
$$
$$
\tau_0 = \gamma\,R\,J = 8829 \cdot 0.0150 \cdot 0.0256\,\mathrm{Pa} = 3.39\,\mathrm{Pa}
$$

The Hagen–Poiseuille law provides an independent check on the energy gradient without requiring the Darcy friction factor. It gives
$$
J = \frac{32\,\nu\,v}{g\,D^2} = \frac{32 \cdot 4 \cdot 10^{-5} \cdot 0.707}{9.81 \cdot 0.060^2} = \frac{9.05 \cdot 10^{-4}}{0.0353} = 0.0256 .
$$
This agrees with the Darcy–Weisbach result.

<div class="spiegazione">

First establish the flow regime, which determines the equation for $\lambda$. The kinematic viscosity of the oil is forty times that of water, so at the same velocity and diameter the Reynolds number is forty times smaller. Here $Re = 1061$, below $2000$, so the **flow is laminar**.

In laminar flow the friction factor depends only on the Reynolds number. The wall roughness does not enter the calculation because its projections remain within the layer of fluid adjacent to the wall. The value $\lambda = 0.0603$ is about three times the friction factor for turbulent water flow in a pipe, which explains why oil pipelines require high hydraulic gradients.

The centerline velocity is twice the mean velocity. Integrating the parabolic velocity profile, with peak $v_{\max}$, over the circular section gives exactly half the volume of a cylinder of the same base area and height $v_{\max}$.

The wall shear stress follows from $\tau_0 = \gamma R J$, where $\gamma$ is the specific weight of the oil. As an independent check, use the **Hagen–Poiseuille law** to calculate the hydraulic gradient. This law follows by integrating Newton's law of viscosity over the cross section. It gives the same result as the Darcy–Weisbach calculation.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A straight pipe of diameter $D = 100\,\mathrm{mm}$ and length $L = 300\,\mathrm{m}$ carries oil with $\rho = 900\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}$ and $\nu = 5 \cdot 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{m^2/s}$. The measured head loss between its ends is $\Delta H = 4.00\,\mathrm{m}$, as shown. Find the discharge assuming laminar flow, then check whether this assumption holds.

::schema{nodi="A,0,1,-;B,12,1,-" condotte="A-B,0.45" etichette="2.4,0.35,D = 100 mm;0.3,1.8,1;11.4,1.8,2;6.4,1.9,ΔH = 4.00 m" quote="A,B,L = 300 m,1.3" riferimento=0 titolo="Oil flow through a pipe with a specified head loss"}

$$
\gamma = 8829\,\mathrm{N/m^3} ,
\qquad
\mu = \rho\,\nu = 900 \cdot 5 \cdot 10^{-5} = 0.0450\,\mathrm{Pa\,s}
$$
$$
J = \frac{\Delta H}{L} = \frac{4.00}{300} = 0.01333
$$

The Hagen–Poiseuille law expresses discharge in terms of the energy gradient, giving
$$
Q = \frac{\pi\,\gamma\,J\,D^4}{128\,\mu}
= \frac{\pi \cdot 8829 \cdot 0.01333 \cdot 0.100^4}{128 \cdot 0.0450}\,\mathrm{m^3/s}
$$
$$
Q = \frac{3.698 \cdot 10^{-2}}{5.76}\,\mathrm{m^3/s} = 6.42 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m^3/s} = 6.42\,\mathrm{L/s}
$$

Check the assumption of laminar flow by calculating the Reynolds number.
$$
A = \frac{\pi \cdot 0.100^2}{4}\,\mathrm{m^2} = 0.00785\,\mathrm{m^2} ,
\qquad
v = \frac{6.42 \cdot 10^{-3}}{0.00785}\,\mathrm{m/s} = 0.818\,\mathrm{m/s}
$$
$$
Re = \frac{0.818 \cdot 0.100}{5 \cdot 10^{-5}} = 1635 < 2000
$$

As a check, use $\lambda = \dfrac{64}{Re} = \dfrac{64}{1635} = 0.0391$ in the Darcy–Weisbach equation to obtain
$$
J = 0.0391 \cdot \frac{1}{0.100} \cdot \frac{0.818^2}{19.62} = 0.0391 \cdot \frac{0.0341}{0.100} = 0.0133 ,
$$
which recovers the specified energy gradient.

<div class="spiegazione">

This is the inverse of the preceding problem. The head loss is known and the discharge is required. In laminar flow the Hagen–Poiseuille law gives discharge directly as a function of the hydraulic gradient, with no iteration. In turbulent flow $\lambda$ depends on the velocity through the Reynolds number.

The calculation assumed laminar flow, so check that assumption against the result. The calculated mean velocity is $0.818\,\mathrm{m/s}$, giving a Reynolds number of $1635$, below $2000$. This **confirms the assumption** and the result is valid. Had the Reynolds number exceeded $4000$, the calculation would have to be repeated using the Colebrook–White equation.

Starting with the calculated discharge, find the Reynolds number and the friction factor, then use the Darcy–Weisbach equation to recover the energy gradient. Agreement with the specified gradient **confirms** the discharge.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A straight pipe of diameter $D = 150\,\mathrm{mm}$ carries oil with $\rho = 900\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}$ and $\nu = 8 \cdot 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{m^2/s}$. Two piezometers $50.0\,\mathrm{m}$ apart show a difference in head of $0.400\,\mathrm{m}$, as illustrated. Find the maximum velocity, discharge, shear stress at the wall and at distance $\dfrac{r_0}{2}$ from the centerline, and the fraction of the discharge passing through the circle of radius $\dfrac{r_0}{2}$.

::schema{nodi="A,0,1,-;B,12,1,-" condotte="A-B,0.5" linee="2,1.25 2,5.0;10,1.25 10,4.2" piezometrica="2,5.0 10,4.2" tratteggi="2,5.0 10.8,5.0" etichette="0.6,5.2,piezometers;6.0,0.3,D = 150 mm" quote="2 0,10 0,50.0 m,1.4;10.8 4.2,10.8 5.0,0.400 m,0.9" riferimento=0 titolo="Piezometric head difference measured over $50.0\,\mathrm{m}$"}

$$
\gamma = 8829\,\mathrm{N/m^3} ,
\qquad
\mu = 900 \cdot 8 \cdot 10^{-5} = 0.0720\,\mathrm{Pa\,s} ,
\qquad
r_0 = \frac{D}{2} = 0.0750\,\mathrm{m}
$$
$$
J = \frac{0.400}{50.0} = 0.00800
$$
$$
v_{\max} = \frac{\gamma\,J\,D^2}{16\,\mu} = \frac{8829 \cdot 0.00800 \cdot 0.150^2}{16 \cdot 0.0720}\,\mathrm{m/s} = \frac{1.589}{1.152}\,\mathrm{m/s} = 1.38\,\mathrm{m/s}
$$
$$
v = \frac{v_{\max}}{2} = 0.690\,\mathrm{m/s} ,
\qquad
Re = \frac{0.690 \cdot 0.150}{8 \cdot 10^{-5}} = 1294 < 2000
$$
$$
A = \frac{\pi \cdot 0.150^2}{4}\,\mathrm{m^2} = 0.01767\,\mathrm{m^2} ,
\qquad
Q = v\,A = 0.690 \cdot 0.01767\,\mathrm{m^3/s} = 0.0122\,\mathrm{m^3/s} = 12.2\,\mathrm{L/s}
$$

The shear stress is
$$
\tau_0 = \gamma\,R\,J = 8829 \cdot 0.0375 \cdot 0.00800\,\mathrm{Pa} = 2.65\,\mathrm{Pa} ,
\qquad
\tau\!\left( \frac{r_0}{2} \right) = \tau_0\,\frac{2r}{D} = \frac{\tau_0}{2} = 1.32\,\mathrm{Pa}
$$

The discharge through the central circle of radius $\dfrac{r_0}{2}$ is
$$
Q_{\mathrm{int}} = \int_0^{r_0/2} v_{\max}\left( 1 - \frac{r^2}{r_0^2} \right) 2\pi r\,dr
= 2\pi\,v_{\max}\left[ \frac{r^2}{2} - \frac{r^4}{4 r_0^2} \right]_0^{r_0/2}
= 2\pi\,v_{\max}\,r_0^2\left( \frac{1}{8} - \frac{1}{64} \right)
$$
$$
\frac{Q_{\mathrm{int}}}{Q} = \frac{2\pi\,v_{\max}\,r_0^2 \cdot \dfrac{7}{64}}{\dfrac{\pi\,v_{\max}\,r_0^2}{2}} = \frac{7}{16} = 0.4375
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
Q_{\mathrm{int}} = 0.4375 \cdot 12.2\,\mathrm{L/s} = 5.33\,\mathrm{L/s}
$$

The Hagen–Poiseuille law provides an independent check on the discharge. It gives
$$
Q = \frac{\pi \cdot 8829 \cdot 0.00800 \cdot 0.150^4}{128 \cdot 0.0720}\,\mathrm{m^3/s} = \frac{0.1123}{9.216}\,\mathrm{m^3/s} = 0.0122\,\mathrm{m^3/s} ,
$$
in agreement with the value found from $v\,A$.

<div class="spiegazione">

The difference between the two piezometer levels is the drop in piezometric head. Since the diameter is constant, this drop equals the head loss. Dividing it by the distance between the taps gives the energy gradient $J = 0.00800$.

Use the energy gradient in the parabolic velocity profile evaluated at $r = 0$ to find the centerline velocity. The mean velocity is half this maximum. It gives both the Reynolds number, which confirms laminar flow, and the discharge.

Shear stress increases linearly from the centerline to the wall, so halfway to the wall its value is half of $\tau_0$. The linear distribution follows from the force balance on a concentric cylinder of fluid and holds for any flow regime.

To answer the last question, integrate the velocity over concentric rings from the pipe axis to radius $\dfrac{r_0}{2}$. The inner circle occupies one quarter of the cross-sectional area, yet carries $43.8\,\%$ of the discharge because the velocity there is close to its maximum. In laminar pipe flow, the central region carries a disproportionately large share of the discharge.

The Hagen–Poiseuille law gives an independent value for the discharge directly from the energy gradient. Agreement with the value based on mean velocity **confirms** the result.

</div>
