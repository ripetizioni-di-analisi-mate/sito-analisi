## Exercise 1

A straight cast-iron pipe of constant diameter $D = 200\,\mathrm{mm}$ and length $L = 800\,\mathrm{m}$ carries $Q = 60\,\mathrm{L/s}$ of water at $20\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$, with $\nu = 10^{-6}\,\mathrm{m^2/s}$. The absolute roughness is $\varepsilon = 0.4\,\mathrm{mm}$. Find the mean velocity, Reynolds number, Darcy friction factor, energy gradient and friction head loss between sections $1$ and $2$ in the diagram.

::schema{nodi="A,0,1,-;B,12,1,-" condotte="A-B,0.45" frecce="4.6,1,6.2,1,Q" etichette="2.8,0.35,D = 200 mm;0.3,1.8,1;11.4,1.8,2" quote="A,B,L = 800 m,1.3" riferimento=0 titolo="Cast-iron pipe between sections $1$ and $2$"}

Assume incompressible, steady, uniform flow. All pressures are gauge pressures.

$$
A = \frac{\pi D^2}{4} = \frac{\pi \cdot 0.200^2}{4}\,\mathrm{m^2} = 0.0314\,\mathrm{m^2}
$$
$$
v = \frac{Q}{A} = \frac{0.060}{0.0314}\,\mathrm{m/s} = 1.91\,\mathrm{m/s}
$$
$$
Re = \frac{v\,D}{\nu} = \frac{1.91 \cdot 0.200}{10^{-6}} = 3.82 \cdot 10^5 ,
\qquad
\frac{\varepsilon}{D} = \frac{0.4}{200} = 0.002
$$

The flow is turbulent. Find $\lambda$ by iterating the Colebrook–White equation from an initial estimate of $\lambda_0 = 0.025$:

| Trial | Assumed $\lambda$ | Calculated $\lambda$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | $0.02500$ | $0.02387$ |
| 2 | $0.02387$ | $0.02388$ |
| 3 | $0.02388$ | $0.02388$ |

Successive estimates differ by less than $10^{-5}$, so stop the iteration and take $\lambda = 0.0239$.

$$
\frac{v^2}{2g} = \frac{1.91^2}{2 \cdot 9.81}\,\mathrm{m} = 0.186\,\mathrm{m}
$$
$$
J = \lambda\,\frac{1}{D}\,\frac{v^2}{2g} = 0.0239 \cdot \frac{0.186}{0.200} = 0.0222
$$
$$
\Delta H = J\,L = 0.0222 \cdot 800\,\mathrm{m} = 17.8\,\mathrm{m}
$$

Check the wall shear stress by two independent methods:
$$
\tau_0 = \gamma\,R\,J = 9810 \cdot 0.0500 \cdot 0.0222\,\mathrm{Pa} = 10.9\,\mathrm{Pa} ,
$$
$$
\tau_0 = \frac{\lambda}{8}\,\rho\,v^2 = \frac{0.0239}{8} \cdot 1000 \cdot 1.91^2\,\mathrm{Pa} = 10.9\,\mathrm{Pa} .
$$
The results agree, so $\lambda$ and $J$ are consistent.

<div class="spiegazione">

By definition $v = \dfrac{Q}{A}$, so mean velocity follows from discharge and area. The calculated $1.91\,\mathrm{m/s}$ lies within the usual operating range for pressurized pipes, from $0.5$ to $3\,\mathrm{m/s}$. The data are realistic.

The Reynolds number is $3.82 \cdot 10^5$, far above $4000$. The flow is turbulent, so the laminar relation $\lambda = \dfrac{64}{Re}$ does not apply. Instead, use the **Colebrook–White equation**, which contains $\lambda$ on both sides. Start with a trial value, evaluate the right-hand side and use the result as the next estimate. Two steps suffice because $\lambda$ appears only inside the logarithm and has little influence on the next estimate.

The **Darcy–Weisbach equation** gives an energy gradient of $0.0222$, so the flow loses $2.22\,\mathrm{cm}$ of head per meter of pipe. Multiplying by the length gives the total loss, $\Delta H = 17.8\,\mathrm{m}$. Because the diameter is constant, the velocity head is the same at both sections and the hydraulic grade line falls by $17.8\,\mathrm{m}$, just as the energy grade line does.

The first check uses $\tau_0 = \gamma R J$, derived from the force balance on a segment of flowing fluid. The second uses the definition of the Darcy friction factor. The two independent calculations give the same wall shear stress and **confirm** the result.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A horizontal steel pipe of diameter $D = 150\,\mathrm{mm}$ and length $L = 1200\,\mathrm{m}$ carries $Q = 35\,\mathrm{L/s}$ of water. Its absolute roughness is $\varepsilon = 0.1\,\mathrm{mm}$, and at section $1$ the gauge pressure is $p_1 = 500\,\mathrm{kPa}$. Find the head loss between sections $1$ and $2$ shown in the diagram and the pressure at section $2$.

::schema{nodi="A,0,1,-;B,12,1,-" condotte="A-B,0.4" frecce="4.6,1,6.2,1,Q" etichette="5.8,0.35,D = 150 mm;0.3,1.8,1;11.4,1.8,2;1.2,0.0,p_1 = 500 kPa" quote="A,B,L = 1200 m,1.3" riferimento=0 titolo="Horizontal steel pipe with section $2$ downstream of section $1$"}

$$
A = \frac{\pi \cdot 0.150^2}{4}\,\mathrm{m^2} = 0.01767\,\mathrm{m^2} ,
\qquad
v = \frac{0.035}{0.01767}\,\mathrm{m/s} = 1.98\,\mathrm{m/s}
$$
$$
Re = \frac{1.98 \cdot 0.150}{10^{-6}} = 2.97 \cdot 10^5 ,
\qquad
\frac{\varepsilon}{D} = \frac{0.1}{150} = 6.67 \cdot 10^{-4}
$$

| Trial | Assumed $\lambda$ | Calculated $\lambda$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | $0.02500$ | $0.01895$ |
| 2 | $0.01895$ | $0.01910$ |
| 3 | $0.01910$ | $0.01910$ |

$$
\frac{v^2}{2g} = \frac{1.98^2}{19.62}\,\mathrm{m} = 0.200\,\mathrm{m}
$$
$$
J = 0.0191 \cdot \frac{0.200}{0.150} = 0.0255 ,
\qquad
\Delta H = 0.0255 \cdot 1200\,\mathrm{m} = 30.5\,\mathrm{m}
$$

The pipe is horizontal and of constant diameter, so $z_1 = z_2$ and $v_1 = v_2$. The energy equation with head loss reduces to
$$
\frac{p_1}{\gamma} = \frac{p_2}{\gamma} + \Delta H
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
p_2 = p_1 - \gamma\,\Delta H .
$$
$$
p_2 = 500 - 9.81 \cdot 30.5\,\mathrm{kPa} = 500 - 299.2\,\mathrm{kPa} = 201\,\mathrm{kPa}
$$

Check the limiting values. In the transition region both terms in the Colebrook–White equation contribute. The friction factor must exceed both the smooth-pipe value and the fully rough value calculated at the same Reynolds number and roughness:
$$
\lambda_{\mathrm{smooth}} = 0.0145 ,
\qquad
\lambda_{\mathrm{a.t.}} = \left[ 2\,\log_{10}\!\left( \frac{3.71 \cdot 150}{0.1} \right) \right]^{-2} = 0.0178 .
$$
The result is $0.0191 > 0.0178 > 0.0145$, as expected. As the Reynolds number increases, $\lambda$ approaches the asymptote $\lambda_{\mathrm{a.t.}}$ from above.

<div class="spiegazione">

Use the same procedure as in the previous exercise to calculate the energy gradient. The relative roughness is only one third as large, so the friction factor falls from $0.0239$ to $0.0191$. The gradient is nevertheless greater because the diameter is smaller and appears in the denominator of the Darcy–Weisbach equation.

Find the pressure at section $2$ from the **energy equation with head loss**. The pipe is horizontal, so the elevations are equal and cancel. Its diameter is constant, so continuity gives equal mean velocities and the velocity heads also cancel. The remaining difference in pressure head therefore equals the head loss.

A head loss of $30.5\,\mathrm{m}$ of water corresponds to a pressure drop of $299\,\mathrm{kPa}$, nearly three atmospheres over $1200\,\mathrm{m}$ of pipe. The remaining gauge pressure at section $2$ is $201\,\mathrm{kPa}$. It is **positive**, so the pipe operates above atmospheric pressure along its whole length.

The check compares the result with both limiting cases. It exceeds both the fully rough value and the smooth-pipe value, as expected in the transition region where both viscosity and roughness matter.

</div>

## Exercise 3

The encrusted cast-iron pipe shown has $D = 300\,\mathrm{mm}$, $L = 1500\,\mathrm{m}$ and $\varepsilon = 0.6\,\mathrm{mm}$ and carries $Q = 150\,\mathrm{L/s}$ of water. Find the head loss using the Colebrook–White equation, establish whether the pipe operates in the fully rough turbulent regime, and compare the result with the Prandtl–Nikuradse law.

::schema{nodi="A,0,1,-;B,12,1,-" condotte="A-B,0.55" frecce="4.6,1,6.2,1,Q" etichette="2.6,0.25,D = 300 mm · ε = 0.6 mm;0.3,1.9,1;11.4,1.9,2" quote="A,B,L = 1500 m,1.4" riferimento=0 titolo="Encrusted cast-iron pipe"}

$$
A = \frac{\pi \cdot 0.300^2}{4}\,\mathrm{m^2} = 0.0707\,\mathrm{m^2} ,
\qquad
v = \frac{0.150}{0.0707}\,\mathrm{m/s} = 2.12\,\mathrm{m/s}
$$
$$
Re = \frac{2.12 \cdot 0.300}{10^{-6}} = 6.37 \cdot 10^5 ,
\qquad
\frac{\varepsilon}{D} = \frac{0.6}{300} = 0.002
$$

| Trial | Assumed $\lambda$ | Calculated $\lambda$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | $0.02500$ | $0.02369$ |
| 2 | $0.02369$ | $0.02370$ |
| 3 | $0.02370$ | $0.02370$ |

$$
\frac{v^2}{2g} = \frac{2.12^2}{19.62}\,\mathrm{m} = 0.230\,\mathrm{m}
$$
$$
J = 0.0237 \cdot \frac{0.230}{0.300} = 0.0181 ,
\qquad
\Delta H = 0.0181 \cdot 1500\,\mathrm{m} = 27.2\,\mathrm{m}
$$

Check the flow regime:
$$
Re\,\sqrt{\lambda}\;\frac{\varepsilon}{D} = 6.37 \cdot 10^5 \cdot \sqrt{0.0237} \cdot 0.002 = 196 .
$$

As an independent check, the Prandtl–Nikuradse law gives
$$
\lambda_{\mathrm{a.t.}} = \left[ 2\,\log_{10}\!\left( \frac{3.71 \cdot 300}{0.6} \right) \right]^{-2}
= \left( 2 \cdot 3.2683 \right)^{-2} = 0.0234 ,
$$
so $\Delta H_{\mathrm{a.t.}} = 27.2 \cdot \dfrac{0.0234}{0.0237}\,\mathrm{m} = 26.9\,\mathrm{m}$, a difference of $1.3\,\%$ from the calculated value.

<div class="spiegazione">

Calculate the energy gradient as in the two preceding exercises. The pipe is encrusted cast iron, with absolute roughness six times that of the steel in Exercise $2$. Its diameter is twice as large, so the relative roughness is again $0.002$ and its friction factor is close to that in Exercise $1$.

The parameter $Re\sqrt{\lambda}\,\dfrac{\varepsilon}{D}$ is $196$, just below the threshold $200$ for fully rough turbulent flow. The pipe therefore operates at the upper limit of the transition region, where viscosity has almost ceased to affect the result.

The check quantifies this conclusion. The **Prandtl–Nikuradse law** neglects viscosity entirely and gives $\lambda = 0.0234$ compared with $0.0237$, changing the head loss from $27.2$ to $26.9\,\mathrm{m}$. The difference of $1.3\,\%$ is smaller than the uncertainty in the roughness of an in-service pipe. The power law is therefore **sufficient** for this type of pipe and avoids iteration.

</div>
