## Exercise 1

A pipe of diameter $D = 200\,\mathrm{mm}$ and Darcy friction factor $\lambda = 0.022$ carries $Q = 50\,\mathrm{L/s}$ of water. The diagram shows a sharp-edged entrance ($\xi = 0.5$), two $90^\circ$ bends ($\xi = 0.3$ each), a fully open gate valve ($\xi = 0.2$), a check valve ($\xi = 2.5$) and an exit ($\xi = 1$). Find the equivalent length of each fitting and their total equivalent length.

::schema{nodi="A,1,4.6,-;B,1,0.8,-;C,11,0.8,-;D,11,2.6,-" condotte="A-B-C-D,0.4" acqua="-1.4,4.1 1.6,4.1 1.6,6.2 -1.4,6.2;9.4,2.2 12.4,2.2 12.4,3.6 9.4,3.6" pelo="-1.4,1.6,6.2,;9.4,12.4,3.6," linee="-1.4,6.6 -1.4,4.1 1.6,4.1 1.6,6.6;9.4,4.0 9.4,2.2 12.4,2.2 12.4,4.0" etichette="3.0,4.3,inlet;1.9,1.3,bend;9.3,1.3,bend;4.3,1.3,gate valve;7.0,1.3,check valve;8.2,2.9,outlet;5.6,-0.05,D = 200 mm · λ = 0.022" riferimento=0 titolo="Pipe with six fittings"}

$$
\frac{D}{\lambda} = \frac{0.200}{0.022}\,\mathrm{m} = 9.09\,\mathrm{m}
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
L_{\mathrm{eq}} = \xi\,\frac{D}{\lambda} = 9.09\,\xi \ \ \mathrm{m}
$$

| Fitting | $\xi$ | $L_{\mathrm{eq}}$ in m | $\dfrac{L_{\mathrm{eq}}}{D}$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Sharp-edged entrance | $0.5$ | $4.55$ | $23$ |
| First bend | $0.3$ | $2.73$ | $14$ |
| Second bend | $0.3$ | $2.73$ | $14$ |
| Open gate valve | $0.2$ | $1.82$ | $9$ |
| Check valve | $2.5$ | $22.7$ | $114$ |
| Outlet | $1.0$ | $9.09$ | $45$ |
| Total | $4.8$ | $43.6$ | $218$ |

As a check, $v = \dfrac{0.050}{0.0314} = 1.59\,\mathrm{m/s}$ gives $\dfrac{v^2}{2g} = 0.129\,\mathrm{m}$, so
$$
\Delta H_{\mathrm{loc}} = \sum_i \xi_i\,\frac{v^2}{2g} = 4.8 \cdot 0.129\,\mathrm{m} = 0.620\,\mathrm{m} ,
$$
$$
\lambda\,\frac{L_{\mathrm{eq}}}{D}\,\frac{v^2}{2g} = 0.022 \cdot \frac{43.6}{0.200} \cdot 0.129\,\mathrm{m} = 0.620\,\mathrm{m} ,
$$
which agrees with the earlier value.

<div class="spiegazione">

Equate the head loss in the fitting to the friction loss along a straight pipe of the same diameter. The velocity head cancels, so the equivalent length $L_{\mathrm{eq}}$ is the pipe diameter multiplied by the ratio $\dfrac{\xi}{\lambda}$. For the specified coefficients it is independent of discharge.

Expressed in pipe diameters, the outlet has an equivalent length of $45$ and the check valve has an equivalent length of $114$. In a $200\,\mathrm{mm}$ pipe, a single check valve produces as much head loss as $22.7\,\mathrm{m}$ of straight pipe. Together, the six fittings are equivalent to $43.6\,\mathrm{m}$ of straight pipe.

The equivalent length expressed in pipe diameters is independent of diameter and therefore applies to geometrically similar systems. Doubling $D$ doubles $L_{\mathrm{eq}}$ because the ratio $\dfrac{L_{\mathrm{eq}}}{D}$ remains equal to $\dfrac{\xi}{\lambda}$.

Calculate the total minor loss from the sum of the loss coefficients and again from the equivalent length. Agreement **confirms** that the two representations give the same loss.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A pipe of diameter $D = 150\,\mathrm{mm}$ with $\lambda = 0.024$ has a sharp-edged entrance, two bends ($\xi = 0.25$ each), an open gate valve ($\xi = 0.2$) and an exit. The mean velocity is $v = 2.00\,\mathrm{m/s}$. Find the fraction of total head loss due to minor losses for pipe lengths $20\,\mathrm{m}$ and $500\,\mathrm{m}$, as shown.

::schema{nodi="A,0,3,-;B,8,3,-;C,0,0.8,-;E,12,0.8,-" condotte="A-B,0.45;C-E,0.45" etichette="3.4,2.3,L = 20 m;5.4,0.1,L = 500 m;9.0,4.0,D = 150 mm · λ = 0.024" riferimento=0 titolo="The same fittings on two pipes of very different lengths"}

$$
\sum_i \xi_i = 0.5 + 0.25 + 0.25 + 0.2 + 1 = 2.20 ,
\qquad
\frac{v^2}{2g} = \frac{2.00^2}{19.62}\,\mathrm{m} = 0.204\,\mathrm{m}
$$

For the pipe of length $20\,\mathrm{m}$, the results are
$$
\lambda\,\frac{L}{D} = 0.024 \cdot \frac{20}{0.150} = 3.20 ,
\qquad
\Delta H_{\mathrm{cont}} = 3.20 \cdot 0.204\,\mathrm{m} = 0.653\,\mathrm{m} ,
\qquad
\Delta H_{\mathrm{loc}} = 2.20 \cdot 0.204\,\mathrm{m} = 0.449\,\mathrm{m}
$$
$$
\frac{\Delta H_{\mathrm{loc}}}{\Delta H} = \frac{2.20}{2.20 + 3.20} = 0.407
$$

For the pipe of length $500\,\mathrm{m}$, the results are
$$
\lambda\,\frac{L}{D} = 0.024 \cdot \frac{500}{0.150} = 80.0 ,
\qquad
\Delta H_{\mathrm{cont}} = 80.0 \cdot 0.204\,\mathrm{m} = 16.3\,\mathrm{m} ,
\qquad
\Delta H_{\mathrm{loc}} = 0.449\,\mathrm{m}
$$
$$
\frac{\Delta H_{\mathrm{loc}}}{\Delta H} = \frac{2.20}{2.20 + 80.0} = 0.0268
$$

As a check, calculate the equivalent length of the fittings, giving
$$
L_{\mathrm{eq}} = 2.20 \cdot \frac{0.150}{0.024}\,\mathrm{m} = 13.75\,\mathrm{m} ,
$$
so the fraction due to minor losses is $\dfrac{L_{\mathrm{eq}}}{L + L_{\mathrm{eq}}}$, or $\dfrac{13.75}{33.75} = 0.407$ in the first case and $\dfrac{13.75}{513.75} = 0.0268$ in the second, in agreement with the earlier results.

<div class="spiegazione">

Minor losses depend on the fittings and the velocity, both of which are the same in the two cases. Only the friction loss changes, increasing in proportion to the pipe length.

Compare the two coefficients that multiply the velocity head. The minor loss coefficient is $\sum \xi_i = 2.20$. The friction coefficient for the full pipe is $\lambda\dfrac{L}{D}$, with values of $3.20$ and $80.0$ in the two cases. The coefficients are comparable in the short pipe, where minor losses account for $40.7\,\%$ of the total. In the long pipe, the share falls to $2.7\,\%$.

Pipe length in diameters helps determine when minor losses matter. The short pipe is $133$ diameters long and the long pipe is $3333$ diameters long. In water transmission mains thousands of diameters long, neglecting the fittings introduces an error of about one percent, comparable to the uncertainty in roughness. In building piping and pumping stations, calculate both friction and minor losses.

Check the fraction geometrically by dividing the equivalent length by the sum of the actual and equivalent lengths. Agreement with the head-loss fractions **confirms** the calculation.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Two reservoirs with a water-surface elevation difference of $\Delta z = 10.00\,\mathrm{m}$ are connected by a cast-iron pipe with $D = 250\,\mathrm{mm}$, $L = 300\,\mathrm{m}$ and $\varepsilon = 0.4\,\mathrm{mm}$. The diagram shows a sharp-edged entrance ($\xi = 0.5$), two bends ($\xi = 0.3$ each), an open gate valve ($\xi = 0.2$) and an exit ($\xi = 1$). Find the discharge using the equivalent length and compare it with the discharge obtained by neglecting minor losses.

::schema{nodi="A,1,4.6,-;B,1,0.8,-;C,11,0.8,-;D,11,2.2,-" condotte="A-B-C-D,0.45" acqua="-1.4,4.1 1.6,4.1 1.6,6.4 -1.4,6.4;9.4,1.8 12.4,1.8 12.4,3.4 9.4,3.4" pelo="-1.4,1.6,6.4,;9.4,12.4,3.4," linee="-1.4,6.8 -1.4,4.1 1.6,4.1 1.6,6.8;9.4,3.8 9.4,1.8 12.4,1.8 12.4,3.8" tratteggi="1.6,6.4 13.4,6.4" quote="13.4 3.4,13.4 6.4,Δz = 10.00 m,0.9" etichette="3.0,4.3,inlet;2.1,1.25,bend;8.0,1.25,bend;5.0,1.25,gate valve;8.2,2.6,outlet;5.4,0.0,D = 250 mm · L = 300 m" riferimento=0 titolo="Pipe between two reservoirs with a head difference of $\Delta z = 10.00\,\mathrm{m}$"}

$$
\sum_i \xi_i = 0.5 + 0.3 + 0.3 + 0.2 + 1 = 2.30 ,
\qquad
\frac{\varepsilon}{D} = \frac{0.4}{250} = 1.60 \cdot 10^{-3}
$$

Replacing the fittings with their equivalent length of straight pipe gives the following energy balance between the reservoir free surfaces.
$$
\Delta z = \lambda\,\frac{L + L_{\mathrm{eq}}}{D}\,\frac{v^2}{2g}
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
v = \sqrt{\frac{2\,g\,\Delta z\,D}{\lambda\,(L + L_{\mathrm{eq}})}} ,
\qquad
L_{\mathrm{eq}} = \sum_i \xi_i\,\frac{D}{\lambda} .
$$

| Trial | Assumed $\lambda$ | $L_{\mathrm{eq}}$ in m | $v$ in m/s | $Re$ | Calculated $\lambda$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | $0.02500$ | $23.00$ | $2.465$ | $6.16 \cdot 10^5$ | $0.02241$ |
| 2 | $0.02241$ | $25.66$ | $2.592$ | $6.48 \cdot 10^5$ | $0.02241$ |

$$
v = 2.59\,\mathrm{m/s} ,
\qquad
A = \frac{\pi \cdot 0.250^2}{4}\,\mathrm{m^2} = 0.0491\,\mathrm{m^2} ,
\qquad
Q = 2.59 \cdot 0.0491\,\mathrm{m^3/s} = 0.127\,\mathrm{m^3/s} = 127\,\mathrm{L/s}
$$

Neglecting minor losses by setting $L_{\mathrm{eq}} = 0$ gives $v = 2.70\,\mathrm{m/s}$ and a discharge of $Q = 132.6\,\mathrm{L/s}$ compared with $127.3\,\mathrm{L/s}$. The predicted discharge is then $4.2\,\%$ higher.

As a check, $v = 2.592\,\mathrm{m/s}$ gives $\dfrac{v^2}{2g} = 0.343\,\mathrm{m}$, so
$$
\Delta H = \left( \lambda\,\frac{L}{D} + \sum_i \xi_i \right)\frac{v^2}{2g}
= \left( 0.02241 \cdot 1200 + 2.30 \right) \cdot 0.343\,\mathrm{m} = 29.19 \cdot 0.343\,\mathrm{m} = 10.0\,\mathrm{m} ,
$$
which equals the available elevation difference.

<div class="spiegazione">

The equivalent length allows the energy balance to be written using only the Darcy–Weisbach equation. Add the length of straight pipe equivalent to the fittings to the actual length. Since the equivalent length depends on $\lambda$, update it together with the friction factor at each iteration.

The calculation converges on the second iteration because velocity varies with $\lambda$ as $\lambda^{-1/2}$, and the equivalent length, $25.7\,\mathrm{m}$, is less than one tenth of the actual pipe length.

Omitting the fittings would overestimate the discharge by $4.2\,\%$. At $1200$ diameters long, the pipe is near the lower end of the range where the long-pipeline approximation is reasonable. The relative discharge error is about half the relative head-loss error because head loss varies with the square of velocity.

Check the energy balance in its original form, using the sum of the loss coefficients in place of the equivalent length. The total head loss is $10.0\,\mathrm{m}$, equal to the elevation difference. This **confirms** the result.

</div>
