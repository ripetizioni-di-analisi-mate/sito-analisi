## Exercise 1

A concrete pipe of diameter $D = 400\,\mathrm{mm}$ and length $L = 1500\,\mathrm{m}$ with roughness coefficient $k_s = 80\,\mathrm{m^{1/3}/s}$ carries $Q = 120\,\mathrm{L/s}$ of water, as shown. Find the energy gradient and head loss using the Gauckler–Strickler equation, then determine the corresponding Darcy friction factor and Chézy coefficient.

::schema{nodi="A,0,1,-;B,12,1,-" condotte="A-B,0.5" frecce="4.6,1,6.2,1,Q" etichette="2.2,0.05,D = 400 mm;7.4,0.05,k_s = 80 m¹ᐟ³/s;0.3,1.9,1;11.4,1.9,2" quote="A,B,L = 1500 m,1.3" riferimento=0 titolo="Concrete pipe between sections $1$ and $2$"}

$$
A = \frac{\pi \cdot 0.400^2}{4}\,\mathrm{m^2} = 0.1257\,\mathrm{m^2} ,
\qquad
v = \frac{0.120}{0.1257}\,\mathrm{m/s} = 0.955\,\mathrm{m/s}
$$
$$
R = \frac{D}{4} = \frac{0.400}{4}\,\mathrm{m} = 0.100\,\mathrm{m} ,
\qquad
R^{\frac{4}{3}} = 0.100^{\frac{4}{3}} = 0.0464
$$
$$
J = \frac{v^2}{k_s^2\,R^{\frac{4}{3}}} = \frac{0.955^2}{80^2 \cdot 0.0464} = \frac{0.912}{297}  = 0.00307
$$
$$
\Delta H = J\,L = 0.00307 \cdot 1500\,\mathrm{m} = 4.61\,\mathrm{m}
$$

The corresponding Darcy friction factor and Chézy coefficient are
$$
\lambda = \frac{2\,g\,D\,J}{v^2} = \frac{2 \cdot 9.81 \cdot 0.400 \cdot 0.00307}{0.912} = 0.0264
$$
$$
\chi = \sqrt{\frac{8g}{\lambda}} = \sqrt{\frac{78.48}{0.0264}}\,\mathrm{m^{1/2}/s} = 54.5\,\mathrm{m^{1/2}/s}
$$

As a check, calculate the Chézy coefficient directly from the Strickler coefficient, giving
$$
\chi = k_s\,R^{\frac{1}{6}} = 80 \cdot 0.100^{\frac{1}{6}}\,\mathrm{m^{1/2}/s} = 80 \cdot 0.681\,\mathrm{m^{1/2}/s} = 54.5\,\mathrm{m^{1/2}/s} ,
$$
which agrees with the value obtained from the Darcy friction factor.

<div class="spiegazione">

The Gauckler–Strickler equation uses hydraulic radius. First calculate $R = \dfrac{A}{C}$, which equals $\dfrac{D}{4}$ for a full circular pipe.

Solving for the energy gradient gives $J$ directly. This is useful in design calculations that require trying several pipe diameters. The head loss over $1500\,\mathrm{m}$ is $4.61\,\mathrm{m}$.

Solving the Darcy–Weisbach equation for the equivalent friction factor gives $0.0264$, consistent with a concrete wall. The Chézy coefficient follows from this friction factor.

As a check, calculate the Chézy coefficient directly from $\chi = k_s R^{\frac{1}{6}}$, the relation obtained by comparing the two equations. The two values agree, **confirming the conversion** between the power-law formula and the Darcy–Weisbach equation. The power-law formula applies because the given roughness and velocity place the pipe in the fully rough turbulent regime.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A pipe of diameter $D = 500\,\mathrm{mm}$ and length $L = 2000\,\mathrm{m}$, with Chézy coefficient $\chi = 60\,\mathrm{m^{1/2}/s}$, carries $Q = 250\,\mathrm{L/s}$ of water, as shown. Find the energy gradient, head loss, Darcy friction factor and equivalent Strickler coefficient.

::schema{nodi="A,0,1,-;B,12,1,-" condotte="A-B,0.6" frecce="4.6,1,6.2,1,Q = 250 L/s" etichette="2.2,0.0,D = 500 mm;7.6,0.0,χ = 60 m¹ᐟ²/s" quote="A,B,L = 2000 m,1.7" riferimento=0 titolo="Pipe with a specified Chézy coefficient"}

$$
A = \frac{\pi \cdot 0.500^2}{4}\,\mathrm{m^2} = 0.196\,\mathrm{m^2} ,
\qquad
v = \frac{0.250}{0.196}\,\mathrm{m/s} = 1.27\,\mathrm{m/s} ,
\qquad
R = 0.125\,\mathrm{m}
$$

Solving the Chézy equation for the energy gradient gives
$$
J = \frac{v^2}{\chi^2\,R} = \frac{1.27^2}{60^2 \cdot 0.125} = \frac{1.621}{450} = 0.00360 ,
$$
$$
\Delta H = 0.00360 \cdot 2000\,\mathrm{m} = 7.21\,\mathrm{m} .
$$
$$
\lambda = \frac{8g}{\chi^2} = \frac{78.48}{3600} = 0.0218 ,
\qquad
k_s = \frac{\chi}{R^{\frac{1}{6}}} = \frac{60}{0.707}\,\mathrm{m^{1/3}/s} = 84.9\,\mathrm{m^{1/3}/s}
$$

As a check, use $\lambda = 0.0218$ in the Darcy–Weisbach equation to obtain
$$
J = \lambda\,\frac{1}{D}\,\frac{v^2}{2g} = 0.0218 \cdot \frac{1}{0.500} \cdot \frac{1.621}{19.62} = 0.0218 \cdot \frac{0.0826}{0.500} = 0.00360 ,
$$
which agrees with the Chézy energy gradient.

<div class="spiegazione">

The Chézy equation gives the mean velocity as $\chi\sqrt{RJ}$. Squaring and solving for $J$ gives $J = \dfrac{v^2}{\chi^2 R}$. Since the coefficient $\chi$ is specified, the calculation is direct.

The relation $\chi = \sqrt{\dfrac{8g}{\lambda}}$ gives the corresponding Darcy friction factor, $0.0218$, a typical value for a metal pipe in service. The equivalent Strickler coefficient, $84.9\,\mathrm{m^{1/3}/s}$, is obtained by dividing $\chi$ by $R^{\frac{1}{6}}$.

The Chézy coefficient $\chi$ depends on hydraulic radius as well as wall roughness, so it can vary even between pipes of the same material. The Strickler coefficient $k_s$ depends only on the wall, and it is this coefficient that is tabulated by material.

Check the energy gradient using the Darcy–Weisbach equation with the friction factor obtained from $\chi$. Agreement **confirms** the conversion between the two formulas.

</div>

## Exercise 3

In a cast-iron pipe of diameter $D = 300\,\mathrm{mm}$ that has been in service for many years, a head loss of $\Delta H = 4.20\,\mathrm{m}$ is measured along a straight section of length $L = 600\,\mathrm{m}$ at discharge $Q = 80\,\mathrm{L/s}$, as shown. Find the Strickler coefficient, Darcy friction factor and equivalent roughness. Compare them with those for new cast iron, for which $k_s = 95\,\mathrm{m^{1/3}/s}$.

::schema{nodi="A,0,1,-;B,12,1,-" condotte="A-B,0.5" linee="1.5,1.25 1.5,5.2;10.5,1.25 10.5,3.6" piezometrica="1.5,5.2 10.5,3.6" tratteggi="1.5,5.2 11.3,5.2" etichette="0.2,5.4,piezometers;5.4,0.3,D = 300 mm · Q = 80 L/s" quote="1.5 0,10.5 0,L = 600 m,1.4;11.3 3.6,11.3 5.2,ΔH = 4.20 m,0.9" riferimento=0 titolo="Head-loss measurement in a pipe in service"}

$$
A = 0.0707\,\mathrm{m^2} ,
\qquad
v = \frac{0.080}{0.0707}\,\mathrm{m/s} = 1.13\,\mathrm{m/s} ,
\qquad
R = 0.0750\,\mathrm{m} ,
\qquad
J = \frac{4.20}{600} = 0.00700
$$

Solving the Gauckler–Strickler equation for $k_s$ gives
$$
k_s = \frac{v}{R^{\frac{2}{3}}\,\sqrt{J}} = \frac{1.13}{0.0750^{\frac{2}{3}} \cdot \sqrt{0.00700}} = \frac{1.13}{0.178 \cdot 0.0837}\,\mathrm{m^{1/3}/s} = 76.0\,\mathrm{m^{1/3}/s}
$$
$$
\lambda = \frac{2\,g\,D\,J}{v^2} = \frac{2 \cdot 9.81 \cdot 0.300 \cdot 0.00700}{1.281} = 0.0322
$$

The flow is fully rough turbulent, so the Prandtl–Nikuradse relation gives the equivalent roughness as
$$
\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda}} = 2\,\log_{10}\!\left( \frac{3.71\,D}{\varepsilon} \right)
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
\varepsilon = 3.71\,D \cdot 10^{-\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\lambda}}}
$$
$$
\varepsilon = 3.71 \cdot 0.300 \cdot 10^{-2.788}\,\mathrm{m} = 1.113 \cdot 1.63 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m} = 1.81\,\mathrm{mm}
$$

Check the flow regime using the Reynolds number $Re = \dfrac{1.13 \cdot 0.300}{10^{-6}} = 3.40 \cdot 10^5$, which gives
$$
Re\,\sqrt{\lambda}\;\frac{\varepsilon}{D} = 3.40 \cdot 10^5 \cdot 0.179 \cdot \frac{1.81}{300} = 367 > 200 ,
$$
confirming fully rough turbulent flow. With $\varepsilon = 1.81\,\mathrm{mm}$ and this Reynolds number, the Colebrook–White equation gives $\lambda = 0.0324$, differing from the measured value by $0.6\,\%$.

<div class="spiegazione">

The field measurement gives the energy gradient. The discharge and pipe diameter give the mean velocity. With these two quantities, the Gauckler–Strickler equation solved for $k_s$ gives the effective roughness coefficient of the pipe.

The measured coefficient, $76.0\,\mathrm{m^{1/3}/s}$, is substantially lower than the value $95\,\mathrm{m^{1/3}/s}$ for new cast iron. Since the energy gradient varies as $k_s^{-2}$, the head loss at a given discharge has increased by a factor of $\left( \dfrac{95}{76.0} \right)^2 = 1.56$, or by $56\,\%$ relative to a new pipe. Deposits have therefore increased the resistance by more than half.

Solving the Prandtl–Nikuradse relation for equivalent roughness gives $1.81\,\mathrm{mm}$, typical of cast-iron pipe with deposits. New cast iron has a roughness of $0.25$–$0.50\,\mathrm{mm}$.

The dimensionless group $Re\sqrt{\lambda}\,\dfrac{\varepsilon}{D}$ is $367$, above the threshold of $200$, so using the Prandtl–Nikuradse relation to find $\varepsilon$ is **justified**. Inserting that roughness into the Colebrook–White equation reproduces the measured friction factor to within $0.6\,\%$.

</div>
