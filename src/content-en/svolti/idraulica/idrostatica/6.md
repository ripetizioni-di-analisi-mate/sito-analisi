## Exercise 1

The closed water-filled pipe shown is at rest and connects to an open reservoir with surface elevation $8.00\,\mathrm{m}$. Points $A$, $B$, and $C$ lie at elevations $3.00\,\mathrm{m}$, $8.00\,\mathrm{m}$, and $11.50\,\mathrm{m}$. Find the gauge pressure at each point and the absolute pressure at $C$.

::schema{nodi="A,2,3;B,4.4,8;C,6.8,11.5" condotte="A-B-C,0.4" acqua="0,2.4 2,2.4 2,8 0,8" solidi="-0.35,2.05 2.35,2.05 2.35,2.4 -0.35,2.4;-0.35,2.4 0,2.4 0,8.6 -0.35,8.6;2,2.4 2.35,2.4 2.35,8.6 2,8.6" pelo="0,2,8" tratteggi="-0.7,8 7.6,8" etichette="1.2,9.1,free surface z = 8.00 m;4.3,2.6,z_A = 3.00 m;6.1,7.2,z_B = 8.00 m;5.2,12.1,z_C = 11.50 m" riferimento=0 titolo="Closed pipe containing stationary water, connected to a reservoir whose free surface is at elevation $8.00\,\mathrm{m}$"}

The pipe and reservoir contain one connected body of water at rest, so the piezometric head is the same throughout. At the open reservoir surface the gauge pressure is zero, giving
$$
h = 8.00\,\mathrm{m} ,
$$
and the plane of zero gauge pressure is horizontal at elevation $8.00\,\mathrm{m}$. Use $p = \gamma\,(h - z)$ to find the pressures at the three points.
$$
\begin{aligned}
p_A &= 9.81 \cdot (8.00 - 3.00) = 49.1\,\mathrm{kPa} \\[6pt]
p_B &= 9.81 \cdot (8.00 - 8.00) = 0 \\[6pt]
p_C &= 9.81 \cdot (8.00 - 11.50) = -34.3\,\mathrm{kPa}
\end{aligned}
$$
The absolute pressure at $C$ is
$$
p_{C,\mathrm{abs}} = p_C + p_{\mathrm{atm}} = -34.3 + 101.3 = 67.0\,\mathrm{kPa} .
$$

Check the absolute pressure at $C$ using the absolute-pressure reference plane, which lies $\dfrac{p_{\mathrm{atm}}}{\gamma} = 10.33\,\mathrm{m}$ above the gauge-pressure reference plane.
$$
h_{\mathrm{abs}} = 8.00 + 10.33 = 18.33\,\mathrm{m} ,
\qquad
p_{C,\mathrm{abs}} = 9.81 \cdot (18.33 - 11.50) = 67.0\,\mathrm{kPa} .
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The reservoir is open, so its free surface at elevation $8.00\,\mathrm{m}$ is the plane of zero gauge pressure. The closed pipe is full of water connected to the reservoir and has the same piezometric head.

Point $A$ lies $5.00\,\mathrm{m}$ below the plane of zero gauge pressure. Its gauge pressure is therefore positive and equals $49.1\,\mathrm{kPa}$. Point $B$ lies on the plane itself, so its gauge pressure is zero. Water would not rise in a piezometer tapped at $B$.

Point $C$ is $3.50\,\mathrm{m}$ above the plane of zero gauge pressure. Its gauge pressure is therefore negative, **$-34.3\,\mathrm{kPa}$**, corresponding to a pressure deficit of $3.50\,\mathrm{m}$ of water head. Its absolute pressure remains positive at $67.0\,\mathrm{kPa}$, and the water at $C$ remains liquid under the stated conditions.

Repeat the calculation with absolute pressures. The zero-absolute-pressure reference plane is $10.33\,\mathrm{m}$ higher, and the vertical distance from $C$ to that plane gives the same absolute pressure.

</div>

## Exercise 2

An open reservoir with free-surface elevation $100.00\,\mathrm{m}$ connects to the rising closed tube shown, full of still water. Given $p_{\mathrm{atm}} = 101.3\,\mathrm{kPa}$ and absolute vapor pressure $p_v = 2.34\,\mathrm{kPa}$ for water at $20\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$, find the maximum elevation at which water can remain liquid.

::schema{nodi="T,4,97,-;U,6,97,-;V,6,111.5,-" condotte="T-U-V,0.35" acqua="0,95.8 4,95.8 4,100 0,100" solidi="-0.4,95.4 4.4,95.4 4.4,95.8 -0.4,95.8;-0.4,95.8 0,95.8 0,100.8 -0.4,100.8;4,95.8 4.4,95.8 4.4,96.8 4,96.8;4,97.2 4.4,97.2 4.4,100.8 4,100.8" pelo="0,4,100" tratteggi="-0.8,100 7.6,100" etichette="2,101.6,free surface z = 100.00 m;6,112.4,closed end;2,98.6,reservoir" riferimento=0 titolo="Closed pipe connected to an open tank, showing the limit to which the water remains liquid"}

In an open reservoir the plane of zero gauge pressure is at the free surface. The plane obtained by extrapolating to zero absolute pressure lies above it by
$$
\frac{p_{\mathrm{atm}}}{\gamma} = \frac{101.3}{9.81} = 10.33\,\mathrm{m} ,
$$
i.e. at the elevation
$$
h_{\mathrm{abs}} = 100.00 + 10.33 = 110.33\,\mathrm{m} .
$$
At elevation $z$ the absolute pressure is $p_{\mathrm{abs}} = \gamma\,(h_{\mathrm{abs}} - z)$. Liquid water can be maintained while this exceeds its vapor pressure. At the limiting elevation, set $p_{\mathrm{abs}} = p_v$.
$$
z_{\max} = h_{\mathrm{abs}} - \frac{p_v}{\gamma} = 110.33 - \frac{2.34}{9.81} = 110.33 - 0.24 = 110.09\,\mathrm{m} ,
$$
or $10.09\,\mathrm{m}$ above the free surface in the reservoir.

Check by directly calculating the greatest allowable pressure deficit.
$$
p_{\mathrm{rel},\min} = p_v - p_{\mathrm{atm}} = 2.34 - 101.3 = -98.96\,\mathrm{kPa} ,
\qquad
\frac{p_{\mathrm{rel},\min}}{\gamma} = -10.09\,\mathrm{m} .
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

At the free surface the absolute pressure is atmospheric. Its equivalent water head, $\dfrac{p_{\mathrm{atm}}}{\gamma}$, is the vertical separation between the absolute-pressure and gauge-pressure reference planes.

Absolute pressure decreases by $\gamma$ for every meter of upward elevation. At vapor pressure, water begins to vaporize and the continuous liquid column breaks.

The limiting elevation is **$110.09\,\mathrm{m}$**, or $10.09\,\mathrm{m}$ above the free surface. This is the theoretical suction lift for water at $20\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$. It explains why pumps are commonly installed only a few meters above the suction reservoir.

The same limit can be checked in gauge-pressure terms. The greatest allowable pressure deficit is $-98.96\,\mathrm{kPa}$, corresponding to $-10.09\,\mathrm{m}$ of water head.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A closed tank holds water to depth $2.00\,\mathrm{m}$ under air maintained at subatmospheric gauge pressure $p_0 = -35\,\mathrm{kPa}$, as shown. Locate the hydrostatic head plane, find the pressure at bottom point $F$, and determine whether any point in the liquid has zero gauge pressure.

::schema{acqua="0,0 3.6,0 3.6,2 0,2" solidi="-0.3,-0.3 3.9,-0.3 3.9,0 -0.3,0;-0.3,0 0,0 0,2.8 -0.3,2.8;3.6,0 3.9,0 3.9,2.8 3.6,2.8;-0.3,2.8 3.9,2.8 3.9,3.1 -0.3,3.1" pelo="0,3.6,2" punti="1.8,0,F" etichette="1.8,2.45,air  p_0 = −35 kPa" quote="0 2,0 0,2.00 m,-0.8" riferimento=0 titolo="Closed tank with subatmospheric air pressure above the free surface"}

The gauge pressure at the free surface is negative. Its plane of zero gauge pressure therefore lies below the surface by
$$
\frac{|p_0|}{\gamma} = \frac{35}{9.81} = 3.57\,\mathrm{m} .
$$
The bottom is $2.00\,\mathrm{m}$ below the surface but still $3.57 - 2.00 = 1.57\,\mathrm{m}$ above the plane of zero gauge pressure, so its gauge pressure is negative.
$$
p_F = p_0 + \gamma \cdot 2.00 = -35 + 19.62 = -15.4\,\mathrm{kPa} .
$$
The hydrostatic head plane lies $1.57\,\mathrm{m}$ below the bottom, outside the water. Every point in the water therefore has pressure below atmospheric pressure.

Check the bottom pressure by multiplying $\gamma$ by the signed vertical distance below the plane of zero gauge pressure.
$$
p_F = \gamma \cdot (-1.57) = 9.81 \cdot (-1.567) = -15.4\,\mathrm{kPa} .
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The air over the water is below atmospheric pressure, imposing a negative gauge pressure at the free surface. Its plane of zero gauge pressure lies $\dfrac{|p_0|}{\gamma} = 3.57\,\mathrm{m}$ below the surface.

Descending through the water increases pressure by $\gamma$ per meter, giving a bottom pressure of $-35 + 19.62 = -15.4\,\mathrm{kPa}$. The pressure deficit is smaller at the bottom but remains positive in magnitude. The water column is only $2.00\,\mathrm{m}$ tall and would need to be $3.57$ tall to restore atmospheric pressure.

Since the reference plane lies below the bottom, **every point in the liquid has negative gauge pressure**. The largest pressure deficit has magnitude $35\,\mathrm{kPa}$, equivalent to $3.57\,\mathrm{m}$ of water head. This is within the allowable limit $10.09\,\mathrm{m}$, so the stated condition is physically possible.

As a check, apply the hydrostatic head relation at the bottom. The signed depth is negative because the bottom lies above the reference plane, giving the same pressure.

</div>
