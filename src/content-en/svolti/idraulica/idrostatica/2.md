## Exercise 1

Mineral oil has specific gravity $\delta = 0.88$ and dynamic viscosity $\mu = 0.22\,\mathrm{Pa\,s}$. The tank shown holds $V = 2.50\,\mathrm{m^3}$ of oil. Find its density, specific weight, kinematic viscosity, and the weight of the oil in the tank.

::schema{acqua="0,0 3,0 3,2.2 0,2.2" solidi="-0.3,-0.3 3.3,-0.3 3.3,0 -0.3,0;-0.3,0 0,0 0,2.6 -0.3,2.6;3,0 3.3,0 3.3,2.6 3,2.6" pelo="0,3,2.2" etichette="1.5,1.0,oil δ = 0.88;1.5,0.5,V = 2.50 m³" riferimento=0 titolo="Tank containing $2.50\,\mathrm{m^3}$ of oil with specific gravity $0.88$"}

Specific gravity is the ratio of the density of the oil to that of water, so
$$
\rho = \delta\,\rho_{\mathrm{water}} = 0.88 \cdot 1000 = 880\,\mathrm{kg/m^3} .
$$
Specific weight is weight per unit volume.
$$
\gamma_{\mathrm{oil}} = \rho\,g = 880 \cdot 9.81 = 8633\,\mathrm{N/m^3} = 8.63\,\mathrm{kN/m^3} .
$$
Dividing dynamic viscosity by density gives the kinematic viscosity.
$$
\nu = \frac{\mu}{\rho} = \frac{0.22}{880} = 2.50 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m^2/s} .
$$
The weight of oil in the tank is
$$
G = \gamma_{\mathrm{oil}}\,V = 8.63 \cdot 2.50 = 21.6\,\mathrm{kN} .
$$

Check by first calculating the mass of oil.
$$
m = \rho\,V = 880 \cdot 2.50 = 2200\,\mathrm{kg} ,
\qquad
G = m\,g = 2200 \cdot 9.81 = 21.6\,\mathrm{kN} .
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Specific gravity is dimensionless. Multiply by the water density, taken as $1000\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}$, to obtain the oil density.

Multiply density by gravitational acceleration to obtain the specific weight, $8.63\,\mathrm{kN/m^3}$. It is less than the specific weight of water, so at the same depth the oil produces a smaller pressure increase.

Kinematic viscosity has dimensions of area per unit time. Here it is $2.50 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m^2/s}$, or $250$ times the value for water, $10^{-6}\,\mathrm{m^2/s}$. The oil thus has a much higher kinematic viscosity.

As a check, first calculate the mass of oil and then its weight. The result is the same because $\gamma\,V = \rho\,g\,V = m\,g$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A rigid pipe section closed at both ends contains $V = 1.20\,\mathrm{m^3}$ of water. For the pressure test shown, its internal pressure is raised to $\Delta p = 15\,\mathrm{MPa}$. Given water bulk modulus $\varepsilon = 2.1 \cdot 10^9\,\mathrm{Pa}$, find the fractional change in volume, the volume of water to be injected, and the speed of a small pressure disturbance.

::schema{nodi="A,0,0;B,6,0" condotte="A-B,0.9" linee="-0.1,-0.55 -0.1,0.55;6.1,-0.55 6.1,0.55" etichette="3,0.05,V = 1.20 m³;3,-1.1,Δp = 15 MPa" riferimento=0 titolo="Pipe section sealed at both ends during a leak test"}

The definition of bulk modulus gives the fractional change in volume.
$$
\frac{\Delta V}{V} = -\frac{\Delta p}{\varepsilon} = -\frac{15 \cdot 10^6}{2.1 \cdot 10^9} = -7.14 \cdot 10^{-3} ,
$$
This is a decrease of $0.714\%$. The pipe is rigid, so more water must be pumped in to fill the volume released by compression.
$$
\Delta V = 7.14 \cdot 10^{-3} \cdot 1200\,\mathrm{L} = 8.57\,\mathrm{L} .
$$
Small pressure disturbances travel through the water at speed
$$
c = \sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon}{\rho}} = \sqrt{\frac{2.1 \cdot 10^9}{1000}} = 1449\,\mathrm{m/s} .
$$

Check by substituting the fractional volume change back into the definition.
$$
\varepsilon = \frac{\Delta p}{\left|\dfrac{\Delta V}{V}\right|} = \frac{15 \cdot 10^6}{7.14 \cdot 10^{-3}} = 2.1 \cdot 10^9\,\mathrm{Pa} .
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Bulk modulus relates the pressure increment to the fractional volume change. The minus sign accounts for the reduction in volume when pressure increases, so the ratio $\dfrac{\Delta V}{V}$ is negative.

The test pressure is high, $15\,\mathrm{MPa}$, equivalent to about $1500\,\mathrm{m}$ of water head. Yet the volume decreases by less than one percent. This supports the incompressible-water approximation used in the hydrostatics problems.

The water already in the pipe contracts by $8.57\,\mathrm{L}$ out of an initial $1200\,\mathrm{L}$. This is the volume to add. A pressure test on a long pipe can therefore use a pump with a small flow rate, provided it can deliver a high head.

The wave speed $c = 1449\,\mathrm{m/s}$ describes how quickly a pressure disturbance travels through the water. It enters the calculation of water hammer. As a check, use the result to recalculate $\varepsilon$ and recover the specified value.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A flat plate of area $A = 0.45\,\mathrm{m^2}$ moves over an oil film of thickness $s = 2.0\,\mathrm{mm}$ at constant speed $V = 1.20\,\mathrm{m/s}$, as shown. The oil has dynamic viscosity $\mu = 0.30\,\mathrm{Pa\,s}$. Find the shear stress on the underside, the applied force needed to maintain motion, and the power dissipated.

::schema{solidi="0,-0.5 6,-0.5 6,0 0,0;0.6,0.5 5.4,0.5 5.4,0.9 0.6,0.9" acqua="0,0 6,0 6,0.5 0,0.5" frecce="2.4,1.05,4.2,1.05,V;0.9,0.5,2.1,0.5;0.9,0.375,1.8,0.375;0.9,0.25,1.5,0.25;0.9,0.125,1.2,0.125" quote="0.2 0,0.2 0.5,s = 2.0 mm,-0.45" etichette="3,1.25,plate  A = 0.45 m²;3,-0.85,oil μ = 0.30 Pa s" riferimento=0 titolo="Plate moving over an oil film of thickness $s = 2.0\,\mathrm{mm}$"}

The oil film is thin and the plate moves at constant speed, so the velocity profile across the film is linear and has a constant gradient.
$$
\frac{dv}{dy} = \frac{V}{s} = \frac{1.20}{0.0020} = 600\,\mathrm{s^{-1}} .
$$
Newton’s law of viscosity gives the shear stress
$$
\tau = \mu\,\frac{V}{s} = 0.30 \cdot 600 = 180\,\mathrm{Pa} ,
$$
and the required force equals this stress times the area.
$$
F = \tau\,A = 180 \cdot 0.45 = 81.0\,\mathrm{N} .
$$
The power dissipated is force times speed.
$$
P = F\,V = 81.0 \cdot 1.20 = 97.2\,\mathrm{W} .
$$

Check the power using the expression obtained by substituting $F$.
$$
P = \mu\,\frac{A\,V^2}{s} = 0.30 \cdot \frac{0.45 \cdot 1.20^2}{0.0020} = 97.2\,\mathrm{W} .
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The no-slip condition applies at both the moving plate and the fixed surface. The oil velocity therefore changes from $V$ to $0$ across the film thickness $s$. Since the film is thin, this change is linear and the velocity gradient is $\dfrac{V}{s}$ throughout.

**Newton’s law of viscosity** relates shear stress to the velocity gradient through dynamic viscosity. The stress is uniform across the film, so multiplying by the contact area gives the force.

The force is **$81.0\,\mathrm{N}$** and the power is **$97.2\,\mathrm{W}$**. The check also shows that power increases with the square of the speed and decreases as the film thickens. Both quantities affect dissipation in a bearing.

</div>

## Exercise 4

A glass tube of inside diameter $D = 1.5\,\mathrm{mm}$ is dipped vertically into water at $20\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$, as shown. The water–air surface tension is $\sigma = 0.0728\,\mathrm{N/m}$ and the contact angle on clean glass is zero. Find the capillary rise and repeat the calculation for a tube of diameter $12\,\mathrm{mm}$.

::schema{acqua="0,0 5,0 5,1.2 0,1.2;2.35,1.2 2.65,1.2 2.65,2.5 2.35,2.5" solidi="-0.3,-0.3 5.3,-0.3 5.3,0 -0.3,0;-0.3,0 0,0 0,1.5 -0.3,1.5;5,0 5.3,0 5.3,1.5 5,1.5" linee="2.33,3.1 2.33,0.9;2.67,3.1 2.67,0.9" pelo="0,2.1,1.2" quote="2.9 1.2,2.9 2.5,h,0.5" etichette="1.0,1.9,glass tube D" riferimento=0 titolo="Capillary rise in a glass tube immersed in water"}

Water wets glass, producing a concave meniscus. Surface tension pulls the water column upward along the circular contact line. For equilibrium, equate the weight of the raised column to the vertical component of this surface force.
$$
\gamma\,\frac{\pi D^2}{4}\,h = \sigma\,\pi D \cos\theta ,
\qquad\text{which gives}\qquad
h = \frac{4\,\sigma\cos\theta}{\gamma\,D} .
$$
Substituting $\theta = 0$ and $D = 1.5\,\mathrm{mm}$ gives
$$
h = \frac{4 \cdot 0.0728}{9810 \cdot 0.0015} = \frac{0.2912}{14.715} = 0.0198\,\mathrm{m} = 19.8\,\mathrm{mm} ,
$$
With $D = 12\,\mathrm{mm}$ the diameter is eight times as large, so the rise is reduced by the same factor.
$$
h = \frac{0.2912}{9810 \cdot 0.012} = 0.00247\,\mathrm{m} = 2.47\,\mathrm{mm} .
$$

Check equilibrium using the calculated values for $D = 1.5\,\mathrm{mm}$.
$$
\sigma\,\pi D = 0.0728 \cdot \pi \cdot 0.0015 = 3.43 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{N} ,
\qquad
\gamma\,\frac{\pi D^2}{4}\,h = 9810 \cdot \frac{\pi \cdot 0.0015^2}{4} \cdot 0.0198 = 3.43 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{N} .
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Surface tension acts along the contact line between water, glass, and air. This line is a circle of diameter $D$ and circumference $\pi D$, giving a total force of $\sigma\,\pi D$. With zero contact angle, the force acts vertically along the wall, so its vertical component equals its full magnitude.

The raised column has cross-sectional area $\dfrac{\pi D^2}{4}$ and height $h$, giving a weight of $\gamma\,\dfrac{\pi D^2}{4}\,h$. Equating the two forces and canceling $\pi D$ gives Jurin’s law.

Capillary rise varies inversely with diameter. Increasing the diameter from $1.5\,\mathrm{mm}$ to $12\,\mathrm{mm}$ reduces the rise from **$19.8\,\mathrm{mm}$** to **$2.47\,\mathrm{mm}$**. Piezometer tubes with a diameter of at least one centimeter therefore limit the reading error to a few millimeters.

As a check, substitute the calculated values. The upward surface-tension force equals the weight of the raised column.

</div>
