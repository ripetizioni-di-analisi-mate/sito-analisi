## Exercise 1

A piezometer connects to a pipe with centerline elevation $z_A = 101.20\,\mathrm{m}$, as shown. Water rises to $3.40\,\mathrm{m}$ above the centerline. Find the centerline gauge pressure, piezometric head, and hydrostatic head plane elevation.

::schema{nodi="A,0,0,-;B,4.5,0,-" condotte="A-B,0.5" acqua="1.4,0 1.6,0 1.6,3.4 1.4,3.4" pelo="1.4,1.6,3.4" tratteggi="0.2,0 4.3,0" punti="1.5,0,A" quote="1.5 0,1.5 3.4,3.40 m,-0.8" etichette="3.2,0.6,centerline z_A = 101.20 m" riferimento=0 titolo="A piezometer connected to the pipe reads $3.40\,\mathrm{m}$"}

Gauge pressure at the piezometer meniscus is zero, so the water surface in the tube lies in the plane of zero gauge pressure. The reading therefore gives the pressure head at $A$.
$$
\frac{p_A}{\gamma} = 3.40\,\mathrm{m} ,
\qquad
p_A = \gamma \cdot 3.40 = 9.81 \cdot 3.40 = 33.4\,\mathrm{kPa} .
$$
The piezometric head is
$$
h_A = z_A + \frac{p_A}{\gamma} = 101.20 + 3.40 = 104.60\,\mathrm{m} ,
$$
The plane of zero gauge pressure is horizontal at elevation $104.60\,\mathrm{m}$.

Check the pressure using the elevation of the plane of zero gauge pressure.
$$
p_A = \gamma\,(h_A - z_A) = 9.81 \cdot (104.60 - 101.20) = 9.81 \cdot 3.40 = 33.4\,\mathrm{kPa} .
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The piezometer is open to the atmosphere, so its meniscus is at zero gauge pressure. The water in the tube and the pipe forms a single connected body at rest, with the same piezometric head throughout.

The reading is the vertical distance from the pipe centerline to the plane of zero gauge pressure. This is the pressure head at $A$. Multiplying by the specific weight gives the pressure, **$33.4\,\mathrm{kPa}$**.

Add the pipe centerline elevation and the pressure head to obtain the piezometric head $104.60\,\mathrm{m}$ relative to the chosen datum. This is also the elevation of the plane of zero gauge pressure.

</div>

## Exercise 2

The pipe shown has centerline elevation $z_A = 0$ and is connected to an open-tube mercury manometer. The water–mercury interface is at elevation $z_1 = -0.30\,\mathrm{m}$ and the mercury in the open limb rises to $z_2 = +0.52\,\mathrm{m}$. Find the gauge pressure at point $A$ and the corresponding pressure head.

::schema{nodi="P,0,0,-;Q,2.2,0,-" condotte="P-Q,0.4" acqua="1.74,0 1.86,0 1.86,-0.3 1.74,-0.3" poligoni="1.74,-0.3 1.74,-0.55 2.52,-0.55 2.52,0.52 2.4,0.52 2.4,-0.43 1.86,-0.43 1.86,-0.3" pelo="2.4,2.52,0.52" tratteggi="2.2,0 3.9,0" punti="1.8,0,A" quote="2.75 0,2.75 -0.3,0.30 m,-0.35;2.6 0,2.6 0.52,0.52 m,1.05" etichette="2.1,-0.78,mercury" riferimento=0 titolo="Simple mercury manometer connected to the pipe at $A$"}

Follow the tube from $A$ to the water–mercury interface using the hydrostatic relation for water, then from the interface to the open mercury surface using the relation for mercury. Let $p_1$ denote the common pressure at the interface.
$$
\begin{cases}
p_1 = p_A + \gamma\,(z_A - z_1) \\[4pt]
p_1 = \gamma_{\mathrm{Hg}}\,(z_2 - z_1)
\end{cases}
$$
The first equation gives the interface pressure calculated through water. The second gives it through mercury, whose open surface is at zero gauge pressure. Equating the two expressions gives
$$
p_A = \gamma_{\mathrm{Hg}}\,(z_2 - z_1) - \gamma\,(z_A - z_1) .
$$
Substituting $\gamma_{\mathrm{Hg}} = 13.6 \cdot 9.81 = 133.4\,\mathrm{kN/m^3}$ gives
$$
p_A = 133.4 \cdot \big(0.52 - (-0.30)\big) - 9.81 \cdot \big(0 - (-0.30)\big) ,
$$
$$
p_A = 133.4 \cdot 0.82 - 9.81 \cdot 0.30 = 109.4 - 2.94 = 106.5\,\mathrm{kPa} ,
$$
which corresponds to a pressure head of
$$
\frac{p_A}{\gamma} = \frac{106.5}{9.81} = 10.9\,\mathrm{m} .
$$

Check by calculating the interface pressure from either side.
$$
p_1 = 106.5 + 9.81 \cdot 0.30 = 109.4\,\mathrm{kPa} ,
\qquad
p_1 = 133.4 \cdot 0.82 = 109.4\,\mathrm{kPa} .
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Apply the hydrostatic relation to each liquid separately as you follow the tube between its menisci. Descending by $0.30\,\mathrm{m}$ through water from $A$ increases the pressure by $\gamma \cdot 0.30$.

Where water and mercury meet, the pressure is the same on both sides of the interface. This condition connects the two equations.

From the interface, rise by $0.82\,\mathrm{m}$ through mercury to the free surface in the open arm, where gauge pressure is zero. The interface pressure is therefore $\gamma_{\mathrm{Hg}}$ times that height difference.

The pressure is $p_A = 106.5\,\mathrm{kPa}$, equivalent to **$10.9\,\mathrm{m}$** of water. The water column contributes $2.94\,\mathrm{kPa}$. This is small compared with the mercury contribution but must be included. A water piezometer would need a tube almost eleven meters tall. The mercury manometer fits within one meter because mercury is $13.6$ times as dense as water.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Water in the pipe shown is below atmospheric pressure. The pipe centerline is at elevation $z_A = 0$, the water–mercury interface at $z_1 = -0.25\,\mathrm{m}$, and the open mercury surface at $z_2 = -0.40\,\mathrm{m}$. Find the gauge pressure at $A$ and check it against the suction limit for water.

::schema{nodi="P,0,0,-;Q,2.2,0,-" condotte="P-Q,0.4" acqua="1.74,0 1.86,0 1.86,-0.25 1.74,-0.25" poligoni="1.74,-0.25 1.74,-0.65 2.52,-0.65 2.52,-0.4 2.4,-0.4 2.4,-0.53 1.86,-0.53 1.86,-0.25" pelo="2.4,2.52,-0.4" tratteggi="2.2,0 3.6,0" punti="1.8,0,A" quote="2.75 0,2.75 -0.25,0.25 m,-0.35;2.9 0,2.9 -0.4,0.40 m,-0.55" etichette="2.1,-0.88,mercury" riferimento=0 titolo="Mercury manometer connected to a pipe at subatmospheric pressure"}

Use the same relation between the two meniscus elevations as in the preceding case.
$$
p_A = \gamma_{\mathrm{Hg}}\,(z_2 - z_1) - \gamma\,(z_A - z_1) .
$$
Here the free surface of the mercury lies below the water–mercury interface, so $z_2 - z_1$ is negative.
$$
p_A = 133.4 \cdot \big(-0.40 - (-0.25)\big) - 9.81 \cdot \big(0 - (-0.25)\big) ,
$$
$$
p_A = 133.4 \cdot (-0.15) - 9.81 \cdot 0.25 = -20.0 - 2.45 = -22.5\,\mathrm{kPa} ,
$$
This corresponds to a pressure deficit below atmospheric pressure equivalent to
$$
\frac{p_A}{\gamma} = \frac{-22.5}{9.81} = -2.29\,\mathrm{m}
$$
of water. This pressure is above the limit $-10.09\,\mathrm{m}$, so the water in the pipe can remain liquid.

Check by calculating the interface pressure from either side.
$$
p_1 = -22.5 + 9.81 \cdot 0.25 = -20.0\,\mathrm{kPa} ,
\qquad
p_1 = 133.4 \cdot (-0.15) = -20.0\,\mathrm{kPa} .
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Subatmospheric pipe pressure draws mercury up the connected arm and lowers the mercury surface in the open arm below the interface. The level difference $z_2 - z_1$ is therefore negative in the manometer equation.

The mercury above the height of the open surface contributes $-20.0\,\mathrm{kPa}$, a negative term. Combining this with the contribution from the water column of $0.25\,\mathrm{m}$ gives a pressure of **$-22.5\,\mathrm{kPa}$** at $A$.

Since the pressure head is negative, the plane of zero gauge pressure lies $2.29\,\mathrm{m}$ below the pipe centerline.

At $20\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ the lower limit for the gauge pressure of liquid water is $-10.09\,\mathrm{m}$ of water head. At a lower pressure the absolute pressure would fall below vapor pressure. The calculated pressure is comfortably above this limit, so liquid flow is possible.

</div>
