## Exercise 1

A plate of weight $W = 80\,\mathrm{N}$ and area $A = 0.25\,\mathrm{m^2}$ slides down a plane inclined at $\alpha = 12^\circ$ to the horizontal on an oil film of thickness $s = 0.80\,\mathrm{mm}$ and dynamic viscosity $\mu = 0.35\,\mathrm{Pa\,s}$, as shown. Find its terminal speed.

::schema{poligoni="0,0 5,1.0395 5,0;2.114,0.633 3.679,0.966 3.617,1.26 2.052,0.926" acqua="2.152,0.457 3.717,0.79 3.679,0.966 2.114,0.633" forze="2.866 0.95,0,-0.8,W = 80 N" frecce="2.0,0.9,1.35,0.76,V" etichette="1.8,0.12,α = 12°;1.7,1.5,plate  A = 0.25 m²;4.0,0.35,oil μ = 0.35 Pa s" riferimento=0 titolo="Plate sliding on an oil film down a plane inclined at $12^\circ$"}

At steady speed the component of weight acting down the slope balances the viscous force exerted by the oil on the underside of the plate.
$$
\begin{cases}
F = W \sin\alpha \\[4pt]
F = \mu\,\dfrac{A\,V}{s}
\end{cases}
$$
The first equation expresses force equilibrium along the slope. The second applies Newton’s law of viscosity to the thin film, where the velocity profile is linear. The first equation gives
$$
F = 80 \cdot \sin 12^\circ = 80 \cdot 0.2079 = 16.6\,\mathrm{N} ,
$$
so the shear stress is
$$
\tau = \frac{F}{A} = \frac{16.63}{0.25} = 66.5\,\mathrm{Pa} .
$$
The second equation then gives the speed
$$
V = \frac{\tau\,s}{\mu} = \frac{66.53 \cdot 0.00080}{0.35} = 0.152\,\mathrm{m/s} ,
$$
directed down the incline.

Check by recalculating the viscous force from the speed just obtained.
$$
F = \mu\,\frac{A\,V}{s} = 0.35 \cdot \frac{0.25 \cdot 0.1521}{0.00080} = 16.6\,\mathrm{N} ,
$$
This equals the component of weight along the incline.

<div class="spiegazione">

The plate descends at constant speed, so its acceleration and the net force along the incline are zero. The forces along the incline are the weight component $W \sin\alpha$ and the opposing viscous force on the wetted face.

The thin film has a linear velocity profile, from $V$ at the moving plate to zero at the fixed surface. The resulting shear stress is $\mu\,\dfrac{V}{s}$ throughout the thickness.

First find the force from the known weight and inclination. Divide by the area to get the shear stress, then use Newton’s law of viscosity to find the speed, **$V = 0.152\,\mathrm{m/s}$**.

The steady speed increases with film thickness and decreases with viscosity. A more viscous oil or a thinner film slows the descent. As a check, the viscous force calculated from the resulting speed equals the downslope component of weight.

</div>

## Exercise 2

In the concentric-cylinder viscometer shown, the inner cylinder has radius $R = 60\,\mathrm{mm}$ and height $H = 150\,\mathrm{mm}$. An oil film of thickness $s = 1.5\,\mathrm{mm}$ and viscosity $\mu = 0.18\,\mathrm{Pa\,s}$ separates it from the fixed outer cylinder. The inner cylinder rotates at angular speed $\omega = 20\,\mathrm{rad/s}$. Neglecting end effects, find the resisting torque and power dissipated.

::schema{poligoni="-0.6,0 0.6,0 0.6,1.5 -0.6,1.5" acqua="-0.72,0 -0.6,0 -0.6,1.5 -0.72,1.5;0.6,0 0.72,0 0.72,1.5 0.6,1.5" solidi="-0.95,-0.22 0.95,-0.22 0.95,0 -0.95,0;-0.95,0 -0.72,0 -0.72,1.75 -0.95,1.75;0.72,0 0.95,0 0.95,1.75 0.72,1.75" frecce="-0.35,2.1,0.35,2.1,ω" quote="0 1.5,0.6 1.5,R = 60 mm,-0.35;0.95 0,0.95 1.5,H = 150 mm,0.5" etichette="0,0.75,inner cylinder;0,-0.55,oil film thickness s = 1.5 mm" riferimento=0 titolo="Cross section of a concentric-cylinder viscometer showing the oil film"}

The peripheral speed of the inner cylinder is
$$
V = \omega\,R = 20 \cdot 0.060 = 1.20\,\mathrm{m/s} .
$$
The gap is thin compared with the radius, so the velocity profile across it is approximately linear. Newton’s law of viscosity then gives
$$
\tau = \mu\,\frac{V}{s} = 0.18 \cdot \frac{1.20}{0.0015} = 144\,\mathrm{Pa} .
$$
The lateral surface area of the inner cylinder is
$$
A = 2\pi R H = 2\pi \cdot 0.060 \cdot 0.150 = 0.0565\,\mathrm{m^2} ,
$$
so the tangential force and the torque about the axis are
$$
F = \tau\,A = 144 \cdot 0.05655 = 8.14\,\mathrm{N} ,
\qquad
M = F\,R = 8.143 \cdot 0.060 = 0.489\,\mathrm{N\,m} .
$$
Multiplying torque by angular velocity gives the dissipated power.
$$
P = M\,\omega = 0.4886 \cdot 20 = 9.77\,\mathrm{W} .
$$

Check the power by multiplying tangential force by peripheral speed.
$$
P = F\,V = 8.143 \cdot 1.20 = 9.77\,\mathrm{W} .
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The inner cylinder rotates while the outer cylinder remains stationary. The oil velocity therefore changes from $V = \omega R$ at the inner surface to zero at the outer surface.

The gap thickness $1.5\,\mathrm{mm}$ is only one fortieth of the radius. Curvature can therefore be neglected and the film treated as if it lay between parallel flat plates. The velocity gradient is $\dfrac{V}{s}$ and the shear stress is the same over the entire cylindrical surface.

Shear stress times the lateral surface area gives the tangential force. All points on that surface have the same moment arm $R$ about the axis, so multiplying by this arm gives the resisting torque, **$0.489\,\mathrm{N\,m}$**.

Measuring the resisting torque with a torsion dynamometer and using the known geometry gives $\mu$. This is the operating principle of the viscometer. Calculating power as force times tangential speed gives the same result as torque times angular speed.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A shaft of diameter $D = 80\,\mathrm{mm}$ rotates at $n = 1500\,\mathrm{rpm}$ in a bearing of length $L = 120\,\mathrm{mm}$. Its radial clearance $s = 0.25\,\mathrm{mm}$ is filled with oil of viscosity $\mu = 0.10\,\mathrm{Pa\,s}$, as shown. Find the resisting torque and power dissipated by viscous friction.

::schema{poligoni="-0.4,0 0.4,0 0.4,1.2 -0.4,1.2" acqua="-0.44,0 -0.4,0 -0.4,1.2 -0.44,1.2;0.4,0 0.44,0 0.44,1.2 0.4,1.2" solidi="-0.75,0 -0.44,0 -0.44,1.2 -0.75,1.2;0.44,0 0.75,0 0.75,1.2 0.44,1.2" frecce="-0.3,1.6,0.3,1.6,n" quote="-0.4 1.2,0.4 1.2,D = 80 mm,-0.3;0.75 0,0.75 1.2,L = 120 mm,0.45" etichette="0,0.6,shaft;0,-0.3,radial clearance s = 0.25 mm" riferimento=0 titolo="Cross section of the shaft in its bearing, with oil filling the radial clearance"}

Convert the rotational speed in revolutions per minute to angular velocity.
$$
\omega = \frac{2\pi n}{60} = \frac{2\pi \cdot 1500}{60} = 157.1\,\mathrm{rad/s} ,
$$
The peripheral speed of the shaft of radius $R = 0.040\,\mathrm{m}$ is therefore
$$
V = \omega\,R = 157.08 \cdot 0.040 = 6.28\,\mathrm{m/s} .
$$
Approximating the narrow clearance as a flat oil film gives the shear stress
$$
\tau = \mu\,\frac{V}{s} = 0.10 \cdot \frac{6.283}{0.00025} = 2513\,\mathrm{Pa} ,
$$
and the cylindrical surface area of the shaft in contact with the oil is
$$
A = 2\pi R L = 2\pi \cdot 0.040 \cdot 0.120 = 0.0302\,\mathrm{m^2} .
$$
The resisting torque and the power dissipated are therefore
$$
M = \tau\,A\,R = 2513 \cdot 0.03016 \cdot 0.040 = 3.03\,\mathrm{N\,m} ,
$$
$$
P = M\,\omega = 3.032 \cdot 157.08 = 476\,\mathrm{W} .
$$

Check the power by multiplying tangential force by peripheral speed.
$$
P = \tau\,A\,V = 2513 \cdot 0.03016 \cdot 6.283 = 476\,\mathrm{W} .
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The radial clearance is very small compared with the radius, $0.25\,\mathrm{mm}$ versus $40\,\mathrm{mm}$. The oil film can therefore be treated as a plane layer with a velocity gradient of $\dfrac{V}{s}$.

The peripheral speed is high, $6.28\,\mathrm{m/s}$, and the clearance is small. The velocity gradient therefore reaches $25\,000\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$, and the shear stress exceeds $2500\,\mathrm{Pa}$, well above the values in the earlier examples.

The resisting torque is **$3.03\,\mathrm{N\,m}$**. The dissipated power, **$476\,\mathrm{W}$**, becomes heat that the lubrication system must remove. Power varies with the square of the peripheral speed, so doubling the rotational speed would quadruple it.

Check the power by multiplying the tangential force by the peripheral speed. The result agrees.

</div>
