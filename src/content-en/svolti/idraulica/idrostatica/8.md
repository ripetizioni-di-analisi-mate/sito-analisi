## Exercise 1

A rectangular tank truck has length $L = 6.00\,\mathrm{m}$ and height $2.60\,\mathrm{m}$. Its mean water depth is $1.50\,\mathrm{m}$, as shown. The truck accelerates horizontally to the right at $a = 2.50\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$. Find the free-surface inclination, the difference in water level between the two end walls, and the bottom pressure at rear point $F$.

::schema{acqua="0,0 6,0 6,1.5 0,1.5" solidi="-0.3,-0.3 6.3,-0.3 6.3,0 -0.3,0;-0.3,0 0,0 0,2.6 -0.3,2.6;6,0 6.3,0 6.3,2.6 6,2.6;-0.3,2.6 6.3,2.6 6.3,2.9 -0.3,2.9" pelo="0,6,1.5" frecce="1.9,4.3,3.7,4.3,a = 2.50 m/s²" punti="0,0,F" quote="0 1.5,0 0,1.50 m,-0.8;0 2.9,6 2.9,L = 6.00 m,-0.6" riferimento=0 titolo="Accelerating tanker truck showing the water level at rest and the direction of acceleration"}

In a reference frame moving with the vehicle the liquid is at rest. Surfaces of constant pressure are planes with slope
$$
\tan\theta = \frac{a}{g} = \frac{2.50}{9.81} = 0.2548 ,
\qquad
\theta = 14.3^\circ ,
$$
with the lower level on the side toward which the vehicle accelerates. The elevation difference between the end walls is
$$
\Delta z = L\,\tan\theta = 6.00 \cdot 0.2548 = 1.529\,\mathrm{m} ,
$$
so the free surface rises by $\dfrac{\Delta z}{2} = 0.765\,\mathrm{m}$ at the rear wall and falls by the same amount at the front. The water volume stays constant, and the plane tilts about midlength. The water depth at the rear is therefore
$$
h_F = 1.50 + 0.765 = 2.265\,\mathrm{m} ,
$$
which is less than the tank height, so the water does not reach the roof. The acceleration is horizontal. Along a vertical line at the rear wall the pressure therefore varies just as in stationary water, and the hydrostatic relation gives
$$
p_F = \gamma\,h_F = 9.81 \cdot 2.265 = 22.2\,\mathrm{kPa} .
$$

Check by starting at the middle of the bottom, where the depth equals the average, and moving $3.00\,\mathrm{m}$ toward the rear wall.
$$
p_F = \gamma \cdot 1.50 + \rho\,a \cdot 3.00 = 14.72 + 1000 \cdot 2.50 \cdot 3.00 \cdot 10^{-3} = 14.72 + 7.50 = 22.2\,\mathrm{kPa} .
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

In the vehicle frame, gravity combines with an inertial force acting opposite to the acceleration. The resultant body force is inclined. Surfaces of constant pressure are perpendicular to it, so the free surface becomes a plane of slope $\tan\theta = \dfrac{a}{g}$.

Acceleration leaves the water volume unchanged. The inclined surface therefore passes through the original water level at midlength. The rise at the rear and the drop at the front are each half the total elevation difference.

The water depth at the rear wall is **$2.265\,\mathrm{m}$**, and the bottom pressure there is **$22.2\,\mathrm{kPa}$**. The usual hydrostatic relation still holds along a vertical line because horizontal acceleration leaves the pressure derivative with respect to $z$ unchanged.

For an independent check, follow the bottom horizontally toward the rear. Pressure rises by $\rho\,a$ per meter, or $2.50\,\mathrm{kPa}$ per meter in the stated units. Over $3.00\,\mathrm{m}$ this gives an increase of $7.50\,\mathrm{kPa}$ above the pressure at midlength.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A container of water of depth $1.80\,\mathrm{m}$ is lifted with constant upward acceleration $a = 3.00\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$, as shown. Find the bottom pressure and compare it with the pressure when the container is at rest.

::schema{acqua="0,0 2.6,0 2.6,1.8 0,1.8" solidi="-0.3,-0.3 2.9,-0.3 2.9,0 -0.3,0;-0.3,0 0,0 0,2.2 -0.3,2.2;2.6,0 2.9,0 2.9,2.2 2.6,2.2" pelo="0,2.6,1.8" frecce="3.5,0.4,3.5,1.9,a" punti="1.3,0,F" quote="0 1.8,0 0,1.80 m,-0.8" riferimento=0 titolo="Container being raised with upward acceleration $a = 3.00\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$"}

Since the acceleration is vertical, the free surface stays horizontal. Only the effective body force per unit mass changes, becoming $g + a$. The differential equilibrium equation in the vessel frame gives
$$
\frac{dp}{dz} = -\rho\,(g + a) ,
$$
Integrating from the free surface to the bottom then gives
$$
p_F = \rho\,(g + a)\,h = 1000 \cdot (9.81 + 3.00) \cdot 1.80 = 23\,058\,\mathrm{Pa} = 23.1\,\mathrm{kPa} .
$$
With the vessel at rest the bottom pressure would be
$$
p_{F,0} = \gamma\,h = 9.81 \cdot 1.80 = 17.7\,\mathrm{kPa} .
$$

Check using the ratio of the two pressures.
$$
\frac{p_F}{p_{F,0}} = \frac{g + a}{g} = \frac{12.81}{9.81} = 1.306 ,
\qquad
17.658 \cdot 1.306 = 23.1\,\mathrm{kPa} .
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The vessel accelerates upward, so in its frame the inertial force acts downward and adds to the weight. The liquid responds as if the gravitational acceleration were $g + a$.

The resultant body force is vertical, so constant-pressure surfaces remain horizontal. The pressure gradient with elevation changes from $-\rho g$ to $-\rho\,(g + a)$.

The bottom pressure is **$23.1\,\mathrm{kPa}$**, which exceeds its value in a stationary vessel by $30.6\%$. Downward acceleration would reverse the sign of the added term and reduce the pressure. In free fall, with $a = g$, gauge pressure would be zero throughout the liquid.

As a check, the ratio of the two pressures equals the ratio of the corresponding effective accelerations.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A cylindrical container of radius $R = 0.40\,\mathrm{m}$ and height $1.00\,\mathrm{m}$ holds water to depth $0.50\,\mathrm{m}$ at rest, as shown. It rotates about its vertical axis at $n = 90\,\mathrm{rpm}$, and the fluid rotates as a rigid body. Find the elevation difference between the rim and vertex of the free surface, their respective elevations, and the bottom pressure at the rim.

::schema{acqua="-0.4,0 0.4,0 0.4,0.5 -0.4,0.5" solidi="-0.52,-0.12 0.52,-0.12 0.52,0 -0.52,0;-0.52,0 -0.4,0 -0.4,1 -0.52,1;0.4,0 0.52,0 0.52,1 0.4,1" pelo="-0.4,0.4,0.5" frecce="-0.25,1.25,0.25,1.25,n = 90 rpm" quote="0 0.5,0.4 0.5,R = 0.40 m,-0.22;0.52 0,0.52 0.5,0.50 m,0.3" riferimento=0 titolo="Cylindrical container with $0.50\,\mathrm{m}$ of water before rotation"}

The angular velocity is
$$
\omega = \frac{2\pi n}{60} = \frac{2\pi \cdot 90}{60} = 9.425\,\mathrm{rad/s} .
$$
In the rotating frame the free surface is a paraboloid. The elevation difference between its rim and vertex is
$$
\Delta z = \frac{\omega^2 R^2}{2 g} = \frac{9.4248^2 \cdot 0.40^2}{2 \cdot 9.81} = \frac{14.212}{19.62} = 0.724\,\mathrm{m} .
$$
The water volume stays constant. The mean height of the paraboloid is halfway between the vertex and rim elevations, so the vertex drops by $\dfrac{\Delta z}{2}$ from the original level and the rim rises by the same amount.
$$
z_v = 0.50 - 0.362 = 0.138\,\mathrm{m} ,
\qquad
z_b = 0.50 + 0.362 = 0.862\,\mathrm{m} .
$$
The vertex lies above the bottom and the water at the wall lies below the rim of the vessel. The whole paraboloid therefore fits inside the cylinder. The depth at the outer edge of the bottom is $0.862\,\mathrm{m}$, and the vertical hydrostatic relation gives
$$
p_b = \gamma\,z_b = 9.81 \cdot 0.862 = 8.46\,\mathrm{kPa} .
$$

Check by starting at the vertex and including the centrifugal pressure change along the bottom to the wall.
$$
p_b = \gamma\,z_v + \rho\,\frac{\omega^2 R^2}{2} = 9.81 \cdot 0.1378 + 1000 \cdot \frac{14.212}{2} \cdot 10^{-3} = 1.35 + 7.11 = 8.46\,\mathrm{kPa} .
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

In the rotating frame the outward centrifugal force per unit mass is $\omega^2 r$. Pressure therefore increases with radius, and surfaces of constant pressure are paraboloids of revolution opening upward.

The free surface is one of these paraboloids, and conservation of volume fixes its elevation. The area average of $r^2$ over a disk of radius $R$ is $\dfrac{R^2}{2}$, so the mean surface height lies halfway between the vertex and rim heights. The undisturbed water level lies at their midpoint.

The rim-to-vertex elevation difference is **$0.724\,\mathrm{m}$**. The vertex is $0.138\,\mathrm{m}$ above the bottom and the rim is $0.862\,\mathrm{m}$ above it. Since the vertex remains above the bottom, the entire base stays wet. At higher speeds a central dry region would require a different calculation.

The centrifugal force is horizontal, so the usual hydrostatic relation still holds vertically. The bottom pressure is $\gamma$ times the local depth. An independent calculation starts at the vertex and adds the centrifugal pressure increase along the bottom to the wall, giving the same result.

</div>
