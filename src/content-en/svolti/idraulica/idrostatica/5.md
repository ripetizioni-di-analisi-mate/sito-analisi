## Exercise 1

The open tank shown contains oil of specific gravity $\delta = 0.80$ and thickness $1.60\,\mathrm{m}$ above water of thickness $2.80\,\mathrm{m}$. Find the interface and bottom pressures and the elevation of the water’s hydrostatic head plane above the bottom.

::schema{acqua="0,0 3.6,0 3.6,2.8 0,2.8" poligoni="0,2.8 3.6,2.8 3.6,4.4 0,4.4" solidi="-0.3,-0.3 3.9,-0.3 3.9,0 -0.3,0;-0.3,0 0,0 0,4.8 -0.3,4.8;3.6,0 3.9,0 3.9,4.8 3.6,4.8" pelo="0,3.6,4.4" punti="1.8,0,F" etichette="1.8,3.5,oil δ = 0.80;1.8,1.3,water" quote="0 4.4,0 2.8,1.60 m,-0.8;0 2.8,0 0,2.80 m,-0.8" riferimento=0 titolo="Open tank with a layer of oil above a layer of water"}

The specific weight of the oil is $\gamma_{\mathrm{oil}} = 0.80 \cdot 9.81 = 7.85\,\mathrm{kN/m^3}$. Apply the hydrostatic relation through the oil, starting at its free surface where gauge pressure is zero, to obtain the interface pressure.
$$
p_i = \gamma_{\mathrm{oil}} \cdot 1.60 = 7.848 \cdot 1.60 = 12.6\,\mathrm{kPa} .
$$
Find the bottom pressure by applying the hydrostatic relation through the water, starting at the interface pressure.
$$
p_F = p_i + \gamma \cdot 2.80 = 12.56 + 9.81 \cdot 2.80 = 12.56 + 27.47 = 40.0\,\mathrm{kPa} .
$$
The hydrostatic head plane for the water is the elevation at which its gauge pressure would vanish, namely
$$
h = \frac{p_F}{\gamma} = \frac{40.025}{9.81} = 4.08\,\mathrm{m}
$$
above the bottom. The free surface of the oil is $1.60 + 2.80 = 4.40\,\mathrm{m}$ above the bottom.

Check using the equivalent water head of the oil layer.
$$
\frac{\gamma_{\mathrm{oil}}}{\gamma} \cdot 1.60 = 0.80 \cdot 1.60 = 1.28\,\mathrm{m} ,
\qquad
p_F = \gamma\,(2.80 + 1.28) = 9.81 \cdot 4.08 = 40.0\,\mathrm{kPa} .
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The two liquids are immiscible. The less dense oil lies on top, and the interface is horizontal because it is a surface of constant pressure in a fluid at rest under gravity.

Apply the hydrostatic relation to each layer using its own specific weight. Start at zero gauge pressure at the oil surface and descend through $1.60\,\mathrm{m}$ of oil. At the interface the pressure is $p_i$. From there descend a further $2.80\,\mathrm{m}$ through the water.

The plane of zero gauge pressure for the water lies **$4.08\,\mathrm{m}$** above the bottom and $0.32\,\mathrm{m}$ below the oil surface. Water in a piezometer connected at the bottom would rise to that plane. It stays below the oil surface because oil is less dense than water.

As a check, replace the oil layer with the water depth $0.80 \cdot 1.60 = 1.28\,\mathrm{m}$ that produces the same pressure. This gives the same bottom pressure.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A constant-area U-tube open at both ends contains water. Oil of specific gravity $\delta = 0.78$ is poured into the left limb until it forms a column of height $0.60\,\mathrm{m}$ above the interface, as shown. Find the height of the right-hand water surface above the interface and the difference between the two free-surface elevations.

::schema{acqua="0,0.6 0,-0.3 1.8,-0.3 1.8,1.07 1.5,1.07 1.5,0 0.3,0 0.3,0.6" poligoni="0,0.6 0.3,0.6 0.3,1.2 0,1.2" linee="-0.02,1.4 -0.02,-0.32 1.82,-0.32 1.82,1.4;0.32,1.4 0.32,0 1.48,0 1.48,1.4" pelo="0,0.3,1.2;1.5,1.8,1.07" tratteggi="0.3,0.6 1.95,0.6" quote="0.15 0.6,0.15 1.2,0.60 m,-0.5" etichette="0.15,1.6,oil;1.65,1.6,water;0.9,-0.6,water" riferimento=0 titolo="Open U-tube with a column of oil of specific gravity $0.78$ and height $0.60\,\mathrm{m}$ in the left limb"}

The oil–water interface is horizontal. At its elevation the pressures in the two arms agree because the points belong to the same connected body of water at rest. Let $y$ be the height of the water in the right arm above the interface elevation. Equating pressures gives
$$
\gamma_{\mathrm{oil}} \cdot 0.60 = \gamma\,y ,
$$
therefore
$$
y = \frac{\gamma_{\mathrm{oil}}}{\gamma} \cdot 0.60 = 0.78 \cdot 0.60 = 0.468\,\mathrm{m} .
$$
The oil surface is $0.60\,\mathrm{m}$ above the interface, so the difference in elevation between the two free surfaces is
$$
0.60 - 0.468 = 0.132\,\mathrm{m} ,
$$
with the oil surface higher.

Check by calculating the pressure at the interface elevation in each arm.
$$
p_{\text{left}} = 0.78 \cdot 9.81 \cdot 0.60 = 4.59\,\mathrm{kPa} ,
\qquad
p_{\text{right}} = 9.81 \cdot 0.468 = 4.59\,\mathrm{kPa} .
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The horizontal plane through the interface meets the water in the right arm. These two points belong to the same connected water column, with the same piezometric head. Since their elevations are identical, their pressures are also equal.

The oil column produces this pressure in the left arm, while the water column produces it in the right arm. Equating their pressures gives a water-column height of **$0.468\,\mathrm{m}$**, less than the oil height $0.60\,\mathrm{m}$ because water is denser.

Subtract the column heights to find the free-surface elevation difference, $0.132\,\mathrm{m}$. Measuring this difference provides a simple way to find the specific gravity of a liquid because the ratio of the column heights gives $\delta$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

The closed tank shown contains $2.00\,\mathrm{m}$ of water beneath $1.20\,\mathrm{m}$ of oil of specific gravity $\delta = 0.85$, with air at gauge pressure $p_0 = 18\,\mathrm{kPa}$ above it. Find the pressures at the oil–water interface and the bottom, and the elevation reached in a piezometer connected to the bottom.

::schema{acqua="0,0 3.6,0 3.6,2 0,2" poligoni="0,2 3.6,2 3.6,3.2 0,3.2" solidi="-0.3,-0.3 3.9,-0.3 3.9,0 -0.3,0;-0.3,0 0,0 0,4 -0.3,4;3.6,0 3.9,0 3.9,4 3.6,4;-0.3,4 3.9,4 3.9,4.3 -0.3,4.3" pelo="0,3.6,3.2" punti="1.8,0,F" etichette="1.8,3.6,air  p_0 = 18 kPa;1.8,2.5,oil δ = 0.85;1.8,0.9,water" quote="0 3.2,0 2,1.20 m,-0.8;0 2,0 0,2.00 m,-0.8" riferimento=0 titolo="Closed tank with pressurized air above layers of oil and water"}

At the oil–water interface, add the pressure of the air to the pressure contributed by the oil layer, whose specific weight is $\gamma_{\mathrm{oil}} = 0.85 \cdot 9.81 = 8.34\,\mathrm{kN/m^3}$.
$$
p_i = p_0 + \gamma_{\mathrm{oil}} \cdot 1.20 = 18 + 8.339 \cdot 1.20 = 18 + 10.01 = 28.0\,\mathrm{kPa} .
$$
To reach the bottom, add the pressure contribution of the water layer.
$$
p_F = p_i + \gamma \cdot 2.00 = 28.01 + 19.62 = 47.6\,\mathrm{kPa} .
$$
In the piezometer connected to the bottom, water rises to its plane of zero gauge pressure. The height above the bottom is
$$
\frac{p_F}{\gamma} = \frac{47.626}{9.81} = 4.85\,\mathrm{m} ,
$$
or $4.85 - 3.20 = 1.65\,\mathrm{m}$ above the oil surface.

Check by adding the equivalent water heads of the three contributions.
$$
\frac{p_0}{\gamma} + \delta \cdot 1.20 + 2.00 = \frac{18}{9.81} + 0.85 \cdot 1.20 + 2.00 = 1.835 + 1.020 + 2.00 = 4.85\,\mathrm{m} .
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The air pressure acts at the oil surface and is transmitted throughout the underlying liquid. It therefore enters every pressure calculation as the same constant.

Start at the air pressure, descend $1.20\,\mathrm{m}$ through oil using its specific weight, then descend $2.00\,\mathrm{m}$ through water using the specific weight of water. The resulting bottom pressure is **$47.6\,\mathrm{kPa}$**.

The piezometer is connected to the water, so its water level rises to the hydrostatic head plane for that liquid, $4.85\,\mathrm{m}$ above the bottom. Here this plane lies above the oil surface because the air pressure more than offsets the difference in weight between the oil and a water column of the same height.

Convert each of the three pressure contributions to an equivalent water head and add them. Their sum provides an independent check on the result.

</div>
