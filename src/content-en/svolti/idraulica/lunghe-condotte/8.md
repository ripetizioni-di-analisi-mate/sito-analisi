## Exercise 1

A pump draws $Q = 50\,\mathrm{L/s}$ from a tank through a suction pipe of length $12\,\mathrm{m}$, with $D = 200\,\mathrm{mm}$ and $\lambda = 0.024$. The combined minor-loss coefficient is $\xi = 1.5$, as shown. At the operating discharge the manufacturer specifies $\mathrm{NPSH}_r = 3.5\,\mathrm{m}$. Given $p_{\mathrm{atm}} = 101.3\,\mathrm{kPa}$ and vapor pressure $p_v = 2.34\,\mathrm{kPa}$, find the maximum height at which the pump can be installed above the free surface.

::schema{nodi="S,1.6,0.4,-;G,1.6,2.4,-;P,5.2,3.2,-" acqua="0,1.6 3,1.6 3,-0.8 0,-0.8" solidi="0,-1.1 3,-1.1 3,-0.8 0,-0.8" condotte="S-G-P,0.14" pompa="5.2,3.2,P" pelo="0,3,1.6,tank" tratteggi="1.6,1.6 6.6,1.6;5.2,3.2 6.6,3.2" quote="6.4 1.6,6.4 3.2,z_a" etichette="2.6,3.5,L=12 m;2.6,2.9,ξ=1.5;3.4,2,D=200 mm" titolo="Suction line with the pump installed above the water surface"}

$$
A = 0.03142\,\mathrm{m^2}
\qquad
v = \frac{0.050}{0.03142}\,\mathrm{m/s} = 1.592\,\mathrm{m/s}
\qquad
\frac{v^2}{2g} = \frac{1.592^2}{19.62}\,\mathrm{m} = 0.129\,\mathrm{m}
$$
$$
\Delta H_a = \left(\lambda\,\frac{L}{D} + \xi\right)\frac{v^2}{2g}
= \left(0.024 \cdot \frac{12}{0.200} + 1.5\right) \cdot 0.129\,\mathrm{m}
= (1.44 + 1.50) \cdot 0.129\,\mathrm{m} = 0.380\,\mathrm{m}
$$
$$
\frac{p_{\mathrm{atm}} - p_v}{\gamma} = \frac{101.3 - 2.34}{9.81}\,\mathrm{m} = 10.09\,\mathrm{m}
$$
$$
z_a \leq \frac{p_{\mathrm{atm}} - p_v}{\gamma} - \Delta H_a - \mathrm{NPSH}_r = 10.09 - 0.380 - 3.50\,\mathrm{m} = 6.21\,\mathrm{m}
$$

Check by evaluating the available $\mathrm{NPSH}$ at the calculated elevation:
$$
\mathrm{NPSH}_d = 10.09 - 6.21 - 0.380\,\mathrm{m} = 3.50\,\mathrm{m} = \mathrm{NPSH}_r
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

For the suction pipe the ratio $\dfrac{L}{D}$ is $60$, so $\lambda\,\dfrac{L}{D} = 1.44$ is of the same order as the minor-loss coefficient. The long-pipeline approximation does not apply. Include all losses in the energy balance.

The operating condition is $\mathrm{NPSH}_d \geq \mathrm{NPSH}_r$. The available net positive suction head equals atmospheric pressure expressed as head of water, less the suction lift $z_a$, the suction-line loss and the vapor-pressure head.

Of the available $10.09\,\mathrm{m}$, the pump requires $3.50\,\mathrm{m}$ and the suction line dissipates $0.38\,\mathrm{m}$. This leaves **$6.21\,\mathrm{m}$** for the static suction lift. The suction-line loss is small here, but it increases with velocity squared. Doubling the discharge would quadruple the loss.

Recalculate the available $\mathrm{NPSH}$ at the maximum elevation found above. It equals the required value, confirming that this elevation satisfies the limiting condition exactly.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A pump installed $5\,\mathrm{m}$ above a tank's free surface draws $Q = 80\,\mathrm{L/s}$ through the suction line shown, of length $15\,\mathrm{m}$, with $D = 250\,\mathrm{mm}$, $\lambda = 0.0235$ and combined minor-loss coefficient $\xi = 2.0$. The manufacturer specifies $\mathrm{NPSH}_r = 4.0\,\mathrm{m}$. Calculate the absolute inlet pressure and check whether the pump can operate under these conditions.

::schema{nodi="S,1.6,0.4,-;G,1.6,2.4,-;P,5.2,3.2,-" acqua="0,1.6 3,1.6 3,-0.8 0,-0.8" solidi="0,-1.1 3,-1.1 3,-0.8 0,-0.8" condotte="S-G-P,0.14" pompa="5.2,3.2,P" pelo="0,3,1.6,tank" tratteggi="1.6,1.6 6.6,1.6;5.2,3.2 6.6,3.2" quote="6.4 1.6,6.4 3.2,5.0 m" etichette="2.6,3.5,L=15 m;2.6,2.9,ξ=2.0;3.4,2,D=250 mm" titolo="Pump installed $5\,\mathrm{m}$ above the tank water surface"}

$$
A = 0.0491\,\mathrm{m^2}
\qquad
v = \frac{0.080}{0.0491}\,\mathrm{m/s} = 1.630\,\mathrm{m/s}
\qquad
\frac{v^2}{2g} = 0.135\,\mathrm{m}
$$
$$
\Delta H_a = \left(0.0235 \cdot \frac{15}{0.250} + 2.0\right) \cdot 0.135\,\mathrm{m} = (1.41 + 2.00) \cdot 0.135\,\mathrm{m} = 0.462\,\mathrm{m}
$$
$$
\frac{p_{\mathrm{abs}}}{\gamma} = \frac{p_{\mathrm{atm}}}{\gamma} - z_a - \Delta H_a - \frac{v^2}{2g}
= 10.33 - 5.00 - 0.462 - 0.135\,\mathrm{m} = 4.73\,\mathrm{m}
$$
$$
p_{\mathrm{abs}} = 9.81 \cdot 4.73\,\mathrm{kPa} = 46.4\,\mathrm{kPa}
\qquad
p_{\mathrm{rel}} = 46.4 - 101.3\,\mathrm{kPa} = -54.9\,\mathrm{kPa}
$$
$$
\mathrm{NPSH}_d = \frac{p_{\mathrm{abs}}}{\gamma} + \frac{v^2}{2g} - \frac{p_v}{\gamma} = 4.73 + 0.135 - 0.239\,\mathrm{m} = 4.63\,\mathrm{m} > 4.0\,\mathrm{m}
$$

Check using the expression in terms of atmospheric pressure:
$$
\mathrm{NPSH}_d = \frac{p_{\mathrm{atm}} - p_v}{\gamma} - z_a - \Delta H_a = 10.09 - 5.00 - 0.462\,\mathrm{m} = 4.63\,\mathrm{m}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The total-head balance between the tank free surface and the pump inlet uses absolute pressures. To obtain inlet pressure head, subtract the suction lift $z_a$, the suction-line loss and the velocity head, which is present at the inlet.

The absolute inlet pressure is $46.4\,\mathrm{kPa}$, less than half atmospheric pressure. This corresponds to a gauge pressure of $-54.9\,\mathrm{kPa}$, or $-5.60\,\mathrm{m}$ of water. The pump inlet is below atmospheric pressure, as for a pump mounted above the liquid surface.

Add the inlet velocity head and subtract the vapor-pressure head to obtain the net positive suction head, $4.63\,\mathrm{m}$. This exceeds the required $4.0\,\mathrm{m}$, leaving an operating margin of $0.63\,\mathrm{m}$.

An equivalent check starts with atmospheric head minus vapor-pressure head, then subtracts lift and suction-line loss. The velocity head subtracted to obtain inlet pressure is added back in the definition of net positive suction head. It cancels, giving the same result.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A pumping station must deliver $Q = 100\,\mathrm{L/s}$ against a static lift of $20\,\mathrm{m}$ through the pipe shown, of length $2000\,\mathrm{m}$ with $\lambda = 0.020$ and $\eta = 0.75$. The station runs $4000$ hours per year and the price of electricity is $0.20\,\text{€/kWh}$. The pipe costs $800\,\text{€}$ per meter of length per meter of diameter and is depreciated over $25$ years. Compare standard diameters $250$, $300$, $400$ and $500\,\mathrm{mm}$ and select the most economical.

::schema{nodi="A,1.6,1,-;P,3.6,1,-;C,7.2,3.6,-;U,10.4,3.6,-" acqua="0,1.4 1.6,1.4 1.6,-0.4 0,-0.4;10.4,4.8 12,4.8 12,3.2 10.4,3.2" solidi="0,-0.7 1.6,-0.7 1.6,-0.4 0,-0.4;10.4,2.9 12,2.9 12,3.2 10.4,3.2" condotte="A-P-C-U,0.16" pompa="3.6,1,P" pelo="0,1.6,1.4,upstream;10.4,12,4.8,downstream" tratteggi="1.6,1.4 13,1.4;10.4,4.8 13,4.8" quote="13 1.4,13 4.8,20.0 m" etichette="8.6,2.7,D=?;8.6,2.1,L=2000 m;8.6,1.5,Q=100 L/s" titolo="Pumping system for which the most economical pipe diameter is sought"}

$$
r = \frac{8 \cdot 0.020 \cdot 2000}{9.87 \cdot 9.81\,D^5} = \frac{3.305}{D^5}
\qquad
\Delta H = r\,Q^2 = \frac{0.03305}{D^5}
$$
$$
P = \frac{\gamma\,Q\,H_p}{\eta} = \frac{9.81 \cdot 0.100}{0.75}\,(20 + \Delta H) = 1.308\,(20 + \Delta H)
$$
$$
C_{\mathrm{en}} = P \cdot 4000 \cdot 0.20
\qquad
C_{\mathrm{cond}} = \frac{800\,D \cdot 2000}{25} = 64\,000\,D
$$

| $D$ (mm) | $v$ (m/s) | $\Delta H$ (m) | $H_p$ (m) | $P$ (kW) | $C_{\mathrm{en}}$ (€/year) | $C_{\mathrm{cond}}$ (€/year) | $C$ (€/year) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $250$ | $2.04$ | $33.8$ | $53.8$ | $70.4$ | $56\,300$ | $16\,000$ | $72\,300$ |
| $300$ | $1.41$ | $13.6$ | $33.6$ | $44.0$ | $35\,200$ | $19\,200$ | $54\,400$ |
| $400$ | $0.80$ | $3.23$ | $23.2$ | $30.4$ | $24\,300$ | $25\,600$ | $49\,900$ |
| $500$ | $0.51$ | $1.06$ | $21.1$ | $27.5$ | $22\,000$ | $32\,000$ | $54\,000$ |

The total cost is lowest at $D = 400\,\mathrm{mm}$.

Check by minimizing the cost function:
$$
C(D) = 20\,930 + \frac{34.6}{D^5} + 64\,000\,D
\qquad
C'(D) = -\frac{173}{D^6} + 64\,000 = 0
$$
$$
D^6 = \frac{173}{64\,000} = 2.70 \cdot 10^{-3}
\qquad
D = 0.373\,\mathrm{m}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The available elevation difference does not fix the diameter because the pump supplies the required head. Compare two opposing costs. The energy cost includes head loss proportional to $D^{-5}$, while the pipe cost increases in proportion to $D$.

In the table the annual energy cost falls from $56\,300$ to $22\,000\,\text{€}$ as diameter increases from $250$ to $500\,\mathrm{mm}$. The annualized pipe cost rises from $16\,000$ to $32\,000\,\text{€}$. Their sum reaches its minimum at $D = 400\,\mathrm{mm}$, the **economic diameter** among the four options.

The corresponding velocity is $0.80\,\mathrm{m/s}$, lower than would normally be chosen for a gravity pipeline. Economic diameters in pumped systems favor low velocities because each meter of head loss incurs an energy cost every year.

As a check, treat total cost as a continuous function of diameter and set its derivative to zero. The minimum occurs at $D = 0.373\,\mathrm{m}$, between $300$ and $400\,\mathrm{mm}$ and closer to the latter, in agreement with the table.

</div>
