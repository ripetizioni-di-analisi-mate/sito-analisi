## Exercise 1

A welded steel pipe of length $1000\,\mathrm{m}$, with $D = 200\,\mathrm{mm}$ and absolute roughness $\varepsilon = 0.1\,\mathrm{mm}$, carries $Q = 60\,\mathrm{L/s}$ between the two reservoirs shown. Find the Darcy friction factor using the Colebrook–White equation and calculate the total head loss.

::schema{acqua="0,4.2 1.8,4.2 1.8,2.4 0,2.4;10.2,1.4 12,1.4 12,-0.2 10.2,-0.2" solidi="0,2.1 1.8,2.1 1.8,2.4 0,2.4;10.2,-0.5 12,-0.5 12,-0.2 10.2,-0.2" nodi="A,1.8,2.8,-;U,10.2,0.6,-" condotte="A-U,0.16,D=200 mm" pelo="0,1.8,4.2,upstream;10.2,12,1.4,downstream" etichette="5.9,1.1,L=1000 m;5.9,0.5,ε=0.1 mm" titolo="Welded steel pipe with $Q = 60\,\mathrm{L/s}$"}

$$
A = \frac{\pi \cdot 0.200^2}{4}\,\mathrm{m^2} = 0.03142\,\mathrm{m^2}
\qquad
v = \frac{Q}{A} = \frac{0.060}{0.03142}\,\mathrm{m/s} = 1.910\,\mathrm{m/s}
$$
$$
Re = \frac{v D}{\nu} = \frac{1.910 \cdot 0.200}{10^{-6}} = 3.820 \cdot 10^{5}
\qquad
\frac{\varepsilon}{3.71\,D} = \frac{0.0001}{3.71 \cdot 0.200} = 1.348 \cdot 10^{-4}
$$

Start the iteration at $\lambda_0 = 0.0200$ and stop when successive values differ by less than $10^{-4}$:

| Step | Initial $\lambda$ | $Re\sqrt{\lambda}$ | $\dfrac{2.51}{Re\sqrt{\lambda}}$ | $\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda}}$ | Calculated $\lambda$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | $0.02000$ | $54\,019$ | $4.65 \cdot 10^{-5}$ | $7.4835$ | $0.017856$ |
| 2 | $0.017856$ | $51\,042$ | $4.92 \cdot 10^{-5}$ | $7.4706$ | $0.017918$ |
| 3 | $0.017918$ | $51\,130$ | $4.91 \cdot 10^{-5}$ | $7.4710$ | $0.017916$ |

$$
\lambda = 0.0179
\qquad
J = \lambda\,\frac{1}{D}\,\frac{v^2}{2g} = \frac{0.0179}{0.200} \cdot \frac{1.910^2}{19.62} = 0.01665
$$
$$
\Delta H = J\,L = 0.01665 \cdot 1000\,\mathrm{m} = 16.7\,\mathrm{m}
$$

Check using the equation expressed in terms of discharge:
$$
J = \frac{8\,\lambda\,Q^2}{\pi^2 g\,D^5} = \frac{8 \cdot 0.0179 \cdot 0.060^2}{9.87 \cdot 9.81 \cdot 0.200^5} = 0.01665
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The discharge and diameter are specified, so velocity and Reynolds number can be calculated immediately and remain fixed throughout. In the Colebrook–White equation the unknown $\lambda$ appears on both sides. Use **successive approximation**: substitute a trial value on the right, calculate $\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda}}$ and use it to update $\lambda$.

Convergence is rapid because $\lambda$ appears on the right only inside a logarithm. The second step already changes only the third decimal place, and the third changes the value by less than $10^{-4}$, meeting the stated stopping criterion. The final value $\lambda = 0.0179$ is within the usual range of $0.01$ to $0.08$.

The total head loss is **$16.7\,\mathrm{m}$**. The free surfaces must differ in elevation by this amount for the pipe to carry the required $60\,\mathrm{L/s}$. As a check, calculate the energy gradient directly from discharge without first finding velocity. The result is the same.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Two reservoirs with water-surface elevation difference $18\,\mathrm{m}$ are connected by a cast-iron pipe of length $1200\,\mathrm{m}$, with $D = 300\,\mathrm{mm}$ and $\varepsilon = 0.25\,\mathrm{mm}$, as shown. Find the discharge without iteration.

::schema{acqua="0,4.4 1.8,4.4 1.8,2.6 0,2.6;10.2,1.6 12,1.6 12,0 10.2,0" solidi="0,2.3 1.8,2.3 1.8,2.6 0,2.6;10.2,-0.3 12,-0.3 12,0 10.2,0" nodi="A,1.8,3,-;U,10.2,0.8,-" condotte="A-U,0.18,D=300 mm" pelo="0,1.8,4.4,upstream;10.2,12,1.6,downstream" tratteggi="1.8,4.4 13,4.4;10.2,1.6 13,1.6" quote="13 4.4,13 1.6,18.0 m" etichette="5.9,1.3,L=1200 m;5.9,0.7,ε=0.25 mm" titolo="Cast-iron pipe between reservoirs with a head difference of $18\,\mathrm{m}$"}

$$
J = \frac{18}{1200} = 0.01500
$$
$$
Re\sqrt{\lambda} = \frac{D}{\nu}\sqrt{2 g D J} = \frac{0.300}{10^{-6}}\sqrt{2 \cdot 9.81 \cdot 0.300 \cdot 0.01500} = 3.00 \cdot 10^{5} \cdot 0.2971 = 8.914 \cdot 10^{4}
$$
$$
\frac{2.51}{Re\sqrt{\lambda}} = 2.816 \cdot 10^{-5}
\qquad
\frac{\varepsilon}{3.71\,D} = \frac{0.00025}{3.71 \cdot 0.300} = 2.246 \cdot 10^{-4}
$$
$$
\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda}} = -2\log_{10}\!\left(2.816 \cdot 10^{-5} + 2.246 \cdot 10^{-4}\right) = -2\log_{10}\!\left(2.528 \cdot 10^{-4}\right) = 7.194
$$
$$
\lambda = \frac{1}{7.194^2} = 0.01932
$$
$$
v = \sqrt{\frac{2 g D J}{\lambda}} = \sqrt{\frac{0.08829}{0.01932}}\,\mathrm{m/s} = 2.14\,\mathrm{m/s}
\qquad
Q = 2.14 \cdot 0.0707\,\mathrm{m^3/s} = 0.151\,\mathrm{m^3/s}
$$

Check using the Reynolds number and the Colebrook–White equation:
$$
Re = \frac{2.14 \cdot 0.300}{10^{-6}} = 6.41 \cdot 10^{5}
\qquad
Re\sqrt{\lambda} = 6.41 \cdot 10^{5} \cdot \sqrt{0.01932} = 8.91 \cdot 10^{4}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

When the elevation difference is given, the hydraulic gradient is known from the outset. The product $Re\sqrt{\lambda}$ follows from the geometry and the known gradient: the unknown velocity cancels because $v\sqrt{\lambda} = \sqrt{2gDJ}$ by the Darcy–Weisbach equation. The unknown then appears only on the left of the Colebrook–White equation, giving $\lambda$ in one step.

The roughness term $2.246 \cdot 10^{-4}$ is almost an order of magnitude larger than the viscous term $2.816 \cdot 10^{-5}$. The pipe is therefore operating close to the fully rough turbulent regime, where $\lambda$ depends only on relative roughness.

The discharge is **$151\,\mathrm{L/s}$** and the velocity is $2.14\,\mathrm{m/s}$, within the usual range. Recalculate $Re$ from this velocity and check that the product $Re\sqrt{\lambda}$ matches the initial estimate. The solution therefore satisfies both equations.

</div>

## Exercise 3

After many years in service, deposits in the pipe from the previous problem increase its roughness to $\varepsilon = 1.5\,\mathrm{mm}$. The diameter and elevation difference remain as before, as shown. Find the new discharge and the reduction from its original value.

::schema{acqua="0,4.4 1.8,4.4 1.8,2.6 0,2.6;10.2,1.6 12,1.6 12,0 10.2,0" solidi="0,2.3 1.8,2.3 1.8,2.6 0,2.6;10.2,-0.3 12,-0.3 12,0 10.2,0" nodi="A,1.8,3,-;U,10.2,0.8,-" condotte="A-U,0.18,D=300 mm" pelo="0,1.8,4.4,upstream;10.2,12,1.6,downstream" tratteggi="1.8,4.4 13,4.4;10.2,1.6 13,1.6" quote="13 4.4,13 1.6,18.0 m" etichette="5.9,1.3,L=1200 m;5.9,0.7,ε=1.5 mm" titolo="The same pipe with roughness increased to $1.5\,\mathrm{mm}$ by deposits"}

$$
Re\sqrt{\lambda} = 8.914 \cdot 10^{4}
\qquad
\frac{\varepsilon}{3.71\,D} = \frac{0.0015}{3.71 \cdot 0.300} = 1.348 \cdot 10^{-3}
$$
$$
\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda}} = -2\log_{10}\!\left(2.816 \cdot 10^{-5} + 1.348 \cdot 10^{-3}\right) = -2\log_{10}\!\left(1.376 \cdot 10^{-3}\right) = 5.723
$$
$$
\lambda = \frac{1}{5.723^2} = 0.03053
$$
$$
v = \sqrt{\frac{0.08829}{0.03053}}\,\mathrm{m/s} = 1.70\,\mathrm{m/s}
\qquad
Q = 1.70 \cdot 0.0707\,\mathrm{m^3/s} = 0.120\,\mathrm{m^3/s}
$$
$$
\frac{151 - 120}{151} = 0.205
$$

Check using the ratio of the two discharges:
$$
\frac{Q_{\text{encrusted}}}{Q_{\text{initial}}} = \sqrt{\frac{\lambda_{\text{initial}}}{\lambda_{\text{encrusted}}}} = \sqrt{\frac{0.01932}{0.03053}} = 0.795
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The product $Re\sqrt{\lambda}$ depends only on $D$, $J$ and viscosity, so it remains the same as in the preceding exercise. The deposits change only the roughness. The term $\dfrac{\varepsilon}{3.71 D}$ increases sixfold and is nearly fifty times the viscous term, placing the flow in the fully rough turbulent regime.

The friction factor rises from $0.0193$ to $0.0305$, an increase of $58\,\%$. At fixed gradient the discharge varies as $\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda}}$ and decreases by $20.5\,\%$. The relative change in discharge is about half that in the friction factor, consistent with the square-root dependence.

The new discharge is **$120\,\mathrm{L/s}$**. Check the ratio of discharges directly using the square root of the inverse ratio of friction factors. The ratio is again $0.795$, a reduction of $20.5\,\%$. This explains why water mains that have been in operation for decades can carry less than their original design discharge.

</div>
