## Exercise 1

A water main must deliver $Q = 80\,\mathrm{L/s}$ from a reservoir to a flow divider $30\,\mathrm{m}$ below it along a route of length $2000\,\mathrm{m}$, as shown. The pipe is lined cast iron with $k_s = 90\,\mathrm{m^{1/3}/s}$. Select a standard diameter and find the residual head.

::schema{acqua="0,4.6 1.8,4.6 1.8,2.8 0,2.8;10.2,1.6 12,1.6 12,0 10.2,0" solidi="0,2.5 1.8,2.5 1.8,2.8 0,2.8;10.2,-0.3 12,-0.3 12,0 10.2,0" nodi="A,1.8,3.2,-;U,10.2,0.8,-" condotte="A-U,0.18,D=?" pelo="0,1.8,4.6,reservoir;10.2,12,1.6,flow divider" tratteggi="1.8,4.6 13,4.6;10.2,1.6 13,1.6" quote="13 4.6,13 1.6,30.0 m" etichette="5.9,1.3,L=2000 m;5.9,0.7,Q=80 L/s" titolo="Route of the water main to be sized"}

$$
J = \frac{30}{2000} = 0.01500
$$
$$
D = \left(\frac{10.29\;Q^2}{k_s^2\,J}\right)^{\frac{3}{16}}
= \left(\frac{10.29 \cdot 0.080^2}{90^2 \cdot 0.01500}\right)^{\frac{3}{16}}
= \left(5.422 \cdot 10^{-4}\right)^{\frac{3}{16}} = 0.244\,\mathrm{m}
$$

Selected standard diameter $D = 250\,\mathrm{mm}$:
$$
J_{\mathrm{eff}} = \frac{10.29 \cdot 0.080^2}{90^2 \cdot 0.250^{\frac{16}{3}}} = \frac{6.586 \cdot 10^{-2}}{8100 \cdot 6.152 \cdot 10^{-4}} = 0.01322
$$
$$
\Delta H_{\mathrm{eff}} = 0.01322 \cdot 2000\,\mathrm{m} = 26.4\,\mathrm{m}
\qquad
\Delta H_{\mathrm{res}} = 30 - 26.4 = 3.6\,\mathrm{m}
$$
$$
v = \frac{Q}{A} = \frac{0.080}{0.0491}\,\mathrm{m/s} = 1.63\,\mathrm{m/s}
$$

Check using the original form of the Gauckler–Strickler equation:
$$
R = \frac{D}{4} = 0.0625\,\mathrm{m}
\qquad
v = k_s\,R^{\frac{2}{3}}\,J_{\mathrm{eff}}^{\frac{1}{2}} = 90 \cdot 0.0625^{\frac{2}{3}} \cdot 0.01322^{\frac{1}{2}}\,\mathrm{m/s} = 1.63\,\mathrm{m/s}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The available head equals the elevation difference between the reservoir and the distribution chamber. The pipe alignment is specified, so the design hydraulic gradient is $J = 0.0150$. Solve the discharge form of the Gauckler–Strickler equation for $D$. The resulting design equation gives $D = 0.244\,\mathrm{m}$.

This diameter is not a standard size, so select the **next larger diameter**, $250\,\mathrm{mm}$. The actual energy gradient falls to $0.01322$ and the pipe consumes $26.4\,\mathrm{m}$ of the available $30\,\mathrm{m}$. The remaining $3.6\,\mathrm{m}$ is **residual head**. It can be dissipated by a gate valve at the outlet or retained as additional pressure at the flow divider.

The next smaller standard diameter, $200\,\mathrm{mm}$, would give a gradient greater by a factor of $\left(\dfrac{250}{200}\right)^{\frac{16}{3}} = 3.29$ and a head loss of about $87\,\mathrm{m}$. The available elevation difference would be insufficient for the required discharge.

The velocity is $1.63\,\mathrm{m/s}$, within the normal range for a water main. As a check, return to the original Gauckler–Strickler equation. Substituting the hydraulic radius $R = \dfrac{D}{4}$ for the full circular pipe reproduces the velocity.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A transmission main of length $2000\,\mathrm{m}$ must carry $Q = 150\,\mathrm{L/s}$ with an available head of $30\,\mathrm{m}$, as shown. The pipe is steel with $\varepsilon = 0.2\,\mathrm{mm}$. Calculate its diameter using the Darcy–Weisbach equation and select a standard size.

::schema{acqua="0,4.6 1.8,4.6 1.8,2.8 0,2.8;10.2,1.6 12,1.6 12,0 10.2,0" solidi="0,2.5 1.8,2.5 1.8,2.8 0,2.8;10.2,-0.3 12,-0.3 12,0 10.2,0" nodi="A,1.8,3.2,-;U,10.2,0.8,-" condotte="A-U,0.18,D=?" pelo="0,1.8,4.6,upstream;10.2,12,1.6,downstream" tratteggi="1.8,4.6 13,4.6;10.2,1.6 13,1.6" quote="13 4.6,13 1.6,30.0 m" etichette="5.9,1.3,L=2000 m;5.9,0.7,Q=150 L/s" titolo="Sizing a pipe using the Darcy–Weisbach equation"}

$$
J = \frac{30}{2000} = 0.01500
\qquad
D = \left(\frac{8\,\lambda\,Q^2}{\pi^2 g\,J}\right)^{\frac{1}{5}} = \left(0.1239\,\lambda\right)^{\frac{1}{5}}
$$

Start the iteration at $\lambda_0 = 0.0190$ and stop when the change in $D$ is less than $1\,\mathrm{mm}$:

| Step | $\lambda$ | $D$ (m) | $v$ (m/s) | $Re$ | $\lambda$ from Colebrook–White |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | $0.01900$ | $0.2981$ | $2.149$ | $6.41 \cdot 10^5$ | $0.018476$ |
| 2 | $0.018476$ | $0.2965$ | $2.173$ | $6.44 \cdot 10^5$ | $0.018493$ |
| 3 | $0.018493$ | $0.2965$ | $2.172$ | $6.44 \cdot 10^5$ | $0.018492$ |

Selected standard diameter $D = 300\,\mathrm{mm}$:
$$
v = \frac{0.150}{0.0707}\,\mathrm{m/s} = 2.12\,\mathrm{m/s}
\qquad
Re = \frac{2.12 \cdot 0.300}{10^{-6}} = 6.37 \cdot 10^{5}
\qquad
\lambda = 0.01846
$$
$$
J_{\mathrm{eff}} = \frac{0.01846}{0.300} \cdot \frac{2.12^2}{19.62} = 0.01412
\qquad
\Delta H_{\mathrm{eff}} = 28.2\,\mathrm{m}
\qquad
\Delta H_{\mathrm{res}} = 1.8\,\mathrm{m}
$$

Check using the next smaller standard diameter:
$$
v_{250} = \frac{0.150}{0.0491}\,\mathrm{m/s} = 3.06\,\mathrm{m/s}
\qquad
\lambda_{250} = 0.01907
\qquad
\Delta H_{250} = \frac{0.01907}{0.250} \cdot \frac{3.06^2}{19.62} \cdot 2000\,\mathrm{m} = 72.6\,\mathrm{m}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The diameter and friction factor are both unknown because $\lambda$ depends on Reynolds number and relative roughness, which depend in turn on $D$. Start with a trial value $\lambda_0 = 0.0190$. Calculate the diameter, velocity and Reynolds number, then use the Colebrook–White equation to update $\lambda$.

The second step changes the diameter by $1.6\,\mathrm{mm}$. On the third it is unchanged to the nearest tenth of a millimeter, meeting the stopping criterion. The calculated diameter is $0.2965\,\mathrm{m}$. Convergence is rapid because $D$ depends on $\lambda^{\frac{1}{5}}$, so a relative error of $10\,\%$ in $\lambda$ gives an error of $2\,\%$ in $D$.

Select $D = 300\,\mathrm{mm}$. The actual loss is $28.2\,\mathrm{m}$ and the residual head is $1.8\,\mathrm{m}$. To check the choice, consider the next smaller standard diameter. At $250\,\mathrm{mm}$ the velocity would rise to $3.06\,\mathrm{m/s}$ and the loss to $72.6\,\mathrm{m}$, more than twice the available head. The pipe would **not** carry the required discharge.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A steel pipe with $k_s = 85\,\mathrm{m^{1/3}/s}$, of length $3000\,\mathrm{m}$, must carry $Q = 100\,\mathrm{L/s}$ with an available head of $40\,\mathrm{m}$, as shown. Select a standard diameter, calculate the residual head, and find the gate-valve loss coefficient needed to dissipate it.

::schema{acqua="0,5 1.8,5 1.8,3.2 0,3.2;10.2,1.2 12,1.2 12,-0.4 10.2,-0.4" solidi="0,2.9 1.8,2.9 1.8,3.2 0,3.2;10.2,-0.7 12,-0.7 12,-0.4 10.2,-0.4" nodi="A,1.8,3.6,-;V,9.4,0.6,-;U,10.2,0.4,-" condotte="A-V-U,0.18,D=?" pelo="0,1.8,5,upstream;10.2,12,1.2,downstream" tratteggi="1.8,5 13,5;10.2,1.2 13,1.2" quote="13 5,13 1.2,40.0 m" etichette="5.4,1.1,L=3000 m;8.6,2.0,gate valve" titolo="Pipe with a regulating gate valve at the outlet"}

$$
J = \frac{40}{3000} = 0.01333
\qquad
D = \left(\frac{10.29 \cdot 0.100^2}{85^2 \cdot 0.01333}\right)^{\frac{3}{16}} = \left(1.068 \cdot 10^{-3}\right)^{\frac{3}{16}} = 0.277\,\mathrm{m}
$$

Selected standard diameter $D = 300\,\mathrm{mm}$:
$$
J_{\mathrm{eff}} = \frac{10.29 \cdot 0.100^2}{85^2 \cdot 0.300^{\frac{16}{3}}} = \frac{0.1029}{7225 \cdot 1.627 \cdot 10^{-3}} = 8.76 \cdot 10^{-3}
$$
$$
\Delta H_{\mathrm{eff}} = 8.76 \cdot 10^{-3} \cdot 3000\,\mathrm{m} = 26.3\,\mathrm{m}
\qquad
\Delta H_{\mathrm{res}} = 40 - 26.3 = 13.7\,\mathrm{m}
$$
$$
v = \frac{0.100}{0.0707}\,\mathrm{m/s} = 1.41\,\mathrm{m/s}
\qquad
\frac{v^2}{2g} = \frac{1.41^2}{19.62}\,\mathrm{m} = 0.102\,\mathrm{m}
$$
$$
\xi = \frac{\Delta H_{\mathrm{res}}}{\dfrac{v^2}{2g}} = \frac{13.7}{0.102} = 134
$$

Check using the overall energy balance:
$$
\Delta H_{\mathrm{eff}} + \xi\,\frac{v^2}{2g} = 26.3 + 134 \cdot 0.102\,\mathrm{m} = 26.3 + 13.7\,\mathrm{m} = 40.0\,\mathrm{m}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The design equation gives $D = 0.277\,\mathrm{m}$, so select $D = 300\,\mathrm{mm}$. The next smaller standard diameter, $250\,\mathrm{mm}$, would give a gradient greater by a factor of $\left(\dfrac{300}{250}\right)^{\frac{16}{3}} = 2.64$, or a loss of about $70\,\mathrm{m}$, exceeding the available elevation difference.

The standard diameters are widely spaced, leaving a substantial residual head. The $300\,\mathrm{mm}$ pipe dissipates $26.3\,\mathrm{m}$ and leaves $13.7\,\mathrm{m}$, more than one third of the elevation difference. A gate valve at the outlet must dissipate this head. Otherwise the pipe would carry more than the required discharge.

Set the gate-valve loss equal to the residual head. The resulting coefficient, $\xi = 134$, means that the valve is heavily throttled: only a small fraction of its opening is available. As a check, add the friction loss and valve loss. Their sum recovers the elevation difference of $40\,\mathrm{m}$, satisfying the energy balance.

</div>
