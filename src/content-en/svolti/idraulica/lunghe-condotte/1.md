## Exercise 1

Two reservoirs at constant level are connected by a steel pipe of length $1200\,\mathrm{m}$ and diameter $D = 300\,\mathrm{mm}$ with Darcy friction factor $\lambda = 0.025$. The upstream water surface lies $20\,\mathrm{m}$ above the downstream water surface, as shown. Find the discharge.

::schema{acqua="0,4.6 1.8,4.6 1.8,2.8 0,2.8;10.2,1.6 12,1.6 12,0 10.2,0" solidi="0,2.5 1.8,2.5 1.8,2.8 0,2.8;10.2,-0.3 12,-0.3 12,0 10.2,0" nodi="A,1.8,3.2,-;U,10.2,0.8,-" condotte="A-U,0.18,D=300 mm" pelo="0,1.8,4.6,upstream;10.2,12,1.6,downstream" tratteggi="1.8,4.6 13,4.6;10.2,1.6 13,1.6" quote="13 4.6,13 1.6,20.0 m" etichette="5.8,1.3,L=1200 m" titolo="Pipe between two reservoirs with a head difference of $20\,\mathrm{m}$"}

$$
J = \frac{z_1 - z_2}{L} = \frac{20}{1200} = 0.01667
$$
$$
v = \sqrt{\frac{2 g D J}{\lambda}} = \sqrt{\frac{2 \cdot 9.81 \cdot 0.300 \cdot 0.01667}{0.025}}\,\mathrm{m/s} = \sqrt{3.924}\,\mathrm{m/s} = 1.98\,\mathrm{m/s}
$$
$$
A = \frac{\pi D^2}{4} = \frac{\pi \cdot 0.300^2}{4}\,\mathrm{m^2} = 0.0707\,\mathrm{m^2}
$$
$$
Q = v\,A = 1.98 \cdot 0.0707\,\mathrm{m^3/s} = 0.140\,\mathrm{m^3/s} = 140\,\mathrm{L/s}
$$

Check the Reynolds number and head loss:
$$
Re = \frac{v D}{\nu} = \frac{1.98 \cdot 0.300}{10^{-6}} = 5.9 \cdot 10^{5}
$$
$$
\Delta H = \lambda\,\frac{L}{D}\,\frac{v^2}{2g} = 0.025 \cdot \frac{1200}{0.300} \cdot \frac{1.98^2}{19.62}\,\mathrm{m} = 100 \cdot 0.200\,\mathrm{m} = 20.0\,\mathrm{m}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Both reservoirs have large surface areas and constant levels. The free surfaces have zero gauge pressure and negligible velocity, so their total heads equal their elevations. The entire elevation difference is available to overcome losses. Here $\lambda\,\dfrac{L}{D} = 100$, which is much greater than the sum of the minor-loss coefficients. The **long-pipeline model** therefore applies, and the entire head difference is consumed by pipe friction.

The energy gradient is known before the discharge. Its value of $J = 0.01667$ means that the hydraulic grade line falls about $1.67\,\mathrm{cm}$ per meter of pipe. Solving the Darcy–Weisbach equation for velocity gives $v = 1.98\,\mathrm{m/s}$, and the continuity equation $Q = vA$ gives the discharge.

The Reynolds number is $5.9 \cdot 10^{5}$, confirming the turbulent flow assumed in assigning $\lambda$. As a final check, use the calculated velocity to find the total head loss. This reproduces the original elevation difference, $20.0\,\mathrm{m}$. The discharge is therefore **$140\,\mathrm{L/s}$** from the upstream reservoir to the downstream reservoir.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A water main of length $2500\,\mathrm{m}$ with $D = 250\,\mathrm{mm}$ and $\lambda = 0.022$ connects two tanks whose surface elevations differ by $35\,\mathrm{m}$, as shown. Find the discharge, mean velocity and velocity head, and verify that the velocity head is negligible compared with the elevation difference.

::schema{acqua="0,5 1.8,5 1.8,3.2 0,3.2;10.2,1.2 12,1.2 12,-0.4 10.2,-0.4" solidi="0,2.9 1.8,2.9 1.8,3.2 0,3.2;10.2,-0.7 12,-0.7 12,-0.4 10.2,-0.4" nodi="A,1.8,3.6,-;U,10.2,0.4,-" condotte="A-U,0.18,D=250 mm" pelo="0,1.8,5,upstream;10.2,12,1.2,downstream" tratteggi="1.8,5 13,5;10.2,1.2 13,1.2" quote="13 5,13 1.2,35.0 m" etichette="5.8,1,L=2500 m" titolo="Transmission main between two tanks with a head difference of $35\,\mathrm{m}$"}

$$
J = \frac{35}{2500} = 0.01400
$$
$$
v = \sqrt{\frac{2 \cdot 9.81 \cdot 0.250 \cdot 0.01400}{0.022}}\,\mathrm{m/s} = \sqrt{3.121}\,\mathrm{m/s} = 1.77\,\mathrm{m/s}
$$
$$
A = \frac{\pi \cdot 0.250^2}{4}\,\mathrm{m^2} = 0.0491\,\mathrm{m^2}
\qquad
Q = 1.77 \cdot 0.0491\,\mathrm{m^3/s} = 0.0867\,\mathrm{m^3/s} = 86.7\,\mathrm{L/s}
$$
$$
\frac{v^2}{2g} = \frac{1.77^2}{19.62}\,\mathrm{m} = 0.159\,\mathrm{m}
\qquad
\frac{0.159}{35} = 0.0045
$$

Check using hydraulic resistance:
$$
r = \frac{8\,\lambda\,L}{\pi^2 g\,D^5} = \frac{8 \cdot 0.022 \cdot 2500}{9.87 \cdot 9.81 \cdot 0.250^5}\,\mathrm{s^2/m^5} = 4654\,\mathrm{s^2/m^5}
$$
$$
\Delta H = r\,Q^2 = 4654 \cdot 0.0867^2\,\mathrm{m} = 35.0\,\mathrm{m}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

As in the previous exercise, the elevation difference gives the energy gradient, the Darcy–Weisbach equation gives the velocity, and continuity gives the discharge. The Reynolds number is $4.4 \cdot 10^{5}$, so the flow is turbulent.

The velocity head is $0.159\,\mathrm{m}$, just $0.45\,\%$ of the elevation difference of $35\,\mathrm{m}$. Neglecting it changes the result by less than half a percentage point. This comparison justifies using the long-pipeline model and explains why the hydraulic and energy grade lines can be drawn as coincident.

Check the result using $\Delta H = r Q^2$. The hydraulic resistance depends only on the given pipe geometry and friction factor. At the calculated discharge, the head loss reproduces the specified elevation difference. The discharge is **$86.7\,\mathrm{L/s}$** and the velocity is $1.77\,\mathrm{m/s}$, a typical value for a transmission main.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A pipe of length $800\,\mathrm{m}$ with $D = 400\,\mathrm{mm}$ and $\lambda = 0.023$ connects two reservoirs with water-surface elevation difference $12\,\mathrm{m}$. The diagram shows an entrance ($\xi = 0.5$), two bends ($\xi = 0.3$ each) and an exit into the downstream reservoir ($\xi = 1$). Calculate the discharge using both the long-pipeline model and the full energy balance, and compare the results.

::schema{acqua="0,4.4 1.8,4.4 1.8,2.6 0,2.6;10.2,1.4 12,1.4 12,-0.2 10.2,-0.2" solidi="0,2.3 1.8,2.3 1.8,2.6 0,2.6;10.2,-0.5 12,-0.5 12,-0.2 10.2,-0.2" nodi="A,1.8,3,-;B,4.6,2.5,-;C,7.4,2.3,-;U,10.2,0.6,-" condotte="A-B-C-U,0.2" pelo="0,1.8,4.4,upstream;10.2,12,1.4,downstream" tratteggi="1.8,4.4 13,4.4;10.2,1.4 13,1.4" quote="13 4.4,13 1.4,12.0 m" etichette="4.6,3.2,bend;7.4,3,bend;5.9,1.6,D=400 mm;5.9,1,L=800 m" titolo="Pipe with an entrance, two bends and an exit"}

Long-pipeline model:
$$
J = \frac{12}{800} = 0.01500
\qquad
v = \sqrt{\frac{2 \cdot 9.81 \cdot 0.400 \cdot 0.01500}{0.023}}\,\mathrm{m/s} = 2.26\,\mathrm{m/s}
$$
$$
A = \frac{\pi \cdot 0.400^2}{4}\,\mathrm{m^2} = 0.1257\,\mathrm{m^2}
\qquad
Q = 2.26 \cdot 0.1257\,\mathrm{m^3/s} = 0.284\,\mathrm{m^3/s}
$$

Full energy balance:
$$
12 = \left(\lambda\,\frac{L}{D} + \sum \xi\right)\frac{v^2}{2g}
= \left(0.023 \cdot \frac{800}{0.400} + 0.5 + 0.3 + 0.3 + 1\right)\frac{v^2}{2g}
$$
$$
12 = (46.0 + 2.1)\,\frac{v^2}{2g} = 48.1\,\frac{v^2}{2g}
$$
$$
v = \sqrt{\frac{2 \cdot 9.81 \cdot 12}{48.1}}\,\mathrm{m/s} = 2.21\,\mathrm{m/s}
\qquad
Q = 2.21 \cdot 0.1257\,\mathrm{m^3/s} = 0.278\,\mathrm{m^3/s}
$$

Comparison:
$$
\frac{0.284 - 0.278}{0.284} = 0.022
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

In the full energy balance, the elevation difference is divided between friction and minor losses. The pipe diameter is constant, so continuity gives the same velocity at every section and each loss can be written as a multiple of the same velocity head. The exit coefficient $\xi = 1$ accounts for the kinetic energy dissipated as the water enters the downstream reservoir.

Compare the coefficients. Pipe friction contributes $46.0$ and all the minor losses together contribute $2.1$, or $4.4\,\%$ of the total. The long-pipeline model therefore overestimates discharge by $2.2\,\%$, about half the relative difference between loss coefficients, because discharge depends on the square root of the combined coefficient.

The two-percentage-point difference is smaller than the uncertainty in $\lambda$ for actual pipes. The long-pipeline approximation is therefore **acceptable** in this case. The simplified model gives $284\,\mathrm{L/s}$ and the full balance gives $278\,\mathrm{L/s}$.

</div>
