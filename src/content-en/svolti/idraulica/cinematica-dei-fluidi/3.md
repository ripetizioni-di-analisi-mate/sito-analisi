## Exercise 1

The water main shown has diameter $D = 200\,\mathrm{mm}$ and carries a discharge of $Q = 45\,\mathrm{L/s}$ at a water temperature of $20\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$. The kinematic viscosity is $\nu = 10^{-6}\,\mathrm{m^2/s}$. Calculate the Reynolds number and identify the flow regime.

::schema{nodi="A,0,0,-;B,7,0,-" condotte="A-B,1.0,D = 200 mm" frecce="1.5,0,3.0,0,Q = 45 L/s" riferimento=0 titolo="Water main carrying water at $20\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$"}

$$
A = \frac{\pi \cdot (0.200)^2}{4}\,\mathrm{m^2} = 0.0314\,\mathrm{m^2}
$$
$$
v = \frac{Q}{A} = \frac{0.0450}{0.0314}\,\mathrm{m/s} = 1.43\,\mathrm{m/s}
$$
$$
Re = \frac{v\,D}{\nu} = \frac{1.43 \cdot 0.200}{10^{-6}} = 2.86 \cdot 10^5
$$

Check the Reynolds number using the discharge directly.
$$
Re = \frac{4\,Q}{\pi\,D\,\nu} = \frac{4 \cdot 0.0450}{\pi \cdot 0.200 \cdot 10^{-6}} = 2.86 \cdot 10^5
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The Reynolds number depends on mean velocity, diameter, and kinematic viscosity. First find mean velocity by dividing the discharge by the cross-sectional area.

The result $2.86 \cdot 10^5$ is much greater than $4000$, so the flow is **turbulent**. This Reynolds number is of the magnitude typical of water mains, generally between $10^5$ and $10^6$.

The check uses $Re = \dfrac{4Q}{\pi D \nu}$, obtained by substituting $v = \dfrac{4Q}{\pi D^2}$ into the definition of the Reynolds number. This bypasses the intermediate velocity calculation and gives the same result.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Water flows through the glass tube of the Reynolds apparatus shown. The tube has diameter $D = 20\,\mathrm{mm}$ and the mean velocity is $v = 0.0800\,\mathrm{m/s}$. Calculate the Reynolds number, the discharge, and the mean velocity at which the flow reaches $Re = 2000$.

::schema{acqua="0,0.8 2.4,0.8 2.4,3.6 0,3.6" solidi="-0.25,0.55 0,0.55 0,3.8 -0.25,3.8;-0.25,0.55 2.65,0.55 2.65,0.8 -0.25,0.8;2.4,0.8 2.65,0.8 2.65,1.55 2.4,1.55;2.4,2.05 2.65,2.05 2.65,3.8 2.4,3.8" pelo="0,2.4,3.6" nodi="A,2.4,1.8,-;B,8.4,1.8,-" condotte="A-B,0.5,D = 20 mm" linee="1.2,4.0 1.2,1.86 8.4,1.86" frecce="5.2,1.66,6.5,1.66,v" etichette="1.2,4.35,dye" riferimento=0 titolo="Glass tube supplied from a constant-head tank"}

$$
Re = \frac{v\,D}{\nu} = \frac{0.0800 \cdot 0.0200}{10^{-6}} = 1600
$$
$$
A = \frac{\pi \cdot (0.0200)^2}{4}\,\mathrm{m^2} = 3.14 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m^2}
$$
$$
Q = A\,v = 3.14 \cdot 10^{-4} \cdot 0.0800\,\mathrm{m^3/s} = 2.51 \cdot 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{m^3/s} = 0.0251\,\mathrm{L/s}
$$
$$
Re_c = \frac{v_c\,D}{\nu} = 2000
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
v_c = \frac{2000\,\nu}{D} = \frac{2000 \cdot 10^{-6}}{0.0200}\,\mathrm{m/s} = 0.100\,\mathrm{m/s}
$$

Check the ratio of the two velocities.
$$
\frac{Re_c}{Re} = \frac{v_c}{v} = \frac{0.100}{0.0800} = 1.25 = \frac{2000}{1600}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The Reynolds number is $1600$, below $2000$. The flow is therefore **laminar**, and the dye filament remains straight along the entire tube.

Multiplying area by mean velocity gives a discharge on the order of hundredths of a liter per second, consistent with a laboratory apparatus.

Set $Re = 2000$ in the Reynolds number definition and solve for the velocity at the critical value. For the same liquid and diameter, Reynolds number is proportional to velocity, so multiplying the current velocity by $\dfrac{2000}{1600}$ gives the critical velocity. The final check verifies this proportionality.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A pressurized rectangular duct with sides $b = 0.600\,\mathrm{m}$ and $a = 0.400\,\mathrm{m}$ carries a water flow rate of $Q = 200\,\mathrm{L/s}$, as shown. Calculate the hydraulic radius, hydraulic diameter, mean velocity, and Reynolds number.

::schema{poligoni="0,0 0.6,0 0.6,0.4 0,0.4" quote="0 0,0.6 0,0.600 m,0.09;0.6 0,0.6 0.4,0.400 m,0.09" riferimento=0 titolo="Cross section of the pressurized rectangular duct"}

$$
A = b\,a = 0.600 \cdot 0.400\,\mathrm{m^2} = 0.240\,\mathrm{m^2}
$$
$$
C = 2\,(b + a) = 2 \cdot (0.600 + 0.400)\,\mathrm{m} = 2.00\,\mathrm{m}
$$
$$
R = \frac{A}{C} = \frac{0.240}{2.00}\,\mathrm{m} = 0.120\,\mathrm{m}
$$
$$
D_e = 4R = 0.480\,\mathrm{m}
$$
$$
v = \frac{Q}{A} = \frac{0.200}{0.240}\,\mathrm{m/s} = 0.833\,\mathrm{m/s}
$$
$$
Re = \frac{v\,D_e}{\nu} = \frac{0.833 \cdot 0.480}{10^{-6}} = 4.00 \cdot 10^5
$$

Check the result against a circular pipe of the same area.
$$
D = \sqrt{\frac{4 \cdot 0.240}{\pi}}\,\mathrm{m} = 0.553\,\mathrm{m},
\qquad
Re = \frac{0.833 \cdot 0.553}{10^{-6}} = 4.61 \cdot 10^5
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The pressurized duct is full, so its entire perimeter $2(b + a)$ is wetted. The **hydraulic radius** is the area divided by the wetted perimeter. For a noncircular section, this ratio takes the place of the quarter-diameter used for a full circular pipe.

The characteristic length in the Reynolds number is therefore the **hydraulic diameter** $D_e = 4R$. For a full circular pipe it equals the actual diameter because $R = \dfrac{D}{4}$ in that case.

The Reynolds number $4.00 \cdot 10^5$ indicates turbulent flow. For comparison, using the diameter of a circular pipe of the same area, $0.553\,\mathrm{m}$, gives $4.61 \cdot 10^5$. The two Reynolds numbers are of the same order of magnitude and predict the same regime, but the hydraulic diameter gives the value appropriate to the rectangular duct.

</div>
