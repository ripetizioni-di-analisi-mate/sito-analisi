## Exercise 1

The pipe shown has length $1500\,\mathrm{m}$, diameter $300\,\mathrm{mm}$ and $\lambda = 0.021$. It receives $Q_m = 120\,\mathrm{L/s}$ upstream, supplies a uniform withdrawal along its length and delivers a throughflow of $Q_v = 60\,\mathrm{L/s}$ downstream. Calculate the withdrawal per unit length, the equivalent discharge and the head loss in the pipe.

::schema{nodi="M,2,6;V,24,6" condotte="M-V,0.45," frecce="4.5,5.4,4.5,3.8,;7,5.4,7,3.8,;9.5,5.4,9.5,3.8,;12,5.4,12,3.8,;14.5,5.4,14.5,3.8,;17,5.4,17,3.8,;19.5,5.4,19.5,3.8,;22,5.4,22,3.8," etichette="2,7.8,Q_m = 120 L/s;24,7.8,Q_v = 60 L/s;13,2.9,L = 1500 m - D = 300 mm" titolo="Pipe with water withdrawn uniformly along its length"}

$$
r = \frac{8 \cdot 0.021 \cdot 1500}{9.81 \cdot \pi^2 \cdot 0.300^5} = 1071\,\mathrm{s^2/m^5}
$$
$$
p = \frac{Q_m - Q_v}{L} = \frac{0.120 - 0.060}{1500}\,\mathrm{m^2/s} = 4.00 \cdot 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{m^2/s} = 0.0400\,\mathrm{L/(s\,m)}
$$
$$
Q_e = \sqrt{\frac{Q_m^2 + Q_m Q_v + Q_v^2}{3}} = \sqrt{\frac{0.0144 + 0.0072 + 0.0036}{3}}\,\mathrm{m^3/s}
$$
$$
Q_e = \sqrt{0.00840}\,\mathrm{m^3/s} = 0.0917\,\mathrm{m^3/s} = 91.7\,\mathrm{L/s}
$$
$$
\Delta H = r\,Q_e^2 = 1071 \cdot 0.00840 = 9.00\,\mathrm{m}
$$

Check by dividing the pipe into two lengths of $750\,\mathrm{m}$, each of resistance $\dfrac{r}{2} = 535.5\,\mathrm{s^2/m^5}$, with discharge $90\,\mathrm{L/s}$ at the midpoint.
$$
\Delta H_1 = \frac{535.5}{3}\left(0.120^2 + 0.120 \cdot 0.090 + 0.090^2\right) = 178.5 \cdot 0.03330 = 5.94\,\mathrm{m}
$$
$$
\Delta H_2 = \frac{535.5}{3}\left(0.090^2 + 0.090 \cdot 0.060 + 0.060^2\right) = 178.5 \cdot 0.01710 = 3.05\,\mathrm{m}
$$
$$
\Delta H_1 + \Delta H_2 = 5.94 + 3.05 = 8.99\,\mathrm{m} \simeq \Delta H
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Divide the total withdrawal $60\,\mathrm{L/s}$ by the pipe length to obtain the withdrawal per unit length, $0.0400\,\mathrm{L/s}$ for each meter of pipe.

As discharge decreases along the pipe, so does the hydraulic gradient. Integrating the head loss over successive pipe elements gives $\Delta H = \dfrac{r}{3}\left(Q_m^2 + Q_m Q_v + Q_v^2\right)$. The result can be expressed in terms of the **equivalent discharge** $Q_e = 91.7\,\mathrm{L/s}$, the constant discharge that would produce the same loss.

The result is $\Delta H = 9.00\,\mathrm{m}$, compared with $15.42\,\mathrm{m}$ using $Q_m$ alone and $3.86\,\mathrm{m}$ using $Q_v$ alone. The correct value lies between the two extremes, with the equivalent discharge closer to the mean of the end discharges.

Velocity falls from $1.70\,\mathrm{m/s}$ at the inlet to $0.85\,\mathrm{m/s}$ at the outlet, remaining within the usual operating range throughout the pipe.

The check applies the same formula to each half of the pipe, using its own resistance and end discharges, then adds the losses. Rounding accounts for the difference of one hundredth of a meter.

</div>

## Exercise 2

The pipe shown has length $2000\,\mathrm{m}$, diameter $250\,\mathrm{mm}$ and $\lambda = 0.022$. It receives $Q_m = 90\,\mathrm{L/s}$ upstream and supplies all of this water uniformly along its length, leaving zero downstream throughflow. Find the equivalent discharge and head loss. Compare the head loss with the value if all the inflow reached the downstream end.

::schema{nodi="M,2,6;V,24,6" condotte="M-V,0.4," frecce="4.5,5.4,4.5,3.8,;7,5.4,7,3.8,;9.5,5.4,9.5,3.8,;12,5.4,12,3.8,;14.5,5.4,14.5,3.8,;17,5.4,17,3.8,;19.5,5.4,19.5,3.8,;22,5.4,22,3.8," etichette="2,7.8,Q_m = 90 L/s;24,7.8,Q_v = 0;13,2.9,L = 2000 m - D = 250 mm" titolo="All the inflow is withdrawn along the pipe"}

$$
r = \frac{8 \cdot 0.022 \cdot 2000}{9.81 \cdot \pi^2 \cdot 0.250^5} = 3723\,\mathrm{s^2/m^5}
$$
$$
Q_v = 0
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
Q_e = \sqrt{\frac{Q_m^2}{3}} = \frac{Q_m}{\sqrt{3}} = \frac{0.090}{1.732}\,\mathrm{m^3/s} = 0.0520\,\mathrm{m^3/s}
$$
$$
Q_e = 52.0\,\mathrm{L/s} = 0.577\,Q_m
$$
$$
\Delta H = r\,Q_e^2 = 3723 \cdot \frac{0.090^2}{3} = 3723 \cdot 0.00270 = 10.05\,\mathrm{m}
$$
$$
\Delta H_{\text{with } Q_m} = 3723 \cdot 0.090^2 = 30.16\,\mathrm{m}
$$

Check using the approximate mean-discharge formula.
$$
Q_e \simeq 0.55\,Q_m = 49.5\,\mathrm{L/s} ,
\qquad
\Delta H \simeq 3723 \cdot 0.0495^2 = 9.12\,\mathrm{m}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

With $Q_v = 0$, the head-loss formula reduces to $\Delta H = \dfrac{r\,Q_m^2}{3}$, giving the equivalent discharge $\dfrac{Q_m}{\sqrt{3}} = 0.577\,Q_m$.

The head loss is $10.05\,\mathrm{m}$, one third of the $30.16\,\mathrm{m}$ obtained using the upstream discharge throughout. Sizing the pipe as though the full inflow continued to the downstream end would therefore give a much larger diameter than required.

The inlet velocity is $1.83\,\mathrm{m/s}$. At the downstream end it falls to zero because all the water has been withdrawn along the pipe.

The check uses the approximation $Q_e \simeq 0.55\,Q_m$, which is common in network design. It gives $9.12\,\mathrm{m}$ compared with $10.05\,\mathrm{m}$, a discrepancy of $9\%$, comparable to the uncertainty in estimating $\lambda$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A reservoir feeds the pipe shown, of length $1800\,\mathrm{m}$. It must receive $Q_m = 100\,\mathrm{L/s}$ upstream, supply a uniform withdrawal along its length and deliver $Q_v = 40\,\mathrm{L/s}$ downstream. The available head between its ends is $15.00\,\mathrm{m}$. Assuming $\lambda = 0.022$, find the minimum diameter and select a standard diameter from $200$, $250$ and $300\,\mathrm{mm}$.

::schema{nodi="M,5,6;V,27,6" condotte="M-V,0.45," acqua="0,4 5,4 5,10 0,10" solidi="0,3 5,3 5,4 0,4" pelo="0,5,10,reservoir" frecce="7.5,5.4,7.5,3.8,;10,5.4,10,3.8,;12.5,5.4,12.5,3.8,;15,5.4,15,3.8,;17.5,5.4,17.5,3.8,;20,5.4,20,3.8,;22.5,5.4,22.5,3.8,;25,5.4,25,3.8,;27.9,6,29.6,6," etichette="10,8.6,Q_m = 100 L/s;29,7.4,Q_v = 40 L/s;16,2.9,L = 1800 m - D = ?" titolo="Reservoir-fed pipe with distributed withdrawal and downstream throughflow"}

$$
Q_e^2 = \frac{0.100^2 + 0.100 \cdot 0.040 + 0.040^2}{3} = \frac{0.0156}{3} = 0.00520\,\mathrm{m^6/s^2}
$$
$$
Q_e = 0.0721\,\mathrm{m^3/s} = 72.1\,\mathrm{L/s}
$$
$$
\Delta H = \frac{8\,\lambda\,L\,Q_e^2}{g\,\pi^2 D^5} \leq 15.00
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
D \geq \left(\frac{8\,\lambda\,L\,Q_e^2}{g\,\pi^2 \cdot 15.00}\right)^{\frac{1}{5}}
$$
$$
D \geq \left(\frac{8 \cdot 0.022 \cdot 1800 \cdot 0.00520}{9.81 \cdot \pi^2 \cdot 15.00}\right)^{\frac{1}{5}} = \left(1.134 \cdot 10^{-3}\right)^{\frac{1}{5}}\,\mathrm{m} = 0.258\,\mathrm{m}
$$
$$
D = 300\,\mathrm{mm} ,
\qquad
r = \frac{8 \cdot 0.022 \cdot 1800}{9.81 \cdot \pi^2 \cdot 0.300^5} = 1346\,\mathrm{s^2/m^5}
$$
$$
\Delta H = 1346 \cdot 0.00520 = 7.00\,\mathrm{m} \leq 15.00\,\mathrm{m}
$$

Check the next smaller standard diameter.
$$
r_{250} = \frac{8 \cdot 0.022 \cdot 1800}{9.81 \cdot \pi^2 \cdot 0.250^5} = 3351\,\mathrm{s^2/m^5} ,
\qquad
\Delta H_{250} = 3351 \cdot 0.00520 = 17.4\,\mathrm{m} > 15.00\,\mathrm{m}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Because water is withdrawn along the pipe, its sizing uses the equivalent discharge, $Q_e = 72.1\,\mathrm{L/s}$.

The design requires the head loss to stay within the available head. Substituting the definition of resistance leaves only the diameter unknown. It appears to the fifth power, and setting the loss equal to the available head gives $D \geq 258\,\mathrm{mm}$.

The next larger standard diameter is $300\,\mathrm{mm}$. With that diameter, the actual head loss is $7.00\,\mathrm{m}$, less than half the head available. The velocities are $1.41\,\mathrm{m/s}$ upstream and $0.57\,\mathrm{m/s}$ downstream, so even at the outlet, where discharge is lowest, the velocity remains acceptable.

The next smaller standard diameter, $250\,\mathrm{mm}$, would give a head loss of $17.4\,\mathrm{m}$, exceeding the available head. Thus $300\,\mathrm{mm}$ is the smallest listed diameter that meets the design requirement.

</div>
