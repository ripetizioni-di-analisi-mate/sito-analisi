## Exercise 1

Prepare $250\,\mathrm{mL}$ of a mixture at $4.2\,\mathrm{g/L}$ from two solutions of the same solute at $3\,\mathrm{g/L}$ and $6\,\mathrm{g/L}$. Assuming additive volumes and conservation of solute, calculate the volumes to mix.

$$
\begin{aligned}
4.2&=3(1-q)+6q\\[6pt]
&=3+3q,\\[6pt]
3q&=4.2-3=1.2,\\[6pt]
q&=\frac{1.2}{3}=\frac{12}{30}=\frac{2}{5},
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
V_{6}&=\frac{2}{5}\cdot250\,\mathrm{mL}
=100\,\mathrm{mL},\\[6pt]
V_{3}&=250\,\mathrm{mL}-100\,\mathrm{mL}\\[6pt]
&=150\,\mathrm{mL}.
\end{aligned}
$$

::grafico{assi=0 uguale=1 xmin=2.6 xmax=6.5 ymin=-0.6 ymax=0.75 vettori="2.8,0,6.4,0,c (g/L)" punti="3,0;4.2,0;6,0" segmenti="3,0.25,4.2,0.25;4.2,0.25,6,0.25;3,0,3,0.25;4.2,0,4.2,0.25;6,0,6,0.25" etichette="3,-0.3,3;4.2,-0.3,4.2;6,-0.3,6;3.6,0.45,1.2;5.1,0.45,1.8" titolo="On the concentration scale, the target is $1.2$ above the lower concentration in an interval of length $3$, so $q=\dfrac{1.2}{3}=\dfrac{2}{5}$."}

<div class="spiegazione">

Let $q$ be the fraction of the total volume supplied by the more concentrated solution. The fraction supplied by the first solution is therefore $1-q$. Since the solute is conserved and the volumes are additive, the mixture concentration is the **weighted average** of the two concentrations, with the volume fractions as weights.

Expanding the right-hand side gives $3-3q+6q=3+3q$. The equation $4.2=3+3q$ is linear in $q$. Subtracting $3$ from both sides leaves $3q=1.2$, and dividing by $3$ gives $q=\dfrac{2}{5}$. The value lies between $0$ and $1$, so it is a valid volume fraction.

Multiply the volume fraction by the total volume. The mixture requires $100\,\mathrm{mL}$ of the solution at $6\,\mathrm{g/L}$, and the remaining $150\,\mathrm{mL}$ comes from the solution at $3\,\mathrm{g/L}$.

Check the result using solute masses. A volume of $0.15\,\mathrm{L}$ at $3\,\mathrm{g/L}$ contains $0.45\,\mathrm{g}$, and $0.1\,\mathrm{L}$ at $6\,\mathrm{g/L}$ contains $0.6\,\mathrm{g}$. In all, $1.05\,\mathrm{g}$ is present in $0.25\,\mathrm{L}$, and $\dfrac{1.05}{0.25}=4.2$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Mix $120\,\mathrm{g}$ of a solution containing $12\%$ solute by mass with $180\,\mathrm{g}$ of a solution of unknown mass percentage. The solute is the same and is conserved. The mixture contains $18\%$ solute by mass. Calculate the mass percentage in the second solution.

$$
\begin{aligned}
m_{\mathrm{tot}}&=(120+180)\,\mathrm{g}=300\,\mathrm{g},\\[6pt]
m_{\mathrm{tot,soluto}}
&=\frac{18}{100}\cdot300\,\mathrm{g}=54\,\mathrm{g},\\[6pt]
m_{\mathrm{1,soluto}}
&=\frac{12}{100}\cdot120\,\mathrm{g}=14.4\,\mathrm{g},\\[6pt]
m_{\mathrm{2,soluto}}
&=(54-14.4)\,\mathrm{g}=39.6\,\mathrm{g},
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{m_{\mathrm{2,soluto}}}{180\,\mathrm{g}}
&=\frac{39.6}{180}=\frac{396}{1800}\\[6pt]
&=\frac{22}{100}=22\%.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The mass of the mixture is the sum of the masses of the two solutions, $300\,\mathrm{g}$. The final percentage refers to this total, so the mixture contains $18\%$ of $300\,\mathrm{g}$, or $54\,\mathrm{g}$ of solute.

The first solution contributes $12\%$ of its own mass, or $14.4\,\mathrm{g}$. The solute is conserved, so the final amount is the **sum of the amounts contributed** by the two solutions. The second solution therefore contributes the difference, $54-14.4=39.6$ grams of solute.

The base for the required percentage is the mass of the second solution, $180\,\mathrm{g}$. Multiplying the numerator and denominator of $\dfrac{39.6}{180}$ by $10$ and then dividing both by $18$ gives twenty-two hundredths. The second solution therefore contains $22\%$ solute by mass.

The result agrees with the range of a weighted average. The value $18\%$ must lie between $12\%$ and the unknown percentage, and indeed $12<18<22$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Can two solutions of the same solute at $5\,\mathrm{g/L}$ and $9\,\mathrm{g/L}$ yield a concentration of $10\,\mathrm{g/L}$ by mixing alone, assuming additive volumes and conservation of solute?

$$
\begin{aligned}
c_f&=\bigl(5(1-q)+9q\bigr)\,\mathrm{g/L}\\[6pt]
&=(5+4q)\,\mathrm{g/L},
\qquad 0\le q\le 1,\\[6pt]
10&=5+4q
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad q=\frac{5}{4}>1.
\end{aligned}
$$

::grafico{xmin=0 xmax=1.45 ymin=0 ymax=11.5 nomi="q,c" curve="t,5+4*t,0,1" punti="0,5,pieno;1,9,pieno;1.25,10,vuoto" segmenti="1,0,1,9;1.25,0,1.25,10;0,10,1.45,10" etichette="0.45,8.4,c = 5 + 4q;0.3,10.5,c = 10" titolo="The attainable concentrations, $c=5+4q$ with $0\le q\le1$, range from $5$ to $9\,\mathrm{g/L}$. The value $c=10$ corresponds to $q=\dfrac{5}{4}$, outside the interval."}

<div class="spiegazione">

The fraction $q$ is the fraction of the volume supplied by the solution at $9\,\mathrm{g/L}$. It is a volume divided by the total volume that contains it, so it must lie between zero and one. By conservation of solute and additivity of volumes, the final concentration is the weighted average $5(1-q)+9q$, which simplifies to $5+4q$.

As $q$ ranges from $0$ to $1$, the concentration $5+4q$ increases from $5$ to $9\,\mathrm{g/L}$. Setting $5+4q=10$ gives $4q=5$, or $q=\dfrac{5}{4}$. This exceeds one, so it corresponds to no mixture of the two solutions. A concentration of $10\,\mathrm{g/L}$ **cannot be obtained** by mixing alone.

The **theorem on the range of a weighted average** gives the same conclusion. If both volumes are positive, the theorem applies and the final concentration lies between $5\,\mathrm{g/L}$ and $9\,\mathrm{g/L}$. If either volume is zero, the concentration is that of the other solution. In both cases $10\,\mathrm{g/L}$ lies outside the interval.

</div>
