## Exercise 1

A $320\,\mathrm{g}$ solution contains $15\%$ solute by mass. It loses $20\%$ of its initial mass through evaporation of the solvent alone. Calculate the mass percentage of solute in the remaining solution.

$$
\begin{aligned}
m_{\mathrm{soluto}}&=320\,\mathrm{g}\cdot\frac{15}{100}
=48\,\mathrm{g},\\[6pt]
m_f&=320\,\mathrm{g}\cdot\left(1-\frac{20}{100}\right)
=320\,\mathrm{g}\cdot\frac{80}{100}
=256\,\mathrm{g},\\[6pt]
\frac{m_{\mathrm{soluto}}}{m_f}
&=\frac{48}{256}=\frac{16\cdot3}{16\cdot16}=\frac{3}{16}\\[6pt]
&=\frac{18.75}{100}=18.75\%.
\end{aligned}
$$

::grafico{assi=0 uguale=1 xmin=-15 xmax=340 ymin=-25 ymax=105 poligoni="0,60 48,60 48,80 0,80;0,10 48,10 48,30 0,30" linee="0,60 320,60 320,80 0,80 0,60;48,60 48,80;0,10 256,10 256,30 0,30 0,10;48,10 48,30" segmenti="256,10,320,10;320,10,320,30;320,30,256,30" etichette="24,90,solute 48 g;184,70,before 320 g;152,20,after 256 g;288,20,−64 g;24,0,solute 48 g" titolo="The solute mass remains $48\,\mathrm{g}$ while the total mass falls from $320\,\mathrm{g}$ to $256\,\mathrm{g}$."}

<div class="spiegazione">

The initial solute mass is $15\%$ of the total, or $48\,\mathrm{g}$. Since only solvent evaporates, the **solute mass is conserved** and remains $48\,\mathrm{g}$ in the remaining solution.

The loss is $20\%$ of the initial mass, so the solution retains $80\%$ of $320\,\mathrm{g}$. The multiplicative factor $\dfrac{80}{100}$ gives a final mass of $256\,\mathrm{g}$. The mass lost to evaporation, in grams, is $64$.

The base for the new percentage is the final mass. Dividing the numerator and denominator of $\dfrac{48}{256}$ by $16$ gives $\dfrac{3}{16}$. Since $100:16=6.25$, we have $\dfrac{3}{16}=\dfrac{3\cdot6.25}{100}=\dfrac{18.75}{100}$. The remaining solution therefore contains $18.75\%$ solute by mass.

The ratio of solute mass to total mass increases because the numerator stays the same and the denominator decreases. Specifically, the percentage is multiplied by the reciprocal of the factor by which the mass changes, namely $15\%\cdot\dfrac{100}{80}=18.75\%$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A quarter of the volume is withdrawn from $240\,\mathrm{mL}$ of a homogeneous solution at $5\,\mathrm{g/L}$. Then $60\,\mathrm{mL}$ of a solution of the same solute at $9\,\mathrm{g/L}$ is added to the remaining volume. Assuming additive volumes and conservation of solute in the mixture, calculate the final concentration.

$$
\begin{aligned}
V_{\mathrm{rim}}&=\left(1-\frac{1}{4}\right)\cdot240\,\mathrm{mL}
=\frac{3}{4}\cdot240\,\mathrm{mL}
=180\,\mathrm{mL},\\[6pt]
V_f&=(180+60)\,\mathrm{mL}=240\,\mathrm{mL},\\[6pt]
c_f&=\left(\frac{180}{240}\cdot5+\frac{60}{240}\cdot9\right)\,\mathrm{g/L}\\[6pt]
&=\left(\frac{3}{4}\cdot5+\frac{1}{4}\cdot9\right)\,\mathrm{g/L}\\[6pt]
&=\frac{15+9}{4}\,\mathrm{g/L}
=6\,\mathrm{g/L}.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Withdrawing one-quarter of the volume leaves three-quarters of $240\,\mathrm{mL}$, or $180\,\mathrm{mL}$. Since the solution is homogeneous, the remaining portion has the **same concentration as the original solution**, $5\,\mathrm{g/L}$, because withdrawal changes the solute mass and volume in the same proportion.

Adding $60\,\mathrm{mL}$ brings the volume back to $240\,\mathrm{mL}$ because the volumes are additive. The remaining solution makes up $\dfrac{180}{240}=\dfrac{3}{4}$ of the final volume and the new sample $\dfrac{1}{4}$. Since the solute is conserved, the final concentration is the **weighted average** of the two concentrations, with those fractions as weights.

This gives $\dfrac{3}{4}\cdot5+\dfrac{1}{4}\cdot9=\dfrac{15}{4}+\dfrac{9}{4}=\dfrac{24}{4}=6$. The final concentration is therefore $6\,\mathrm{g/L}$, higher than $5\,\mathrm{g/L}$ because the added sample is more concentrated.

The solute masses give the same result. The remaining $0.18\,\mathrm{L}$ contains $0.9\,\mathrm{g}$ of solute, and the added $0.06\,\mathrm{L}$ contains $0.54\,\mathrm{g}$. In all, $1.44\,\mathrm{g}$ is present in $0.24\,\mathrm{L}$, and $\dfrac{1.44}{0.24}=6$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Mix $180\,\mathrm{g}$ of a solution containing $10\%$ solute by mass with $120\,\mathrm{g}$ of a solution of the same solute at $25\%$ by mass. Remove one-sixth of the mass of the homogeneous mixture and add $50\,\mathrm{g}$ of pure solvent. Calculate the final mass percentage, assuming the solute is conserved during mixing.

$$
\begin{aligned}
m_{\mathrm{soluto,1}}
&=\left(180\cdot\frac{10}{100}+120\cdot\frac{25}{100}\right)\,\mathrm{g}\\[6pt]
&=(18+30)\,\mathrm{g}=48\,\mathrm{g},\\[6pt]
m_1&=(180+120)\,\mathrm{g}=300\,\mathrm{g},
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
m_{\mathrm{soluto,f}}
&=\frac{5}{6}\cdot48\,\mathrm{g}=40\,\mathrm{g},\\[6pt]
m_f&=\left(\frac{5}{6}\cdot300+50\right)\,\mathrm{g}
=(250+50)\,\mathrm{g}=300\,\mathrm{g},\\[6pt]
\frac{m_{\mathrm{soluto,f}}}{m_f}
&=\frac{40}{300}=\frac{2}{15}=\frac{\frac{40}{3}}{100}\\[6pt]
&=\frac{40}{3}\%.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Find the initial solute masses using the two respective bases, $18\,\mathrm{g}$ from the first solution and $30\,\mathrm{g}$ from the second, and add them because the solute is conserved. The mixture has a mass of $300\,\mathrm{g}$ and contains $48\,\mathrm{g}$ of solute, so it contains $16\%$ solute by mass.

Removing a sample from a homogeneous mixture takes away the **same fraction** of its total mass and its solute mass. Removing one-sixth leaves five-sixths, giving $\dfrac{5}{6}\cdot48=40$ grams of solute in $\dfrac{5}{6}\cdot300=250$ grams of solution.

Adding $50\,\mathrm{g}$ of pure solvent changes only the total mass, bringing it back to $300\,\mathrm{g}$ while the solute mass remains $40\,\mathrm{g}$.

The numerator of the final mass fraction is the solute mass remaining after withdrawal, and the denominator is the total mass after addition. The ratio $\dfrac{40}{300}$ reduces to $\dfrac{2}{15}$. To write it in hundredths, multiply by $100$ to obtain $\dfrac{200}{15}=\dfrac{40}{3}$. The final percentage is therefore $\dfrac{40}{3}\%$, or about $13.3\%$.

</div>
