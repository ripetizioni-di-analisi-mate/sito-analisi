## Exercise 1

Two samples of masses $80\,\mathrm{g}$ and $120\,\mathrm{g}$ are combined. The same component makes up $10\%$ and $25\%$ of their masses, respectively. The component is conserved. Calculate its mass percentage in the mixture.

$$
\begin{aligned}
m_{\mathrm{comp}}
&=80\,\mathrm{g}\cdot\frac{10}{100}
 +120\,\mathrm{g}\cdot\frac{25}{100}\\[6pt]
&=(8+30)\,\mathrm{g}=38\,\mathrm{g},\\[6pt]
m_{\mathrm{tot}}&=(80+120)\,\mathrm{g}=200\,\mathrm{g},\\[6pt]
\frac{m_{\mathrm{comp}}}{m_{\mathrm{tot}}}
&=\frac{38}{200}=\frac{19}{100}\\[6pt]
&=19\%.
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\frac{80}{200}\cdot10\%+\frac{120}{200}\cdot25\%
=\frac{2}{5}\cdot10\%+\frac{3}{5}\cdot25\%
=(4+15)\%=19\%.
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Each percentage uses the mass of its own sample as its base. Taking $10\%$ of $80\,\mathrm{g}$ gives $8\,\mathrm{g}$, and taking $25\%$ of $120\,\mathrm{g}$ gives $30\,\mathrm{g}$. The component is conserved, so the mixture contains $8+30=38$ grams of it.

The total mass of the mixture is the sum of the two sample masses, $200\,\mathrm{g}$. The mass percentage in the mixture is the mass of the component divided by this total, expressed as a percentage. Dividing the numerator and denominator of $\dfrac{38}{200}$ by $2$ gives nineteen hundredths, or $19\%$.

The last line gives the same result as a **weighted average** of the two percentages, with the sample masses as weights. The first sample makes up $\dfrac{2}{5}$ of the mixture and the second $\dfrac{3}{5}$. The arithmetic mean would be $17.5\%$, less than $19\%$, because it gives the two samples equal weight despite their different masses. The result lies between $10\%$ and $25\%$ and is closer to $25\%$, the percentage in the heavier sample.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Mix $250\,\mathrm{mL}$ of a solution at $1.6\,\mathrm{g/L}$ with $750\,\mathrm{mL}$ of a solution of the same solute at $4.8\,\mathrm{g/L}$. The volumes are additive and the solute is conserved. Calculate the final concentration.

$$
\begin{aligned}
V_f&=(250+750)\,\mathrm{mL}=1000\,\mathrm{mL}=1\,\mathrm{L},\\[6pt]
c_f
&=\frac{250}{1000}\cdot1.6\,\mathrm{g/L}
 +\frac{750}{1000}\cdot4.8\,\mathrm{g/L}\\[6pt]
&=\left(\frac{1}{4}\cdot1.6+\frac{3}{4}\cdot4.8\right)\,\mathrm{g/L}\\[6pt]
&=(0.4+3.6)\,\mathrm{g/L}\\[6pt]
&=4\,\mathrm{g/L}.
\end{aligned}
$$

::grafico{assi=0 uguale=1 xmin=-0.3 xmax=4.4 ymin=-0.7 ymax=1.4 poligoni="1,0 4,0 4,0.6 1,0.6" linee="0,0 4,0 4,0.6 0,0.6 0,0;1,0 1,0.6" etichette="0.5,0.3,1.6 g/L;2.5,0.3,4.8 g/L;0.5,0.9,250 mL;2.5,0.9,750 mL;2,-0.35,final volume 1000 mL" titolo="The widths represent the volumes. The second solution makes up $\dfrac{3}{4}$ of the final volume and has three times the weight of the first."}

<div class="spiegazione">

The volumes are additive, so the final volume is $250+750=1000$ milliliters, or one liter. The solute is conserved, so the final concentration is the **weighted average** of the two concentrations, with the volumes as weights.

To find the volume fractions, divide each volume by the total. The first volume is $\dfrac{250}{1000}=\dfrac{1}{4}$ of the total and the second $\dfrac{750}{1000}=\dfrac{3}{4}$. Multiplying each concentration by its volume fraction gives the respective contributions, $0.4\,\mathrm{g/L}$ and $3.6\,\mathrm{g/L}$.

We can repeat the calculation using solute masses. The first sample contains $0.25\,\mathrm{L}\cdot1.6\,\mathrm{g/L}=0.4\,\mathrm{g}$ and the second contains $0.75\,\mathrm{L}\cdot4.8\,\mathrm{g/L}=3.6\,\mathrm{g}$. In all, $4\,\mathrm{g}$ of solute is present in $1\,\mathrm{L}$, so the final concentration is $4\,\mathrm{g/L}$.

The result lies between the two initial concentrations and is closer to that of the larger sample.

</div>

## Exercise 3

In a piecewise model, a chamber remains at $18\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ for $10\,\mathrm{min}$, at $22\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ for $15\,\mathrm{min}$, and at $30\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ for $5\,\mathrm{min}$. Calculate the mean temperature over the entire time, using the durations as weights.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\overline{T}
&=\frac{10\cdot18+15\cdot22+5\cdot30}{10+15+5}\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\\[6pt]
&=\frac{180+330+150}{30}\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\\[6pt]
&=\frac{660}{30}\,^\circ\mathrm{C}
=22\,^\circ\mathrm{C}.
\end{aligned}
$$

::grafico{xmin=0 xmax=33 ymin=0 ymax=34 nomi="t,T" area="18;0;0;10|22;0;10;25|30;0;25;30" f="22" segmenti="0,18,10,18;10,22,25,22;25,30,30,30;10,18,10,22;25,22,25,30;30,0,30,30" legenda="\overline{T}=22" titolo="Chamber temperature as a function of time ($t$ in minutes, $T$ in degrees Celsius). The weighted average is the height of the rectangle with the same area as the region under the graph."}

<div class="spiegazione">

Each temperature is maintained for a different length of time. Its contribution to the average is therefore proportional to its duration, and the **weighted average** uses the three durations as weights.

The numerator is the sum of the three products of duration and temperature, $180$, $330$, and $150$, totaling $660$. The denominator is the total duration, $10+15+5=30$ minutes. Since all times are in minutes, the time units cancel and the average is in degrees Celsius. The quotient $\dfrac{660}{30}$ equals $22$.

On the graph, each product of duration and temperature is the area of a rectangle. A rectangle of base $30\,\mathrm{min}$ and height $22\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ has the same total area, $660$, as the three rectangles together. The average lies between the minimum temperature $18\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ and the maximum $30\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$. It differs from the arithmetic mean $\dfrac{18+22+30}{3}\,^\circ\mathrm{C}=\dfrac{70}{3}\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ because the temperature of $30\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ lasts only $5$ minutes.

</div>
