## Exercise 1

A rectangle has area $270\,\mathrm{cm^2}$. A second rectangle has a base $\dfrac{4}{9}$ as long as the first rectangle’s base and a height $\dfrac{3}{5}$ as great as its height. Calculate the area of the second rectangle.

$$
\begin{aligned}
A_1&=b\,h=270\,\mathrm{cm^2},\\[6pt]
A_2&=\left(\frac{4}{9}\,b\right)\cdot\left(\frac{3}{5}\,h\right)
=\frac{4}{9}\cdot\frac{3}{5}\cdot b\,h,
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{4}{9}\cdot\frac{3}{5}&=\frac{4\cdot3}{9\cdot5}=\frac{12}{45}=\frac{4}{15},\\[6pt]
A_2&=270\,\mathrm{cm^2}\cdot\frac{4}{15}\\[6pt]
&=18\cdot4\,\mathrm{cm^2}
=72\,\mathrm{cm^2}.
\end{aligned}
$$

::grafico{assi=0 uguale=1 xmin=-0.5 xmax=12 ymin=-0.9 ymax=5.8 poligoni="0,0 4,0 4,3 0,3" linee="0,0 9,0 9,5 0,5 0,0;1,0 1,5;2,0 2,5;3,0 3,5;4,0 4,5;5,0 5,5;6,0 6,5;7,0 7,5;8,0 8,5;0,1 9,1;0,2 9,2;0,3 9,3;0,4 9,4" etichette="2,-0.5,4 of 9 columns;10.5,1.5,3 of 5 rows;2,1.5,12 rectangles" titolo="The second rectangle covers $12$ of the $45$ equal small rectangles that make up the first, or $\dfrac{4}{15}$ of its area."}

<div class="spiegazione">

The area of the first rectangle is its base times its height and equals $270\,\mathrm{cm^2}$. The base and height are not given separately. The base of the second rectangle is $\dfrac{4}{9}\,b$ and its height is $\dfrac{3}{5}\,h$, so its area is the product $\dfrac{4}{9}\cdot\dfrac{3}{5}\cdot b\,h$, which contains the known product $b\,h$.

In a **product of fractions**, the numerator is the product of the numerators and the denominator is the product of the denominators. This gives $\dfrac{12}{45}$, which reduces to $\dfrac{4}{15}$ when its numerator and denominator are divided by $3$. In the figure, the base is divided into $9$ parts and the height into $5$. Thus the first rectangle consists of $45$ equal small rectangles, and the second contains $4\cdot3=12$ of them.

Finally, $270$ divided by $15$ gives $18$, and $18\cdot4=72$. The second rectangle therefore has an area of $72\,\mathrm{cm^2}$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A vessel holds $\dfrac{11}{6}\,\mathrm{L}$. Each container to be filled has a capacity of $\dfrac{2}{5}\,\mathrm{L}$. Find the number of full containers and the volume left over.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\frac{11}{6}\,\mathrm{L}}{\frac{2}{5}\,\mathrm{L}}
&=\frac{11}{6}\cdot\frac{5}{2}
=\frac{55}{12},\\[6pt]
4=\frac{48}{12}&<\frac{55}{12}<\frac{60}{12}=5,
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
V_{\mathrm{usato}}&=4\cdot\frac{2}{5}\,\mathrm{L}=\frac{8}{5}\,\mathrm{L},\\[6pt]
V_{\mathrm{avanzo}}
&=\left(\frac{11}{6}-\frac{8}{5}\right)\,\mathrm{L}\\[6pt]
&=\left(\frac{55}{30}-\frac{48}{30}\right)\,\mathrm{L}
=\frac{7}{30}\,\mathrm{L}.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The quotient tells us how many portions, each measuring two-fifths of a liter, the available volume can supply. Both quantities are in liters, so the units cancel and the quotient is dimensionless. To divide by $\dfrac{2}{5}$, multiply by its **reciprocal** $\dfrac{5}{2}$, giving $\dfrac{55}{12}$.

To find how many containers can be filled completely, compare $\dfrac{55}{12}$ with the adjacent integers written in twelfths. Since $48<55<60$, the quotient lies between $4$ and $5$. Therefore **four containers** can be filled completely, but the fifth is only partially filled.

The four containers require $4\cdot\dfrac{2}{5}=\dfrac{8}{5}$ of a liter. The volume left over is the initial volume minus this amount. The least common multiple of $6$ and $5$ is $30$, so $\dfrac{11}{6}=\dfrac{55}{30}$ and $\dfrac{8}{5}=\dfrac{48}{30}$. The difference is $\dfrac{7}{30}\,\mathrm{L}$, or about $233\,\mathrm{mL}$. This is less than the capacity of one container, as expected since the fifth container is only partially filled.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Calculate $\left(\dfrac{5}{6}-\dfrac{1}{4}\right):\left(\dfrac{7}{9}\cdot\dfrac{3}{14}\right)$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{5}{6}-\frac{1}{4}
&=\frac{5\cdot2}{12}-\frac{1\cdot3}{12}\\[6pt]
&=\frac{10-3}{12}=\frac{7}{12},
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{7}{9}\cdot\frac{3}{14}
&=\frac{7\cdot3}{3\cdot3\cdot2\cdot7}
=\frac{1}{3\cdot2}=\frac{1}{6},\\[6pt]
\frac{7}{12}:\frac{1}{6}
&=\frac{7}{12}\cdot\frac{6}{1}
=\frac{7\cdot6}{2\cdot6}=\frac{7}{2}.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The expression is a difference divided by a product. First evaluate the dividend $\dfrac{5}{6}-\dfrac{1}{4}$ and the divisor $\dfrac{7}{9}\cdot\dfrac{3}{14}$ separately, then divide.

For the difference, use the common denominator $12$, the least common multiple of $6$ and $4$. The two fractions become $\dfrac{10}{12}$ and $\dfrac{3}{12}$, and their difference is $\dfrac{7}{12}$.

In the product, factor $9=3\cdot3$ and $14=2\cdot7$. The factor $7$ appears in both the numerator and denominator, as does a factor of $3$. Canceling them leaves $\dfrac{1}{6}$. The divisor is nonzero, so the division is defined.

Dividing by $\dfrac{1}{6}$ is equivalent to multiplying by its reciprocal $6$. Since $12=2\cdot6$, the factor $6$ cancels, and the expression equals $\dfrac{7}{2}$. The quotient is greater than $1$, as expected because the dividend $\dfrac{7}{12}$ is greater than the divisor $\dfrac{2}{12}=\dfrac{1}{6}$. Indeed, when both fractions have denominator $12$, their quotient is the ratio of their numerators, $\dfrac{7}{2}$.

</div>
