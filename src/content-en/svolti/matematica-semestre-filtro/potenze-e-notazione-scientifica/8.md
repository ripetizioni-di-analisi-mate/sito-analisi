## Exercise 1

An object starts from rest and travels $5.0\cdot10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m}$ in $2.0\cdot10^{-2}\,\mathrm{s}$ with constant acceleration. Use $s=\dfrac{1}{2}at^2$ to calculate $a$ to two significant figures.

$$
s=\frac{1}{2}at^2
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
a=\frac{2s}{t^2}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
a&=\frac{2\cdot5.0\cdot10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m}}
{(2.0\cdot10^{-2}\,\mathrm{s})^2}\\[6pt]
&=\frac{10\cdot10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m}}
{(2.0)^2\cdot10^{-4}\,\mathrm{s^2}}\\[6pt]
&=\frac{1.0\cdot10^{-2}\,\mathrm{m}}
{4.0\cdot10^{-4}\,\mathrm{s^2}}\\[6pt]
&=0.25\cdot10^{-2-(-4)}\,\mathrm{m/s^2}\\[6pt]
&=0.25\cdot10^{2}\,\mathrm{m/s^2}\\[6pt]
&=2.5\cdot10^1\,\mathrm{m/s^2}.
\end{aligned}
$$

::grafico{f="1.25*x^2" xmin=0 xmax=2.6 ymin=0 ymax=8.5 punti="2,5,pieno" segmenti="2,0,2,5;0,5,2,5" nomi="t,s" legenda="s=\dfrac{1}{2}at^2" titolo="Time $t$ is in hundredths of a second and distance $s$ is in millimeters. With $a=25\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$, the distance traveled is $5.0\,\mathrm{mm}$ at time $t=2.0\cdot10^{-2}\,\mathrm{s}$."}

<div class="spiegazione">

The relation holds because the object starts from rest and its acceleration is constant. To solve for $a$, multiply both sides by $2$ and divide by $t^2$, which is positive.

Squaring the time gives the coefficient $4.0$ and the exponent of ten $-4$. Indeed, $(2.0)^2=4.0$ and, by the rule for a power of a power, $\left(10^{-2}\right)^2=10^{-4}$. The unit is also squared and becomes $\mathrm{s^2}$. In the numerator, the product $2\cdot5.0=10$ gives $10\cdot10^{-3}=1.0\cdot10^{-2}$.

The quotient of the coefficients is $\dfrac{1.0}{4.0}=0.25$ and the exponent is $-2-(-4)=2$. The coefficient $0.25$ is less than one, so multiply it by ten and decrease the exponent by one, from $2$ to $1$.

The final quotient has units of acceleration, meters per second squared. Both measurements have two significant figures, so report the result to two significant figures. The acceleration is **$2.5\cdot10^1\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$**.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Object $1$ has mass $4.0\cdot10^{-4}\,\mathrm{kg}$ and kinetic energy $2.0\cdot10^{-5}\,\mathrm{J}$. Object $2$ has mass $1.6\cdot10^{-3}\,\mathrm{kg}$ and kinetic energy $3.2\cdot10^{-4}\,\mathrm{J}$. Calculate the ratio of their speeds $\dfrac{v_2}{v_1}$ and report it to two significant figures.

$$
E_c=\frac{1}{2}mv^2
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
v^2=\frac{2E_c}{m}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\left(\frac{v_2}{v_1}\right)^2=\frac{E_{c,2}}{E_{c,1}}\cdot\frac{m_1}{m_2}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{E_{c,2}}{E_{c,1}}&=\frac{3.2\cdot10^{-4}}{2.0\cdot10^{-5}}=\frac{3.2}{2.0}\cdot10^1=16,\\[6pt]
\frac{m_2}{m_1}&=\frac{1.6\cdot10^{-3}}{4.0\cdot10^{-4}}=\frac{1.6}{4.0}\cdot10^1=4,\\[6pt]
\left(\frac{v_2}{v_1}\right)^2&=\frac{16}{4}=4,\\[6pt]
\frac{v_2}{v_1}&=\sqrt{4}=2.0.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Kinetic energy is proportional to mass times speed squared. From $E_c=\dfrac{1}{2}mv^2$ we obtain $v^2=\dfrac{2E_c}{m}$ for each object. The factor $2$ cancels in the ratio of the two expressions, so $\left(\dfrac{v_2}{v_1}\right)^2$ is the ratio of the energies multiplied by $\dfrac{m_1}{m_2}$.

Thus, dividing the ratio of the energies by the ratio of the masses gives the square of the speed ratio. In the energy ratio, the exponent is $-4-(-5)=1$ and the coefficient is $1.6$, giving $16$. In the mass ratio, the exponent is $-3-(-4)=1$ and the coefficient is $0.4$, giving $4$. The units cancel, so both ratios are dimensionless.

The square of the speed ratio is $\dfrac{16}{4}=4$. Since both speeds are positive, take the positive square root.

All the data have two significant figures, so write the result as $2.0$. Object $2$ moves at **twice** the speed of object $1$, that is, $\dfrac{v_2}{v_1}=2.0$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A sample contains $2.4\cdot10^{17}$ molecules and has molar mass $1.8\cdot10^1\,\mathrm{g/mol}$. Use $6.0\cdot10^{23}\,\mathrm{mol^{-1}}$ as an approximation to the Avogadro constant. Calculate the mass of the sample to two significant figures.

$$
\begin{aligned}
n&=\frac{N}{N_A}\\[6pt]
&=\frac{2.4\cdot10^{17}}
{6.0\cdot10^{23}\,\mathrm{mol^{-1}}}\\[6pt]
&=\frac{2.4}{6.0}\cdot10^{17-23}\,\mathrm{mol}\\[6pt]
&=0.40\cdot10^{-6}\,\mathrm{mol}\\[6pt]
&=4.0\cdot10^{-7}\,\mathrm{mol},
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
m&=nM\\[6pt]
&=(4.0\cdot10^{-7}\,\mathrm{mol})(1.8\cdot10^1\,\mathrm{g/mol})\\[6pt]
&=(4.0\cdot1.8)\cdot10^{-7+1}\,\mathrm{g}\\[6pt]
&=7.2\cdot10^{-6}\,\mathrm{g}.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

To convert the number of molecules to the amount of substance, divide by the number of molecules per mole. Then multiply by the mass of one mole. The first result is in moles and the second in grams, so the two conversions can be checked separately.

In the first division, the quotient of the coefficients is $\dfrac{2.4}{6.0}=0.40$ and the exponent is $17-23=-6$. The coefficient $0.40$ is less than one, so it becomes $4.0$ and the exponent decreases from $-6$ to $-7$. Dividing by $\mathrm{mol^{-1}}$ is equivalent to multiplying by $\mathrm{mol}$, so $n$ is expressed in moles.

In the product $nM$ the coefficients give $4.0\cdot1.8=7.2$, already between $1$ and $10$, and the exponents give $-7+1=-6$. The moles cancel, leaving grams.

All the data have two significant figures, so the result has two. The mass of the sample is **$7.2\cdot10^{-6}\,\mathrm{g}$**, or a few millionths of a gram.

</div>
