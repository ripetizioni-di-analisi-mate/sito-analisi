## Exercise 1

Calculate $\log_5\left(\dfrac{125}{\sqrt5}\right)$.

$$
125=5^3,\qquad \sqrt5=5^{\frac12}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\log_5\left(\frac{125}{\sqrt5}\right)
&=\log_5 125-\log_5\sqrt5\\[6pt]
&=\log_5(5^3)-\log_5\left(5^{\frac12}\right)\\[6pt]
&=3-\frac12\\[6pt]
&=\frac52.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The numerator and denominator are positive, and the base $5$ is positive and different from $1$. We can therefore apply the **quotient rule for logarithms**, which expresses the logarithm of the ratio as the difference $\log_5 125-\log_5\sqrt5$.

Since both quantities are powers of five, their logarithms equal their respective exponents. Indeed, $125=5\cdot5\cdot5=5^3$, while the square root can be written as a power with exponent one-half, $\sqrt5=5^{\frac12}$. By the definition of a logarithm, $\log_5\left(5^r\right)=r$ for every real $r$, because $r$ is precisely the exponent to which $5$ must be raised to obtain $5^r$. Therefore the first term is $3$ and the second is $\dfrac12$.

The difference $3-\dfrac12=\dfrac62-\dfrac12$ equals $\dfrac52$. We obtain the same result by writing the argument as a single power, since $\dfrac{5^3}{5^{\frac12}}=5^{3-\frac12}=5^{\frac52}$. The logarithm **therefore equals** $\dfrac52$. The result lies between $2$ and $3$, consistent with the argument $\dfrac{125}{\sqrt5}=25\sqrt5\approx55.9$ lying between $5^2=25$ and $5^3=125$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Calculate $\log_9 27$ using a change of base.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\log_9 27&=\frac{\ln 27}{\ln 9}\\[6pt]
&=\frac{\ln\left(3^3\right)}{\ln\left(3^2\right)}\\[6pt]
&=\frac{3\ln 3}{2\ln 3}\\[6pt]
&=\frac32.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The number $27$ is not an integer power of $9$, so the value cannot be read directly from the definition. The **change-of-base** formula $\log_a x=\dfrac{\log_b x}{\log_b a}$ holds for positive $a$ and $b$ different from $1$ and for $x>0$. The conditions hold for $a=9$, $b=e$, and $x=27$.

Use the natural logarithm of both the argument and the original base. Because twenty-seven is the cube of three and nine is its square, apply the power rule $\ln\left(x^r\right)=r\ln x$. This gives $3\ln3$ in the numerator and $2\ln3$ in the denominator.

The natural logarithm of three is a nonzero factor because $3\ne1$, so it cancels, leaving $\dfrac32$. The result also follows from the definition, since $9^{\frac32}=\left(\sqrt9\right)^3=3^3=27$. The logarithm **therefore equals** $\dfrac32$. The same calculation can be performed with base-ten logarithms instead of natural logarithms, because the new base is arbitrary as long as it is the same in the numerator and denominator.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Determine the real domain of $\log_3(x+2)-\log_3(5-2x)$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\begin{cases}
x+2>0,\\
5-2x>0
\end{cases}\\[6pt]
\Longleftrightarrow\;&\begin{cases}
x>-2,\\
-2x>-5
\end{cases}\\[6pt]
\Longleftrightarrow\;&\begin{cases}
x>-2,\\
x<\dfrac52
\end{cases}\\[6pt]
D&=\left(-2,\frac52\right).
\end{aligned}
$$

::grafico{f="log(x+2)/log(3)-log(5-2*x)/log(3)" xmin=-3 xmax=3.5 ymin=-4 ymax=4 asintoti="x=-2;x=2.5" legenda="y=\log_3(x+2)-\log_3(5-2x)" titolo="The function is defined only between the vertical asymptotes $x=-2$ and $x=\dfrac52$."}

<div class="spiegazione">

The argument of each logarithm must be positive. Both conditions must hold because the difference is defined only where both terms are defined. Therefore we write them as a system of inequalities.

The first inequality, $x+2>0$, imposes a lower bound, since subtracting $2$ from both sides gives $x>-2$. The second imposes an upper bound. Subtracting $5$ gives $-2x>-5$. Next divide by $-2$, which is negative, so the inequality reverses and we obtain $x<\dfrac52$.

The solutions of the system are the numbers greater than $-2$ and less than $\dfrac52$. Both endpoints are excluded because they make one of the arguments zero, and the logarithm of zero is undefined. The domain **is the open interval** $\left(-2,\dfrac52\right)$.

On the graph the lines $x=-2$ and $x=\dfrac52$ are vertical asymptotes. As the input approaches $-2$, the first logarithm tends to $-\infty$. As it approaches $\dfrac52$, the second logarithm tends to $-\infty$ and the difference tends to $+\infty$.

</div>
