## Exercise 1

An initial mass of $96\,\mathrm{mg}$ decays exponentially with a half-life of $4\,\mathrm h$. Calculate the mass remaining after $12\,\mathrm h$.

$$
N(t)=N_0\left(\frac12\right)^{\frac{t}{T_{\frac12}}}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
n&=\frac{12\,\mathrm h}{4\,\mathrm h}=3,\\[6pt]
N(12\,\mathrm h)&=96\left(\frac12\right)^3\,\mathrm{mg}\\[6pt]
&=\frac{96}{8}\,\mathrm{mg}\\[6pt]
&=12\,\mathrm{mg}.
\end{aligned}
$$

::grafico{f="96*(1/2)^(x/4)" xmin=-0.5 xmax=14 ymin=0 ymax=110 nomi="t,N (mg)" punti="0,96;4,48;8,24;12,12" segmenti="4,0,4,48;8,0,8,24;12,0,12,12" legenda="N(t)=96\left(\dfrac12\right)^{\frac{t}{4\,\mathrm h}}\,\mathrm{mg}" titolo="Time $t$ is in hours. The mass halves every four hours, decreasing from $96$ to $48$, then $24$, and then $12$ mg."}

<div class="spiegazione">

In exponential decay with half-life $T_{\frac12}$, the fraction remaining after time $t$ is $\left(\dfrac12\right)^{\frac{t}{T_{\frac12}}}$, where the exponent counts the elapsed half-lives. The elapsed time spans three half-lives, since $\dfrac{12\,\mathrm h}{4\,\mathrm h}=3$ and the units of hours cancel.

In each interval the mass is multiplied by one-half. Therefore $48\,\mathrm{mg}$ remains after $4\,\mathrm h$, $24\,\mathrm{mg}$ after $8\,\mathrm h$, and $12\,\mathrm{mg}$ after $12\,\mathrm h$. We obtain the same result in a single step because $\left(\dfrac12\right)^3=\dfrac18$. The remaining mass is thus one-eighth of the initial mass, or $\dfrac{96}{8}\,\mathrm{mg}$.

After twelve hours, $12\,\mathrm{mg}$ **remains**. The mass lost is $84\,\mathrm{mg}$, consisting of $48$, $24$, and $12\,\mathrm{mg}$ over the three intervals, because half the mass present at the beginning of each interval is lost during it. After $16\,\mathrm h$, or four half-lives, $6\,\mathrm{mg}$ would remain.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A quantity decays exponentially from $108$ to $12\,\mathrm{mg}$. Its half-life is $5\,\mathrm h$. Find the elapsed time.

$$
\begin{aligned}
108\left(\frac12\right)^{\frac{t}{5\,\mathrm h}}\,\mathrm{mg}&=12\,\mathrm{mg},\\[6pt]
\left(\frac12\right)^{\frac{t}{5\,\mathrm h}}
&=\frac{12}{108}=\frac19,\\[6pt]
\frac{t}{5\,\mathrm h}\ln\left(\frac12\right)&=\ln\left(\frac19\right),\\[6pt]
-\frac{t}{5\,\mathrm h}\ln 2&=-\ln9,\\[6pt]
t&=\frac{5\ln9}{\ln2}\,\mathrm h\\[6pt]
&\approx15.85\,\mathrm h.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Write the decay law with initial quantity $108\,\mathrm{mg}$ and half-life $5\,\mathrm h$, and require the quantity to equal $12\,\mathrm{mg}$. Dividing by $108\,\mathrm{mg}$ gives the ratio of final to initial mass, a positive, dimensionless number equal to $\dfrac19$.

Since $9$ is not an integer power of $2$, we cannot find the exponent by directly comparing powers. Take the natural logarithm of both sides, which are positive, and apply the power rule $\ln\left(x^r\right)=r\ln x$. Also, $\ln\left(\dfrac12\right)=-\ln2$ and, by the quotient rule, $\ln\left(\dfrac19\right)=-\ln9$, since $\ln1=0$. Changing the sign of both sides and multiplying by $\dfrac{5\,\mathrm h}{\ln2}$ isolates the time. With $\ln9\approx2.197$ and $\ln2\approx0.693$, the result is approximately $15.85\,\mathrm h$.

The elapsed time **is approximately** $15.85\,\mathrm h$. Since one-ninth is less than one-eighth, more than three half-lives must elapse, or more than $15\,\mathrm h$, consistent with the calculated value.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A mass follows $N(t)=N_0e^{-kt}$ with $k>0$. The measurements are $N(2\,\mathrm h)=120\,\mathrm{mg}$ and $N(8\,\mathrm h)=15\,\mathrm{mg}$. Determine $k$ and $N_0$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{N(8\,\mathrm h)}{N(2\,\mathrm h)}&=\frac{N_0e^{-k\cdot8\,\mathrm h}}{N_0e^{-k\cdot2\,\mathrm h}}=e^{-k\cdot6\,\mathrm h},\\[6pt]
e^{-k\cdot6\,\mathrm h}&=\frac{15}{120}=\frac18,\\[6pt]
k\cdot6\,\mathrm h&=\ln8=3\ln2,\\[6pt]
k&=\frac{3\ln2}{6\,\mathrm h}=\frac{\ln2}{2\,\mathrm h},\\[6pt]
N_0&=N(2\,\mathrm h)e^{k\cdot2\,\mathrm h}\\[6pt]
&=120\,e^{\ln2}\,\mathrm{mg}\\[6pt]
&=240\,\mathrm{mg}.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Taking the ratio of the measurements eliminates the initial mass and leaves the difference between the measurement times in the exponent. Dividing exponentials with the same base subtracts their exponents, so we are left with $e^{-k\cdot6\,\mathrm h}$, where $6\,\mathrm h=8\,\mathrm h-2\,\mathrm h$.

The ratio of the measurements is $\dfrac18$. Taking the natural logarithm gives $-k\cdot6\,\mathrm h=\ln\left(\dfrac18\right)=-\ln8$, with $\ln8=3\ln2$ because $8=2^3$. Dividing by six hours gives $k=\dfrac{\ln2}{2\,\mathrm h}\approx0.347\,\mathrm h^{-1}$. A decrease to one-eighth in six hours corresponds to three half-lives, so the half-life is $2\,\mathrm h$, in agreement with $T_{\frac12}=\dfrac{\ln2}{k}$.

The first measurement gives the initial mass, because $N(2\,\mathrm h)=N_0e^{-k\cdot2\,\mathrm h}$ yields $N_0=N(2\,\mathrm h)e^{k\cdot2\,\mathrm h}$. The exponent is $\ln2$ and $e^{\ln2}=2$. The initial mass was twice the measured mass because the measurement was taken after two hours, or one half-life. **The constant equals** $\dfrac{\ln2}{2\,\mathrm h}$ and **the initial mass** is $240\,\mathrm{mg}$.

</div>
