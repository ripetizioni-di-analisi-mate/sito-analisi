## Exercise 1

Three measurements of a quantity at equally spaced times are $125$, $100$, and $80$. Determine whether the data are consistent with a constant-factor model and use that model to calculate the next measurement.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{Q_1}{Q_0}&=\frac{100}{125}=\frac45,\\[6pt]
\frac{Q_2}{Q_1}&=\frac{80}{100}=\frac45,\\[6pt]
Q_3&=Q_2\cdot\frac45=80\cdot\frac45=64.
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
Q_1-Q_0=-25,\qquad Q_2-Q_1=-20
$$

::grafico{f="125*(4/5)^x" xmin=-0.4 xmax=3.6 ymin=0 ymax=140 nomi="n,Q" punti="0,125;1,100;2,80;3,64,vuoto" legenda="Q=125\left(\dfrac45\right)^n" titolo="The three data points and the predicted value $64$, marked by an open circle, lie on the same exponential curve."}

<div class="spiegazione">

A constant-factor model requires the ratio of each measurement to the preceding one to be the same over intervals of equal duration. The two intervals have the same duration, so the ratios can be compared directly.

The first ratio is $\dfrac{100}{125}$, which reduces to $\dfrac45$ after dividing the numerator and denominator by $25$. The second is $\dfrac{80}{100}$, which likewise reduces to $\dfrac45$ after dividing by $20$. The ratios of successive measurements are equal, so the three data points are consistent with a model with constant factor $q=\dfrac45=0.8$, or a decrease of $20\%$ per interval. The differences, on the other hand, are $-25$ and $-20$, so the data are inconsistent with a constant-difference model.

Apply the same factor to the last measurement to obtain the prediction, since the model multiplies the quantity by $\dfrac45$ in each interval. The next **predicted measurement is** $64$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

At times $0$, $2$, and $6\,\mathrm h$, the measured values are $9$, $27$, and $243$, respectively. Check whether they are consistent with $Q(t)=Q_0e^{\lambda t}$ and find the growth factor over one hour.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\lambda_1&=\frac{\ln\left(\frac{27}{9}\right)}{2\,\mathrm h-0\,\mathrm h}=\frac{\ln3}{2\,\mathrm h},\\[6pt]
\lambda_2&=\frac{\ln\left(\frac{243}{27}\right)}{6\,\mathrm h-2\,\mathrm h}=\frac{\ln9}{4\,\mathrm h}=\frac{2\ln3}{4\,\mathrm h}=\frac{\ln3}{2\,\mathrm h},\\[6pt]
e^{\lambda_1\cdot1\,\mathrm h}&=e^{\frac{\ln3}{2}}=\left(e^{\ln3}\right)^{\frac12}=\sqrt3.
\end{aligned}
$$

::grafico{f="log(9)+x*log(3)/2" xmin=-0.5 xmax=7 ymin=0 ymax=6.5 nomi="t,ln Q" punti="0,2.1972;2,3.2958;6,5.4931" legenda="\ln Q=\ln9+\dfrac{\ln3}{2\,\mathrm h}\,t" titolo="Time $t$ is in hours. The logarithms of the three measurements lie on the same straight line with slope $\dfrac{\ln3}{2\,\mathrm h}$."}

<div class="spiegazione">

In the model $Q(t)=Q_0e^{\lambda t}$, the logarithm $\ln Q(t)=\ln Q_0+\lambda t$ is a linear function of time, so the slope between any two measurements must be the same. The intervals have different durations of two and four hours. Therefore compare the change in the logarithm divided by the duration of each interval.

The ratio of the measurements is $3$ in the first interval and $9$ in the second. Since $9=3^2$, we have $\ln9=2\ln3$, and dividing by four hours again gives $\dfrac{\ln3}{2\,\mathrm h}$. The two slopes agree, so the data **are consistent** with the exponential model.

The growth factor over one hour is $e^{\lambda\cdot1\,\mathrm h}$. Since the factor over two hours is three, the factor over one hour is its positive square root, $\sqrt3\approx1.73$. Indeed, two consecutive hours multiply the quantity by $\sqrt3\cdot\sqrt3=3$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

One model predicts an increase of $3\%$ every hour. A second starts at the same quantity and predicts an increase of $6\%$ every two hours. Which model gives the larger value after two hours, and by how much, expressed as a percentage of the initial quantity?

$$
\begin{aligned}
Q_A(1\,\mathrm h)&=1.03\,Q_0,\\[6pt]
Q_A(2\,\mathrm h)&=1.03\cdot1.03\,Q_0=1.0609\,Q_0,\\[6pt]
Q_B(2\,\mathrm h)&=(1+0.06)\,Q_0=1.06\,Q_0,\\[6pt]
Q_A(2\,\mathrm h)-Q_B(2\,\mathrm h)&=(1.0609-1.06)\,Q_0\\[6pt]
&=0.0009\,Q_0\\[6pt]
&=0.09\%\,Q_0.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

An increase of $3\%$ multiplies the quantity by the factor $1+\dfrac{3}{100}=1.03$, so after the first hour the first model gives $1.03\,Q_0$. In this model the second three percent increase applies to the value that has already increased during the first hour. Thus the two factors multiply, and $(1.03)^2=1.0609$. The term $0.0009=0.03\cdot0.03$ is three percent of the increase during the first hour.

The second model applies a single increase of $6\%$ over the two-hour interval, so the factor is $1.06$. Adding the two hourly rates, $3\%+3\%=6\%$, overlooks the increase calculated on the previous increase.

After two hours **the first model gives the larger value** by 0.09 percent of the initial quantity, or $0.0009\,Q_0$. For example, with $Q_0=10\,000$, the two models give $10\,609$ and $10\,600$.

</div>
