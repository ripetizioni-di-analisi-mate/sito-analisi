## Exercise 1

A solution of final volume $400\,\mathrm{mL}$ contains $9\,\mathrm{g}$ of a solute with molar mass $90\,\mathrm{g/mol}$. Calculate the molar concentration.

$$
\begin{aligned}
n&=\frac{m_{\mathrm{s}}}{\mathcal{M}}=\frac{9\,\mathrm{g}}{90\,\mathrm{g/mol}}
=0.10\,\mathrm{mol}\\[6pt]
V&=400\,\mathrm{mL}=0.400\,\mathrm{L}\\[6pt]
c&=\frac{n}{V}=\frac{0.10\,\mathrm{mol}}{0.400\,\mathrm{L}}
=0.25\,\mathrm{mol/L}.
\end{aligned}
$$
$$
c_m=\frac{9\,\mathrm{g}}{0.400\,\mathrm{L}}=22.5\,\mathrm{g/L},
\qquad
\frac{c_m}{\mathcal{M}}=\frac{22.5\,\mathrm{g/L}}{90\,\mathrm{g/mol}}=0.25\,\mathrm{mol/L}.
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Molar concentration requires the amount of solute in moles, whereas the given quantity is a mass. The molar mass $\mathcal{M}$ is the mass of one mole, so divide the mass by $\mathcal{M}$ to find the number of moles. The ratio of mass to molar mass has units of moles because $\dfrac{\mathrm{g}}{\mathrm{g/mol}}=\mathrm{mol}$. This gives $n=\dfrac{9}{90}=0.10\,\mathrm{mol}$.

Express the final volume in liters, giving $400\,\mathrm{mL}=0.400\,\mathrm{L}$. Dividing by the final volume in liters then gives the molar concentration, $\dfrac{0.10}{0.400}=0.25$. One liter is $2.5$ times the volume of the solution, so one liter of the same homogeneous solution would contain $2.5\cdot0.10=0.25\,\mathrm{mol}$ of solute.

Check the result using the relation $c_m=\mathcal{M}c$ between mass and molar concentration. The mass concentration is $\dfrac{9\,\mathrm{g}}{0.400\,\mathrm{L}}=22.5\,\mathrm{g/L}$, and dividing by the molar mass again gives $0.25\,\mathrm{mol/L}$.

The molar concentration is **$0.25\,\mathrm{mol/L}$**.

</div>


## Exercise 2

Mix $250\,\mathrm{mL}$ of a solution at $0.12\,\mathrm{mol/L}$ with $150\,\mathrm{mL}$ of a solution of the same solute at $0.32\,\mathrm{mol/L}$. The volumes are additive and the solute is conserved. Calculate the concentration of the mixture.

$$
\begin{aligned}
n_1&=0.12\,\mathrm{mol/L}\cdot0.250\,\mathrm{L}
=0.030\,\mathrm{mol}\\[6pt]
n_2&=0.32\,\mathrm{mol/L}\cdot0.150\,\mathrm{L}
=0.048\,\mathrm{mol}\\[6pt]
V_{\mathrm{f}}&=(0.250+0.150)\,\mathrm{L}=0.400\,\mathrm{L}\\[6pt]
c_{\mathrm{f}}&=\frac{(0.030+0.048)\,\mathrm{mol}}
{0.400\,\mathrm{L}}
=\frac{0.078\,\mathrm{mol}}{0.400\,\mathrm{L}}\\[6pt]
&=0.195\,\mathrm{mol/L}.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The amounts of solute are extensive quantities, so they add when the solutions are mixed. Each amount is the concentration times the volume in liters, giving $n_1=0.030\,\mathrm{mol}$ and $n_2=0.048\,\mathrm{mol}$, so the mixture contains a total of $0.078\,\mathrm{mol}$.

The problem states that the volumes are additive, so the final volume is the sum of the volumes, $0.400\,\mathrm{L}$. Therefore the concentration of the mixture is $\dfrac{0.078}{0.400}=0.195\,\mathrm{mol/L}$.

Its value lies between $0.12$ and $0.32\,\mathrm{mol/L}$, as stated in the theorem that **the concentration of a mixture lies between the initial concentrations**. The result is closer to 0.12 moles per liter because the solution at that concentration contributes the larger volume. The differences between $0.195$ and the two initial concentrations are $0.075$ and $0.125$. Their ratio $\dfrac{3}{5}$ equals the ratio $\dfrac{150}{250}$ of the volumes, as shown in the figure.

The concentration of the mixture is **$0.195\,\mathrm{mol/L}$**.

</div>


## Exercise 3

Take $30\,\mathrm{mL}$ from a solution at $0.90\,\mathrm{mol/L}$ and dilute to $90\,\mathrm{mL}$. Take $25\,\mathrm{mL}$ of the resulting homogeneous solution and dilute this second sample to $125\,\mathrm{mL}$. The solute is conserved during each dilution. Calculate the final concentration.

$$
\begin{aligned}
c_2&=c_1\,\frac{V_1}{V_2}=0.90\,\mathrm{mol/L}\cdot\frac{30}{90}
=0.30\,\mathrm{mol/L}\\[6pt]
c_3&=c_2\,\frac{V_2'}{V_3}=0.30\,\mathrm{mol/L}\cdot\frac{25}{125}
=0.060\,\mathrm{mol/L}.
\end{aligned}
$$
$$
c_3=0.90\,\mathrm{mol/L}\cdot\frac{1}{3}\cdot\frac{1}{5}
=\frac{0.90}{15}\,\mathrm{mol/L}=0.060\,\mathrm{mol/L}.
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The solute is conserved during each dilution, so $c_1V_1=c_2V_2$ holds, and the final concentration is the initial concentration multiplied by the ratio of the sample volume to the final volume. The volumes are all in milliliters, so the ratios are dimensionless and the volumes need not be converted to liters.

The first dilution reduces the concentration to one-third of its initial value because $\dfrac{30}{90}=\dfrac{1}{3}$, bringing the solution to $0.30\,\mathrm{mol/L}$. The second sample has this same concentration because the solution is homogeneous. In the second dilution, the initial volume $V_2'$ is the $25\,\mathrm{mL}$ taken and the final volume is $V_3=125\,\mathrm{mL}$. The $90\,\mathrm{mL}$ of the first solution is used only to calculate $c_2$.

The second dilution further reduces the concentration to one-fifth of the intermediate value because $\dfrac{25}{125}=\dfrac{1}{5}$. The two factors multiply, so the final concentration is $\dfrac{1}{15}$ of the initial concentration, and $\dfrac{0.90}{15}=0.060$.

The final concentration is **$0.060\,\mathrm{mol/L}$**.

</div>
