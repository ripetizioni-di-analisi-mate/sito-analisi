## Exercise 1

A $50\,\mathrm{mL}$ sample at a concentration of $2\,\mathrm{mol/L}$ is diluted to $500\,\mathrm{mL}$. The solute is conserved. Calculate the final concentration.

$$
\begin{aligned}
c_1V_1&=c_2V_2\\[6pt]
c_2&=c_1\,\frac{V_1}{V_2}\\[6pt]
&=2\,\mathrm{mol/L}\cdot\frac{50\,\mathrm{mL}}{500\,\mathrm{mL}}\\[6pt]
&=2\,\mathrm{mol/L}\cdot\frac{1}{10}
=0.2\,\mathrm{mol/L}.
\end{aligned}
$$
$$
n=2\,\mathrm{mol/L}\cdot0.050\,\mathrm{L}=0.10\,\mathrm{mol},
\qquad
\frac{0.10\,\mathrm{mol}}{0.500\,\mathrm{L}}=0.2\,\mathrm{mol/L}.
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The product of concentration and volume is the same before and after dilution because the solute is conserved. Indeed, $c_1V_1$ is the amount of solute in the initial sample and $c_2V_2$ is its amount in the final solution. The two amounts are equal by assumption. Dividing both sides by $V_2$ gives $c_2=c_1\dfrac{V_1}{V_2}$.

The two volumes have the same unit, so their ratio is dimensionless and can be calculated directly in milliliters. The ratio is $\dfrac{50}{500}=\dfrac{1}{10}$, so the final concentration is one-tenth of the initial concentration. The phrase “diluted to $500\,\mathrm{mL}$” specifies the final volume of the solution, which is therefore the value to use for $V_2$.

Check the result by calculating the amount of solute. To multiply concentration by volume, use the volume in liters, $50\,\mathrm{mL}=0.050\,\mathrm{L}$, giving $0.10\,\mathrm{mol}$. Dividing this number of moles by $0.500\,\mathrm{L}$ again gives $0.2\,\mathrm{mol/L}$.

The final concentration is **$0.2\,\mathrm{mol/L}$**.

</div>


## Exercise 2

Find the dimension of acceleration using the ratio of a change in velocity to a time interval. Use $L$ for length and $T$ for time.

$$
\begin{aligned}
a&=\frac{\Delta v}{\Delta t}\\[6pt]
[\Delta v]&=[v]=\frac{L}{T}=LT^{-1}, \qquad [\Delta t]=T\\[6pt]
[a]&=\frac{[\Delta v]}{[\Delta t]}
=\frac{LT^{-1}}{T}\\[6pt]
&=LT^{-1-1}=LT^{-2}.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Velocity is displacement divided by a time interval, so its dimension is length divided by time, $LT^{-1}$. A change in velocity has the same dimension as velocity because it is the difference between two velocities and only quantities with the same dimension can be subtracted. The interval $\Delta t$, the difference between two times, likewise has dimension $T$.

In a quotient the dimensions are divided. Dividing by time introduces another factor of time to the power of minus one, so the overall exponent of time is minus two. In terms of powers, $T^{-1}\cdot T^{-1}=T^{-2}$ because the exponents $-1$ and $-1$ are added.

The result corresponds to the SI unit of acceleration, the meter per second squared. For example, a velocity that increases by $10\,\mathrm{m/s}$ in $5\,\mathrm{s}$ corresponds to an average acceleration of $\dfrac{10\,\mathrm{m/s}}{5\,\mathrm{s}}=2\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$, and the unit $\mathrm{m/s^2}$ has precisely the form of length divided by time squared.

The dimension of acceleration is **$[a]=LT^{-2}$**.

</div>


## Exercise 3

Dissolve $24\,\mathrm{g}$ of a solute to make a final volume of $600\,\mathrm{mL}$. Take $150\,\mathrm{mL}$ of the homogeneous solution and dilute it to $750\,\mathrm{mL}$ without losing any solute. Calculate the final mass concentration.

$$
\begin{aligned}
c_{m,1}&=\frac{24\,\mathrm{g}}{0.600\,\mathrm{L}}
=40\,\mathrm{g/L}\\[6pt]
m_{\mathrm{s}}&=40\,\mathrm{g/L}\cdot0.150\,\mathrm{L}
=6\,\mathrm{g}\\[6pt]
c_{m,2}&=\frac{6\,\mathrm{g}}{0.750\,\mathrm{L}}
=8\,\mathrm{g/L}.
\end{aligned}
$$
$$
c_{m,2}=40\,\mathrm{g/L}\cdot\frac{150}{750}
=40\,\mathrm{g/L}\cdot\frac{1}{5}=8\,\mathrm{g/L}.
$$

The product $c_m V$ remains equal to $6\,\mathrm{g}$."}

<div class="spiegazione">

The mass concentration of the first solution is the mass of solute divided by the final volume in liters, $\dfrac{24}{0.600}=40\,\mathrm{g/L}$. The sample has the same concentration as the homogeneous solution because concentration is an intensive quantity.

Multiplying that concentration by the volume taken gives six grams of solute. The sample volume is one-quarter of $600\,\mathrm{mL}$, so its mass of solute is also one-quarter of $24\,\mathrm{g}$, in agreement with the calculated value. This mass is conserved during dilution and must be divided by the new volume, $0.750\,\mathrm{L}$, so the final concentration is $8\,\mathrm{g/L}$.

The dilution relation gives the same result. The volume increases from $150$ to $750\,\mathrm{mL}$, a factor of five, so the concentration falls to one-fifth of $40\,\mathrm{g/L}$. The remaining $450\,\mathrm{mL}$ of the first solution is not used in the second step.

In the figure the two states of the sampled solute lie on the same hyperbola because the mass of solute is fixed.

The final mass concentration is **$8\,\mathrm{g/L}$**.

</div>
