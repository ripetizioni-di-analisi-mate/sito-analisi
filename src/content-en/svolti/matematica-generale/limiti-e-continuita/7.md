## Exercise 1

Prove that the equation $e^{x} + x = 0$ has a solution in the interval $(-1, 0)$ and approximate it with the bisection method down to an interval of width less than $0.1$.

$$
f(x) = e^{x} + x,
\qquad
f(-1) = e^{-1} - 1 \approx -0.632 < 0,
\qquad
f(0) = 1 > 0
$$

| interval | midpoint $c$ | $f(c)$ | new interval |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $[-1;\ 0]$ | $-0.5$ | $\approx 0.107$ | $[-1;\ -0.5]$ |
| $[-1;\ -0.5]$ | $-0.75$ | $\approx -0.278$ | $[-0.75;\ -0.5]$ |
| $[-0.75;\ -0.5]$ | $-0.625$ | $\approx -0.090$ | $[-0.625;\ -0.5]$ |
| $[-0.625;\ -0.5]$ | $-0.5625$ | $\approx 0.007$ | $[-0.625;\ -0.5625]$ |

$$
x^* \in (-0.625;\ -0.5625),
\qquad
x^* \approx -0.59
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The function $f(x) = e^{x} + x$ is a sum of continuous functions, so it is continuous on $[-1, 0]$, and at the endpoints it takes values of opposite sign. By the **intermediate zero theorem** there exists at least one point of the interval at which $f$ vanishes, that is, a solution of the equation. To approximate it we compute $f$ at the midpoint and keep the half of the interval at whose endpoints $f$ still has opposite sign; each step halves the width. After four steps the interval has width $0.0625 < 0.1$, and its midpoint, approximately $-0.59$, approximates the solution with an error less than $0.032$. The function is increasing, because it is the sum of two increasing functions, so the solution is *unique*; its value is approximately $-0.567$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Prove that the equation $\ln x = 2 - x$ has a solution between $1$ and $2$.

$$
g(x) = \ln x + x - 2,
\qquad
g(1) = 0 + 1 - 2 = -1 < 0,
\qquad
g(2) = \ln 2 > 0
$$

::grafico{f="log(x);2-x" punti="1.557,0.443,pieno" segmenti="1.557,0,1.557,0.443" xmin=-1 xmax=4 ymin=-3 ymax=3 legenda="y = \ln x;y = 2 - x" titolo="The graph of $\ln x$ meets the line $y = 2 - x$ at a point with abscissa between $1$ and $2$, approximately $1.557$"}

<div class="spiegazione">

We move all the terms to the left-hand side, so the equation becomes $g(x) = 0$ with $g(x) = \ln x + x - 2$. The function $g$ is continuous on $[1, 2]$, because the logarithm is continuous for $x > 0$, and at the endpoints it takes the values $-1$ and $\ln 2 \approx 0.69$, of opposite sign. By the **intermediate zero theorem** there exists $c \in (1, 2)$ with $g(c) = 0$, that is $\ln c = 2 - c$. Graphically, $c$ is the abscissa of the point where the increasing graph of $\ln x$ meets the decreasing line $y = 2 - x$, and it is approximately $1.557$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Determine whether the following functions have a maximum and a minimum on the given interval, and say whether the Weierstrass theorem applies:

- $f(x) = \dfrac{1}{x}$ on $[1, 3]$ and on $(0, 1]$;
- $g(x) = x$ for $0 \leq x < 1$, with $g(1) = 0$, on $[0, 1]$.

$$
f \text{ on } [1, 3]: \qquad \max = f(1) = 1, \qquad \min = f(3) = \frac{1}{3}
$$
$$
f \text{ on } (0, 1]: \qquad \lim_{x \to 0^+} \frac{1}{x} = +\infty, \qquad \min = f(1) = 1, \qquad \text{no maximum}
$$
$$
g \text{ on } [0, 1]: \qquad g(x) < 1 \ \text{ for all } x, \qquad \lim_{x \to 1^-} g(x) = 1, \qquad \text{no maximum}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

On $[1, 3]$ the function $\dfrac{1}{x}$ is continuous and the interval is closed and bounded, so the **Weierstrass theorem** applies and guarantees a maximum and a minimum; since the function is decreasing, the maximum is at the left endpoint and the minimum at the right one. On $(0, 1]$ the interval is not closed and the function diverges near $0$, therefore there is no maximum; there is, however, the minimum, $f(1) = 1$, and this shows that the hypotheses of the theorem are *sufficient* for the existence of a maximum and a minimum, but not *necessary*. The function $g$ is defined on a closed interval, but it is not continuous at $1$; its values are as close to $1$ as we like but always less than $1$, so the maximum **does not exist**, while the minimum is $0$, attained at $0$ and at $1$.

</div>
