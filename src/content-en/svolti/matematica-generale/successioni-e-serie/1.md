## Exercise 1

For the sequence $a_n = \dfrac{n}{n^2 + 1}$ with $n \geq 1$, find the first three terms and determine whether the sequence is monotone and whether it is bounded.

$$
a_1 = \frac{1}{2},
\qquad
a_2 = \frac{2}{5},
\qquad
a_3 = \frac{3}{10}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
a_{n+1} - a_n
&= \frac{n + 1}{(n + 1)^2 + 1} - \frac{n}{n^2 + 1} \\[6pt]
&= \frac{(n + 1)(n^2 + 1) - n\,(n^2 + 2n + 2)}{(n^2 + 2n + 2)(n^2 + 1)} \\[6pt]
&= \frac{-n^2 - n + 1}{(n^2 + 2n + 2)(n^2 + 1)}
\end{aligned}
$$
$$
n \geq 1
\implies
-n^2 - n + 1 \leq -1 < 0
\implies
a_{n+1} < a_n
$$
$$
0 < a_n \leq a_1 = \frac{1}{2}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Substituting $n = 1, 2, 3$ into the general term gives the first terms, each smaller than the preceding one. To establish that the sequence decreases, consider the sign of $a_{n+1} - a_n$. Putting the fractions over a common denominator gives the numerator $n^3 + n^2 + n + 1 - n^3 - 2n^2 - 2n = -n^2 - n + 1$. For $n \geq 1$, the numerator is at most $-1$ and the denominator is positive. The difference is therefore negative, so the sequence is **decreasing**. Its terms are positive, and since it decreases, the first term is its largest. Thus $0 < a_n \leq \dfrac{1}{2}$, and the sequence is **bounded**.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Find the first four terms of the sequence defined recursively by $a_0 = 3$ and $a_{n+1} = 2\,a_n - 1$. Then verify that the proposed general term $b_n = 2^{\,n+1} + 1$ satisfies the same recurrence and gives the same first term.

$$
a_1 = 2 \cdot 3 - 1 = 5,
\qquad
a_2 = 2 \cdot 5 - 1 = 9,
\qquad
a_3 = 2 \cdot 9 - 1 = 17
$$
$$
b_0 = 2^{1} + 1 = 3
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
2\,b_n - 1
&= 2\left( 2^{\,n+1} + 1 \right) - 1 \\[6pt]
&= 2^{\,n+2} + 1 = b_{n+1}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The sequence is defined **recursively**, so each term is twice the preceding term minus $1$. Applying the rule three times starting at $a_0 = 3$ gives $5$, $9$, and $17$. The proposed general term gives $3$ when $n = 0$ and produces $b_{n+1}$ when substituted into the recurrence. The two sequences have the same initial term and recurrence, so they are **identical** and $a_n = 2^{\,n+1} + 1$ for every $n$. As a check, $b_3 = 2^4 + 1 = 17 = a_3$. The sequence is neither arithmetic nor geometric because each step involves multiplication *and* addition.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Determine the monotonicity, boundedness, and limit of the sequence $a_n = (-1)^n\,\dfrac{3}{n}$ with $n \geq 1$.

$$
a_1 = -3,
\qquad
a_2 = \frac{3}{2},
\qquad
a_3 = -1,
\qquad
a_4 = \frac{3}{4}
$$
$$
|a_n| = \frac{3}{n} \leq 3
$$
$$
|a_n - 0| = \frac{3}{n} < \varepsilon
\iff
n > \frac{3}{\varepsilon}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\lim_{n \to +\infty} a_n = 0
$$

::grafico{punti="1,-3.0000,pieno;2,1.5000,pieno;3,-1.0000,pieno;4,0.7500,pieno;5,-0.6000,pieno;6,0.5000,pieno;7,-0.4286,pieno;8,0.3750,pieno;9,-0.3333,pieno;10,0.3000,pieno;11,-0.2727,pieno;12,0.2500,pieno" xmin=-2 xmax=14 ymin=-4 ymax=4 nomi="n,aₙ" titolo="The terms of $a_n = (-1)^n\,\dfrac{3}{n}$ have alternating signs and approach $0$"}

<div class="spiegazione">

The factor $(-1)^n$ makes the terms alternate in sign, so the sequence is **not monotone**. For example, $a_1 < a_2$ but $a_2 > a_3$. The absolute value of each term is $\dfrac{3}{n}$, which is at most $3$, so the sequence is **bounded**. To compute the limit, apply the definition with $L = 0$. The distance from $a_n$ to $0$ is $\dfrac{3}{n}$, which is less than any given tolerance $\varepsilon$ whenever $n > \dfrac{3}{\varepsilon}$. Therefore the sequence **converges** to $0$, approaching $0$ from alternating sides.

</div>
