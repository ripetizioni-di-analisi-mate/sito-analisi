## Exercise 1

Given the sequence $a_n = \dfrac{n}{n^2 + 1}$, with $n \geq 1$, compute its first three terms and determine whether it is monotonic and whether it is bounded.

$$
a_1 = \frac{1}{2},
\qquad
a_2 = \frac{2}{5},
\qquad
a_3 = \frac{3}{10}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
a_{n+1} - a_n
&= \frac{n + 1}{(n + 1)^2 + 1} - \frac{n}{n^2 + 1} \\[6pt]
&= \frac{(n + 1)(n^2 + 1) - n\,(n^2 + 2n + 2)}{(n^2 + 2n + 2)(n^2 + 1)} \\[6pt]
&= \frac{-n^2 - n + 1}{(n^2 + 2n + 2)(n^2 + 1)}
\end{aligned}
$$
$$
n \geq 1
\implies
-n^2 - n + 1 \leq -1 < 0
\implies
a_{n+1} < a_n
$$
$$
0 < a_n \leq a_1 = \frac{1}{2}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The first terms are obtained by substituting $n = 1, 2, 3$ into the general term, and they get smaller and smaller. To prove that the sequence decreases, one studies the sign of the difference $a_{n+1} - a_n$. Bringing to a common denominator, the numerator equals $n^3 + n^2 + n + 1 - n^3 - 2n^2 - 2n = -n^2 - n + 1$. For $n \geq 1$ the numerator is at most $-1$, while the denominator is positive, so the difference is negative and the sequence is **decreasing**. All the terms are positive, and since the sequence decreases the largest one is the first; therefore $0 < a_n \leq \dfrac{1}{2}$ and the sequence is **bounded**.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Compute the first four terms of the sequence defined by recursion by $a_0 = 3$ and $a_{n+1} = 2\,a_n - 1$. Then verify that the general term $b_n = 2^{\,n+1} + 1$ satisfies the same rule and has the same first term.

$$
a_1 = 2 \cdot 3 - 1 = 5,
\qquad
a_2 = 2 \cdot 5 - 1 = 9,
\qquad
a_3 = 2 \cdot 9 - 1 = 17
$$
$$
b_0 = 2^{1} + 1 = 3
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
2\,b_n - 1
&= 2\left( 2^{\,n+1} + 1 \right) - 1 \\[6pt]
&= 2^{\,n+2} + 1 = b_{n+1}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The sequence is given **by recursion**, so each term is obtained from the previous one by doubling it and subtracting $1$. Starting from $a_0 = 3$, the rule is applied three times, giving $5$, $9$, $17$. The proposed general term equals $3$ for $n = 0$, and substituted into the rule it gives exactly the next term $b_{n+1}$. The two sequences therefore have the same first term and the same rule, so they **coincide** and $a_n = 2^{\,n+1} + 1$ for every $n$. As a check, $b_3 = 2^4 + 1 = 17 = a_3$. The sequence is neither arithmetic nor geometric, because at each step one multiplies *and* adds.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Study the monotonicity, the boundedness and the limit of the sequence $a_n = (-1)^n\,\dfrac{3}{n}$, with $n \geq 1$.

$$
a_1 = -3,
\qquad
a_2 = \frac{3}{2},
\qquad
a_3 = -1,
\qquad
a_4 = \frac{3}{4}
$$
$$
|a_n| = \frac{3}{n} \leq 3
$$
$$
|a_n - 0| = \frac{3}{n} < \varepsilon
\iff
n > \frac{3}{\varepsilon}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\lim_{n \to +\infty} a_n = 0
$$

::grafico{punti="1,-3.0000,pieno;2,1.5000,pieno;3,-1.0000,pieno;4,0.7500,pieno;5,-0.6000,pieno;6,0.5000,pieno;7,-0.4286,pieno;8,0.3750,pieno;9,-0.3333,pieno;10,0.3000,pieno;11,-0.2727,pieno;12,0.2500,pieno" xmin=-2 xmax=14 ymin=-4 ymax=4 nomi="n,aₙ" titolo="The terms of $a_n = (-1)^n\,\dfrac{3}{n}$ have alternating signs and approach $0$"}

<div class="spiegazione">

The factor $(-1)^n$ makes the terms alternately negative and positive, therefore the sequence is **not monotonic**; for example $a_1 < a_2$ but $a_2 > a_3$. The absolute value of the terms is $\dfrac{3}{n}$, which does not exceed $3$, so the sequence is **bounded**. For the limit, one uses the definition with $L = 0$. The distance of $a_n$ from $0$ is $\dfrac{3}{n}$, and this is less than any tolerance $\varepsilon$ from $n > \dfrac{3}{\varepsilon}$ on. Therefore the sequence **converges** to $0$, even though it approaches $0$ from alternating sides.

</div>
