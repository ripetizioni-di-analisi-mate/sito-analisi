## Exercise 1

Classify the series $\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{2k + 1}{k + 3}$.

$$
\lim_{k \to +\infty} \frac{2k + 1}{k + 3} = 2 \neq 0
$$
$$
\frac{2k + 1}{k + 3} > 0 \ \text{ for every } k
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
S_n \ \text{increasing}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The general term is a quotient of polynomials of the same degree, so it tends to the ratio of their leading coefficients, $2$. The **necessary condition for convergence** says that the general term of a convergent series tends to $0$. Here the limit is nonzero, so the series **does not converge**. All its terms are positive, so each partial sum adds a positive quantity to the preceding one and $S_n$ is increasing. By the monotone sequence theorem, an increasing sequence that does not converge diverges to $+\infty$, so the series **diverges** to $+\infty$. From some index onward every term exceeds $1$, so $S_n$ grows at least as fast as $n$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Compute the sum of the series $\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{k\,(k + 1)}$, writing the general term as $\dfrac{1}{k} - \dfrac{1}{k + 1}$.

$$
\frac{1}{k} - \frac{1}{k + 1} = \frac{k + 1 - k}{k\,(k + 1)} = \frac{1}{k\,(k + 1)}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
S_n
&= \left( 1 - \frac{1}{2} \right) + \left( \frac{1}{2} - \frac{1}{3} \right) + \dots + \left( \frac{1}{n} - \frac{1}{n + 1} \right) \\[6pt]
&= 1 - \frac{1}{n + 1}
\end{aligned}
$$
$$
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{k\,(k + 1)} = \lim_{n \to +\infty} \left( 1 - \frac{1}{n + 1} \right) = 1
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Putting the two fractions over a common denominator verifies the proposed decomposition. In the partial sum, the second addend of each term then cancels the first addend of the next term. For example, $-\dfrac{1}{2}$ cancels $+\dfrac{1}{2}$, $-\dfrac{1}{3}$ cancels $+\dfrac{1}{3}$, and so on. Only the first addend of the first term, $1$, and the last addend of the last term, $-\dfrac{1}{n + 1}$, remain. This makes the series **telescoping**. The sum of the **series** is the limit of the partial sums, so it equals $1$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Show that the series $\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \ln\frac{k + 1}{k}$ diverges, even though its general term tends to $0$.

$$
\ln\frac{k + 1}{k} = \ln(k + 1) - \ln k,
\qquad
\lim_{k \to +\infty} \ln\frac{k + 1}{k} = \ln 1 = 0
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
S_n
&= (\ln 2 - \ln 1) + (\ln 3 - \ln 2) + \dots + \big( \ln(n + 1) - \ln n \big) \\[6pt]
&= \ln(n + 1) - \ln 1 = \ln(n + 1)
\end{aligned}
$$
$$
\lim_{n \to +\infty} S_n = \lim_{n \to +\infty} \ln(n + 1) = +\infty
$$

::grafico{curve="t,log(t+1),0,18" punti="1,0.6931,pieno;2,1.0986,pieno;3,1.3863,pieno;4,1.6094,pieno;5,1.7918,pieno;6,1.9459,pieno;7,2.0794,pieno;8,2.1972,pieno;9,2.3026,pieno;10,2.3979,pieno;11,2.4849,pieno;12,2.5649,pieno;13,2.6391,pieno;14,2.7081,pieno;15,2.7726,pieno" xmin=-2 xmax=18 ymin=-1 ymax=4 nomi="n,Sₙ" titolo="The partial sums $S_n = \ln(n + 1)$ lie on the graph of $y = \ln(x + 1)$, which grows without bound"}

<div class="spiegazione">

The general term tends to $0$ because $\dfrac{k + 1}{k} = 1 + \dfrac{1}{k}$ tends to $1$ and the logarithm of $1$ is $0$. Thus the **necessary condition** holds and gives no conclusion. The logarithm of a quotient can be written as a difference, so the series telescopes. In each partial sum, the intermediate logarithms cancel and $\ln(n + 1)$ remains. This sequence tends to $+\infty$, so the series **diverges**. The example shows that $a_k \to 0$ is *necessary* for convergence, but *not sufficient*.

</div>
