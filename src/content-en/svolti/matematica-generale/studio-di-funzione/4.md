## Exercise 1

Study the function $f(x) = x^2\,e^{-x}$ and sketch its graph.

**Domain, symmetries, sign.**
$$
D = \mathbb{R},
\qquad
f(x) \geq 0 \ \text{ for every } x,
\qquad
f(x) = 0 \iff x = 0
$$

**Limits and asymptotes.**
$$
\lim_{x \to +\infty} \frac{x^2}{e^{x}} = 0,
\qquad
\lim_{x \to -\infty} x^2\,e^{-x} = +\infty,
\qquad
\lim_{x \to -\infty} \frac{f(x)}{x} = \lim_{x \to -\infty} x\,e^{-x} = -\infty
$$

**First derivative.**
$$
f'(x) = 2x\,e^{-x} - x^2\,e^{-x} = x\,(2 - x)\,e^{-x}
$$

| | $x < 0$ | $0 < x < 2$ | $x > 2$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $x$ | $-$ | $+$ | $+$ |
| $2 - x$ | $+$ | $+$ | $-$ |
| $f'(x)$ | $-$ | $+$ | $-$ |

$$
f(0) = 0,
\qquad
f(2) = \frac{4}{e^2} \approx 0.54
$$

**Second derivative.**
$$
\begin{aligned}
f''(x)
&= (2 - 2x)\,e^{-x} - (2x - x^2)\,e^{-x} \\[6pt]
&= (x^2 - 4x + 2)\,e^{-x}
\end{aligned}
$$
$$
f''(x) = 0 \iff x = 2 \pm \sqrt{2},
\qquad
f''(x) < 0 \iff 2 - \sqrt{2} < x < 2 + \sqrt{2}
$$
$$
f\left( 2 - \sqrt{2} \right) \approx 0.19,
\qquad
f\left( 2 + \sqrt{2} \right) \approx 0.38
$$

The absolute minimum is $0$, at $x = 0$; the point $x = 2$ is a relative maximum point, with value $\dfrac{4}{e^2}$. The line $y = 0$ is a horizontal asymptote on the right; the function is convex on $\left( -\infty, 2 - \sqrt{2} \right]$ and on $\left[ 2 + \sqrt{2}, +\infty \right)$, concave between the two inflections $x = 2 \pm \sqrt{2}$.

<div class="spiegazione">

The function is the product of a polynomial and an exponential, so it is defined and differentiable on all of $\mathbb{R}$. It is neither even nor odd, because $f(-1) = e$ and $f(1) = \dfrac{1}{e}$. The two factors $x^2$ and $e^{-x}$ are nonnegative, hence $f \geq 0$, and it vanishes only at $0$.

As $x \to +\infty$ the function is the quotient $\dfrac{x^2}{e^{x}}$, which tends to $0$ by the **hierarchy of infinities**, so $y = 0$ is a horizontal asymptote on the right. As $x \to -\infty$ the two factors both tend to $+\infty$. The quotient $\dfrac{f(x)}{x}$ also tends to $-\infty$, so on the left there is no oblique asymptote.

In the derivative $x\,e^{-x}$ is taken out, and the sign of $f'$ is that of $x\,(2 - x)$. By the first derivative test $x = 0$ is a relative minimum point and $x = 2$ a relative maximum point. Since $f \geq 0 = f(0)$ everywhere, the minimum at $0$ is also **absolute**. The maximum at $2$ is only relative, because $f$ tends to $+\infty$ as $x \to -\infty$.

The second derivative is obtained by differentiating the product $(2x - x^2)\,e^{-x}$. The exponential factor is positive, so the sign of $f''$ is that of the trinomial $x^2 - 4x + 2$, which has roots $2 \pm \sqrt{2}$ and is negative between them. The sign changes at both roots, hence there are two **inflections**, the first between the minimum and the maximum, the second after the maximum, beyond which the function is convex and tends to $0$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Study the function $f(x) = \dfrac{e^{x}}{x}$ and sketch its graph.

**Domain, symmetries, sign.**
$$
D = \mathbb{R} \setminus \{0\},
\qquad
f(x) > 0 \iff x > 0
$$

**Limits and asymptotes.**
$$
\lim_{x \to 0^{+}} f(x) = +\infty,
\qquad
\lim_{x \to 0^{-}} f(x) = -\infty,
\qquad
\lim_{x \to -\infty} f(x) = 0
$$
$$
\lim_{x \to +\infty} f(x) = +\infty,
\qquad
\lim_{x \to +\infty} \frac{f(x)}{x} = \lim_{x \to +\infty} \frac{e^{x}}{x^2} = +\infty
$$

**First derivative.**
$$
f'(x) = \frac{e^{x}\,x - e^{x}}{x^2} = \frac{(x - 1)\,e^{x}}{x^2}
$$
$$
f'(x) > 0 \iff x > 1,
\qquad
f(1) = e
$$

**Second derivative.**
$$
\begin{aligned}
f''(x)
&= \frac{x\,e^{x} \cdot x^2 - (x - 1)\,e^{x} \cdot 2x}{x^4} \\[6pt]
&= \frac{(x^2 - 2x + 2)\,e^{x}}{x^3}
\end{aligned}
$$
$$
f''(x) > 0 \iff x > 0
$$

The function decreases on $(-\infty, 0)$ and on $(0, 1]$, increases on $[1, +\infty)$, and has the relative minimum $(1, e)$; it has the vertical asymptote $x = 0$ and the horizontal asymptote $y = 0$ on the left, is concave for $x < 0$, convex for $x > 0$, and has no inflections.

::grafico{f="exp(x)/x" asintoti="x=0;y=0" punti="1,2.718,pieno" xmin=-6 xmax=4 ymin=-6 ymax=12 titolo="Graph of $f(x) = \dfrac{e^{x}}{x}$ with the vertical asymptote $x = 0$, the horizontal asymptote $y = 0$ on the left and the relative minimum $(1, e)$"}

<div class="spiegazione">

The only condition is that the denominator be different from zero, so the domain is $\mathbb{R}$ without $0$, and the function is neither even nor odd. The numerator $e^{x}$ is always positive, hence the sign of $f$ is that of $x$, and the graph does not meet the axes.

At $0$ the numerator tends to $1$ and the denominator to $0$ with the sign of $x$, so $x = 0$ is a **vertical asymptote**. As $x \to -\infty$ the numerator tends to $0$ and the denominator to $-\infty$, hence $f \to 0$ and the line $y = 0$ is a horizontal asymptote on the left. As $x \to +\infty$ the exponential grows faster than any power, by the hierarchy of infinities; so $f \to +\infty$, and also $\dfrac{f(x)}{x} \to +\infty$, hence there is no oblique asymptote.

The derivative is computed with the quotient rule; the denominator $x^2$ and the factor $e^{x}$ are positive, so the sign of $f'$ is that of $x - 1$. By the **first derivative test** the point $x = 1$ is a relative minimum point, with value $e$. On the branch $x > 0$ this minimum is the smallest value, but it is not absolute, because for $x < 0$ the function is negative.

In the second derivative $x\,e^{x}$ is taken out in the numerator and one $x$ is canceled. The trinomial $x^2 - 2x + 2$ has negative discriminant, so it is always positive, and the sign of $f''$ is that of $x^3$, that is, of $x$. The concavity changes at $0$, which does not belong to the domain, hence the function has no **inflections**.

</div>
