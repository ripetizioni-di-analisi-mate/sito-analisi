## Exercise 1

Study the function $f(x) = \dfrac{\ln x}{x}$ and sketch its graph.

**Domain, symmetries, sign.**
$$
D = (0, +\infty),
\qquad
f(x) = 0 \iff x = 1,
\qquad
f(x) > 0 \iff x > 1
$$

**Limits and asymptotes.**
$$
\lim_{x \to 0^{+}} \frac{\ln x}{x} = -\infty,
\qquad
\lim_{x \to +\infty} \frac{\ln x}{x} = 0
$$

**First derivative.**
$$
f'(x) = \frac{\dfrac{1}{x} \cdot x - \ln x}{x^2} = \frac{1 - \ln x}{x^2}
$$
$$
f'(x) > 0 \iff \ln x < 1 \iff 0 < x < e,
\qquad
f(e) = \frac{1}{e} \approx 0.37
$$

**Second derivative.**
$$
\begin{aligned}
f''(x)
&= \frac{-\dfrac{1}{x} \cdot x^2 - (1 - \ln x) \cdot 2x}{x^4} \\[6pt]
&= \frac{2\ln x - 3}{x^3}
\end{aligned}
$$
$$
f''(x) > 0 \iff \ln x > \frac{3}{2} \iff x > e^{\frac{3}{2}} \approx 4.48,
\qquad
f\left( e^{\frac{3}{2}} \right) = \frac{3}{2\,e^{\frac{3}{2}}} \approx 0.33
$$

The function has the vertical asymptote $x = 0$ and the horizontal asymptote $y = 0$; it increases on $(0, e]$ and decreases on $[e, +\infty)$, with the absolute maximum $\dfrac{1}{e}$ at $x = e$; it is concave on $\left( 0, e^{\frac{3}{2}} \right]$, convex on $\left[ e^{\frac{3}{2}}, +\infty \right)$, with an inflection at $x = e^{\frac{3}{2}}$.

::grafico{f="log(x)/x" asintoti="x=0;y=0" punti="2.718,0.368,pieno;4.482,0.335,vuoto;1,0,pieno" xmin=-2 xmax=18 ymin=-2 ymax=1 titolo="Graph of $f(x) = \dfrac{\ln x}{x}$ with the absolute maximum $\left( e, \dfrac{1}{e} \right)$, the inflection (hollow point) at $x = e^{\frac{3}{2}}$ and the asymptotes $x = 0$ and $y = 0$"}

<div class="spiegazione">

The logarithm requires $x > 0$, and this condition also excludes the zero of the denominator, so the domain is $(0, +\infty)$; since it is not symmetric, the function is neither even nor odd. The denominator is positive, hence the sign of $f$ is that of $\ln x$.

As $x \to 0^{+}$ the numerator tends to $-\infty$ and the denominator to $0^{+}$, so the quotient tends to $-\infty$ and the line $x = 0$ is a **vertical asymptote**. As $x \to +\infty$ the limit equals $0$ by the hierarchy of infinities (or by L'Hôpital's rule), so $y = 0$ is a horizontal asymptote.

The sign of $f'$ is that of $1 - \ln x$, positive for $x < e$. The derivative is negative to the right of $e$ and positive to the left, hence by the theorem on **functions with a single stationary point** the value $\dfrac{1}{e}$ is the absolute maximum of $f$ on its domain.

The denominator $x^3$ of the second derivative is positive, so the sign of $f''$ is that of $2\ln x - 3$. The second derivative changes sign at $e^{\frac{3}{2}}$, which is therefore an **inflection**; after the maximum the function decreases, first concave and then convex, and tends to $0$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Study the function $f(x) = x^2 \ln x$ and sketch its graph.

**Domain, symmetries, sign.**
$$
D = (0, +\infty),
\qquad
f(x) > 0 \iff \ln x > 0 \iff x > 1
$$

**Limits and asymptotes.**
$$
\lim_{x \to 0^{+}} x^2 \ln x = \lim_{x \to 0^{+}} \frac{\ln x}{x^{-2}} = \lim_{x \to 0^{+}} \frac{\dfrac{1}{x}}{-2x^{-3}} = \lim_{x \to 0^{+}} \left( -\frac{x^2}{2} \right) = 0
$$
$$
\lim_{x \to +\infty} x^2 \ln x = +\infty,
\qquad
\lim_{x \to +\infty} \frac{f(x)}{x} = \lim_{x \to +\infty} x \ln x = +\infty
$$

**First derivative.**
$$
f'(x) = 2x \ln x + x^2 \cdot \frac{1}{x} = x\,(2\ln x + 1)
$$
$$
f'(x) > 0 \iff \ln x > -\frac{1}{2} \iff x > e^{-\frac{1}{2}} \approx 0.61
$$
$$
f\left( e^{-\frac{1}{2}} \right) = e^{-1} \cdot \left( -\frac{1}{2} \right) = -\frac{1}{2e} \approx -0.18
$$

**Second derivative.**
$$
f''(x) = 2\ln x + 1 + x \cdot \frac{2}{x} = 2\ln x + 3
$$
$$
f''(x) > 0 \iff x > e^{-\frac{3}{2}} \approx 0.22,
\qquad
f\left( e^{-\frac{3}{2}} \right) = -\frac{3}{2e^3} \approx -0.07
$$

The function tends to $0$ as $x \to 0^{+}$ and has no asymptotes; it decreases on $\left( 0, e^{-\frac{1}{2}} \right]$ and increases on $\left[ e^{-\frac{1}{2}}, +\infty \right)$, with the absolute minimum $-\dfrac{1}{2e}$; it is concave on $\left( 0, e^{-\frac{3}{2}} \right]$, convex on $\left[ e^{-\frac{3}{2}}, +\infty \right)$, with an inflection at $x = e^{-\frac{3}{2}}$.

::grafico{f="x^2*log(x)" punti="0.607,-0.184,pieno;0.223,-0.075,vuoto;1,0,pieno;0,0,vuoto" xmin=-0.5 xmax=1.5 ymin=-0.5 ymax=1.5 titolo="Graph of $f(x) = x^2 \ln x$: it tends to $0$ as $x \to 0^{+}$, has the absolute minimum at $x = e^{-\frac{1}{2}}$, the inflection (hollow point) at $x = e^{-\frac{3}{2}}$ and vanishes at $x = 1$"}

<div class="spiegazione">

The domain is that of the logarithm, $(0, +\infty)$. The factor $x^2$ is positive, hence the sign of $f$ is that of $\ln x$, and the function vanishes at $x = 1$.

The limit at $0^{+}$ is an indeterminate form $0 \cdot \infty$. Writing the product as the quotient $\dfrac{\ln x}{x^{-2}}$ one obtains the form $\dfrac{\infty}{\infty}$, and **L'Hôpital's rule** applies; the limit is $0$, so $x = 0$ is *not* a vertical asymptote, and the graph approaches the origin, which however does not belong to it. As $x \to +\infty$ the function and the quotient $\dfrac{f(x)}{x}$ tend to $+\infty$, hence there are neither horizontal nor oblique asymptotes.

The first derivative is computed with the product rule. The factor $x$ is positive, so the sign of $f'$ is that of $2\ln x + 1$, which changes from negative to positive at $e^{-\frac{1}{2}}$; by the theorem on functions with a single stationary point that point is an **absolute minimum** point. The minimum value is computed with $\left( e^{-\frac{1}{2}} \right)^2 = e^{-1}$ and $\ln e^{-\frac{1}{2}} = -\dfrac{1}{2}$.

The second derivative $2\ln x + 3$ is negative for $x < e^{-\frac{3}{2}}$ and positive after, so at $e^{-\frac{3}{2}}$ there is an **inflection**, which falls between the origin and the minimum point.

</div>
