## Exercise 1

Find the extrema of $f(x, y) = x^2 + 2y^2$ on the line $x + y = 3$.

$$
x = 3 - y,
\qquad
\varphi(y) = (3 - y)^2 + 2y^2 = 3y^2 - 6y + 9,
\qquad
y \in \mathbb{R}
$$
$$
\varphi'(y) = 6y - 6 = 0 \iff y = 1,
\qquad
\varphi'(y) < 0 \ \text{ for } y < 1, \quad \varphi'(y) > 0 \ \text{ for } y > 1
$$
$$
\min f = f(2, 1) = 6,
\qquad
\lim_{y \to \pm\infty} \varphi(y) = +\infty \ \implies \ \text{no maximum}
$$

::grafico{f="3-x" curve="sqrt(6)*cos(t),sqrt(3)*sin(t),0,6.29" punti="2,1,pieno" xmin=-3 xmax=5 ymin=-3 ymax=4 uguale=1 legenda="x + y = 3;x^2 + 2y^2 = 6" titolo="The level curve $x^2 + 2y^2 = 6$ is tangent to the line $x + y = 3$ at the constrained minimum point $(2, 1)$"}

<div class="spiegazione">

From the constraint one obtains $x = 3 - y$, and substituting into $f$ gives the function of one variable $\varphi(y)$, defined for every real $y$ because the line is unbounded. Expanding, $(3 - y)^2 + 2y^2 = 9 - 6y + y^2 + 2y^2 = 3y^2 - 6y + 9$. The derivative is negative for $y < 1$ and positive for $y > 1$, so $\varphi$ has its **absolute minimum** at $y = 1$. The corresponding point on the line is $(2, 1)$, and the constrained minimum equals $6$.

Since $\varphi(y) \to +\infty$ as $y \to \pm\infty$, the function $f$ on the constraint **has no maximum**. At the minimum point the gradient $\nabla f = (2x, 4y) = (4, 4)$ is parallel to $(1, 1)$, the gradient of the constraint, in agreement with the geometric interpretation, by which the level curve $x^2 + 2y^2 = 6$ is tangent to the line.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Find the maximum and minimum of $f(x, y) = x y^2$ on the segment $x + y = 3$, $x \geq 0$, $y \geq 0$.

$$
x = 3 - y,
\qquad
\varphi(y) = (3 - y)\,y^2 = 3y^2 - y^3,
\qquad
y \in [0, 3]
$$
$$
\varphi'(y) = 6y - 3y^2 = 3y\,(2 - y) = 0 \iff y = 0 \ \text{ or } \ y = 2
$$
$$
\varphi(0) = 0, \qquad \varphi(2) = 4, \qquad \varphi(3) = 0
$$
$$
\max f = f(1, 2) = 4,
\qquad
\min f = f(3, 0) = f(0, 3) = 0
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The segment is described by $x = 3 - y$ and $y \in [0, 3]$; the condition $x \geq 0$ is equivalent to $y \leq 3$. On this closed and bounded interval the function $\varphi(y) = 3y^2 - y^3$ is continuous, so it has a maximum and a minimum by the Weierstrass theorem, to be sought among the points where $\varphi' = 0$ and the endpoints of the interval.

The derivative vanishes at $y = 0$, which is also an endpoint, and at $y = 2$. Comparing the values, the **maximum** is $4$, at $y = 2$, that is, at the point $(1, 2)$, and the **minimum** is $0$, at the endpoints $y = 0$ and $y = 3$, that is, at the points $(3, 0)$ and $(0, 3)$. The minimum was predictable, because on the segment $x y^2 \geq 0$ and the value $0$ occurs when one of the two coordinates is zero.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Find the maximum and minimum of $f(x, y) = x^2 + xy + y^2$ on the circle $x^2 + y^2 = 1$.

$$
x = \cos t, \quad y = \sin t, \qquad t \in [0, 2\pi]
$$
$$
\varphi(t) = \cos^2 t + \sin t \cos t + \sin^2 t = 1 + \frac{1}{2}\,\sin 2t
$$
$$
\sin 2t = 1 \iff t = \frac{\pi}{4}, \ \frac{5\pi}{4},
\qquad
\sin 2t = -1 \iff t = \frac{3\pi}{4}, \ \frac{7\pi}{4}
$$
$$
\max f = \frac{3}{2} \ \text{ at } \left( \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}, \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} \right), \ \left( -\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}, -\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} \right)
$$
$$
\min f = \frac{1}{2} \ \text{ at } \left( -\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}, \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} \right), \ \left( \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}, -\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} \right)
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

From the circle one cannot obtain a single function $y = h(x)$, because to each $x \in (-1, 1)$ there correspond two values of $y$; we then use the description with the parameter $t$, the angle of the point. Substituting, $\cos^2 t + \sin^2 t = 1$, and by the double-angle formula $\sin t \cos t = \dfrac{1}{2}\,\sin 2t$.

The function $\varphi(t) = 1 + \dfrac{1}{2}\,\sin 2t$ is maximum when $\sin 2t = 1$ and minimum when $\sin 2t = -1$, because the sine takes all the values of $[-1, 1]$. The constrained **maximum** is therefore $1 + \dfrac{1}{2} = \dfrac{3}{2}$, at the two points of the bisector line $y = x$, and the **minimum** is $1 - \dfrac{1}{2} = \dfrac{1}{2}$, at the two points of the bisector line $y = -x$.

</div>
