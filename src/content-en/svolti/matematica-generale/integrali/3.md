## Exercise 1

Compute
$$
\int_1^4 \left( 3\sqrt{x} - \frac{1}{x^2} \right) dx .
$$

$$
\int \left( 3\,x^{\frac{1}{2}} - x^{-2} \right) dx = 3 \cdot \frac{2}{3}\,x^{\frac{3}{2}} + x^{-1} + c = 2\,x^{\frac{3}{2}} + \frac{1}{x} + c
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_1^4 \left( 3\sqrt{x} - \frac{1}{x^2} \right) dx
&= \left[ 2\,x^{\frac{3}{2}} + \frac{1}{x} \right]_1^4
= \left( 2 \cdot 8 + \frac{1}{4} \right) - (2 + 1) \\[6pt]
&= \frac{65}{4} - 3
= \frac{53}{4}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

By the **fundamental formula of calculus**, an antiderivative of the integrand, which is continuous on $[1, 4]$, is enough. Writing the root and the reciprocal as powers, the antiderivative is found with the power formula; the constant $c$ can be omitted, because it cancels in the difference. In the computation $4^{\frac{3}{2}} = \left( \sqrt{4} \right)^3 = 8$. We subtract the value at $1$ from the value at $4$, and obtain $\dfrac{53}{4}$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Compute $\displaystyle\int_0^3 \left( x^2 - 2x \right) dx$ and the area of the region between the graph of $f(x) = x^2 - 2x$ and the $x$-axis for $0 \leq x \leq 3$.

$$
\int_0^3 \left( x^2 - 2x \right) dx = \left[ \frac{x^3}{3} - x^2 \right]_0^3 = 9 - 9 = 0
$$
$$
x^2 - 2x = x\,(x - 2) < 0 \ \text{ for } 0 < x < 2,
\qquad
x^2 - 2x > 0 \ \text{ for } 2 < x \leq 3
$$
$$
\int_0^2 \left( 2x - x^2 \right) dx = \left[ x^2 - \frac{x^3}{3} \right]_0^2 = 4 - \frac{8}{3} = \frac{4}{3}
$$
$$
\int_2^3 \left( x^2 - 2x \right) dx = \left[ \frac{x^3}{3} - x^2 \right]_2^3 = 0 - \left( \frac{8}{3} - 4 \right) = \frac{4}{3}
$$
$$
A = \frac{4}{3} + \frac{4}{3} = \frac{8}{3}
$$

::grafico{f="x^2-2*x" area="x^2-2*x;0;0;3" etichette="0.7,-0.6,−;2.7,0.6,+" punti="2,0,pieno" xmin=-1 xmax=4 ymin=-2 ymax=4 legenda="y = x^2 - 2x" titolo="The two regions between $y = x^2 - 2x$ and the $x$-axis both have area $\dfrac{4}{3}$, so the integral on $[0, 3]$ equals $0$ and the area equals $\dfrac{8}{3}$"}

<div class="spiegazione">

The integral is computed with the fundamental formula and equals $0$. This does *not* mean that the area is zero, because the integral counts **with a minus sign** the parts of the region that lie below the axis. The function $x\,(x - 2)$ is negative between the two zeros $0$ and $2$ and positive for $x > 2$. We therefore split the interval at $x = 2$ and, by **additivity**, compute the area of each part with the integral of the positive function: on the first piece $2x - x^2$, on the second $x^2 - 2x$. The two areas both equal $\dfrac{4}{3}$, so the integral, which is their difference, is $0$, and the area, which is their sum, is $\dfrac{8}{3}$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Compute the integral mean of $f(x) = 6x - 3x^2$ on the interval $[0, 2]$ and find the points $c \in [0, 2]$ at which $f(c)$ is equal to the mean.

$$
\frac{1}{2 - 0} \int_0^2 \left( 6x - 3x^2 \right) dx = \frac{1}{2} \Big[ 3x^2 - x^3 \Big]_0^2 = \frac{1}{2}\,(12 - 8) = 2
$$
$$
6c - 3c^2 = 2
\iff
3c^2 - 6c + 2 = 0
\iff
c = \frac{6 \pm \sqrt{36 - 24}}{6} = 1 \pm \frac{\sqrt{3}}{3}
$$
$$
c_1 = 1 - \frac{\sqrt{3}}{3} \approx 0.42,
\qquad
c_2 = 1 + \frac{\sqrt{3}}{3} \approx 1.58
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The **integral mean** is the integral divided by the width of the interval, here $2$, and it equals $2$. The function is continuous on $[0, 2]$, so the **integral mean value theorem** guarantees that there exists at least one point at which $f$ takes the value $2$. To find it we solve the second-degree equation $f(c) = 2$. The discriminant is $36 - 24 = 12$ and $\dfrac{\sqrt{12}}{6} = \dfrac{2\sqrt{3}}{6} = \dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{3}$. Both solutions, about $0.42$ and $1.58$, lie in $[0, 2]$, therefore the required points are **two**; the theorem ensures the existence of at least one point, and not its uniqueness.

</div>

## Exercise 4

Let
$$
F(x) = \int_0^x \left( t^2 - 4t + 3 \right) e^{-t}\,dt, \qquad x \in \mathbb{R} .
$$
Compute $F(0)$ and $F'(x)$, find the intervals on which $F$ increases and decreases, and its relative maximum and minimum points.

$$
F(0) = \int_0^0 \left( t^2 - 4t + 3 \right) e^{-t}\,dt = 0
$$
$$
F'(x) = \left( x^2 - 4x + 3 \right) e^{-x} = (x - 1)(x - 3)\,e^{-x}
$$

| | $x < 1$ | $1 < x < 3$ | $x > 3$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $x - 1$ | $-$ | $+$ | $+$ |
| $x - 3$ | $-$ | $-$ | $+$ |
| $F'(x)$ | $+$ | $-$ | $+$ |
| $F$ | increasing | decreasing | increasing |

$$
x = 1 \ \text{ relative maximum point},
\qquad
x = 3 \ \text{ relative minimum point}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

An integral with equal bounds equals $0$, so $F(0) = 0$. The integrand is continuous on $\mathbb{R}$, therefore by the **fundamental theorem of integral calculus** the integral function is differentiable and its derivative is the integrand evaluated at $x$; there is no need to compute the integral. The factor $e^{-x}$ is always positive, so the sign of $F'$ is that of the polynomial $(x - 1)(x - 3)$, positive outside $[1, 3]$ and negative inside. By the criterion of the **sign of the derivative and monotonicity**, $F$ is increasing on $(-\infty, 1]$ and on $[3, +\infty)$ and decreasing on $[1, 3]$. Consequently $x = 1$, where $F$ goes from increasing to decreasing, is a relative maximum point, and $x = 3$ is a relative minimum point.

As a check, an antiderivative of the integrand is $-(t - 1)^2\,e^{-t}$, as one verifies by differentiating; therefore $F(x) = 1 - (x - 1)^2\,e^{-x}$, with $F(1) = 1$ and $F(3) = 1 - 4e^{-3} \approx 0.80$.

</div>
