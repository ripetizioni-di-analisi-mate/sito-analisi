## Exercise 1

Determine whether they converge and, if so, compute
$$
\text{(a)} \ \int_1^{+\infty} \frac{1}{x^3}\,dx,
\qquad
\text{(b)} \ \int_1^{+\infty} \frac{1}{\sqrt{x}}\,dx .
$$

$$
\text{(a)} \quad \int_1^{t} x^{-3}\,dx = \left[ -\frac{1}{2x^2} \right]_1^{t} = \frac{1}{2} - \frac{1}{2t^2} \to \frac{1}{2} \quad (t \to +\infty)
$$
$$
\text{(b)} \quad \int_1^{t} x^{-\frac{1}{2}}\,dx = \Big[ 2\sqrt{x} \Big]_1^{t} = 2\sqrt{t} - 2 \to +\infty \quad (t \to +\infty)
$$

::grafico{f="1/x^3+0*sqrt(x);1/sqrt(x)" area="1/x^3;0;1;9" segmenti="1,0,1,1" xmin=-1 xmax=9 ymin=-1 ymax=2 legenda="y = \dfrac{1}{x^3};y = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{x}}" titolo="For $x \geq 1$ the region under $y = \dfrac{1}{x^3}$ has area $\dfrac{1}{2}$; under $y = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{x}}$, which tends to zero more slowly, the area is infinite"}

<div class="spiegazione">

By the definition of **improper integral**, we compute the integral on $[1, t]$ and then the limit for $t \to +\infty$. In the first case $\dfrac{1}{2t^2} \to 0$, so the integral **converges** and equals $\dfrac{1}{2}$. In the second case $2\sqrt{t} \to +\infty$, so the integral **diverges**. The results are those predicted by the theorem on the improper integral of powers; indeed with $a = 3 > 1$ the integral converges to $\dfrac{1}{a - 1} = \dfrac{1}{2}$, with $a = \dfrac{1}{2} \leq 1$ it diverges.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Compute
$$
\text{(a)} \ \int_0^{+\infty} e^{-3x}\,dx,
\qquad
\text{(b)} \ \int_2^{+\infty} e^{-\frac{x}{2}}\,dx .
$$

$$
\text{(a)} \quad \int_0^{t} e^{-3x}\,dx = \left[ -\frac{e^{-3x}}{3} \right]_0^{t} = \frac{1 - e^{-3t}}{3} \to \frac{1}{3}
$$
$$
\text{(b)} \quad \int_2^{t} e^{-\frac{x}{2}}\,dx = \Big[ -2\,e^{-\frac{x}{2}} \Big]_2^{t} = 2\,e^{-1} - 2\,e^{-\frac{t}{2}} \to \frac{2}{e}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The first integral is the one in the theorem on the **improper integral of the exponential** with $k = 3 > 0$, and it equals $\dfrac{1}{3}$; the computation confirms it, because $e^{-3t} \to 0$. In the second integral the lower bound is $2$, so the result of the theorem does not apply directly and the computation is repeated. An antiderivative of $e^{-\frac{x}{2}}$ is $-2\,e^{-\frac{x}{2}}$, and at $x = 2$ it equals $-2\,e^{-1}$. Since $e^{-\frac{t}{2}} \to 0$, the integral **converges** to $\dfrac{2}{e} \approx 0.74$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Compute
$$
\int_0^{+\infty} x\,e^{-x}\,dx .
$$

$$
\int x\,e^{-x}\,dx = -x\,e^{-x} + \int e^{-x}\,dx = -(x + 1)\,e^{-x} + c
$$
$$
\int_0^{t} x\,e^{-x}\,dx = \Big[ -(x + 1)\,e^{-x} \Big]_0^{t} = 1 - \frac{t + 1}{e^{t}}
$$
$$
\lim_{t \to +\infty} \frac{t + 1}{e^{t}} = 0
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
\int_0^{+\infty} x\,e^{-x}\,dx = 1
$$

::grafico{f="x*exp(-x)+0*sqrt(x)" area="x*exp(-x);0;0;9" punti="1,0.3679,pieno" xmin=-1 xmax=9 ymin=-0.2 ymax=0.6 legenda="y = x\,e^{-x}" titolo="The unbounded region under $y = x\,e^{-x}$ for $x \geq 0$ has area $1$; the graph has its maximum at $\left( 1, \dfrac{1}{e} \right)$ and tends to $0$"}

<div class="spiegazione">

First we find an antiderivative with **integration by parts**, taking $x$ as finite factor and $e^{-x}$ as differential factor, with antiderivative $-e^{-x}$. Then we compute the integral on $[0, t]$; at $x = 0$ the antiderivative equals $-1$, so subtracting it gives $+1$. The limit of $\dfrac{t + 1}{e^{t}}$ is of the form $\dfrac{\infty}{\infty}$, and by the **comparison of infinities** the exponential grows faster than any polynomial, so the quotient tends to $0$. The integral **converges** and equals $1$.

</div>

## Exercise 4

Compute
$$
\int_1^{+\infty} \frac{2}{x\,(x + 2)}\,dx .
$$

$$
\frac{2}{x\,(x + 2)} = \frac{A}{x} + \frac{B}{x + 2}
\iff
2 = A\,(x + 2) + B\,x
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
A = 1,
\quad
B = -1
$$
$$
\int_1^{t} \left( \frac{1}{x} - \frac{1}{x + 2} \right) dx = \Big[ \ln x - \ln(x + 2) \Big]_1^{t} = \ln \frac{t}{t + 2} - \ln \frac{1}{3}
$$
$$
\lim_{t \to +\infty} \ln \frac{t}{t + 2} = \ln 1 = 0
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
\int_1^{+\infty} \frac{2}{x\,(x + 2)}\,dx = \ln 3
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The integrand is rational with a second-degree denominator and roots $0$ and $-2$, so it is decomposed into **partial fractions**; with $x = 0$ we get $2 = 2A$, that is, $A = 1$, and with $x = -2$ we get $2 = -2B$, that is, $B = -1$. On $[1, t]$ we have $x > 0$ and $x + 2 > 0$, therefore the logarithms do not require the absolute value, and their difference is written as the logarithm of the quotient. The two terms $\ln x$ and $\ln(x + 2)$ separately tend to $+\infty$, while the quotient $\dfrac{t}{t + 2}$ tends to $1$, so the logarithm of the quotient tends to $0$. Since $-\ln \dfrac{1}{3} = \ln 3$, the integral **converges** and equals $\ln 3 \approx 1.10$.

</div>
