## Exercise 1

Given the matrices
$$
A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ 0 & 3 \end{pmatrix},
\qquad
B = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 4 \\ -2 & 1 \end{pmatrix},
$$
compute $3A - 2B^{T}$.

$$
B^{T} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -2 \\ 4 & 1 \end{pmatrix}
$$
$$
3A = \begin{pmatrix} 6 & -3 \\ 0 & 9 \end{pmatrix},
\qquad
2B^{T} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -4 \\ 8 & 2 \end{pmatrix}
$$
$$
3A - 2B^{T} = \begin{pmatrix} 6 - 2 & -3 + 4 \\ 0 - 8 & 9 - 2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 1 \\ -8 & 7 \end{pmatrix}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The **transpose** of $B$ is obtained by writing the rows of $B$ as columns, so the first row $(1, 4)$ becomes the first column and the second row $(-2, 1)$ becomes the second column. The product by a scalar multiplies every entry, hence $3A$ has the entries of $A$ multiplied by $3$ and $2B^{T}$ those of $B^{T}$ multiplied by $2$. The two matrices are both $2 \times 2$, so the difference is defined and is computed entry by entry. The result is $3A - 2B^{T} = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 1 \\ -8 & 7 \end{pmatrix}$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Given the matrices
$$
A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 2 \\ -1 & 3 & 1 \end{pmatrix},
\qquad
B = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 4 \end{pmatrix},
$$
compute, if they are defined, the products $AB$ and $BA$.

$$
AB = \begin{pmatrix}
1 \cdot 2 + 0 \cdot 0 + 2 \cdot 1 & 1 \cdot 1 + 0 \cdot (-1) + 2 \cdot 4 \\
-1 \cdot 2 + 3 \cdot 0 + 1 \cdot 1 & -1 \cdot 1 + 3 \cdot (-1) + 1 \cdot 4
\end{pmatrix}
= \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 9 \\ -1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}
$$
$$
BA = \begin{pmatrix}
2 - 1 & 0 + 3 & 4 + 1 \\
0 + 1 & 0 - 3 & 0 - 1 \\
1 - 4 & 0 + 12 & 2 + 4
\end{pmatrix}
= \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 3 & 5 \\ 1 & -3 & -1 \\ -3 & 12 & 6 \end{pmatrix}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The matrix $A$ is $2 \times 3$ and the matrix $B$ is $3 \times 2$. The product $AB$ is defined because the number of columns of $A$, that is $3$, equals the number of rows of $B$, and the result is a $2 \times 2$ matrix. Each entry of $AB$ is obtained with the **row-by-column product**; for example the entry in position $(1, 2)$ is the first row of $A$, $(1, 0, 2)$, times the second column of $B$, $(1, -1, 4)$, that is $1 + 0 + 8 = 9$.

$BA$ is also defined, because $B$ has $2$ columns and $A$ has $2$ rows, but it is a $3 \times 3$ matrix. The entry in position $(3, 2)$, for example, is the third row of $B$, $(1, 4)$, times the second column of $A$, $(0, 3)$, that is $0 + 12 = 12$. The two products have different dimensions, so in this case $AB$ and $BA$ cannot even be compared.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Find all matrices $X$ of order $2$ that commute with
$$
A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix},
$$
that is, such that $AX = XA$.

$$
X = \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix}
$$
$$
AX = \begin{pmatrix} a + 2c & b + 2d \\ c & d \end{pmatrix},
\qquad
XA = \begin{pmatrix} a & 2a + b \\ c & 2c + d \end{pmatrix}
$$
$$
AX = XA
\iff
\begin{cases}
a + 2c = a \\[4pt]
b + 2d = 2a + b \\[4pt]
c = c \\[4pt]
d = 2c + d
\end{cases}
\iff
\begin{cases}
c = 0 \\[4pt]
d = a
\end{cases}
$$
$$
X = \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ 0 & a \end{pmatrix}, \qquad a, b \in \mathbb{R}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

We write the unknown matrix with four entries $a$, $b$, $c$, $d$ and compute the two products with the row-by-column rule. Two matrices are equal when all their corresponding entries are equal, so the equality $AX = XA$ is equivalent to a system of four equations. The first equation gives $2c = 0$, that is, $c = 0$; the second gives $2d = 2a$, that is, $d = a$; the third is always true and the fourth again gives $c = 0$. The entries $a$ and $b$ remain free, hence the matrices that commute with $A$ are **exactly** those of the form $\begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ 0 & a \end{pmatrix}$. For example $I_2$ and $A$ itself are of this form, whereas $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ is not, and indeed it does not commute with $A$.

</div>
