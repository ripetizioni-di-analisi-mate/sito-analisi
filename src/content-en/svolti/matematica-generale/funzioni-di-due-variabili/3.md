## Exercise 1

Compute the partial derivatives of $f(x, y) = 3x^2 y - 2xy^3 + 5x$ and the gradient at the point $(1, -1)$.

$$
f_x(x, y) = 6xy - 2y^3 + 5,
\qquad
f_y(x, y) = 3x^2 - 6xy^2
$$
$$
f_x(1, -1) = -6 + 2 + 5 = 1,
\qquad
f_y(1, -1) = 3 - 6 = -3
$$
$$
\nabla f(1, -1) = (1, -3)
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

In computing $f_x$ the variable $y$ is treated as a **constant**. The term $3x^2 y$ is the constant $3y$ times $x^2$, so its derivative is $6xy$; the term $-2xy^3$ is the constant $-2y^3$ times $x$, so its derivative is $-2y^3$; the term $5x$ has derivative $5$. In computing $f_y$ it is $x$ that is treated as a constant. The term $3x^2 y$ has derivative $3x^2$, the term $-2xy^3$ has derivative $-6xy^2$, and the term $5x$, which does not contain $y$, has derivative $0$.

Then one substitutes $(x, y) = (1, -1)$, taking into account that $(-1)^3 = -1$ and $(-1)^2 = 1$. The **gradient** is the vector of the two partial derivatives, $(1, -3)$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Compute the partial derivatives of $f(x, y) = x\,e^{2y} + \ln(x + y^2)$ and the gradient at the point $(1, 0)$.

$$
f_x(x, y) = e^{2y} + \frac{1}{x + y^2},
\qquad
f_y(x, y) = 2x\,e^{2y} + \frac{2y}{x + y^2}
$$
$$
f_x(1, 0) = 1 + 1 = 2,
\qquad
f_y(1, 0) = 2 + 0 = 2
$$
$$
\nabla f(1, 0) = (2, 2)
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The function is defined where $x + y^2 > 0$, and the point $(1, 0)$ satisfies the condition, because $1 > 0$. With respect to $x$, the term $x\,e^{2y}$ is $x$ multiplied by the constant $e^{2y}$, so its derivative is $e^{2y}$. The logarithm is differentiated with the **rule for composite functions**, according to which the derivative of $\ln u$ is $\dfrac{u'}{u}$, and the derivative of $u = x + y^2$ with respect to $x$ is $1$.

With respect to $y$, the factor $x$ is constant and the derivative of $e^{2y}$ is $2e^{2y}$, so the first term gives $2x\,e^{2y}$; the derivative of $x + y^2$ with respect to $y$ is $2y$, so the second gives $\dfrac{2y}{x + y^2}$. At the point $(1, 0)$ we have $e^{0} = 1$ and $x + y^2 = 1$, and the gradient is $(2, 2)$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Compute the partial derivatives of $f(x, y) = \dfrac{x - y}{x^2 + y^2}$ and the gradient at the point $(1, 1)$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
f_x(x, y)
&= \frac{1 \cdot (x^2 + y^2) - (x - y) \cdot 2x}{(x^2 + y^2)^2} \\[6pt]
&= \frac{-x^2 + 2xy + y^2}{(x^2 + y^2)^2}
\end{aligned}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
f_y(x, y)
&= \frac{(-1) \cdot (x^2 + y^2) - (x - y) \cdot 2y}{(x^2 + y^2)^2} \\[6pt]
&= \frac{-x^2 - 2xy + y^2}{(x^2 + y^2)^2}
\end{aligned}
$$
$$
f_x(1, 1) = \frac{-1 + 2 + 1}{4} = \frac{1}{2},
\qquad
f_y(1, 1) = \frac{-1 - 2 + 1}{4} = -\frac{1}{2}
$$
$$
\nabla f(1, 1) = \left( \frac{1}{2}, -\frac{1}{2} \right)
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The function is defined outside the origin, where the denominator vanishes. It is a quotient, so one applies the **quotient rule**, once with respect to $x$ and once with respect to $y$. With respect to $x$ the derivative of the numerator $x - y$ is $1$ and that of the denominator $x^2 + y^2$ is $2x$. Expanding the numerator gives $x^2 + y^2 - 2x^2 + 2xy = -x^2 + 2xy + y^2$.

With respect to $y$ the derivative of the numerator is $-1$ and that of the denominator is $2y$. The numerator becomes $-x^2 - y^2 - 2xy + 2y^2 = -x^2 - 2xy + y^2$. At the point $(1, 1)$ the denominator equals $(1 + 1)^2 = 4$, and one obtains the gradient $\left( \dfrac{1}{2}, -\dfrac{1}{2} \right)$.

</div>

## Exercise 4

Compute the partial derivatives of $f(x, y) = x^{y}$, defined for $x > 0$, and the gradient at the point $(2, 3)$.

$$
f_x(x, y) = y\,x^{y - 1},
\qquad
f_y(x, y) = x^{y} \ln x
$$
$$
f_x(2, 3) = 3 \cdot 2^{2} = 12,
\qquad
f_y(2, 3) = 2^{3} \ln 2 = 8 \ln 2
$$
$$
\nabla f(2, 3) = (12, \ 8 \ln 2)
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The two partial derivatives require two different formulas, because the variable appears once in the base and once in the exponent. In computing $f_x$ the exponent $y$ is a constant, so $x^{y}$ is a **power** and its derivative is $y\,x^{y - 1}$. In computing $f_y$ the base $x$ is a positive constant, so $x^{y}$ is an **exponential** with base $x$ and its derivative is $x^{y} \ln x$, according to the table of derivatives of elementary functions.

At the point $(2, 3)$ one obtains $f_x = 3 \cdot 4 = 12$ and $f_y = 8 \ln 2 \approx 5.55$. Swapping the two formulas is a frequent error, and it is avoided by establishing, before differentiating, which of the two letters is held fixed.

</div>
