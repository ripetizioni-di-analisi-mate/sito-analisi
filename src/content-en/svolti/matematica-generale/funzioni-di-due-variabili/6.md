## Exercise 1

Let $f(x, y) = x^2 y - 3y$ and the curve $x(t) = t^2$, $y(t) = 2t$. Compute the derivative of $g(t) = f(x(t), y(t))$ with the formula for the derivative along a curve, check the result by direct substitution, and compute $g'(1)$.

$$
f_x = 2xy,
\qquad
f_y = x^2 - 3,
\qquad
x'(t) = 2t,
\qquad
y'(t) = 2
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
g'(t)
&= f_x(x(t), y(t))\,x'(t) + f_y(x(t), y(t))\,y'(t) \\[6pt]
&= 2 \cdot t^2 \cdot 2t \cdot 2t + (t^4 - 3) \cdot 2 \\[6pt]
&= 8t^4 + 2t^4 - 6 = 10t^4 - 6
\end{aligned}
$$
$$
g(t) = t^4 \cdot 2t - 3 \cdot 2t = 2t^5 - 6t,
\qquad
g'(t) = 10t^4 - 6
$$
$$
g'(1) = 4
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The function $f$ is a polynomial, so it is differentiable on the whole plane, and the functions $x(t)$ and $y(t)$ have a derivative, so the hypotheses of the theorem on the **derivative along a curve** are satisfied. The formula requires the partial derivatives of $f$ evaluated at the point of the curve, that is, with $x = t^2$ and $y = 2t$, and the derivatives of the two functions of $t$. The term $f_x\,x'(t)$ equals $2 \cdot t^2 \cdot 2t$ times $2t$, that is, $8t^4$; the term $f_y\,y'(t)$ equals $(t^4 - 3) \cdot 2$.

As a check, one substitutes $x = t^2$ and $y = 2t$ directly into the formula of $f$, obtaining a function of $t$ alone, which is differentiated with the rules for functions of one variable. The two procedures give the same derivative $10t^4 - 6$, and for $t = 1$, that is, at the point $(1, 2)$ of the curve, the derivative of $f$ along the curve equals $4$, so $f$ increases along the curve.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Let $f(x, y) = x^2 + y^2$ and the circle $x(t) = 2\cos t$, $y(t) = 2\sin t$, with $t \in [0, 2\pi]$. Compute the derivative of $f$ along the circle and explain the result using the gradient.

$$
f_x = 2x,
\qquad
f_y = 2y,
\qquad
x'(t) = -2\sin t,
\qquad
y'(t) = 2\cos t
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
g'(t)
&= 2 \cdot 2\cos t \cdot (-2\sin t) + 2 \cdot 2\sin t \cdot 2\cos t \\[6pt]
&= -8\sin t \cos t + 8\sin t \cos t = 0
\end{aligned}
$$
$$
t = 0 : \qquad \nabla f(2, 0) = (4, 0),
\qquad
\big( x'(0), y'(0) \big) = (0, 2)
$$

::grafico{curve="2*cos(t),2*sin(t),0,6.29" vettori="2,0,6,0,∇f;2,0,2,2,velocity" punti="2,0,pieno" xmin=-3 xmax=7 ymin=-3 ymax=3 uguale=1 legenda="x^2 + y^2 = 4" titolo="At the point $(2, 0)$ the gradient $(4, 0)$ of $f(x, y) = x^2 + y^2$ is perpendicular to the velocity vector $(0, 2)$, tangent to the circle"}

<div class="spiegazione">

One applies the formula for the **derivative along a curve**, with the partial derivatives evaluated at $x = 2\cos t$ and $y = 2\sin t$ and the derivatives $(\cos t)' = -\sin t$ and $(\sin t)' = \cos t$. The two terms are opposite, so the derivative is **zero** for every $t$.

The result has a direct explanation. Along the circle $x^2 + y^2 = 4$, so $g(t) = 4$ is constant and its derivative is zero; the circle is the level curve $4$ of $f$. By the theorem on **gradient and level curves**, at every point the gradient is perpendicular to the velocity vector. At the point $(2, 0)$, which corresponds to $t = 0$, the gradient $(4, 0)$ is horizontal and the velocity $(0, 2)$ is vertical, and their dot product is $4 \cdot 0 + 0 \cdot 2 = 0$, as the figure shows.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Let $f(x, y) = xy$ and the segment $x(t) = t$, $y(t) = 4 - t$, with $t \in [0, 4]$. Compute the derivative of $f$ along the segment and find the point of the segment where $f$ is maximum.

$$
f_x = y,
\qquad
f_y = x,
\qquad
x'(t) = 1,
\qquad
y'(t) = -1
$$
$$
g'(t) = (4 - t) \cdot 1 + t \cdot (-1) = 4 - 2t
$$
$$
g'(t) > 0 \iff t < 2,
\qquad
g'(t) < 0 \iff t > 2
$$
$$
t = 2 : \qquad (x, y) = (2, 2),
\qquad
f(2, 2) = 4
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The partial derivatives of $f$ evaluated on the segment are $f_x = y = 4 - t$ and $f_y = x = t$, and the velocity is $(1, -1)$. By the formula for the **derivative along a curve** one obtains $g'(t) = 4 - 2t$, which can be checked by differentiating $g(t) = t\,(4 - t) = 4t - t^2$.

The derivative is positive for $t < 2$ and negative for $t > 2$, so, by the test on the **sign of the derivative and monotonicity**, $g$ increases on $[0, 2]$ and decreases on $[2, 4]$. The maximum of $f$ on the segment is therefore at the point corresponding to $t = 2$, that is, $(2, 2)$, and it equals $4$. At that point $\nabla f(2, 2) = (2, 2)$ is perpendicular to the direction $(1, -1)$ of the segment, because $2 - 2 = 0$. It is the first example of a constrained maximum, the type of problem solved with Lagrange multipliers in the topic on unconstrained and constrained extrema.

</div>
