## Exercise 1

Determine which of the following functions are homogeneous and, if so, of what degree:
$$
f(x, y) = x^3 + 2x^2 y - y^3,
\qquad
g(x, y) = \frac{xy}{x + y} \ \ (x > 0, \ y > 0),
\qquad
h(x, y) = x^2 + y .
$$

$$
f(tx, ty) = t^3 x^3 + 2t^3 x^2 y - t^3 y^3 = t^3 f(x, y)
$$
$$
g(tx, ty) = \frac{t^2 xy}{t\,(x + y)} = t\,g(x, y)
$$
$$
h(t, t) = t^2 + t,
\qquad
t^{r} h(1, 1) = 2\,t^{r}
$$
$$
t = 1 : \ 2 = 2,
\qquad
t = 2 : \ 6 = 2 \cdot 2^{r} \iff r = \log_2 3,
\qquad
t = 4 : \ 20 \neq 2 \cdot 4^{\log_2 3} = 18
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

To check **homogeneity**, one substitutes $tx$ and $ty$ for $x$ and $y$, with $t > 0$, and tries to factor out a power of $t$. In $f$ every term has total degree $3$ in the two variables, so $t^3$ can be factored out of every term, and $f$ is homogeneous of degree $3$. In $g$ the numerator carries the factor $t^2$ and the denominator the factor $t$, so the quotient carries the factor $t$, and $g$ is homogeneous of degree $1$.

The function $h$ has one term of degree $2$ and one of degree $1$, and it is not homogeneous. To prove this it suffices to find a point where the equality $h(tx, ty) = t^{r} h(x, y)$ fails for every $r$. At the point $(1, 1)$, with $t = 2$, the equality requires $r = \log_2 3$, and with this value, for $t = 4$, one should have $h(4, 4) = 2 \cdot 4^{\log_2 3} = 2 \cdot 3^{2} = 18$, whereas $h(4, 4) = 20$. Hence $h$ **is not homogeneous**.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Let $Q(K, L) = 6\,K^{a} L^{\frac{1}{2}}$, with $a > 0$. Determine for which values of $a$ the function has increasing, constant or decreasing returns to scale. For $a = \dfrac{1}{4}$, compute the number by which production is multiplied when capital and labor double.

$$
Q(tK, tL) = 6\,t^{a} K^{a}\,t^{\frac{1}{2}} L^{\frac{1}{2}} = t^{a + \frac{1}{2}}\,Q(K, L)
$$
$$
r = a + \frac{1}{2}
$$

- $a > \dfrac{1}{2}$: $r > 1$, increasing returns;
- $a = \dfrac{1}{2}$: $r = 1$, constant returns;
- $0 < a < \dfrac{1}{2}$: $r < 1$, decreasing returns.

$$
a = \frac{1}{4} :
\qquad
r = \frac{3}{4},
\qquad
Q(2K, 2L) = 2^{\frac{3}{4}}\,Q(K, L) \approx 1.68\,Q(K, L)
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The function is a **Cobb-Douglas**, so it is homogeneous of degree equal to the sum of the exponents, $r = a + \dfrac{1}{2}$, as the computation with $tK$ and $tL$ confirms. The **returns to scale** depend on the comparison of $r$ with $1$, and the condition on $r$ translates into a condition on $a$ by subtracting $\dfrac{1}{2}$.

For $a = \dfrac{1}{4}$ the degree is $\dfrac{3}{4} < 1$, so the returns are decreasing. Doubling the factors, that is, with $t = 2$, production is multiplied by $2^{\frac{3}{4}} \approx 1.68$, a number less than $2$; production therefore grows less than in proportion to the factors.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Verify Euler's theorem for the function $f(x, y) = x^2 y + 3xy^2$.

$$
f(tx, ty) = t^3 x^2 y + 3\,t^3 x y^2 = t^3 f(x, y),
\qquad
r = 3
$$
$$
f_x = 2xy + 3y^2,
\qquad
f_y = x^2 + 6xy
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
x\,f_x + y\,f_y
&= 2x^2 y + 3xy^2 + x^2 y + 6xy^2 \\[6pt]
&= 3x^2 y + 9xy^2 \\[6pt]
&= 3\,(x^2 y + 3xy^2) = 3\,f(x, y)
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Both terms of $f$ have degree $3$, so $f$ is **homogeneous of degree** $3$. The polynomial is differentiable on the whole plane, so **Euler's theorem** applies and predicts $x\,f_x + y\,f_y = 3f$. One computes the partial derivatives, multiplies $f_x$ by $x$ and $f_y$ by $y$, and adds like terms. Factoring out $3$ gives back exactly $3f(x, y)$, as predicted.

</div>

## Exercise 4

A firm has production function $Q(K, L) = 4\,K^{\frac{1}{2}} L^{\frac{1}{2}}$ and employs $K = 9$ units of capital and $L = 16$ units of labor. Verify that, if each factor is paid its marginal productivity, the payments exhaust the product.

$$
Q(9, 16) = 4 \cdot 3 \cdot 4 = 48
$$
$$
Q_K = 2\,K^{-\frac{1}{2}} L^{\frac{1}{2}},
\qquad
Q_K(9, 16) = 2 \cdot \frac{4}{3} = \frac{8}{3}
$$
$$
Q_L = 2\,K^{\frac{1}{2}} L^{-\frac{1}{2}},
\qquad
Q_L(9, 16) = 2 \cdot \frac{3}{4} = \frac{3}{2}
$$
$$
K\,Q_K + L\,Q_L = 9 \cdot \frac{8}{3} + 16 \cdot \frac{3}{2} = 24 + 24 = 48 = Q(9, 16)
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The function is a Cobb-Douglas with exponents $\dfrac{1}{2} + \dfrac{1}{2} = 1$, so it is homogeneous of degree $1$ and has **constant returns**. The marginal productivities are the partial derivatives; at $(9, 16)$ one uses $\sqrt{9} = 3$ and $\sqrt{16} = 4$.

If each unit of capital receives the marginal productivity $Q_K$ and each unit of labor the marginal productivity $Q_L$, measured in units of product, the total payments are $K\,Q_K = 24$ and $L\,Q_L = 24$. Their sum is $48$, equal to the production. This is **Euler's theorem** with $r = 1$, which ensures the equality $K\,Q_K + L\,Q_L = Q$ at every point $(K, L)$.

</div>
