## Exercise 1

A firm has cost $C(q) = 0.5\,q^2 + 10q + 800$ and sells each unit at $70$ euros. Compute the marginal cost for $q = 30$ and compare it with the actual cost of the thirty-first unit. Find the marginal profit and the quantity at which it vanishes.

$$
C'(q) = q + 10,
\qquad
C'(30) = 40
$$
$$
C(31) - C(30) = 0.5\,(961 - 900) + 10 = 40.5
$$
$$
\pi'(q) = R'(q) - C'(q) = 70 - (q + 10) = 60 - q,
\qquad
\pi'(q) = 0
\iff
q = 60
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The **marginal cost** is the derivative of the cost, and at $q = 30$ it equals $40$ euros, so producing one more unit costs about $40$ euros. The actual cost of the thirty-first unit is $40.50$ euros; the difference of $0.50$ euros is due to the quadratic term, because in going from $30$ to $31$ the marginal cost itself grows. The revenue is $70q$, with constant marginal revenue $70$. The **marginal profit** is positive for $q < 60$, where one more unit brings in more revenue than cost, and negative for $q > 60$. At the quantity $q = 60$ the marginal revenue equals the marginal cost; there the profit is maximum, as will be verified with curve sketching.

</div>

## Exercise 2

The inverse demand for a good is $p = 120 - 2q$. Compute the marginal revenue for $q = 20$, compare it with the price and find the quantity at which the marginal revenue vanishes.

$$
R(q) = (120 - 2q)\,q = 120q - 2q^2,
\qquad
R'(q) = 120 - 4q
$$
$$
R'(20) = 40,
\qquad
p(20) = 80
$$
$$
R'(q) = 0
\iff
q = 30
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The revenue is the price times the quantity, and the **marginal revenue** is its derivative. At $q = 20$ the marginal revenue equals $40$ euros, half the selling price of $80$ euros. To sell one more unit the price must fall by $2$ euros, and the discount applies to all $20$ units; hence the additional unit brings in about $80$ euros, but $40$ are lost on the others. The marginal revenue vanishes for $q = 30$ and is negative for $q > 30$, so beyond that quantity selling more lowers total revenue.

</div>

## Exercise 3

The production cost is $C(q) = 0.02\,q^2 + 8q + 200$. Find the quantity at which average cost and marginal cost are equal, and determine where the average cost increases and where it decreases.

$$
\bar{C}(q) = 0.02\,q + 8 + \frac{200}{q},
\qquad
C'(q) = 0.04\,q + 8
$$
$$
C'(q) = \bar{C}(q)
\iff
0.02\,q = \frac{200}{q}
\iff
q^2 = 10\,000
\iff
q = 100
$$
$$
\bar{C}(100) = C'(100) = 12
$$
$$
\bar{C}'(q) = \frac{1}{q}\big( C'(q) - \bar{C}(q) \big) = \frac{1}{q} \left( 0.02\,q - \frac{200}{q} \right) = \frac{0.02\,(q^2 - 10\,000)}{q^2}
$$

::grafico{f="0.02*x+8+200/x+0*sqrt(x);0.04*x+8+0*sqrt(x)" punti="100,12,pieno" segmenti="100,0,100,12" xmin=-20 xmax=180 ymin=-4 ymax=36 nomi="q,€" legenda="\bar{C}(q) = 0.02\,q + 8 + \dfrac{200}{q};C'(q) = 0.04\,q + 8" titolo="The graph of the marginal cost intersects that of the average cost at the minimum point of the average cost, $(100, 12)$"}

<div class="spiegazione">

The average cost is obtained by dividing each term of the cost by $q$. Setting average cost equal to marginal cost, the terms $8$ cancel, and what remains is $0.02\,q = \dfrac{200}{q}$, with the positive solution $q = 100$, where both equal $12$ euros. By the relation between **marginal cost and average cost**, the derivative of the average cost has the sign of $C'(q) - \bar{C}(q)$, that is, of $q^2 - 10\,000$. Therefore the average cost **decreases** for $0 < q < 100$, when each further unit costs less than the average, and **increases** for $q > 100$. The quantity $q = 100$ is the one of minimum average cost.

</div>
