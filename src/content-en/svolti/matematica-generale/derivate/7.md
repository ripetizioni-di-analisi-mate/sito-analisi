## Exercise 1

Compute
$$
\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\ln(1 + x) - x}{x^2} .
$$

$$
\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\ln(1 + x) - x}{x^2}
= \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\dfrac{1}{1 + x} - 1}{2x}
= \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{-\dfrac{x}{1 + x}}{2x}
= \lim_{x \to 0} \left( -\frac{1}{2\,(1 + x)} \right)
= -\frac{1}{2}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

As $x \to 0$ the numerator and the denominator tend to $0$, so **de l'Hôpital's rule** can be applied, differentiating the numerator and the denominator separately. After differentiation the form is still $\dfrac{0}{0}$, but bringing to a common denominator one has $\dfrac{1}{1 + x} - 1 = -\dfrac{x}{1 + x}$, and the factor $x$ cancels with the $2x$ in the denominator. The limit equals $-\dfrac{1}{2}$. From the negative sign it follows that, near $0$, $\ln(1 + x)$ is slightly less than $x$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Compute
$$
\lim_{x \to +\infty} \frac{x^3}{e^{x}} .
$$

$$
\lim_{x \to +\infty} \frac{x^3}{e^{x}}
= \lim_{x \to +\infty} \frac{3x^2}{e^{x}}
= \lim_{x \to +\infty} \frac{6x}{e^{x}}
= \lim_{x \to +\infty} \frac{6}{e^{x}}
= 0
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The form is $\dfrac{\infty}{\infty}$. At each application of **de l'Hôpital's rule** the exponent of the power in the numerator decreases by one, while the denominator remains $e^{x}$. After three applications the numerator is the constant $6$, and the quotient tends to $0$ because the denominator diverges. At each step the form was still $\dfrac{\infty}{\infty}$, and the rule could be applied again. The result confirms the comparison of infinities, according to which the exponential grows faster than any power.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Compute
$$
\lim_{x \to 0^+} x \ln x
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
\lim_{x \to 1} \frac{x^4 - 1}{\ln x} .
$$

$$
x \ln x = \frac{\ln x}{\dfrac{1}{x}},
\qquad
\lim_{x \to 0^+} \frac{\ln x}{\dfrac{1}{x}} = \lim_{x \to 0^+} \frac{\dfrac{1}{x}}{-\dfrac{1}{x^2}} = \lim_{x \to 0^+} (-x) = 0
$$
$$
\lim_{x \to 1} \frac{x^4 - 1}{\ln x} = \lim_{x \to 1} \frac{4x^3}{\dfrac{1}{x}} = \lim_{x \to 1} 4x^4 = 4
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The first limit is of the form $0 \cdot \infty$, to which the rule does not apply directly. The product is rewritten as a quotient, dividing by the reciprocal of $x$, and the form $\dfrac{\infty}{\infty}$ is obtained. After differentiation the quotient simplifies to $-x$, which tends to $0$. In the second limit numerator and denominator vanish at $1$, so the form is $\dfrac{0}{0}$ and **de l'Hôpital's rule** applies immediately; the quotient of the derivatives is continuous at $1$ and the limit equals $4$.

</div>
