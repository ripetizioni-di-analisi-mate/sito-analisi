## Exercise 1

A liability has a duration of $8$ years and a present value of $900\,\mathrm{EUR}$. Two available zero-coupon bonds mature at times $2$ and $6$. Solve the value and duration equations and determine whether the solution satisfies a long-only constraint.

$$
\begin{cases}
X_1+X_2=900,\\
2X_1+6X_2=8\cdot900=7200,
\end{cases}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
w_1&=\frac{6-8}{6-2}=-\frac12,\qquad
w_2=\frac{8-2}{6-2}=\frac32,\\[6pt]
X_2&=900\cdot\frac32=1350\,\mathrm{EUR},\\[6pt]
X_1&=900-1350=-450\,\mathrm{EUR},\\[6pt]
2(-450)+6\cdot1350&=-900+8100=7200.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The two equations have a unique solution because the maturities $2$ and $6$ are distinct, giving a nonzero determinant $6-2$. The two-bond portfolio formulas $w_1=\dfrac{T_2-D_L}{T_2-T_1}$ and $w_2=\dfrac{D_L-T_1}{T_2-T_1}$ give the weights.

The required duration exceeds that of the longer bond, so its algebraic weight $w_2=\dfrac{3}{2}$ is greater than one. The weights sum to $1$, giving the shorter bond a weight of $-\dfrac{1}{2}$. The final line checks that the investments satisfy the second equation.

The negative holding in the shorter bond would require a short sale worth $450\,\mathrm{EUR}$. The proceeds would be invested in the longer bond along with the $900\,\mathrm{EUR}$ already available.

With purchases alone the weights must be nonnegative. Portfolio duration, a nonnegative weighted average of maturities, must therefore lie between $2$ and $6$ years. The required duration of $8$ years falls outside that interval, so the hedge is **infeasible** under the constraint.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A portfolio has net present values at dates $1$, $2$ and $3$ of $600$, $-1200$ and $600\,\mathrm{EUR}$, respectively. The discount factor for date $2$ rises by $3\%$ in relative terms and all other factors remain unchanged. Calculate the surplus after the change. By how much would the initial present value of receipts at date $2$ have to increase to eliminate sensitivity to that date's discount factor?

$$
\begin{aligned}
S_0&=600-1200+600=0,\\[6pt]
S'_0&=-1\cdot600+2\cdot1200-3\cdot600\\[6pt]
    &=-600+2400-1800=0,\\[6pt]
S_{\mathrm{new}}&=600-1200\cdot1.03+600\\[6pt]
  &=1200-1236=-36\,\mathrm{EUR},\\[6pt]
X_2&=1200\,\mathrm{EUR}.
\end{aligned}
$$

They are positive at times $1$ and $3$ (upward) and negative at time $2$ (downward)."}

<div class="spiegazione">

The initial surplus is zero because the net values sum to zero. The derivative for a common shift in the force of interest is the sum of the net values times $-t$, which also vanishes. The portfolio therefore has zero surplus and zero first-order sensitivity to parallel shifts.

The given shock changes only the value at date $2$. Its new value is the old value times $1.03$, giving $-1236\,\mathrm{EUR}$. The net cash flow at that maturity is a liability, and its magnitude increases by $3\%$, so the surplus falls by $1200\cdot0.03=36\,\mathrm{EUR}$.

A relative increase of $r$ in the discount factor for date $2$ changes the surplus by $r$ times the net value at that node. Sensitivity to the discount factor for date $2$ alone therefore vanishes when the net cash flow there is zero.

To offset the liability, the initial present value of receipts at the same date must rise by $1200\,\mathrm{EUR}$. This brings the net value at date $2$ to zero and achieves **cash flow matching** on that date.

</div>
