## Exercise 1

A zero-coupon bond costs $184\,\mathrm{EUR}$ and pays $200\,\mathrm{EUR}$ in three years. Calculate the three-year discount factor and the price today of a payment of $50\,\mathrm{EUR}$ on the same date.

$$
\begin{aligned}
v(0,3)&=\frac{P}{N}=\frac{184}{200}=0.92,\\[6pt]
P_{50}&=50\cdot v(0,3)\\[6pt]
&=50\cdot0.92=46\,\mathrm{EUR}.
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\frac{50}{200}=\frac{1}{4},\qquad \frac{184}{4}=46\,\mathrm{EUR}.
$$

The buyer pays $184$ today and receives $200$ at time $3$."}

<div class="spiegazione">

The **discount factor** $v(0,3)$ is the price today of one euro payable with certainty at time $3$. The zero-coupon bond has just two cash flows, an outlay of $184\,\mathrm{EUR}$ today and a redemption payment of $200\,\mathrm{EUR}$ in three years. The initial price buys the entire payment of $200\,\mathrm{EUR}$. Dividing by $200$ gives the cost of one future euro, which is $0.92\,\mathrm{EUR}$. The factor is less than $1$ because the redemption payment exceeds the purchase price.

Both payments are equally certain and fall on the same date, so the same factor applies to the second payment. The price of $50\,\mathrm{EUR}$ payable in three years is therefore $50$ times $0.92$. As a check, $50\,\mathrm{EUR}$ is one quarter of the original redemption payment, and one quarter of $184\,\mathrm{EUR}$ is $46\,\mathrm{EUR}$.

The three-year discount factor is $0.92$, and a payment of $50\,\mathrm{EUR}$ in three years costs $46\,\mathrm{EUR}$ today.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A one-year zero-coupon bond with face value $100\,\mathrm{EUR}$ costs $95\,\mathrm{EUR}$. A two-year bond with face value $200\,\mathrm{EUR}$ costs $192\,\mathrm{EUR}$. Calculate the unit discount factors and the forward rate from year one to year two.

$$
\begin{aligned}
v(0,1)&=\frac{95}{100}=0.95,\\[6pt]
v(0,2)&=\frac{192}{200}=0.96,
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
1+f_{1,2}&=\left(\frac{v(0,1)}{v(0,2)}\right)^{\frac{1}{2-1}}=\frac{0.95}{0.96}=\frac{95}{96},\\[6pt]
f_{1,2}&=\frac{95}{96}-1=-\frac{1}{96}\approx-1.0417\%.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Divide each price by its redemption payment to find the cost of one euro at each maturity. This gives $v(0,1)=0.95$ and $v(0,2)=0.96$.

The **implied forward rate** follows from $1+f_{s,t}=\left(\dfrac{v(0,s)}{v(0,t)}\right)^{\frac{1}{t-s}}$, which applies because both factors are positive. Here $s=1$ and $t=2$, so the interval is one year and the exponent is $1$.

The two-year factor exceeds the one-year factor. Dividing the factor at the start of the loan by the factor at its end gives a ratio below one. Each euro lent at the end of year one is therefore repaid with less than one euro at the end of year two, specifically $\dfrac{95}{96}\approx0.9896\,\mathrm{EUR}$. The identity $\dfrac{95}{96}\cdot0.96=0.95$ confirms the sign by equating the present values of the loan and the repayment.

The forward rate from year one to year two is therefore **negative**, approximately $-1.04\%$. Both discount factors can still be positive. If the model allowed one euro to be held from year one to year two at no cost, this curve would create an arbitrage opportunity.

</div>
