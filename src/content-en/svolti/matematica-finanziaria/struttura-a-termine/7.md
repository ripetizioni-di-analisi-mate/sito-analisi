## Exercise 1

A bond pays $40\,\mathrm{EUR}$ in year one and $80\,\mathrm{EUR}$ in year two. It costs $112\,\mathrm{EUR}$. Unit zero-coupon bonds maturing on those dates cost $0.95$ and $0.9\,\mathrm{EUR}$. Short selling is allowed at no cost. Construct an arbitrage and calculate the initial proceeds.

$$
\begin{aligned}
P_{\mathrm{rep}}&=40\cdot0.95+80\cdot0.9\\[6pt]
&=38+72=110\,\mathrm{EUR},\\[6pt]
I_0&=112-110=2\,\mathrm{EUR},\\[6pt]
F_1&=40-40=0,\\[6pt]
F_2&=80-80=0.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The bond makes two certain payments, so unit zero-coupon bonds with the same maturities can replicate it. Buy $40$ one-year unit zero-coupon bonds and $80$ two-year unit zero-coupon bonds. Their cost is the bond's **price on the discount curve**, $38+72=110\,\mathrm{EUR}$, below the market price of $112\,\mathrm{EUR}$.

Since the portfolio costs less than the bond, sell the bond short and use part of the proceeds to buy the portfolio. The sale brings in $112\,\mathrm{EUR}$ and the portfolio costs $110$, leaving $2\,\mathrm{EUR}$ at time zero.

At each maturity the zero-coupon bonds pay exactly what the short seller owes. In year one the portfolio pays $40\,\mathrm{EUR}$ and the seller owes $40$. In year two the portfolio pays $80\,\mathrm{EUR}$ and the seller owes $80$. The strategy yields cash today and has zero net cash flow on both future dates.

All the conditions for an **arbitrage** hold. The initial cost is $-2\,\mathrm{EUR}$, which is negative. The future cash flows at times $1$ and $2$ are both zero and hence nonnegative. The initial proceeds give the strictly positive gain. The arbitrage brings in $2\,\mathrm{EUR}$ today with no net future obligation.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Two bonds each pay $100\,\mathrm{EUR}$ in year one. Bond $A$ can be sold for $95$ or bought for $96\,\mathrm{EUR}$. Bond $B$ can be sold for $97$ or bought for $98\,\mathrm{EUR}$. Each order incurs a fee of $0.20\,\mathrm{EUR}$. Short selling is allowed. Find today's gain from buying $A$ and selling $B$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
I_{\mathrm{sale}}&=97-0.20=96.80\,\mathrm{EUR},\\[6pt]
C_{\mathrm{purchase}}&=96+0.20=96.20\,\mathrm{EUR},\\[6pt]
I_0&=96.80-96.20=0.60\,\mathrm{EUR},\\[6pt]
F_1&=100-100=0.
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{buy }B,\ \text{sell }A:\quad
&(95-0.20)-(98+0.20)\\[6pt]
&=94.80-98.20=-3.40\,\mathrm{EUR}.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The two securities have the same cash flow. By the **law of one price**, an arbitrage is possible only if the net sale proceeds on one exceed the total purchase cost of the other. Selling bond $B$ gives its bid price, $97\,\mathrm{EUR}$. The fee reduces the proceeds to $96.80\,\mathrm{EUR}$.

Buying $A$ costs its ask price plus the second fee, bringing the total cost to $96.20\,\mathrm{EUR}$. The net initial proceeds are therefore $0.60\,\mathrm{EUR}$. Before fees the proceeds would be $1\,\mathrm{EUR}$, and the two fees reduce them by $0.40$.

In one year the redemption payments occur on the same date and have the same amount, so they offset without further trades. The redemption payment on $A$ exactly covers the $100\,\mathrm{EUR}$ owed to the buyer of $B$.

The reverse strategy, buying $B$ and selling $A$, requires an outlay of $3.40\,\mathrm{EUR}$ and produces zero future net cash flows, so it makes a loss. Buying $A$ and selling $B$ yields $0.60\,\mathrm{EUR}$ today and constitutes an arbitrage.

</div>
