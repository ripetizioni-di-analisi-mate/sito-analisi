## Exercise 1

A bond is worth $1000\,\mathrm{EUR}$, with modified duration $4$ and convexity $30$. Estimate its price to second order for a yield increase of two percentage points and a decrease of the same size.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta y_{\pm}&=\pm0.02,\\[6pt]
D_{\mathrm{mod}}\,|\Delta y|&=4\cdot0.02=0.08,\\[6pt]
\frac{\mathcal C}{2}(\Delta y)^2&=15\cdot0.0004=0.006,\\[6pt]
P_{+}&\approx1000\left(1-4\cdot0.02+15\cdot0.02^2\right)
=926\,\mathrm{EUR},\\[6pt]
P_{-}&\approx1000\left(1+4\cdot0.02+15\cdot0.02^2\right)
=1086\,\mathrm{EUR}.
\end{aligned}
$$

**The estimated price is 926 euros for the rise and 1,086 euros for the fall in yield.**

<div class="spiegazione">

Apply the **duration–convexity approximation**, which gives a relative price change of $-D_{\mathrm{mod}}\Delta y+\dfrac{\mathcal C}{2}(\Delta y)^2$. The coefficient $15$ is half the convexity $30$. The linear term is $0.08$ of the price, or $80\,\mathrm{EUR}$ in absolute terms, and reverses sign when the shift reverses. The convexity term adds $0.006\cdot1000=6\,\mathrm{EUR}$ in both cases because the shift is squared. The estimated loss when the yield rises is therefore $74\,\mathrm{EUR}$, and the estimated gain when it falls is $86\,\mathrm{EUR}$. The difference $86-74=12\,\mathrm{EUR}$ is twice the convexity adjustment, so the asymmetry is entirely due to curvature.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A zero-coupon bond pays $110.25\,\mathrm{EUR}$ in two years and its effective annual yield rises from $5\%$ to $7\%$. Calculate both Taylor estimates and compare them with the exact price.

$$
\begin{aligned}
P_0&=\frac{110.25}{1.05^2}=\frac{110.25}{1.1025}=100,\\[6pt]
D_{\mathrm{mod}}&=\frac{2}{1.05},\qquad
\mathcal C=\frac{6}{1.05^2},\\[6pt]
P_1&=100\left(1-\frac{2\cdot0.02}{1.05}\right)
\approx100-3.8095=96.1905,\\[6pt]
P_2&=P_1+\frac{300\cdot0.02^2}{1.05^2}
\approx96.1905+0.1088=96.2993,\\[6pt]
P_{\mathrm{exact}}&=\frac{110.25}{1.07^2}
\approx96.2966,\\[6pt]
P_{\mathrm{exact}}-P_1&\approx0.1061,\qquad P_2-P_{\mathrm{exact}}\approx0.0027.
\end{aligned}
$$

**The second-order estimate is closer to the exact price.**

At $7\%$ the three curves nearly coincide. Farther from $5\%$ the straight line moves away from the exact price."}

<div class="spiegazione">

The initial price is $100\,\mathrm{EUR}$ because $1.05^2=1.1025$. For a two-year zero-coupon bond, use $D_{\mathrm{mod}}=\dfrac{T}{1+y}$ and $\mathcal C=\dfrac{T(T+1)}{(1+y)^2}$ with $T=2$, evaluated at $5\%$. The shift is $\Delta y=0.02$. The coefficient $300$ in the quadratic term is $100\cdot\dfrac{6}{2}$, the price times half the numerator of convexity, giving a term of approximately $0.1088\,\mathrm{EUR}$. The exact price discounts the same payment at $7\%$. The linear estimate differs from the exact price by about $0.1061\,\mathrm{EUR}$ and the second-order estimate by about $0.0027\,\mathrm{EUR}$. In the second comparison the prices agree to the nearest cent, so more digits are needed to measure the error. The linear estimate is below the exact price because the price curve is convex and lies above its tangent.

</div>
