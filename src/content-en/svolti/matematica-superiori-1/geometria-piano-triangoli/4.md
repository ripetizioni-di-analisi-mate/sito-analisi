## Exercise 1

An isosceles triangle has an exterior angle of $127^\circ$ at its base. Find the interior angles.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\beta&=180^\circ-127^\circ=53^\circ,\\[6pt]
\gamma&=\beta=53^\circ,\\[6pt]
\alpha&=180^\circ-2\cdot53^\circ=74^\circ.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The phrase at the base identifies the interior angle adjacent to the given exterior angle. Its supplement of $53^\circ$ is therefore one of the two congruent base angles. Subtracting $53^\circ$ twice from the interior angle sum gives the vertex angle. To check without using the sum of all three angles, add the two interior angles that are not adjacent to the given exterior angle. Their sum $74^\circ+53^\circ=127^\circ$ matches the original value.

The exterior angle at the other end of the base is also $127^\circ$, since it supplements an equal interior angle.

</div>

## Exercise 2

In $ABC$, $AB=AC$. Point $M$ is the midpoint of $BC$. Prove that $AM$ bisects the angle at $A$ and is also an altitude.

$$
\begin{aligned}
AB&=AC,\quad BM=CM,\quad AM=AM,\\[6pt]
ABM&\cong ACM,\\[6pt]
\widehat{BAM}&=\widehat{CAM},\\[6pt]
\widehat{BMA}&=\widehat{CMA}=90^\circ.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The two triangles have equal legs by hypothesis, equal parts of the base by the midpoint definition, and the shared side $AM$. SSS justifies congruence. The corresponding angles at $A$ are equal, so $AM$ is an angle bisector. The angles at $M$ are congruent and form a linear pair on the base. They add to a straight angle, so each is right. The perpendicularity shows that $AM$ is also an altitude.

Equality of the legs enters the congruence criterion and gives the segment's properties in addition to being a median.

</div>

## Exercise 3

In $ABC$, $\widehat B=\widehat C$. Given $AC=7$ cm and $BC=10$ cm, find the perimeter and identify the side opposite the angle at $A$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
AB&=AC=7\,\mathrm{cm},\\[6pt]
P&=7+7+10=24\,\mathrm{cm}.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The two equal angles are opposite sides $AC$ and $AB$. The converse of the isosceles triangle theorem makes those sides congruent, so the length $7$ cm is counted twice in the perimeter. Side $BC$ lies opposite the angle at $A$, because it joins the two vertices other than $A$. The base of length $10$ cm is shorter than the sum $7+7=14$ cm, so the triangle can be constructed.

The perimeter therefore comes from just one leg length and the base, without computing the angle measures.

</div>
