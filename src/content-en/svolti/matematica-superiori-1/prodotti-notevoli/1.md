## Exercise 1

Expand $(x+3)^2$ and identify where the linear term comes from.

$$
\begin{aligned}
(x+3)^2&=(x+3)(x+3)\\[6pt]
&=x^2+3x+3x+9\\[6pt]
&=x^2+6x+9 .
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The term $3x$ appears twice, because you can choose $x$ from the first factor and $3$ from the second or reverse those choices. If $x>0$, the two choices correspond to the two $x$ by $3$ rectangles in the figure. The smaller square has area $9$. The distributive argument also holds when $x$ is zero or negative. At $x=-3$ the expansion gives $9-18+9=0$.

The four areas let you reconstruct the expansion from the figure by including each region's contribution exactly once.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Expand $(2x-5)^2$ and check the result where the binomial is zero.

$$
\begin{aligned}
(2x-5)^2&=(2x)^2+2\cdot(2x)\cdot(-5)+(-5)^2\\[6pt]
&=4x^2-20x+25,\\[6pt]
4\cdot\left(\dfrac52\right)^2-20\cdot\dfrac52+25&=25-50+25=0 .
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

In both factors the term with $x$ is $2x$. Squaring it gives coefficient $4$, while doubling its product with $-5$ gives $-20x$. The constant is positive because it comes from $(-5)(-5)$. At $x=\dfrac52$ the binomial is zero, so the expansion must also be zero. A mixed term with the wrong sign would give $25+50+25$, a value inconsistent with the square of zero.

If the coefficient of the monomial is lost when squaring, the second-degree terms will also fail to match.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Compare $(-3a+2b)^2$ and $(3a-2b)^2$. Give one expansion valid for both.

$$
\begin{aligned}
(-3a+2b)^2&=\bigl(-(3a-2b)\bigr)^2=(3a-2b)^2\\[6pt]
&=9a^2-12ab+4b^2 .
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The binomials in brackets are opposites, so multiplying either by itself gives the same result. Twice the product is $2\cdot(3a)\cdot(-2b)=-12ab$. Reversing both signs still gives $2\cdot(-3a)\cdot(2b)=-12ab$. At $a=b=1$ the binomials are $-1$ and $1$, and the expansion gives $9-12+4=1$. With a positive sign in front of $12ab$, the value would rise to $25$.

The same symmetry lets you choose the sign of the first monomial when recognizing a square in an expanded polynomial.

</div>
