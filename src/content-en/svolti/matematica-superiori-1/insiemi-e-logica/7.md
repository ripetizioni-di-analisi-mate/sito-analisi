## Exercise 1

Let $D=\{-3;-2;-1;0;1;2;3\}$. Consider the property $P(x)$ given by $x^2\ge4$. Decide whether it holds for at least one element and whether it holds for every element of $D$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
x^2&\ge4\ \Longleftrightarrow\ |x|\ge2,\\[5pt]
\{x\in D\mid P(x)\}&=\{-3;-2;2;3\},\\[5pt]
(-2)^2&=4\ge4,\qquad 1^2=1<4.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Calculating the squares identifies four elements that satisfy the property. A complete list is optional for the existential statement because one **witness** suffices. The value $-2$ works even though it gives equality. The universal statement would require the property to hold throughout $D$. The number $1$ belongs to $D$ and its square is less than $4$, so it is a **counterexample**. The existential statement is true and the universal statement is false.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Negate “For every $x\in D$, $2x+1<7$,” where $D=\{0;1;2;3\}$. Decide which statement is true.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\neg\bigl(\forall x\in D,\ 2x+1<7\bigr)
&\iff\exists x\in D,\ 2x+1\ge7,\\[5pt]
x=3&\ \Longrightarrow\ 2x+1=7.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Negating the universal statement requires **at least one value** in the domain that fails the inequality. The failure of $2x+1<7$ includes both values above $7$ and the value equal to $7$. Here $3$ produces exactly $7$, so the negation is true and the original statement is false. The choice of $D$ matters. If we remove $3$, all remaining values satisfy the strict inequality, and the universal statement becomes true.

</div>

## Exercise 3

With $A=\{2;3;5\}$ and $B=\{6;10;15\}$, compare $\forall a\in A,\ \exists b\in B,\ a\mid b$ and $\exists b\in B,\ \forall a\in A,\ a\mid b$. The symbol $a\mid b$ means that $a$ divides $b$.

| $a$ | Multiples of $a$ in $B$ |
|---|---|
| $2$ | $6$, $10$ |
| $3$ | $6$, $15$ |
| $5$ | $10$, $15$ |

<div class="spiegazione">

In the first statement we **choose $a$ first**. Every row offers two choices for $b$, so for every $a$ there is at least one multiple in $B$. The second statement requires choosing one $b$ before testing all values of $a$. To check this, look for a number appearing in all three rows. The third row lacks $6$, the second lacks $10$, and the first lacks $15$. Even though each pair of rows shares a number, the second statement is false.

</div>
