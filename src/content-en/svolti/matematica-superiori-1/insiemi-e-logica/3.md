## Exercise 1

Of $32$ students, $18$ join the science club, $14$ join the computing club, and $7$ join both. How many join exactly one club? How many join neither?

$$
\begin{aligned}
|S\setminus I|&=18-7=11,& |I\setminus S|&=14-7=7,\\[5pt]
|(S\setminus I)\cup(I\setminus S)|&=11+7=18,\\[5pt]
|U\setminus(S\cup I)|&=32-(11+7+7)=7.
\end{aligned}
$$

::venn{tipo="due" nomi="S,I" zone="a,b" universo=1 elementi="a:11;ab:7;b:7;u:7" titolo="Each number is a region count"}

<div class="spiegazione">

Put the overlap count, $7$, in the middle first. The $18$ science members already include these students, leaving $11$ in science only. The same step leaves $7$ in computing only. The two side regions are disjoint and represent **exactly one**. To count the students in neither club, subtract all three regions inside the circles, including the overlap. The final check is $11+7+7+7=32$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Among $40$ summer camp participants, $23$ choose swimming and $19$ choose drama. Six choose neither activity. How many choose both?

$$
\begin{aligned}
|N\cup T|&=40-6=34,\\[5pt]
|N\cap T|&=|N|+|T|-|N\cup T|=23+19-34=8,\\[5pt]
|N\setminus T|&=15,& |T\setminus N|&=11.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The word “neither” identifies the region outside the union. Subtracting that count from the total gives the number who choose at least one activity. The sum $23+19=42$ exceeds it by $8$ because each person choosing both activities is counted in each total. The excess is therefore the **size of the intersection**. As a check, swimming only, drama only, both, and neither add up to $15+11+8+6=40$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

In a group of $26$ people, $17$ know one game and $15$ know another. Find the minimum and maximum numbers who know both games. Show that both values are possible.

$$
\begin{aligned}
|A\cup B|&=32-|A\cap B|\le26 \ \Longrightarrow\ |A\cap B|\ge6,\\[5pt]
|A\cap B|&\le 15.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

At least $6$ people must lie in the overlap because there are $26$ people overall and the two counts add up to $32$. If the overlap has $6$ people, the two side regions have $11$ and $9$ and no one is left outside. At most, the overlap can contain all $15$ people of the smaller group. Then only $2$ are exclusively in $A$, none are exclusively in $B$, and $9$ are outside both. These two distributions show that **both bounds are attained**.

</div>
