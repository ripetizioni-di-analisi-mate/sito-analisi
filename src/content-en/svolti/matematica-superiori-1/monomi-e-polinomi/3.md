## Exercise 1

Square the monomial $-8x^6y$.

$$
(-8x^6y)^2=64x^{12}y^2
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The square of a product is the product of the squares of its factors. The negative coefficient becomes positive. The exponents in the variable part double.

The result is $64 x^{12} y^{2}$.

The positive sign comes from squaring the coefficient $-8$. The variable factor $x^6$ is squared, so its new exponent is $6\cdot2$ and the factor $y$ becomes $y^2$. For $x=1$ and $y=-1$, the original monomial evaluates to $8$ and its square to $64$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

For $x\ne0$ and $y\ne0$, simplify $\dfrac{27x^9y^3}{9x^2y}$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{27}{9}&=3, \\[6pt] x^{9-2}&=x^7, \\[6pt] y^{3-1}&=y^2
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The stated conditions make the denominator nonzero. Divide the coefficients and subtract the exponents of matching variables. The original conditions still apply after simplification.

The result is $3 x^{7} y^{2}$.

The identity $x^9=x^2x^7$ shows which power remains after cancellation. Likewise, $y^3=yy^2$. Multiplying $3x^7y^2$ by the denominator $9x^2y$ recovers the numerator $27x^9y^3$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Simplify $(\dfrac{3 x^{2}}{2} + 10 x)-(x^{2} + x)$.

$$
\left(\frac32-1\right)x^2+(10-1)x=\frac{x^{2}}{2} + 9 x
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Subtract the coefficients of the quadratic terms and those of the linear terms. Subtracting one from three halves requires a common denominator. The two variable parts remain separate.

The result is $\dfrac{x^{2}}{2} + 9 x$.

The coefficient $1$ of the second quadratic term can be written as $\dfrac22$. The coefficient of the quadratic term in the difference is therefore $\dfrac{3-2}2$. The linear term $9x$ comes separately from the subtraction $10x-x$.

</div>
