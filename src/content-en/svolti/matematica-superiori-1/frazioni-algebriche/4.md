## Exercise 1

For $x\ne 8$ and $x\ne -8$, calculate $\dfrac1{x-8}+\dfrac1{x+8}$.

$$
\frac{x+8+x-8}{(x-8)(x+8)}=\frac{2x}{x^2-64}
$$

::grafico{f="2*x/(x^2-64)" xmin=-12 xmax=12 ymin=-2.5 ymax=2.5 punti="0,0,pieno" asintoti="x=-8;x=8;y=0" titolo="The sum has one zero and two exclusions"}

<div class="spiegazione">

The first fraction is missing the factor $x+8$. The second is missing $x-8$. The new numerators add to $2x$. At $x=0$, the original fractions are opposites and the result is zero too.

At $x=0$ the fractions have opposite denominators and equal numerators, so their values cancel each other. The resulting formula shows this zero through its factor $2x$. The two exclusions remain visible in the denominator and separate the branches of the graph.

</div>

## Exercise 2

For $x\ne 9$ and $x\ne -9$, calculate $\dfrac1{x-9}-\dfrac1{x+9}$.

$$
\frac{(x+9)-(x-9)}{x^2-81}=\frac{18}{x^2-81}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

For the subtraction, the numerators become $x+9$ and $x-9$. Subtract the whole second numerator to get $x+9-x+9=18$. For $-9<x<9$ the denominator $x^2-81$ is negative. The difference is therefore negative throughout that interval.

For example, at $x=0$ the first fraction equals $-\dfrac19$ and the second equals $\dfrac19$. Their difference is $-\dfrac29$, the same value obtained from $\dfrac{18}{-81}$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

For $x\ne10$, rewrite $1+\dfrac{10}{x-10}$ as a single fraction.

$$
1+\frac{10}{x-10}=\frac{x-10+10}{x-10}=\frac x{x-10}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The number $1$ contributes $x-10$ to the combined numerator. Adding $10$ leaves $x$. The new fraction has exactly one zero, $x=0$, where the denominator equals $-10$.

The original calculation explains that same zero through cancellation. At $x=0$ the second term equals $-1$, which exactly cancels the first. At $x=10$ the second term is undefined, so the whole sum is undefined as well.

</div>
