## Exercise 1

A parallelogram has congruent perpendicular diagonals. What is the most specific classification guaranteed by this information?

$$
\begin{gathered} AC=BD \\ AC\perp BD \end{gathered}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The quadrilateral is already known to be a parallelogram. Congruent diagonals therefore make it a rectangle, while perpendicular diagonals make it a rhombus. Both conditions hold, so the parallelogram is a square.

</div>

## Exercise 2

The diagonals of convex quadrilateral $ABCD$ meet at $O$. Suppose $AO=OC$ and $BO=OD$. Prove that $AB\parallel CD$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\widehat{AOB}&=\widehat{COD} \\[6pt]
\triangle AOB&\cong\triangle COD \\[6pt]
\widehat{BAO}&=\widehat{DCO} \\[6pt]
AB&\parallel CD
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The angles at $O$ are vertical angles. Together with the two equalities of lengths, this allows SAS for triangles $AOB$ and $COD$. The corresponding angles $\widehat{BAO}$ and $\widehat{DCO}$ are alternate interior angles with transversal $AC$. Their equality gives parallelism by the converse theorem.

</div>

## Exercise 3

An isosceles trapezoid has bases of $12\,\mathrm{cm}$ and $7\,\mathrm{cm}$. Its diagonals are congruent. Can it be classified as a rectangle?

$$
12\,\mathrm{cm}\ne7\,\mathrm{cm}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

In a rectangle, opposite sides have equal lengths. Here the bases are opposite sides with different lengths, so the trapezoid is not a rectangle. The criterion that uses congruent diagonals to establish that a quadrilateral is a rectangle requires a parallelogram, which is not given.

</div>
