## Exercise 1

A $2$-litre jug contains $\dfrac56$ of a litre of water. You add $\dfrac23$ of a litre. How much water is in the jug, and how much more can it hold?

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac56+\frac23&=\frac{5+4}{6}=\frac32,\\[6pt]
2-\frac32&=\frac12.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The two amounts are fractions of the same unit, the litre. One third of a litre contains two sixths, so the two thirds added are four sixths. The jug holds nine sixths of a litre, or one and a half litres. To find the free space, subtract the water from the two-litre capacity, leaving half a litre. The jug is therefore three quarters full, because $\dfrac32:2=\dfrac34$. The two volumes are $\dfrac32$ and $\dfrac12$ litres, and the fraction of the jug's capacity occupied is $\dfrac34$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A ribbon is $\dfrac{11}{4}\,\mathrm m$ long and is cut into pieces of $\dfrac38\,\mathrm m$. How many complete pieces can you cut? How much ribbon is left?

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{11}{4}:\frac38&=\frac{22}{3}=7+\frac13,\\[6pt]
\frac{11}{4}-7\cdot\frac38&=\frac{22-21}{8}=\frac18.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The quotient counts lengths of three eighths of a metre. Its whole part $7$ gives the number of complete pieces. The third after $7$ is one third of a piece, equivalent to $\dfrac13\cdot\dfrac38=\dfrac18$ of a metre. We can also check by counting in eighths of a metre. There are twenty-two eighths available, seven pieces use twenty-one, and one remains. Eight pieces would take twenty-four eighths, so there is enough ribbon for seven pieces but too little for eight.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A tank is $\dfrac57$ full. After removing $\dfrac25$ of the water in the tank, you add $\dfrac17$ of the tank’s capacity. What fraction of its capacity is now occupied?

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac25\cdot\frac57&=\frac27,\\[6pt]
\frac57-\frac27+\frac17&=\frac47.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The five sevenths at the start can be pictured as five one-seventh parts, shaded in the first row. Two fifths of the water in the tank are two of those five parts. They therefore account for two sevenths of the tank, leaving three sevenths after the removal. Adding one seventh takes the level to four sevenths. One check works in sevenths: two are removed and one is restored, so the final level must be one seventh lower than the initial one. The diagram compares the five sevenths initially present with the four left at the end.

</div>
