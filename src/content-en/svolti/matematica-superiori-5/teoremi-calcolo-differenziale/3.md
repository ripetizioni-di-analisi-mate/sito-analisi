## Exercise 1

Evaluate $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 2} \frac{x^3 - 8}{x^2 - x - 2}$.

$$
\lim_{x \to 2} \left( x^3 - 8 \right) = 0,
\qquad
\lim_{x \to 2} \left( x^2 - x - 2 \right) = 4 - 2 - 2 = 0
$$
$$
\lim_{x \to 2} \frac{x^3 - 8}{x^2 - x - 2}
= \lim_{x \to 2} \frac{3x^2}{2x - 1}
= \frac{12}{3}
= 4
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

By substitution, both the numerator and denominator tend to $0$, so the limit has the indeterminate form $\dfrac{0}{0}$. The numerator and denominator are polynomials and therefore differentiable, and the derivative of the denominator, $2x - 1$, differs from $0$ near $2$. The hypotheses of **l'Hôpital's rule** hold.

Differentiating the numerator and denominator separately gives $\dfrac{3x^2}{2x - 1}$. This quotient no longer has an indeterminate form, and substitution gives $\dfrac{12}{3} = 4$. The original limit therefore equals $4$. The same result follows by factoring $x^3 - 8 = (x - 2)\left( x^2 + 2x + 4 \right)$ and $x^2 - x - 2 = (x - 2)(x + 1)$ and canceling the factor $x - 2$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Evaluate $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{1 - \cos (2x)}{x^2}$.

$$
\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{1 - \cos (2x)}{x^2}
\qquad
\text{form } \frac{0}{0}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{1 - \cos (2x)}{x^2}
&= \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{2 \sin (2x)}{2x} \\[6pt]
&= \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{4 \cos (2x)}{2} \\[6pt]
&= \frac{4}{2} = 2
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

As $x \to 0$, we have $\cos (2x) \to 1$, so the numerator and denominator tend to $0$ and the limit has the form $\dfrac{0}{0}$. Use the chain rule to differentiate the numerator. The derivative of $\cos (2x)$ is $-\sin (2x) \cdot 2$, so the derivative of $1 - \cos (2x)$ is $2 \sin (2x)$. The derivative of the denominator is $2x$.

The new quotient $\dfrac{2 \sin (2x)}{2x}$ still has the form $\dfrac{0}{0}$, so **l'Hôpital's rule** applies a second time. The derivative of $2 \sin (2x)$ is $4 \cos (2x)$, and the derivative of $2x$ is $2$. The quotient $\dfrac{4 \cos (2x)}{2}$ tends to $2$, so the original limit equals $2$. Alternatively, after the first application one can use the standard limit $\dfrac{\sin t}{t} \to 1$ with $t = 2x$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Evaluate $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to +\infty} \frac{\ln x}{\sqrt{x}}$.

$$
\lim_{x \to +\infty} \frac{\ln x}{\sqrt{x}}
\qquad
\text{form } \frac{\infty}{\infty}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
\lim_{x \to +\infty} \frac{\ln x}{\sqrt{x}}
&= \lim_{x \to +\infty} \frac{\dfrac{1}{x}}{\dfrac{1}{2\sqrt{x}}} \\[6pt]
&= \lim_{x \to +\infty} \frac{2\sqrt{x}}{x} \\[6pt]
&= \lim_{x \to +\infty} \frac{2}{\sqrt{x}} = 0
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The numerator and denominator both tend to $+\infty$, so the limit has the form $\dfrac{\infty}{\infty}$. Both functions are differentiable for $x > 0$, and the derivative of the denominator, $\dfrac{1}{2\sqrt{x}}$, is positive. **L'Hôpital's rule** also holds for $x \to +\infty$, so it applies here.

The quotient of the derivatives is a fraction divided by another fraction. Dividing by $\dfrac{1}{2\sqrt{x}}$ is equivalent to multiplying by $2\sqrt{x}$, which gives $\dfrac{2\sqrt{x}}{x}$. Since $x = \sqrt{x} \cdot \sqrt{x}$, the fraction simplifies to $\dfrac{2}{\sqrt{x}}$, which tends to $0$. The limit is $0$, in agreement with the relative growth rates at infinity for the exponent $\dfrac{1}{2}$.

</div>

## Exercise 4

Evaluate $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{x - \sin x}{x - \tan x}$.

$$
\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{x - \sin x}{x - \tan x}
\qquad
\text{form } \frac{0}{0}
$$
$$
\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{x - \sin x}{x - \tan x}
= \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{1 - \cos x}{1 - \dfrac{1}{\cos^2 x}}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{1 - \cos x}{1 - \dfrac{1}{\cos^2 x}}
&= \frac{(1 - \cos x)\cos^2 x}{\cos^2 x - 1} \\[6pt]
&= \frac{(1 - \cos x)\cos^2 x}{-(1 - \cos x)(1 + \cos x)} \\[6pt]
&= -\frac{\cos^2 x}{1 + \cos x}
\end{aligned}
$$
$$
\lim_{x \to 0} \left( -\frac{\cos^2 x}{1 + \cos x} \right) = -\frac{1}{2}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

As $x \to 0$, we have $\sin x \to 0$ and $\tan x \to 0$, so the numerator and denominator tend to $0$. The derivative of the denominator is $1 - \dfrac{1}{\cos^2 x} = -\tan^2 x$, which differs from $0$ for $x \neq 0$ near $0$. The hypotheses of **l'Hôpital's rule** therefore hold.

The quotient of the derivatives still has the form $\dfrac{0}{0}$. This time, simplify the expression instead of differentiating again. Multiplying the numerator and denominator by $\cos^2 x$ changes the denominator to $\cos^2 x - 1$. This is a difference of squares equal to $-(1 - \cos x)(1 + \cos x)$, and the factor $1 - \cos x$ cancels with the factor in the numerator because it differs from $0$ for $x \neq 0$ near $0$.

The expression $-\dfrac{\cos^2 x}{1 + \cos x}$ is no longer indeterminate and, as $x \to 0$, tends to $-\dfrac{1}{1 + 1} = -\dfrac{1}{2}$. The original limit is $-\dfrac{1}{2}$.

</div>
