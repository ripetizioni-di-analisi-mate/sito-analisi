## Exercise 1

Evaluate $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 0^+} x^{\sin x}$.

$$
x^{\sin x} = e^{\sin x \cdot \ln x}
\qquad
\text{form } 0^{0}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
\lim_{x \to 0^+} \sin x \cdot \ln x
&= \lim_{x \to 0^+} \frac{\ln x}{\dfrac{1}{\sin x}} \\[6pt]
&= \lim_{x \to 0^+} \frac{\dfrac{1}{x}}{-\dfrac{\cos x}{\sin^2 x}} \\[6pt]
&= \lim_{x \to 0^+} \left( -\frac{\sin x}{x} \cdot \frac{\sin x}{\cos x} \right) \\[6pt]
&= -1 \cdot 0 = 0
\end{aligned}
$$
$$
\lim_{x \to 0^+} x^{\sin x} = e^{0} = 1
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

As $x \to 0^+$, the base $x$ and the exponent $\sin x$ both tend to $0$, so the limit has the form $0^{0}$. The base is positive, so the power can be written as an exponential with base $e$ and exponent $\sin x \cdot \ln x$. The exponent has the form $0 \cdot \infty$. Moving $\sin x$ to the denominator writes it as a quotient of the form $\dfrac{\infty}{\infty}$.

The derivative of $\dfrac{1}{\sin x}$ is $-\dfrac{\cos x}{\sin^2 x}$ by the reciprocal rule. The quotient of the derivatives is $\dfrac{1}{x} \cdot \left( -\dfrac{\sin^2 x}{\cos x} \right)$, which can be written as the product of $-\dfrac{\sin x}{x}$, which tends to $-1$ by the standard limit, and $\dfrac{\sin x}{\cos x} = \tan x$, which tends to $0$. Thus the exponent tends to $0$.

The exponential function is continuous, so the original limit is $e^{0} = 1$. The result equals the limit of $x^{x}$ as $x \to 0^+$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Evaluate $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 0} \left( 1 + \sin (3x) \right)^{\frac{1}{x}}$.

$$
\left( 1 + \sin (3x) \right)^{\frac{1}{x}} = e^{\frac{\ln (1 + \sin (3x))}{x}}
\qquad
\text{form } 1^{\infty}
$$
$$
\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\ln \left( 1 + \sin (3x) \right)}{x}
= \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\dfrac{3 \cos (3x)}{1 + \sin (3x)}}{1}
= \frac{3 \cdot 1}{1 + 0}
= 3
$$
$$
\lim_{x \to 0} \left( 1 + \sin (3x) \right)^{\frac{1}{x}} = e^{3}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

As $x \to 0$, the base tends to $1$ and the exponent $\dfrac{1}{x}$ tends to $\infty$, so the limit has the form $1^{\infty}$. Near $0$ the base is positive, so the power can be written as an exponential with base $e$. The exponent is $\dfrac{1}{x} \cdot \ln \left( 1 + \sin (3x) \right)$, that is, the quotient $\dfrac{\ln \left( 1 + \sin (3x) \right)}{x}$ of the form $\dfrac{0}{0}$.

Differentiate the numerator by applying the chain rule twice. The derivative of $\ln t$ is $\dfrac{1}{t}$ and the derivative of $1 + \sin (3x)$ is $3 \cos (3x)$, so the derivative of the numerator is $\dfrac{3 \cos (3x)}{1 + \sin (3x)}$. The derivative of the denominator is $1$. The quotient of the derivatives tends to $3$, and by **l'Hôpital's rule** the exponent also tends to $3$.

By continuity of the exponential function, the original limit is $e^{3}$. The result is *not* $1$ even though the base tends to $1$, because the exponent tends to $\infty$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Evaluate $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to +\infty} \left( x^2 + 1 \right)^{\frac{1}{\ln x}}$.

$$
\left( x^2 + 1 \right)^{\frac{1}{\ln x}} = e^{\frac{\ln (x^2 + 1)}{\ln x}}
\qquad
\text{form } \infty^{0}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
\lim_{x \to +\infty} \frac{\ln \left( x^2 + 1 \right)}{\ln x}
&= \lim_{x \to +\infty} \frac{\dfrac{2x}{x^2 + 1}}{\dfrac{1}{x}} \\[6pt]
&= \lim_{x \to +\infty} \frac{2x^2}{x^2 + 1} = 2
\end{aligned}
$$
$$
\lim_{x \to +\infty} \left( x^2 + 1 \right)^{\frac{1}{\ln x}} = e^{2}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

As $x \to +\infty$, the base $x^2 + 1$ tends to $+\infty$ and the exponent $\dfrac{1}{\ln x}$ tends to $0$, so the limit has the form $\infty^{0}$. Writing the power as an exponential with base $e$ gives the exponent as the quotient $\dfrac{\ln \left( x^2 + 1 \right)}{\ln x}$, of the form $\dfrac{\infty}{\infty}$.

The derivative of $\ln \left( x^2 + 1 \right)$ is $\dfrac{2x}{x^2 + 1}$, and the derivative of $\ln x$ is $\dfrac{1}{x}$. Dividing by $\dfrac{1}{x}$ is equivalent to multiplying by $x$, so the quotient of the derivatives is $\dfrac{2x^2}{x^2 + 1}$. The numerator and denominator are polynomials of the same degree, so the quotient tends to the ratio of the coefficients of $x^2$, namely $2$. By **l'Hôpital's rule**, the exponent tends to $2$, and the original limit is $e^{2}$.

</div>

## Exercise 4

Evaluate $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to +\infty} \left( \frac{x + 1}{x - 1} \right)^{x}$.

$$
\left( \frac{x + 1}{x - 1} \right)^{x} = e^{\,x \ln \frac{x + 1}{x - 1}}
\qquad
\text{form } 1^{\infty}
$$
$$
\left( \ln \frac{x + 1}{x - 1} \right)' = \left( \ln (x + 1) - \ln (x - 1) \right)' = \frac{1}{x + 1} - \frac{1}{x - 1} = -\frac{2}{x^2 - 1}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
\lim_{x \to +\infty} x \ln \frac{x + 1}{x - 1}
&= \lim_{x \to +\infty} \frac{\ln \dfrac{x + 1}{x - 1}}{\dfrac{1}{x}} \\[6pt]
&= \lim_{x \to +\infty} \frac{-\dfrac{2}{x^2 - 1}}{-\dfrac{1}{x^2}} \\[6pt]
&= \lim_{x \to +\infty} \frac{2x^2}{x^2 - 1} = 2
\end{aligned}
$$
$$
\lim_{x \to +\infty} \left( \frac{x + 1}{x - 1} \right)^{x} = e^{2}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

For $x > 1$ the base $\dfrac{x + 1}{x - 1}$ is positive, and as $x \to +\infty$ it tends to $1$ because the numerator and denominator are linear polynomials with the same coefficient of $x$. The exponent tends to $+\infty$, so the limit has the form $1^{\infty}$. We therefore examine the exponent $x \ln \dfrac{x + 1}{x - 1}$, which has the form $\infty \cdot 0$.

To differentiate the logarithm, use the logarithm rule for a quotient to write it as the difference $\ln (x + 1) - \ln (x - 1)$, valid for $x > 1$. Its derivative is the difference $\dfrac{1}{x + 1} - \dfrac{1}{x - 1}$, which, with the common denominator $x^2 - 1$, becomes $\dfrac{(x - 1) - (x + 1)}{x^2 - 1} = -\dfrac{2}{x^2 - 1}$.

Moving $x$ to the denominator as $\dfrac{1}{x}$ turns the exponent into a quotient of the form $\dfrac{0}{0}$. The derivative of $\dfrac{1}{x}$ is $-\dfrac{1}{x^2}$, and the quotient of the derivatives is $\dfrac{2}{x^2 - 1} \cdot x^2 = \dfrac{2x^2}{x^2 - 1}$, which tends to $2$. By **l'Hôpital's rule**, the exponent tends to $2$ and the original limit is $e^{2}$.

</div>
