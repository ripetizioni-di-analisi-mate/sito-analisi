## Exercise 1

Evaluate $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 0^+} x^2 \ln x$.

$$
\lim_{x \to 0^+} x^2 = 0,
\qquad
\lim_{x \to 0^+} \ln x = -\infty
\qquad
\text{form } 0 \cdot \infty
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
\lim_{x \to 0^+} x^2 \ln x
&= \lim_{x \to 0^+} \frac{\ln x}{\dfrac{1}{x^2}} \\[6pt]
&= \lim_{x \to 0^+} \frac{\dfrac{1}{x}}{-\dfrac{2}{x^3}} \\[6pt]
&= \lim_{x \to 0^+} \left( -\frac{x^2}{2} \right) = 0
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The first factor tends to $0$ and the second to $-\infty$, so the limit has the indeterminate form $0 \cdot \infty$. To apply l'Hôpital's rule, write the product as a quotient. Dividing by $\dfrac{1}{x^2}$ is equivalent to multiplying by $x^2$, so $x^2 \ln x = \dfrac{\ln x}{\dfrac{1}{x^2}}$, which has the form $\dfrac{\infty}{\infty}$.

The derivative of $\ln x$ is $\dfrac{1}{x}$, and the derivative of $\dfrac{1}{x^2} = x^{-2}$ is $-2x^{-3} = -\dfrac{2}{x^3}$. The quotient of the derivatives is $\dfrac{1}{x} \cdot \left( -\dfrac{x^3}{2} \right) = -\dfrac{x^2}{2}$, which tends to $0$. By **l'Hôpital's rule**, the original limit also equals $0$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Evaluate $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to +\infty} x \left( e^{\frac{1}{x}} - 1 \right)$.

$$
\lim_{x \to +\infty} \left( e^{\frac{1}{x}} - 1 \right) = e^{0} - 1 = 0
\qquad
\text{form } \infty \cdot 0
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
\lim_{x \to +\infty} x \left( e^{\frac{1}{x}} - 1 \right)
&= \lim_{x \to +\infty} \frac{e^{\frac{1}{x}} - 1}{\dfrac{1}{x}} \\[6pt]
&= \lim_{x \to +\infty} \frac{e^{\frac{1}{x}} \cdot \left( -\dfrac{1}{x^2} \right)}{-\dfrac{1}{x^2}} \\[6pt]
&= \lim_{x \to +\infty} e^{\frac{1}{x}} = 1
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

As $x \to +\infty$, the exponent $\dfrac{1}{x}$ tends to $0$, so $e^{\frac{1}{x}}$ tends to $1$ and the second factor tends to $0$. The first factor tends to $+\infty$, so the limit has the form $\infty \cdot 0$. Rewriting $x$ in the denominator as $\dfrac{1}{x}$ gives a quotient of the form $\dfrac{0}{0}$.

Differentiate the numerator using the chain rule. The derivative of $e^{\frac{1}{x}}$ is $e^{\frac{1}{x}}$ times the derivative of the exponent, which is $-\dfrac{1}{x^2}$. This is also the derivative of the denominator, so the factor $-\dfrac{1}{x^2}$ cancels, leaving $e^{\frac{1}{x}}$, which tends to $1$. By **l'Hôpital's rule**, the limit is $1$. The same result follows from the substitution $t = \dfrac{1}{x}$ and the standard limit $\dfrac{e^{t} - 1}{t} \to 1$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Evaluate $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 0^+} \left( \frac{1}{\sin x} - \frac{1}{x} \right)$.

$$
\lim_{x \to 0^+} \frac{1}{\sin x} = +\infty,
\qquad
\lim_{x \to 0^+} \frac{1}{x} = +\infty
\qquad
\text{form } \infty - \infty
$$
$$
\frac{1}{\sin x} - \frac{1}{x} = \frac{x - \sin x}{x \sin x}
\qquad
\text{form } \frac{0}{0}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
\lim_{x \to 0^+} \frac{x - \sin x}{x \sin x}
&= \lim_{x \to 0^+} \frac{1 - \cos x}{\sin x + x \cos x} \\[6pt]
&= \lim_{x \to 0^+} \frac{\sin x}{2 \cos x - x \sin x} \\[6pt]
&= \frac{0}{2 - 0} = 0
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

As $x \to 0^+$, both $\sin x$ and $x$ approach $0$ through positive values, so the two fractions tend to $+\infty$ and the limit has the form $\infty - \infty$. Writing the difference over the common denominator $x \sin x$ gives a quotient of the form $\dfrac{0}{0}$.

The derivative of the numerator $x - \sin x$ is $1 - \cos x$. The derivative of the denominator $x \sin x$ is found by the product rule and equals $\sin x + x \cos x$. The new quotient still has the form $\dfrac{0}{0}$, so the rule applies again. The derivative of $1 - \cos x$ is $\sin x$, and the derivative of $\sin x + x \cos x$ is $\cos x + \cos x - x \sin x = 2 \cos x - x \sin x$.

The final quotient is no longer indeterminate and tends to $\dfrac{0}{2} = 0$. Applying **l'Hôpital's rule** twice therefore gives $0$ for the original limit. The two fractions thus tend to $+\infty$, and their difference tends to $0$.

</div>

## Exercise 4

Evaluate $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 1} \left( \frac{x}{x - 1} - \frac{1}{\ln x} \right)$.

$$
\frac{x}{x - 1} - \frac{1}{\ln x} = \frac{x \ln x - x + 1}{(x - 1) \ln x}
\qquad
\text{form } \frac{0}{0}
$$
$$
\lim_{x \to 1} \frac{x \ln x - x + 1}{(x - 1) \ln x}
= \lim_{x \to 1} \frac{\ln x + 1 - 1}{\ln x + \dfrac{x - 1}{x}}
= \lim_{x \to 1} \frac{x \ln x}{x \ln x + x - 1}
$$
$$
\lim_{x \to 1} \frac{x \ln x}{x \ln x + x - 1}
= \lim_{x \to 1} \frac{\ln x + 1}{\ln x + 1 + 1}
= \frac{0 + 1}{0 + 2}
= \frac{1}{2}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

As $x \to 1^+$, both fractions tend to $+\infty$ because $x - 1$ and $\ln x$ approach $0$ through positive values. As $x \to 1^-$, both tend to $-\infty$. In either case the limit has the form $\infty - \infty$. With the common denominator $(x - 1) \ln x$, the numerator becomes $x \ln x - (x - 1) = x \ln x - x + 1$, which tends to $0$, as does the denominator.

Apply **l'Hôpital's rule**. The derivative of $x \ln x$ is $\ln x + x \cdot \dfrac{1}{x} = \ln x + 1$ by the product rule, so the derivative of the numerator is $\ln x + 1 - 1 = \ln x$. The derivative of the denominator is $1 \cdot \ln x + (x - 1) \cdot \dfrac{1}{x}$. Multiplying the numerator and denominator by $x$ clears the fractions and gives $\dfrac{x \ln x}{x \ln x + x - 1}$.

This quotient also has the form $\dfrac{0}{0}$, so the rule applies a second time. The derivative of the numerator is $\ln x + 1$, and that of the denominator is $\ln x + 1 + 1$. As $x \to 1$, the quotient approaches $\dfrac{1}{2}$, the value of the original limit from both the right and the left.

</div>
