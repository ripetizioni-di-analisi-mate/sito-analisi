## Exercise 1

Prove that $\arcsin x + \arccos x = \dfrac{\pi}{2}$ for every $x$ in the interval $[-1, 1]$.

$$
f(x) = \arcsin x + \arccos x,
\qquad
-1 \leq x \leq 1
$$
$$
f'(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - x^2}} - \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - x^2}} = 0
\qquad
\text{for } -1 < x < 1
$$
$$
f(0) = 0 + \frac{\pi}{2} = \frac{\pi}{2}
$$
$$
f(1) = \frac{\pi}{2} + 0 = \frac{\pi}{2},
\qquad
f(-1) = -\frac{\pi}{2} + \pi = \frac{\pi}{2}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The derivatives of arcsine and arccosine are negatives of each other on the open interval $(-1, 1)$, as shown in the chapter on derivatives, so their sum has zero derivative at every point of $(-1, 1)$. The set $(-1, 1)$ is an interval, so by the theorem on **functions with zero derivative**, $f$ is constant on $(-1, 1)$.

To find the constant, evaluate $f$ at a convenient point. At $x = 0$ we have $\arcsin 0 = 0$ and $\arccos 0 = \dfrac{\pi}{2}$, so the constant is $\dfrac{\pi}{2}$.

At the endpoints $\pm 1$ the two functions are not differentiable, so the theorem does not apply there and the values must be checked directly. Since $\arcsin 1 = \dfrac{\pi}{2}$, $\arccos 1 = 0$, $\arcsin (-1) = -\dfrac{\pi}{2}$, and $\arccos (-1) = \pi$, the sum equals $\dfrac{\pi}{2}$ at these points as well. The identity therefore holds throughout $[-1, 1]$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Find the function $f$, differentiable on $\mathbb{R}$, such that $f'(x) = 6x^2 - 2$ for every $x$ and such that $f(1) = 3$.

$$
g(x) = 2x^3 - 2x,
\qquad
g'(x) = 6x^2 - 2
$$
$$
f(x) = 2x^3 - 2x + k
$$
$$
f(1) = 2 - 2 + k = k = 3
$$
$$
f(x) = 2x^3 - 2x + 3
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

First find a function whose derivative is $6x^2 - 2$. The derivative of $x^3$ is $3x^2$, so the derivative of $2x^3$ is $6x^2$. The derivative of $-2x$ is $-2$. The function $g(x) = 2x^3 - 2x$ therefore has the required derivative.

The functions $f$ and $g$ have the same derivative on the interval $\mathbb{R}$. By the theorem on **functions with the same derivative**, there is a constant $k$ such that $f(x) = g(x) + k$ for every $x$. The condition $f(1) = 3$ determines the constant because $g(1) = 0$ and therefore $k = 3$. The required function is **unique** and equals $f(x) = 2x^3 - 2x + 3$. Finding a function from its derivative is studied in the chapter on indefinite integrals.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Prove that $e^{x} \geq 1 + x$ for every real $x$ and that equality holds only for $x = 0$.

$$
g(x) = e^{x} - 1 - x,
\qquad
g'(x) = e^{x} - 1
$$
$$
g'(x) > 0
\iff
e^{x} > 1
\iff
x > 0
$$

| | $x < 0$ | $x > 0$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $g'(x)$ | $-$ | $+$ |
| $g$ | decreasing | increasing |

$$
g(0) = 1 - 1 - 0 = 0
$$
$$
g(x) > g(0) = 0 \quad \text{for every } x \neq 0
$$

::grafico{f="exp(x);1+x" punti="0,1,pieno" xmin=-3 xmax=3 ymin=-2 ymax=6 legenda="y = e^{x};y = 1 + x" titolo="The graph of $e^{x}$ lies above the line $y = 1 + x$, its tangent at $(0, 1)$. The two graphs share only this point"}

<div class="spiegazione">

The inequality $e^{x} \geq 1 + x$ is equivalent to $g(x) \geq 0$, where $g$ is the difference between the two sides. The function $g$ is differentiable on $\mathbb{R}$, and its derivative $e^{x} - 1$ is negative for $x < 0$ and positive for $x > 0$ because the exponential function is increasing and equals $1$ at $0$.

By the **monotonicity test**, $g$ is strictly decreasing on $(-\infty, 0]$ and strictly increasing on $[0, +\infty)$. If $x < 0$, then $g(x) > g(0)$ because $g$ decreases. If $x > 0$, then $g(x) > g(0)$ because $g$ increases. In both cases $g(x) > 0$, so $e^{x} > 1 + x$ for every $x \neq 0$, and at $x = 0$ the equality $e^{0} = 1$ holds.

The line $y = 1 + x$ is tangent to the graph of $e^{x}$ at $(0, 1)$ because $\left( e^{x} \right)' = 1$ at $0$. The inequality just proved therefore says that the graph of the exponential function **lies above** this tangent.

</div>

## Exercise 4

Prove that $|\sin b - \sin a| \leq |b - a|$ for every pair of real numbers $a$ and $b$, and deduce that $|\sin x| \leq |x|$ for every $x$.

$$
a < b:
\qquad
\sin b - \sin a = \cos c \cdot (b - a)
\qquad
\text{with } a < c < b
$$
$$
|\sin b - \sin a| = |\cos c| \cdot |b - a| \leq 1 \cdot |b - a| = |b - a|
$$
$$
a = 0, \quad b = x:
\qquad
|\sin x - \sin 0| \leq |x - 0|
\iff
|\sin x| \leq |x|
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

If $a = b$, both sides are zero and the inequality holds. Otherwise we may assume $a < b$ because interchanging $a$ and $b$ leaves both absolute values unchanged. The function $\sin x$ is continuous and differentiable on $\mathbb{R}$, so in particular it is continuous on $[a, b]$ and differentiable on $(a, b)$.

By the **mean value theorem**, there is a point $c$ in $(a, b)$ with $\sin b - \sin a = \cos c \cdot (b - a)$, since the derivative of sine is cosine. The absolute value of a product is the product of the absolute values, and $|\cos c| \leq 1$ for every $c$. It follows that $|\sin b - \sin a| \leq |b - a|$.

Setting $a = 0$ and $b = x$ gives $|\sin x| \leq |x|$ because $\sin 0 = 0$. For example, $\sin (0.5) \approx 0.479$, which is less than $0.5$.

</div>
