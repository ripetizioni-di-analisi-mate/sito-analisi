## Exercise 1

Prove that the equation $x^5 + 3x - 2 = 0$ has exactly one real solution and that it lies between $0$ and $1$.

$$
f(x) = x^5 + 3x - 2
$$
$$
f(0) = -2 < 0,
\qquad
f(1) = 1 + 3 - 2 = 2 > 0
$$
$$
f'(x) = 5x^4 + 3 > 0
\qquad
\text{for every } x
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Existence follows from Bolzano's theorem, discussed in the chapter on continuity. The function $f$ is a polynomial, so it is continuous on the interval $[0, 1]$, and its endpoint values $f(0) = -2$ and $f(1) = 2$ have opposite signs. The theorem therefore guarantees at least one point in $(0, 1)$ where $f$ is zero.

To prove uniqueness, examine the derivative. Since $x^4 \geq 0$, we have $5x^4 + 3 \geq 3 > 0$ for every real $x$. By the **monotonicity test**, $f$ is strictly increasing on $\mathbb{R}$, so it takes each value at most once and, in particular, vanishes at only one point.

Together the two parts show that the equation has **exactly one real solution**, between $0$ and $1$. The same result follows from Rolle's theorem. If $f$ had two zeros $x_1 < x_2$, then Rolle's theorem applied on $[x_1, x_2]$ would give a zero of the derivative in $(x_1, x_2)$, yet $f'(x) > 0$ for every $x$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Prove that the equation $e^{x} = 1 + 2x$ has exactly two real solutions, one equal to $x = 0$ and the other between $1$ and $2$.

$$
g(x) = e^{x} - 1 - 2x,
\qquad
g(0) = 1 - 1 - 0 = 0
$$
$$
g(1) = e - 3 \approx -0.28 < 0,
\qquad
g(2) = e^2 - 5 \approx 2.39 > 0
$$
$$
g'(x) = e^{x} - 2,
\qquad
g'(x) = 0
\iff
x = \ln 2
$$

::grafico{f="exp(x);1+2*x" punti="0,1,pieno;1.2564,3.5129,pieno" xmin=-2 xmax=3 ymin=-2 ymax=8 legenda="y = e^{x};y = 1 + 2x" titolo="The graph of $e^{x}$ and the line $y = 1 + 2x$ intersect at two points, one with horizontal coordinate $0$ and the other with horizontal coordinate between $1$ and $2$"}

<div class="spiegazione">

The solutions of the equation are the zeros of $g(x) = e^{x} - 1 - 2x$, which is continuous and differentiable on $\mathbb{R}$. Substituting $x = 0$ gives the first zero because $e^{0} = 1$. For the second, apply Bolzano's theorem on the interval $[1, 2]$. Since $e \approx 2.72$, we have $g(1) < 0$, and since $e^2 \approx 7.39$, we have $g(2) > 0$. Thus there is a zero of $g$ in $(1, 2)$ distinct from $0$.

It remains to show that there are only two zeros. Suppose $g$ has three zeros $x_1 < x_2 < x_3$. **Rolle's theorem** applies to $g$ on the intervals $[x_1, x_2]$ and $[x_2, x_3]$ because $g$ is continuous and differentiable and takes the value $0$ at both endpoints of each interval. There would therefore be one zero of $g'$ in $(x_1, x_2)$ and another in $(x_2, x_3)$, that is, two distinct zeros of $g'$.

However, the derivative $g'(x) = e^{x} - 2$ is zero only at $x = \ln 2$ because the exponential function is strictly increasing. Thus $g$ cannot have three zeros, and the equation has **exactly two solutions**. Numerically the second solution is $x \approx 1.26$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Consider the function $f(x) = x\,(a - \ln x)$, defined for $x > 0$, where $a$ is a real parameter.

1. Find $a$ so that the tangent to the graph of $f$ at the point with horizontal coordinate $e$ is horizontal.
2. Set $a = 2$ and find the intervals on which $f$ increases or decreases, its maximum, and the zeros of $f$.
3. Evaluate $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 0^+} f(x)$. Let $F$ be the function that equals $f(x)$ for $x > 0$ and $0$ for $x = 0$. Verify that $F$ satisfies the hypotheses of Rolle's theorem on the interval $\left[ 0, e^2 \right]$, and find the point $c$ given by the theorem.
4. Determine whether $F$ is differentiable at $x = 0$ and describe the graph near the origin.

**Part 1**
$$
f'(x) = 1 \cdot (a - \ln x) + x \cdot \left( -\frac{1}{x} \right) = a - \ln x - 1
$$
$$
f'(e) = a - 1 - 1 = 0
\iff
a = 2
$$

**Part 2**
$$
f(x) = x\,(2 - \ln x),
\qquad
f'(x) = 1 - \ln x
$$
$$
f'(x) > 0
\iff
\ln x < 1
\iff
0 < x < e
$$
$$
f(e) = e\,(2 - 1) = e
$$
$$
f(x) = 0
\iff
2 - \ln x = 0
\iff
x = e^2
$$

**Part 3**
$$
\lim_{x \to 0^+} f(x) = \lim_{x \to 0^+} \left( 2x - x \ln x \right) = 0 - 0 = 0
$$
$$
F(0) = 0,
\qquad
F\left( e^2 \right) = e^2 \left( 2 - 2 \right) = 0
$$
$$
F'(c) = 1 - \ln c = 0
\iff
c = e
$$

**Part 4**
$$
\frac{F(h) - F(0)}{h} = \frac{h\,(2 - \ln h)}{h} = 2 - \ln h
\qquad
\text{for } h > 0
$$
$$
F'_+(0) = \lim_{h \to 0^+} (2 - \ln h) = +\infty
$$

::grafico{f="x*(2-log(x))" punti="0,0,pieno;2.7183,2.7183,pieno;7.3891,0,pieno" segmenti="1.7183,2.7183,3.7183,2.7183;2.7183,0,2.7183,2.7183" xmin=-1 xmax=9 ymin=-2 ymax=4 titolo="The graph of $F$ has a vertical tangent at the origin. The function attains its maximum value $e$ at $x = e$, where the tangent is horizontal, and is zero again at $x = e^2$"}

<div class="spiegazione">

**Part 1.** Find the derivative using the product rule. The derivative of $x$ is $1$ and the derivative of $a - \ln x$ is $-\dfrac{1}{x}$, so $f'(x) = a - \ln x - 1$. The tangent at the point with horizontal coordinate $e$ is horizontal if its slope $f'(e)$ is zero. Since $\ln e = 1$, the condition is $a - 2 = 0$, that is, $a = 2$.

**Part 2.** With $a = 2$ the derivative is $1 - \ln x$, which is positive for $\ln x < 1$, that is, for $0 < x < e$, and negative for $x > e$. By the **monotonicity test**, $f$ is increasing on $(0, e]$ and decreasing on $[e, +\infty)$. The function increases up to $e$ and then decreases throughout the rest of its domain, so $f(e) = e$ is the maximum value of $f$. The factor $x$ is positive on the domain, so $f$ vanishes only where $2 - \ln x = 0$, that is, at $x = e^2 \approx 7.39$.

**Part 3.** The limit of $2x$ is $0$, and the limit of $x \ln x$ as $x \to 0^+$ is $0$, as shown for the indeterminate form $0 \cdot \infty$. Thus $f(x)$ tends to $0 = F(0)$, and $F$ is continuous at $0$. At the other points of $\left[ 0, e^2 \right]$ the function $F$ agrees with $f$, which is continuous and differentiable for $x > 0$. Therefore $F$ is continuous on $\left[ 0, e^2 \right]$ and differentiable on $\left( 0, e^2 \right)$, and $F(0) = F\left( e^2 \right) = 0$ also holds. The hypotheses of **Rolle's theorem** hold, and the equation $1 - \ln c = 0$ gives $c = e$, which belongs to $\left( 0, e^2 \right)$. The point $c$ is the maximum point found in part 2, in agreement with Fermat's theorem.

**Part 4.** For $h > 0$ the difference quotient of $F$ at $0$ simplifies to $2 - \ln h$. As $h \to 0^+$, we have $\ln h \to -\infty$, so the difference quotient tends to $+\infty$. The right-hand derivative at $0$ is infinite, and therefore $F$ is *not* differentiable at $0$. The graph thus has a vertical tangent at the origin, namely the $y$-axis.

</div>
