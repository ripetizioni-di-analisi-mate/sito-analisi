## Exercise 1

Verify that the function $f(x) = x^3 - 9x$ satisfies the hypotheses of Rolle's theorem on the interval $[-3, 3]$, and find the points $c$ whose existence the theorem guarantees.

$$
f(-3) = -27 + 27 = 0,
\qquad
f(3) = 27 - 27 = 0
$$
$$
f'(x) = 3x^2 - 9
$$
$$
3x^2 - 9 = 0
\iff
x^2 = 3
\iff
x = -\sqrt{3} \quad \text{or} \quad x = \sqrt{3}
$$
$$
c_1 = -\sqrt{3} \approx -1.73,
\qquad
c_2 = \sqrt{3} \approx 1.73
$$

::grafico{f="x^3-9*x" punti="-3,0,pieno;3,0,pieno;-1.7321,10.3923,pieno;1.7321,-10.3923,pieno" segmenti="-2.7321,10.3923,-0.7321,10.3923;0.7321,-10.3923,2.7321,-10.3923" xmin=-4 xmax=4 ymin=-12 ymax=12 titolo="The function $f(x) = x^3 - 9x$ equals $0$ at $-3$ and at $3$. At $-\sqrt{3}$ and $\sqrt{3}$ the dashed tangent lines are horizontal"}

<div class="spiegazione">

The function is a polynomial, so it is continuous and differentiable throughout $\mathbb{R}$. In particular, it is continuous on $[-3, 3]$ and differentiable on $(-3, 3)$. Its values at the endpoints of the interval agree because $f(-3) = f(3) = 0$. All three hypotheses of **Rolle's theorem** therefore hold, and the theorem guarantees at least one point in $(-3, 3)$ at which the derivative is zero.

To find these points, solve $f'(x) = 0$. Dividing by $3$ gives $x^2 = 3$, whose two solutions are $\pm\sqrt{3}$. Since $\sqrt{3} \approx 1.73$, both belong to $(-3, 3)$, so there are **two** such points, $c_1 = -\sqrt{3}$ and $c_2 = \sqrt{3}$. The theorem guarantees at least one point, and in this case there is more than one.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Verify that the function $f(x) = \sin x + \cos x$ satisfies the hypotheses of Rolle's theorem on the interval $[0, 2\pi]$, and find the points $c$ whose existence the theorem guarantees.

$$
f(0) = 0 + 1 = 1,
\qquad
f(2\pi) = 0 + 1 = 1
$$
$$
f'(x) = \cos x - \sin x
$$
$$
\cos x - \sin x = 0
\iff
\tan x = 1
\qquad
\text{with } \cos x \neq 0
$$
$$
x = \frac{\pi}{4} + k\pi
\qquad
\Longrightarrow
\qquad
c_1 = \frac{\pi}{4},
\quad
c_2 = \frac{5}{4}\pi
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Sine and cosine are continuous and differentiable on $\mathbb{R}$, so their sum is as well. In particular, $f$ is continuous on $[0, 2\pi]$ and differentiable on $(0, 2\pi)$. Moreover, $f(0) = f(2\pi) = 1$ because sine and cosine both have period $2\pi$. The hypotheses of **Rolle's theorem** therefore hold.

In the equation $\cos x = \sin x$, division by $\cos x$ is valid because wherever $\cos x = 0$ we have $\sin x = \pm 1$, and the equality does not hold. This gives $\tan x = 1$, whose solutions are $x = \dfrac{\pi}{4} + k\pi$ with $k$ an integer. The values in the open interval $(0, 2\pi)$ correspond to $k = 0$ and $k = 1$, namely $c_1 = \dfrac{\pi}{4}$ and $c_2 = \dfrac{5}{4}\pi$. The first is a maximum point, where $f$ equals $\sqrt{2}$, and the second is a minimum point, where $f$ equals $-\sqrt{2}$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Find the value of the parameter $k$ for which $f(x) = x^3 + kx$ satisfies the hypotheses of Rolle's theorem on the interval $[0, 2]$. For that value, find the point $c$ given by the theorem.

$$
f(0) = 0,
\qquad
f(2) = 8 + 2k
$$
$$
f(0) = f(2)
\iff
8 + 2k = 0
\iff
k = -4
$$
$$
f(x) = x^3 - 4x,
\qquad
f'(x) = 3x^2 - 4
$$
$$
3x^2 - 4 = 0
\iff
x = \pm \frac{2}{\sqrt{3}} = \pm \frac{2\sqrt{3}}{3}
$$
$$
c = \frac{2\sqrt{3}}{3} \approx 1.15
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

For every value of $k$ the function is a polynomial, so it is continuous on $[0, 2]$ and differentiable on $(0, 2)$. The only hypothesis that depends on $k$ is that the endpoint values agree. Since $f(0) = 0$ and $f(2) = 8 + 2k$, the hypothesis holds *only if* $8 + 2k = 0$, that is, when $k = -4$.

With $k = -4$ the derivative is $3x^2 - 4$, which is zero when $x^2 = \dfrac{4}{3}$, that is, when $x = \pm \dfrac{2}{\sqrt{3}}$. Rationalizing the denominator gives $\pm \dfrac{2\sqrt{3}}{3}$. The negative value does not belong to $(0, 2)$, while the positive value, approximately $1.15$, does. The point given by **Rolle's theorem** is therefore $c = \dfrac{2\sqrt{3}}{3}$.

</div>

## Exercise 4

Determine whether Rolle's theorem applies to the function $f(x) = \sqrt[3]{(x - 1)^2}$ on the interval $[0, 2]$ and whether there is a point in $(0, 2)$ where the derivative is zero.

$$
f(0) = \sqrt[3]{1} = 1,
\qquad
f(2) = \sqrt[3]{1} = 1
$$
$$
f'(x) = \frac{2}{3}\,(x - 1)^{-\frac{1}{3}} = \frac{2}{3\sqrt[3]{x - 1}}
\qquad
\text{for } x \neq 1
$$
$$
\lim_{x \to 1^+} f'(x) = +\infty,
\qquad
\lim_{x \to 1^-} f'(x) = -\infty
$$
$$
f'(x) \neq 0 \quad \text{for every } x \neq 1
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The function is continuous throughout $\mathbb{R}$ because it is the composition of the polynomial $(x - 1)^2$ with the continuous cube root function. The endpoint values agree, since $f(0) = f(2) = 1$. It remains to check differentiability on $(0, 2)$.

For $x \neq 1$, write $f(x) = (x - 1)^{\frac{2}{3}}$ and use the chain rule to find the derivative. The right-hand limit of $f'$ at $1$ is $+\infty$ and the left-hand limit is $-\infty$, so the differentiability test shows that $x = 1$ is a **cusp** and $f$ is not differentiable at $1$. Since $1$ belongs to $(0, 2)$, the differentiability hypothesis fails and **Rolle's theorem does not apply**.

In this case the conclusion of the theorem is false. The numerator of $f'(x)$ is $2$, so $f'(x)$ is never zero on $(0, 2)$. The minimum of $f$, which equals $0$, occurs at the cusp, where the tangent line does not exist.

</div>
