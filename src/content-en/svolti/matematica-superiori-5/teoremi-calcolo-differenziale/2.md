## Exercise 1

Verify that the function $f(x) = x^2 - 3x + 1$ satisfies the hypotheses of the mean value theorem on the interval $[1, 4]$, and find the point $c$ whose existence the theorem guarantees.

$$
f(1) = 1 - 3 + 1 = -1,
\qquad
f(4) = 16 - 12 + 1 = 5
$$
$$
\frac{f(4) - f(1)}{4 - 1} = \frac{5 - (-1)}{3} = 2
$$
$$
f'(x) = 2x - 3
$$
$$
2c - 3 = 2
\iff
c = \frac{5}{2}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The function is a polynomial, so it is continuous on $[1, 4]$ and differentiable on $(1, 4)$, and the hypotheses of the **mean value theorem** hold. The theorem guarantees a point $c$ in $(1, 4)$ where the derivative equals the slope of the line joining the points $(1, -1)$ and $(4, 5)$ on the graph.

The slope is $\dfrac{6}{3} = 2$. The linear equation $f'(c) = 2$ gives $c = \dfrac{5}{2}$, which belongs to $(1, 4)$. The point found is the midpoint of the interval, as it is for every parabola.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Verify that the function $f(x) = \ln x$ satisfies the hypotheses of the mean value theorem on the interval $[1, e]$, find the point $c$ given by the theorem, and write the equation of the tangent line to the graph at the point with horizontal coordinate $c$.

$$
\frac{f(e) - f(1)}{e - 1} = \frac{1 - 0}{e - 1} = \frac{1}{e - 1}
$$
$$
f'(x) = \frac{1}{x}
$$
$$
\frac{1}{c} = \frac{1}{e - 1}
\iff
c = e - 1 \approx 1.72
$$
$$
y - \ln (e - 1) = \frac{1}{e - 1}\,\big( x - (e - 1) \big)
\qquad
\Longrightarrow
\qquad
y = \frac{x}{e - 1} + \ln (e - 1) - 1
$$

::grafico{f="log(x);x/(exp(1)-1)+log(exp(1)-1)-1" punti="1,0,pieno,A;2.7183,1,pieno,B;1.7183,0.5413,pieno" segmenti="1,0,2.7183,1" xmin=-1 xmax=4 ymin=-2 ymax=2 legenda="y = \ln x;y = \dfrac{x}{e - 1} + \ln (e - 1) - 1" titolo="The segment $AB$ through $(1, 0)$ and $(e, 1)$ and the tangent to the graph of $\ln x$ at the point with horizontal coordinate $c = e - 1$ have the same slope $\dfrac{1}{e - 1}$"}

<div class="spiegazione">

The logarithm is continuous and differentiable for $x > 0$, so it is continuous on $[1, e]$ and differentiable on $(1, e)$, and the **mean value theorem** applies. Since $\ln 1 = 0$ and $\ln e = 1$, the line $AB$ joining the endpoints of the graph has slope $\dfrac{1}{e - 1}$.

The equation $\dfrac{1}{c} = \dfrac{1}{e - 1}$ has the solution $c = e - 1$. Since $e \approx 2.72$, we have $c \approx 1.72$, which lies in $(1, e)$.

The tangent line at the point with horizontal coordinate $c$ passes through $\big( e - 1, \ln (e - 1) \big)$ and has slope $f'(c) = \dfrac{1}{e - 1}$. Expanding the product gives $\dfrac{x}{e - 1} - 1$, to which we add $\ln (e - 1)$. The tangent is **parallel** to the line $AB$, as the theorem states.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Consider the function
$$
f(x) =
\begin{cases}
x^2 + a x & \text{if } 0 \leq x \leq 1 \\
b \sqrt{x} & \text{if } 1 < x \leq 9
\end{cases}
$$
Find $a$ and $b$ so that $f$ satisfies the hypotheses of the mean value theorem on the interval $[0, 9]$, and find the point $c$ given by the theorem.

$$
\lim_{x \to 1^-} f(x) = 1 + a,
\qquad
\lim_{x \to 1^+} f(x) = b
$$
$$
f'(x) =
\begin{cases}
2x + a & \text{if } 0 < x < 1 \\
\dfrac{b}{2\sqrt{x}} & \text{if } 1 < x < 9
\end{cases}
\qquad
\lim_{x \to 1^-} f'(x) = 2 + a,
\quad
\lim_{x \to 1^+} f'(x) = \frac{b}{2}
$$
$$
\begin{cases}
1 + a = b \\
2 + a = \dfrac{b}{2}
\end{cases}
\qquad
\Longrightarrow
\qquad
\begin{cases}
a = -3 \\
b = -2
\end{cases}
$$
$$
f(0) = 0,
\qquad
f(9) = -2 \cdot 3 = -6,
\qquad
\frac{f(9) - f(0)}{9 - 0} = -\frac{2}{3}
$$
$$
0 < x < 1:
\quad
2x - 3 = -\frac{2}{3}
\iff
x = \frac{7}{6} \notin (0, 1)
$$
$$
1 < x < 9:
\quad
-\frac{1}{\sqrt{x}} = -\frac{2}{3}
\iff
\sqrt{x} = \frac{3}{2}
\iff
x = \frac{9}{4}
$$
$$
c = \frac{9}{4}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Each of the two pieces is continuous and differentiable on its own interval, so the hypotheses need to be checked only at the point $x = 1$ where the pieces meet. The function is continuous at $1$ if the left-hand and right-hand limits equal the value $f(1) = 1 + a$, that is, if $1 + a = b$.

To check differentiability at $1$, use the test from the chapter on derivatives. The function is continuous at $1$ and differentiable near $1$, so the left-hand and right-hand derivatives at $1$ are the limits of $f'(x)$ from the left and right, respectively, namely $2 + a$ and $\dfrac{b}{2}$. The function is differentiable at $1$ if these two numbers agree. Both conditions must hold, so they form a system. The first equation gives $b = 1 + a$, and substituting into the second gives $2 + a = \dfrac{1 + a}{2}$, or $4 + 2a = 1 + a$, so $a = -3$ and $b = -2$.

With these values $f$ is continuous on $[0, 9]$ and differentiable on $(0, 9)$, and the **mean value theorem** applies. The line through the endpoints has slope $-\dfrac{6}{9} = -\dfrac{2}{3}$. Solve the equation $f'(x) = -\dfrac{2}{3}$ separately on each piece. On the first piece the solution $\dfrac{7}{6}$ is greater than $1$, so it is inadmissible. On the second piece $f'(x) = -\dfrac{2}{2\sqrt{x}} = -\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{x}}$, and the solution $\dfrac{9}{4}$ belongs to $(1, 9)$. Where the pieces meet the derivative is $f'(1) = -1$, which differs from $-\dfrac{2}{3}$. Therefore the point given by the theorem is **unique** and equals $c = \dfrac{9}{4}$.

</div>

## Exercise 4

Two highway speed cameras are $12\,\mathrm{km}$ apart. A car passes the second camera $6$ minutes after passing the first. Show that at some point during the trip the car was traveling at $120\,\mathrm{km/h}$.

$$
\Delta t = 6\,\mathrm{min} = \frac{6}{60}\,\mathrm{h} = \frac{1}{10}\,\mathrm{h}
$$
$$
v_{\text{avg}} = \frac{s(t_2) - s(t_1)}{t_2 - t_1} = \frac{12\,\mathrm{km}}{\dfrac{1}{10}\,\mathrm{h}} = 120\,\mathrm{km/h}
$$
$$
\text{there exists } \bar{t} \in (t_1, t_2) \text{ such that}
\qquad
s'(\bar{t}) = \frac{s(t_2) - s(t_1)}{t_2 - t_1} = 120\,\mathrm{km/h}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Let $s(t)$ be the car's position along the highway at time $t$, and let $t_1$ and $t_2$ be the times when it passes the two cameras. Its average speed between $t_1$ and $t_2$ is the distance traveled divided by the elapsed time. Express the time in hours: $6$ minutes is $\dfrac{1}{10}$ of an hour, so the average speed is $120\,\mathrm{km/h}$.

The car's position changes continuously, and the car has a velocity at every instant, so $s$ is continuous on $[t_1, t_2]$ and differentiable on $(t_1, t_2)$. By the **mean value theorem**, there is a time $\bar{t}$ between $t_1$ and $t_2$ when the derivative $s'(\bar{t})$ equals the average velocity. The derivative of position is instantaneous velocity, so at that time the car was traveling at $120\,\mathrm{km/h}$. This argument does not specify when this happened or rule out a higher speed at other times.

</div>
