## Exercise 1

Find the average value of $f(x) = 3x^2 - 2x$ on the interval $[0, 2]$ and the points $c$ in $[0, 2]$ where $f(c)$ equals its average value.

$$
\int_0^2 \left( 3x^2 - 2x \right) dx = \Big[ x^3 - x^2 \Big]_0^2 = 8 - 4 = 4
$$
$$
\text{average value} = \frac{1}{2 - 0} \cdot 4 = 2
$$
$$
3c^2 - 2c = 2
\iff
3c^2 - 2c - 2 = 0
\iff
c = \frac{1 \pm \sqrt{7}}{3}
$$
$$
c = \frac{1 + \sqrt{7}}{3} \approx 1.22
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The average value of $f$ on $[a, b]$ is the integral divided by the interval length. The integral equals $4$ and the length is $2$, so the average value is $2$.

The function is continuous on $[0, 2]$, so by the **mean value theorem for integrals** there is at least one point $c$ in $[0, 2]$ with $f(c) = 2$. To find it, solve $3c^2 - 2c - 2 = 0$. One quarter of the discriminant is $1 + 6 = 7$, so the solutions are $\dfrac{1 \pm \sqrt{7}}{3}$. Since $\sqrt{7} \approx 2.65$, the solution with the minus sign is negative and lies outside the interval, while the solution with the plus sign is approximately $1.22$ and lies between $0$ and $2$. The required point is therefore **unique**.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Let $F(x) = \displaystyle\int_0^x \left( t^2 - 1 \right) e^{t}\,dt$. Find the intervals on which $F$ is increasing or decreasing, its local maximum and minimum points, and the equation of the tangent line to the graph of $F$ at the point with horizontal coordinate $0$.

$$
F'(x) = \left( x^2 - 1 \right) e^{x}
$$
$$
F'(x) > 0
\iff
x^2 - 1 > 0
\iff
x < -1 \quad \text{or} \quad x > 1
$$
$$
F \text{ increasing on } (-\infty, -1] \text{ and on } [1, +\infty) ,
\qquad
F \text{ decreasing on } [-1, 1]
$$
$$
x = -1 \ \text{ local maximum point} ,
\qquad
x = 1 \ \text{ local minimum point}
$$
$$
F(0) = \int_0^0 \left( t^2 - 1 \right) e^{t}\,dt = 0 ,
\qquad
F'(0) = (0 - 1) \cdot e^{0} = -1
\qquad
\Longrightarrow
\qquad
y = -x
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The integrand $\left( t^2 - 1 \right) e^{t}$ is continuous on $\mathbb{R}$. By the **fundamental theorem of calculus**, $F$ is differentiable and its derivative is obtained by substituting $x$ for $t$ in the integrand. It is therefore unnecessary to evaluate the integral.

The exponential function is always positive, so the sign of $F'$ is the sign of $x^2 - 1$, which is positive outside the interval between its roots $-1$ and $1$ and negative between them. By the monotonicity test from the chapter on theorems of differential calculus, $F$ increases up to $-1$, decreases between $-1$ and $1$, and then increases again. Therefore $x = -1$ is a point of **local maximum** and $x = 1$ is a point of **local minimum**.

At the starting point $0$ the accumulation function equals $0$ because the limits of integration coincide. The slope of the tangent is $F'(0) = -1$, so the tangent passes through the origin and has equation $y = -x$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Let $G(x) = \displaystyle\int_1^{x^2} \frac{e^{t}}{t}\,dt$, with $x > 0$. Find $G'(x)$ and write the equation of the tangent line to the graph of $G$ at the point with horizontal coordinate $1$.

$$
G'(x) = \frac{e^{x^2}}{x^2} \cdot 2x = \frac{2\,e^{x^2}}{x}
$$
$$
G(1) = \int_1^1 \frac{e^{t}}{t}\,dt = 0 ,
\qquad
G'(1) = 2e
$$
$$
y - 0 = 2e\,(x - 1)
\qquad
\Longrightarrow
\qquad
y = 2e\,x - 2e
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The integrand $\dfrac{e^{t}}{t}$ is continuous for $t > 0$, and for $x > 0$ the upper limit $x^2$ is positive. The function $G$ is the composition of the accumulation function $F(u) = \displaystyle\int_1^{u} \dfrac{e^{t}}{t}\,dt$ with $u = x^2$. To find the **derivative of an integral with a variable limit**, evaluate the integrand at the limit $x^2$, obtaining $\dfrac{e^{x^2}}{x^2}$, and multiply by the derivative of the limit, which is $2x$. Canceling $x$ gives $\dfrac{2\,e^{x^2}}{x}$.

For $x = 1$ the upper limit is $1$ and coincides with the lower limit, so $G(1) = 0$. The slope of the tangent is $G'(1) = 2e$, and the tangent passes through $(1, 0)$.

</div>

## Exercise 4

Evaluate $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\displaystyle\int_0^x \sin\left( t^2 \right) dt}{x^3}$.

$$
\lim_{x \to 0} \int_0^x \sin\left( t^2 \right) dt = 0 ,
\qquad
\lim_{x \to 0} x^3 = 0
$$
$$
\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\displaystyle\int_0^x \sin\left( t^2 \right) dt}{x^3}
= \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sin\left( x^2 \right)}{3x^2}
= \frac{1}{3} \cdot \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sin\left( x^2 \right)}{x^2}
= \frac{1}{3}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The accumulation function $\displaystyle\int_0^x \sin\left( t^2 \right) dt$ is differentiable and therefore continuous, and at $x = 0$ it equals $0$. The denominator also tends to $0$, so the limit has the indeterminate form $\dfrac{0}{0}$. The hypotheses of **l'Hôpital's rule**, discussed in the chapter on theorems of differential calculus, hold because the numerator and denominator are differentiable and the derivative of the denominator, $3x^2$, differs from $0$ for $x \neq 0$.

By the fundamental theorem, the derivative of the numerator is $\sin\left( x^2 \right)$. Evaluate the limit of the quotient of the derivatives using the standard limit $\displaystyle\lim_{u \to 0} \dfrac{\sin u}{u} = 1$ with $u = x^2$, which tends to $0$ as $x \to 0$. The required limit is therefore $\dfrac{1}{3}$.

</div>
