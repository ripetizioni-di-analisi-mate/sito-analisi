## Exercise 1

Evaluate $\displaystyle\int_0^{\sqrt{3}} x\,\sqrt{x^2 + 1}\,dx$.

$$
t = x^2 + 1 ,
\qquad
dt = 2x\,dx ,
\qquad
x = 0 \ \Rightarrow \ t = 1 ,
\qquad
x = \sqrt{3} \ \Rightarrow \ t = 4
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_0^{\sqrt{3}} x\,\sqrt{x^2 + 1}\,dx
&= \frac{1}{2} \int_1^4 \sqrt{t}\,dt \\[6pt]
&= \frac{1}{2} \left[ \frac{2}{3}\,t\sqrt{t} \right]_1^4 \\[6pt]
&= \frac{1}{3}\,(8 - 1) = \frac{7}{3}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The expression under the radical is $x^2 + 1$, and outside it is $x$, which is the derivative of $x^2 + 1$ up to a factor of $2$. For this reason set $t = x^2 + 1$. Then $dt = 2x\,dx$, so $x\,dx = \dfrac{1}{2}\,dt$.

For **integration by substitution in definite integrals**, the limits of integration also change. When $x = 0$ we have $t = 1$, and when $x = \sqrt{3}$ we have $t = 3 + 1 = 4$. The integral becomes $\dfrac{1}{2} \displaystyle\int_1^4 \sqrt{t}\,dt$, with no need to return to the variable $x$. An antiderivative of $\sqrt{t}$ is $\dfrac{2}{3}\,t\sqrt{t}$, which equals $\dfrac{16}{3}$ at $4$ and $\dfrac{2}{3}$ at $1$. Multiplying the difference by $\dfrac{1}{2}$ gives $\dfrac{7}{3}$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Evaluate $\displaystyle\int_1^e \frac{(\ln x)^2}{x}\,dx$.

$$
t = \ln x ,
\qquad
dt = \frac{1}{x}\,dx ,
\qquad
x = 1 \ \Rightarrow \ t = 0 ,
\qquad
x = e \ \Rightarrow \ t = 1
$$
$$
\int_1^e \frac{(\ln x)^2}{x}\,dx = \int_0^1 t^2\,dt = \left[ \frac{t^3}{3} \right]_0^1 = \frac{1}{3}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The factor $\dfrac{1}{x}$ is the derivative of $\ln x$, so the substitution $t = \ln x$ turns $\dfrac{1}{x}\,dx$ into $dt$ and $(\ln x)^2$ into $t^2$. The limits become $\ln 1 = 0$ and $\ln e = 1$. The integral of $t^2$ over $[0, 1]$ equals $\dfrac{1}{3}$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Evaluate $\displaystyle\int_0^{\pi} x \sin x\,dx$.

$$
f(x) = x , \quad f'(x) = 1 ,
\qquad
g'(x) = \sin x , \quad g(x) = -\cos x
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_0^{\pi} x \sin x\,dx
&= \Big[ -x \cos x \Big]_0^{\pi} - \int_0^{\pi} (-\cos x)\,dx \\[6pt]
&= (-\pi \cos \pi - 0) + \int_0^{\pi} \cos x\,dx \\[6pt]
&= \pi + \Big[ \sin x \Big]_0^{\pi} \\[6pt]
&= \pi + 0 = \pi
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The integrand is the product of a polynomial and a trigonometric function, so **integration by parts** applies. Choose $f(x) = x$ as the factor to differentiate, giving $1$, and $g'(x) = \sin x$ as the factor to integrate, with antiderivative $-\cos x$. The remaining integral then contains only $\cos x$.

The term $\Big[ -x \cos x \Big]_0^{\pi}$ equals $-\pi \cdot (-1) - 0 = \pi$ because $\cos \pi = -1$. With the minus sign in front of $-\cos x$, the remaining integral becomes $+\displaystyle\int_0^{\pi} \cos x\,dx$, which equals $\sin \pi - \sin 0 = 0$. The result is therefore $\pi$.

</div>

## Exercise 4

Evaluate $\displaystyle\int_0^1 \sqrt{1 - x^2}\,dx$ using the substitution $x = \sin t$ and interpret the result as an area.

$$
x = \sin t , \quad 0 \leq t \leq \frac{\pi}{2} ,
\qquad
dx = \cos t\,dt ,
\qquad
x = 0 \ \Rightarrow \ t = 0 ,
\qquad
x = 1 \ \Rightarrow \ t = \frac{\pi}{2}
$$
$$
\sqrt{1 - \sin^2 t} = \sqrt{\cos^2 t} = \cos t \quad \text{for } 0 \leq t \leq \frac{\pi}{2}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_0^1 \sqrt{1 - x^2}\,dx
&= \int_0^{\frac{\pi}{2}} \cos^2 t\,dt
= \int_0^{\frac{\pi}{2}} \frac{1 + \cos 2t}{2}\,dt \\[6pt]
&= \left[ \frac{t}{2} + \frac{\sin 2t}{4} \right]_0^{\frac{\pi}{2}} \\[6pt]
&= \frac{\pi}{4} + \frac{\sin \pi}{4} - 0 = \frac{\pi}{4}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Here the substitution expresses $x$ in terms of the new variable $t$. As $t$ runs from $0$ to $\dfrac{\pi}{2}$, sine increases from $0$ to $1$, so the limits in $t$ are $0$ and $\dfrac{\pi}{2}$. By the Pythagorean identity, $1 - \sin^2 t = \cos^2 t$, and the square root of $\cos^2 t$ is $|\cos t|$, which equals $\cos t$ because cosine is nonnegative on $\left[ 0, \dfrac{\pi}{2} \right]$. Combining this with $dx = \cos t\,dt$ gives the integral of $\cos^2 t$.

To integrate $\cos^2 t$, use the double-angle formula $\cos 2t = 2\cos^2 t - 1$, which gives $\cos^2 t = \dfrac{1 + \cos 2t}{2}$. An antiderivative of $\cos 2t$ is $\dfrac{\sin 2t}{2}$, so an antiderivative of $\cos^2 t$ is $\dfrac{t}{2} + \dfrac{\sin 2t}{4}$. Evaluating at the endpoints gives $\dfrac{\pi}{4}$.

The graph of $y = \sqrt{1 - x^2}$ is the upper semicircle centered at the origin with radius $1$. For $0 \leq x \leq 1$ the curvilinear trapezoid is therefore one quarter of the disk of radius $1$ and has area $\dfrac{\pi \cdot 1^2}{4} = \dfrac{\pi}{4}$, in agreement with the calculation.

</div>
