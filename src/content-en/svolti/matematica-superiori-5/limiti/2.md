## Exercise 1

Calculate $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 1} \frac{x^2 + 3x}{x + 1}$.

$$
\lim_{x \to 1} \frac{x^2 + 3x}{x + 1} = \frac{1 + 3}{1 + 1} = \frac{4}{2} = 2
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The function is rational and its domain is $\mathbb{R} \setminus \{-1\}$. The point $1$ belongs to the domain, so by the theorem on **limits of elementary functions** the limit can be calculated by substituting $x = 1$. The numerator equals $4$ and the denominator equals $2$, which differs from $0$, so the limit is $2$. Direct substitution does not give an indeterminate form, so no further steps are needed.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Calculate the right-hand and left-hand limits of $f(x) = \dfrac{x + 1}{x - 2}$ as $x \to 2$.

$$
\lim_{x \to 2^+} \frac{x + 1}{x - 2} = \frac{3}{0^+} = +\infty,
\qquad
\lim_{x \to 2^-} \frac{x + 1}{x - 2} = \frac{3}{0^-} = -\infty
$$

::grafico{f="(x+1)/(x-2)" asintoti="x=2;y=1" xmin=-4 xmax=8 ymin=-8 ymax=10 legenda="y = \dfrac{x + 1}{x - 2}" titolo="As $x \to 2^+$ the function $y = \dfrac{x + 1}{x - 2}$ approaches $+\infty$, and as $x \to 2^-$ it approaches $-\infty$"}

<div class="spiegazione">

Substituting $x = 2$ gives a numerator of $3$ and a denominator of $0$, so the limit is infinite and its sign depends on the sign of the denominator near $2$. For $x > 2$ we have $x - 2 > 0$, meaning that the denominator approaches $0^+$. Dividing $3$ by a small positive number gives a large positive number, so the **right-hand limit** is $+\infty$. For $x < 2$ the denominator is negative, and the **left-hand limit** is $-\infty$.

The two limits differ, so the limit as $x \to 2$ does not exist. As the figure shows, the line $x = 2$ is a vertical asymptote of the graph.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Calculate $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to -1} \frac{x^2 - 1}{x^2 + 3x + 2}$.

$$
\frac{x^2 - 1}{x^2 + 3x + 2} \;\to\; \frac{1 - 1}{1 - 3 + 2} = \frac{0}{0}
$$
$$
\frac{x^2 - 1}{x^2 + 3x + 2} = \frac{(x - 1)(x + 1)}{(x + 1)(x + 2)} = \frac{x - 1}{x + 2}
\qquad (x \neq -1)
$$
$$
\lim_{x \to -1} \frac{x - 1}{x + 2} = \frac{-2}{1} = -2
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Substituting $x = -1$ makes both numerator and denominator zero, so the limit has the **indeterminate form** $\dfrac{0}{0}$. Since both polynomials vanish at $-1$, the factor theorem implies that both are divisible by $x + 1$. The numerator is a difference of squares, and the denominator is the trinomial with roots $-1$ and $-2$, namely $(x + 1)(x + 2)$.

The point $x = -1$ is excluded when calculating the limit, so the factor $x + 1$ cancels. The function $\dfrac{x - 1}{x + 2}$ is defined at $-1$, and by substitution the limit **equals** $-2$.

</div>

## Exercise 4

Calculate $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 1} \frac{x^3 - 3x + 2}{x^2 - 1}$.

$$
x^3 - 3x + 2 \;\to\; 1 - 3 + 2 = 0,
\qquad
x^2 - 1 \;\to\; 0
$$

| | $1$ | $0$ | $-3$ | $2$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $1$ | | $1$ | $1$ | $-2$ |
| | $1$ | $1$ | $-2$ | $0$ |

$$
x^3 - 3x + 2 = (x - 1)(x^2 + x - 2) = (x - 1)(x - 1)(x + 2) = (x - 1)^2 (x + 2)
$$
$$
\lim_{x \to 1} \frac{(x - 1)^2 (x + 2)}{(x - 1)(x + 1)} = \lim_{x \to 1} \frac{(x - 1)(x + 2)}{x + 1} = \frac{0 \cdot 3}{2} = 0
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Here too, substituting $x = 1$ gives the form $\dfrac{0}{0}$. The denominator factors as a difference of squares. For the numerator, which is cubic and vanishes at $1$, use **synthetic division** with the root $1$. The coefficients are $1$, $0$, $-3$ and $2$, where the $0$ corresponds to the missing term in $x^2$. The quotient is $x^2 + x - 2$ and the remainder is $0$. The trinomial $x^2 + x - 2$ has roots $1$ and $-2$, so the numerator is $(x - 1)^2 (x + 2)$.

After one factor of $x - 1$ is canceled, the numerator still has a factor of $x - 1$, which approaches $0$, while the denominator approaches $2$. Therefore the limit **equals** $0$. The numerator contains the factor $x - 1$ twice and the denominator contains it only once, which is why the quotient approaches $0$.

</div>
