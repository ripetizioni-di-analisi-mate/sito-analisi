## Exercise 1

Choose an integer at random from $1$ to $30$. Calculate the probability that it is a multiple of $3$ or a multiple of $5$.

$$
A = \{\text{multiples of } 3\}, \qquad P(A) = \frac{10}{30}
$$
$$
B = \{\text{multiples of } 5\}, \qquad P(B) = \frac{6}{30}
$$
$$
A \cap B = \{15, 30\}, \qquad P(A \cap B) = \frac{2}{30}
$$
$$
P(A \cup B) = \frac{10}{30} + \frac{6}{30} - \frac{2}{30} = \frac{14}{30} = \frac{7}{15}
$$

::venn{zone="a,ab,b" nomi="A,B" ambiente="Ω" elementi="a:8 numbers;ab:15, 30;b:4 numbers;u:16 numbers" titolo="Of the $30$ numbers, $8$ are multiples of $3$ but not of $5$, $2$ are multiples of both, and $4$ are multiples of $5$ but not of $3$, so $14$ numbers belong to $A \cup B$"}

<div class="spiegazione">

The multiples of $3$ from $1$ to $30$ are $3, 6, \dots, 30$, a total of $\dfrac{30}{3} = 10$ numbers, and the multiples of $5$ are $\dfrac{30}{5} = 6$. A number is a multiple of both $3$ and $5$ if it is a multiple of $15$. Between $1$ and $30$, the only such numbers are $15$ and $30$.

The events $A$ and $B$ are not mutually exclusive because they share two numbers. Therefore apply the general **addition rule**, subtracting $P(A \cap B)$. The numbers $15$ and $30$ occur both among the $10$ multiples of $3$ and among the $6$ multiples of $5$, so without the subtraction they would be counted twice.

The required probability is $\dfrac{7}{15}$. As the diagram shows, the number of favorable outcomes is $8 + 2 + 4 = 14$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Roll a die $4$ times. Calculate the probability of rolling a $6$ at least once.

$$
P(\text{no } 6) = \left( \frac{5}{6} \right)^4 = \frac{625}{1296}
$$
$$
P(\text{at least one } 6) = 1 - \frac{625}{1296} = \frac{671}{1296} \approx 0.518
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The event “at least one $6$” includes the cases of rolling one, two, three, or four $6$s, which would have to be calculated separately. Its complement is “no $6$ in four rolls,” which can be calculated in one step, so we use the **probability of the complement**.

On each roll the probability of not getting a $6$ is $\dfrac{5}{6}$. The rolls are independent, so the probability of never rolling a $6$ in all four rolls is the product $\left( \dfrac{5}{6} \right)^4 = \dfrac{625}{1296}$. Subtracting this from $1$ gives $\dfrac{671}{1296}$, a value just above $\dfrac{1}{2}$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Two archers shoot at the same target at the same time, independently of each other. The first hits the target with probability $0.8$ and the second with probability $0.6$. Calculate the probability that the target is hit by both archers, by at least one, and by exactly one.

$$
P(A) = 0.8, \quad P\big( \overline{A} \big) = 0.2,
\qquad
P(B) = 0.6, \quad P\big( \overline{B} \big) = 0.4
$$
$$
P(A \cap B) = 0.8 \cdot 0.6 = 0.48
$$
$$
P(A \cup B) = 1 - P\big( \overline{A} \cap \overline{B} \big) = 1 - 0.2 \cdot 0.4 = 0.92
$$
$$
P(\text{exactly one}) = 0.8 \cdot 0.4 + 0.2 \cdot 0.6 = 0.32 + 0.12 = 0.44
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Let $A$ and $B$ denote the events “the first archer hits” and “the second archer hits.” The shots are independent, so the probability that both hit is the product of the probabilities, which equals $0.48$. Their complements are also independent.

At least one archer hits the target if and only if they do *not* both miss. Therefore we find the probability that both miss, $0.2 \cdot 0.4 = 0.08$, and subtract it from $1$. The **addition rule** gives the same value, $0.8 + 0.6 - 0.48 = 0.92$.

Exactly one archer hits in two mutually exclusive cases: the first hits and the second misses, or the first misses and the second hits. The two probabilities add to $0.44$. We have $0.92 = 0.48 + 0.44$ because “at least one” is the union of the mutually exclusive events “both” and “exactly one.”

</div>

## Exercise 4

An urn contains $4$ white balls and $6$ black balls, and two balls are drawn in succession. Calculate the probability that both are white if the first ball is replaced and if it is not replaced. In the second case, also calculate the probability that the second ball is white and determine whether the events “the first is white” and “the second is white” are independent.

**With replacement**
$$
P(B_1 \cap B_2) = \frac{4}{10} \cdot \frac{4}{10} = \frac{16}{100} = \frac{4}{25}
$$

**Without replacement**
$$
P(B_1 \cap B_2) = P(B_1) \cdot P(B_2 \mid B_1) = \frac{4}{10} \cdot \frac{3}{9} = \frac{12}{90} = \frac{2}{15}
$$
$$
P(B_2) = \frac{4}{10} \cdot \frac{3}{9} + \frac{6}{10} \cdot \frac{4}{9} = \frac{12}{90} + \frac{24}{90} = \frac{36}{90} = \frac{2}{5}
$$
$$
P(B_1) \cdot P(B_2) = \frac{2}{5} \cdot \frac{2}{5} = \frac{4}{25} \neq \frac{2}{15} = P(B_1 \cap B_2)
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Let $B_1$ and $B_2$ denote the events “the first ball is white” and “the second ball is white.” If the first ball is replaced, the second draw takes place under the same conditions as the first, so the events are independent and the probability is $\left( \dfrac{4}{10} \right)^2 = \dfrac{4}{25}$.

Without replacement, use the **multiplication rule**. After drawing a white ball first, $3$ white balls remain out of $9$ balls, so $P(B_2 \mid B_1) = \dfrac{3}{9}$ and the probability of two white balls is $\dfrac{2}{15}$.

The second ball is white in two mutually exclusive cases: the first is white and the second is white, or the first is black and the second is white. In the second case, after drawing a black ball there are $4$ white balls left out of $9$. Adding gives $P(B_2) = \dfrac{2}{5}$, which equals $P(B_1)$. Therefore, if the color of the first ball is unknown, the second has the same probability of being white as the first.

The product $P(B_1) \cdot P(B_2) = \dfrac{4}{25}$ differs from $P(B_1 \cap B_2) = \dfrac{2}{15}$, so without replacement the events $B_1$ and $B_2$ **are not independent**. Indeed, knowing that the first ball is white lowers the probability that the second is white from $\dfrac{2}{5}$ to $\dfrac{1}{3}$.

</div>
