## Exercise 1

A car travels along a straight road with velocity $12\,\mathrm{m/s}$. At time $t = 0$ it begins to brake. While braking, its acceleration is $a(t) = -6t$, with $a$ in $\mathrm{m/s^2}$ and $t$ in seconds. Find its velocity $v(t)$ and position $s(t)$, taking $s(0) = 0$. Determine when the car stops and its braking distance.

$$
v(t) = \int (-6t)\,dt = -3t^2 + c_1,
\qquad
v(0) = 12 \;\Longrightarrow\; c_1 = 12
$$
$$
s(t) = \int \left( 12 - 3t^2 \right) dt = 12t - t^3 + c_2,
\qquad
s(0) = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; c_2 = 0
$$
$$
v(t) = 0
\iff
12 - 3t^2 = 0
\iff
t^2 = 4
\iff
t = 2\,\mathrm{s}
$$
$$
s(2) = 24 - 8 = 16\,\mathrm{m}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Acceleration is the derivative of velocity, so velocity is an antiderivative of acceleration. Integrating $-6t$ gives $-3t^2 + c_1$, and the initial velocity determines the constant. At $t = 0$, we have $v(0) = c_1$, so $c_1 = 12$ and $v(t) = 12 - 3t^2$.

Likewise, position is an antiderivative of velocity. Integrating $12 - 3t^2$ gives $12t - t^3 + c_2$, and the condition $s(0) = 0$ gives $c_2 = 0$. The car stops when its velocity is zero. The equation $t^2 = 4$ has solutions $t = 2$ and $t = -2$. Only $t = 2\,\mathrm{s}$ is admissible because braking begins at $t = 0$. The **braking distance** is the position at that time, namely $16\,\mathrm{m}$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

In a circuit, the current for $t \geq 0$ is $i(t) = 4\,e^{-2t}$, with $i$ in amperes and $t$ in seconds. Let $q(t)$ be the charge that has passed through a cross section of the conductor from time $0$ to time $t$, with $q(0) = 0$. Find $q(t)$, the value that $q(t)$ approaches as $t \to +\infty$, and the time when $q(t) = 1\,\mathrm{C}$.

$$
q(t) = \int 4\,e^{-2t}\,dt = 4 \cdot \left( -\frac{1}{2} \right) e^{-2t} + c = -2\,e^{-2t} + c
$$
$$
q(0) = 0
\iff
-2 + c = 0
\iff
c = 2,
\qquad
q(t) = 2\left( 1 - e^{-2t} \right)
$$
$$
\lim_{t \to +\infty} 2\left( 1 - e^{-2t} \right) = 2\,\mathrm{C}
$$
$$
2\left( 1 - e^{-2t} \right) = 1
\iff
e^{-2t} = \frac{1}{2}
\iff
-2t = -\ln 2
\iff
t = \frac{\ln 2}{2} \approx 0.35\,\mathrm{s}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Current is the derivative of charge, so $q(t)$ is an antiderivative of $i(t)$. The function $e^{-2t}$ has a linear inner function $-2t$, so the formula for a linear inner function gives the antiderivative $-\dfrac{1}{2}\,e^{-2t}$. The condition $q(0) = 0$ gives $-2 + c = 0$, that is, $c = 2$.

As $t \to +\infty$, the exponential $e^{-2t}$ approaches $0$, so the charge approaches $2\,\mathrm{C}$. To find the time when $q(t) = 1$, divide by $2$ to obtain $1 - e^{-2t} = \dfrac{1}{2}$, that is, $e^{-2t} = \dfrac{1}{2}$. Taking logarithms gives $-2t = \ln \dfrac{1}{2} = -\ln 2$ and hence $t = \dfrac{\ln 2}{2}$. By this time, half the total charge has passed.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Consider the function $f(x) = \dfrac{a x + b}{x^2 + 1}$, where $a$ and $b$ are real parameters.

1. Find $a$ and $b$ so that the graph of $f$ passes through the point $(0, 1)$ and the tangent line there is parallel to the line $y = 2x$.
2. Using these values, evaluate $\displaystyle\int f(x)\,dx$ and find the antiderivative $F$ such that $F(0) = 0$.
3. Determine where $F$ is increasing or decreasing, and find its minimum point and minimum value.
4. Evaluate the limits of $F$ as $x \to +\infty$ and as $x \to -\infty$.

**Part 1**
$$
f'(x) = \frac{a\left( x^2 + 1 \right) - (a x + b) \cdot 2x}{\left( x^2 + 1 \right)^2}
$$
$$
\begin{cases}
f(0) = 1 \\
f'(0) = 2
\end{cases}
\qquad
\Longrightarrow
\qquad
\begin{cases}
b = 1 \\
a = 2
\end{cases}
\qquad
\Longrightarrow
\qquad
f(x) = \frac{2x + 1}{x^2 + 1}
$$

**Part 2**
$$
\begin{aligned}
\int \frac{2x + 1}{x^2 + 1}\,dx
&= \int \frac{2x}{x^2 + 1}\,dx + \int \frac{1}{x^2 + 1}\,dx \\[6pt]
&= \ln \left( x^2 + 1 \right) + \arctan x + c
\end{aligned}
$$
$$
F(0) = \ln 1 + \arctan 0 + c = c = 0,
\qquad
F(x) = \ln \left( x^2 + 1 \right) + \arctan x
$$

**Part 3**
$$
F'(x) = f(x) = \frac{2x + 1}{x^2 + 1} > 0
\iff
x > -\frac{1}{2}
$$
$$
F\left( -\frac{1}{2} \right) = \ln \frac{5}{4} + \arctan \left( -\frac{1}{2} \right) = \ln \frac{5}{4} - \arctan \frac{1}{2} \approx -0.24
$$

**Part 4**
$$
\lim_{x \to \pm\infty} \ln \left( x^2 + 1 \right) = +\infty,
\qquad
\lim_{x \to \pm\infty} \arctan x = \pm \frac{\pi}{2}
\qquad
\Longrightarrow
\qquad
\lim_{x \to \pm\infty} F(x) = +\infty
$$

::grafico{f="(2*x+1)/(x^2+1);log(x^2+1)+atan(x)" punti="-0.5,-0.24,pieno;0,1,pieno;0,0,pieno" xmin=-4 xmax=4 ymin=-1 ymax=5 legenda="y = \dfrac{2x + 1}{x^2 + 1};y = \ln \left( x^2 + 1 \right) + \arctan x" titolo="The graph of the antiderivative $F(x) = \ln \left( x^2 + 1 \right) + \arctan x$ passes through the origin and has its minimum at $x = -\dfrac{1}{2}$, where $f$ changes sign"}

<div class="spiegazione">

**Part 1.** Since the graph passes through $(0, 1)$, we have $f(0) = b = 1$. The line $y = 2x$ has slope $2$, so the tangent at $(0, 1)$ is parallel to it if $f'(0) = 2$. Use the quotient rule to find the derivative. At $x = 0$, the term $(a x + b) \cdot 2x$ vanishes and the denominator equals $1$, so $f'(0) = a$. The two conditions give $a = 2$ and $b = 1$.

**Part 2.** The denominator $x^2 + 1$ has a negative discriminant and derivative $2x$. The numerator $2x + 1$ splits into the derivative of the denominator plus the constant $1$. The first integral is therefore $\ln \left( x^2 + 1 \right)$, with no absolute value because $x^2 + 1 > 0$, and the second is the basic integral $\arctan x$. Since $\ln 1 = 0$ and $\arctan 0 = 0$, the condition $F(0) = 0$ gives $c = 0$.

**Part 3.** The derivative of $F$ is $f$. The denominator $x^2 + 1$ is positive, so the sign of $F'$ is the sign of $2x + 1$. Therefore, $F$ is **decreasing** for $x < -\dfrac{1}{2}$ and **increasing** for $x > -\dfrac{1}{2}$, and $x = -\dfrac{1}{2}$ is the minimum point. Substitution gives the minimum value, using $\left( -\dfrac{1}{2} \right)^2 + 1 = \dfrac{5}{4}$ and the fact that the arctangent is an odd function.

**Part 4.** As $x \to \pm\infty$, the logarithm tends to $+\infty$, while the arctangent approaches the finite values $\pm \dfrac{\pi}{2}$. The sum of a function tending to $+\infty$ and a bounded function tends to $+\infty$, so $F$ tends to $+\infty$ at both ends and its graph has no horizontal asymptotes. This agrees with the figure, in which $F$ has a single minimum and grows without bound to the right and left.

</div>

## Exercise 4

For each real number $k \neq 0$, consider the function $f_k(x) = \dfrac{k}{x^2 - 4x + k}$.

1. Find the values of $k$ for which the denominator has two distinct real roots, a double root, or no real roots.
2. Evaluate $\displaystyle\int f_3(x)\,dx$.
3. Evaluate $\displaystyle\int f_4(x)\,dx$.
4. Find the antiderivative $F$ of $f_8$ such that $F(2) = 0$ and the horizontal asymptotes of its graph.

**Part 1**
$$
\Delta = 16 - 4k
\qquad
\begin{aligned}
&\Delta > 0 \iff k < 4 && \text{(with } k \neq 0 \text{), two distinct roots} \\
&\Delta = 0 \iff k = 4 && \text{double root} \\
&\Delta < 0 \iff k > 4 && \text{no real roots}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Part 2**
$$
x^2 - 4x + 3 = (x - 1)(x - 3),
\qquad
3 = A\,(x - 3) + B\,(x - 1)
$$
$$
x = 1: \quad 3 = -2A \;\Longrightarrow\; A = -\frac{3}{2},
\qquad
x = 3: \quad 3 = 2B \;\Longrightarrow\; B = \frac{3}{2}
$$
$$
\int \frac{3}{x^2 - 4x + 3}\,dx = -\frac{3}{2} \ln \lvert x - 1 \rvert + \frac{3}{2} \ln \lvert x - 3 \rvert + c = \frac{3}{2} \ln \left\lvert \frac{x - 3}{x - 1} \right\rvert + c
$$

**Part 3**
$$
\int \frac{4}{x^2 - 4x + 4}\,dx = 4 \int (x - 2)^{-2}\,dx = 4 \cdot \frac{(x - 2)^{-1}}{-1} + c = -\frac{4}{x - 2} + c
$$

**Part 4**
$$
x^2 - 4x + 8 = (x - 2)^2 + 4,
\qquad
\int \frac{8}{(x - 2)^2 + 4}\,dx = 8 \cdot \frac{1}{2} \arctan \frac{x - 2}{2} + c = 4 \arctan \frac{x - 2}{2} + c
$$
$$
F(2) = 4 \arctan 0 + c = c = 0,
\qquad
F(x) = 4 \arctan \frac{x - 2}{2}
$$
$$
\lim_{x \to +\infty} F(x) = 4 \cdot \frac{\pi}{2} = 2\pi,
\qquad
\lim_{x \to -\infty} F(x) = -2\pi
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

**Part 1.** The denominator $x^2 - 4x + k$ has discriminant $\Delta = (-4)^2 - 4k = 16 - 4k$. The sign of $\Delta$ depends on $k$. The discriminant is positive for $k < 4$, zero for $k = 4$, and negative for $k > 4$. Each case corresponds to one of the three methods for rational functions with a quadratic denominator.

**Part 2.** For $k = 3$, the discriminant is $4 > 0$ and the roots are $1$ and $3$, so we decompose the integrand into **partial fractions**. Substituting $x = 1$ and $x = 3$ into the identity between the numerators gives $A = -\dfrac{3}{2}$ and $B = \dfrac{3}{2}$. By the quotient rule for logarithms, the two terms combine into a single logarithm.

**Part 3.** For $k = 4$, the denominator is the square $(x - 2)^2$. The numerator is constant, so there is no need to rewrite it, and we integrate a power with exponent $-2$ and inner function $x - 2$.

**Part 4.** For $k = 8$, the discriminant is $-16 < 0$. Completing the square gives the denominator $(x - 2)^2 + 2^2$. By the arctangent formula, the integral of $\dfrac{1}{(x - 2)^2 + 2^2}$ is $\dfrac{1}{2} \arctan \dfrac{x - 2}{2}$, so the integral of $f_8$ is $4 \arctan \dfrac{x - 2}{2} + c$. The condition $F(2) = 0$ gives $c = 0$ because $\arctan 0 = 0$. The arctangent approaches $\dfrac{\pi}{2}$ as $x \to +\infty$ and $-\dfrac{\pi}{2}$ as $x \to -\infty$, so the graph of $F$ has the **horizontal asymptotes** $y = 2\pi$ on the right and $y = -2\pi$ on the left.

</div>
