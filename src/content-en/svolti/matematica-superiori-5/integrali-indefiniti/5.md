## Exercise 1

Evaluate $\displaystyle\int (2x + 1)\,e^{x}\,dx$ and find the point where the antiderivative $F$ obtained with $c = 0$ has its minimum.

$$
f(x) = 2x + 1,
\quad
f'(x) = 2,
\qquad
g'(x) = e^{x},
\quad
g(x) = e^{x}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
\int (2x + 1)\,e^{x}\,dx
&= (2x + 1)\,e^{x} - \int 2\,e^{x}\,dx \\[6pt]
&= (2x + 1)\,e^{x} - 2\,e^{x} + c \\[6pt]
&= (2x - 1)\,e^{x} + c
\end{aligned}
$$
$$
F(x) = (2x - 1)\,e^{x},
\qquad
F'(x) = (2x + 1)\,e^{x} > 0
\iff
x > -\frac{1}{2},
\qquad
F\left( -\frac{1}{2} \right) = -2\,e^{-\frac{1}{2}} = -\frac{2}{\sqrt{e}}
$$

::grafico{f="(2*x+1)*exp(x);(2*x-1)*exp(x)" punti="-0.5,-1.213,pieno;-0.5,0,pieno" segmenti="-0.5,0,-0.5,-1.213" xmin=-4 xmax=2 ymin=-2 ymax=4 legenda="y = (2x + 1)\,e^{x};y = (2x - 1)\,e^{x}" titolo="The antiderivative $F(x) = (2x - 1)\,e^{x}$ has its minimum at $x = -\dfrac{1}{2}$, where the integrand $(2x + 1)\,e^{x}$ changes from negative to positive"}

<div class="spiegazione">

The integrand is the product of a polynomial and $e^{x}$, so we use **integration by parts**, choosing the polynomial as the factor to differentiate. The derivative of $2x + 1$ is the constant $2$, and an antiderivative of $e^{x}$ is $e^{x}$. Applying the formula leaves the integral of $2\,e^{x}$, which we can evaluate directly. Factoring out $e^{x}$ gives $(2x + 1 - 2)\,e^{x} = (2x - 1)\,e^{x}$.

The derivative of $F$ is the integrand $(2x + 1)\,e^{x}$. The factor $e^{x}$ is always positive, so the sign of $F'$ is the sign of $2x + 1$. Therefore, $F$ decreases for $x < -\dfrac{1}{2}$ and increases for $x > -\dfrac{1}{2}$, and at $x = -\dfrac{1}{2}$ it attains a **minimum**. The minimum value is $F\left( -\dfrac{1}{2} \right) = -\dfrac{2}{\sqrt{e}} \approx -1.21$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Evaluate $\displaystyle\int x \ln x\,dx$ for $x > 0$.

$$
f(x) = \ln x,
\quad
f'(x) = \frac{1}{x},
\qquad
g'(x) = x,
\quad
g(x) = \frac{x^2}{2}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
\int x \ln x\,dx
&= \frac{x^2}{2} \ln x - \int \frac{x^2}{2} \cdot \frac{1}{x}\,dx \\[6pt]
&= \frac{x^2}{2} \ln x - \frac{1}{2} \int x\,dx \\[6pt]
&= \frac{x^2}{2} \ln x - \frac{x^2}{4} + c
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The product contains $\ln x$, whose derivative $\dfrac{1}{x}$ is simpler than the logarithm itself. For this reason, the factor to differentiate is $\ln x$ and the factor to integrate is $x$, which has antiderivative $\dfrac{x^2}{2}$. Reversing these choices would mean integrating $\ln x$ and differentiating $x$, and the integral on the right would be more complicated than the original one.

In the remaining integral, the product $\dfrac{x^2}{2} \cdot \dfrac{1}{x}$ simplifies to $\dfrac{x}{2}$, which we can integrate directly. As a check, the derivative of $\dfrac{x^2}{2} \ln x - \dfrac{x^2}{4}$ is $x \ln x + \dfrac{x^2}{2} \cdot \dfrac{1}{x} - \dfrac{x}{2} = x \ln x$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Evaluate $\displaystyle\int x^2 \sin x\,dx$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\int x^2 \sin x\,dx
&= -x^2 \cos x - \int 2x\,(-\cos x)\,dx \\[6pt]
&= -x^2 \cos x + 2 \int x \cos x\,dx \\[6pt]
&= -x^2 \cos x + 2 \left( x \sin x - \int \sin x\,dx \right) \\[6pt]
&= -x^2 \cos x + 2x \sin x + 2\cos x + c
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

In the first integration by parts, we differentiate $x^2$, whose derivative is $2x$, and integrate $\sin x$, whose antiderivative is $-\cos x$. The two minus signs, one before the integral and one before $\cos x$, give a plus sign, so we are left with $2 \displaystyle\int x \cos x\,dx$.

The degree of the polynomial has fallen from $2$ to $1$, and we integrate by parts a second time, differentiating $x$ and integrating $\cos x$. This gives $x \sin x - \displaystyle\int \sin x\,dx = x \sin x + \cos x$. Multiplying by $2$ and adding the first term gives the result. Two integrations by parts are needed because the polynomial has degree $2$.

</div>

## Exercise 4

Evaluate $I = \displaystyle\int e^{2x} \cos x\,dx$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
I
&= e^{2x} \sin x - \int 2\,e^{2x} \sin x\,dx \\[6pt]
&= e^{2x} \sin x - 2 \left( -e^{2x} \cos x + \int 2\,e^{2x} \cos x\,dx \right) \\[6pt]
&= e^{2x} \sin x + 2\,e^{2x} \cos x - 4I
\end{aligned}
$$
$$
5I = e^{2x} \left( \sin x + 2\cos x \right)
$$
$$
I = \frac{e^{2x} \left( \sin x + 2\cos x \right)}{5} + c
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

We choose $e^{2x}$ as the factor to differentiate, with derivative $2\,e^{2x}$, and $\cos x$ as the factor to integrate, with antiderivative $\sin x$. The remaining integral contains $e^{2x} \sin x$, and we integrate by parts again, differentiating the same factor $e^{2x}$. The factor to integrate is now $\sin x$, with antiderivative $-\cos x$.

After the second integration, the original integral $I$ reappears, multiplied by $2 \cdot 2 = 4$ and preceded by a minus sign. The resulting equality is a linear equation in the unknown $I$. Moving $-4I$ to the left gives $5I = e^{2x} (\sin x + 2\cos x)$, and dividing by $5$ gives $I$. We add the constant $c$ at the end. In both integrations, we differentiate the exponential. Reversing the choice in the second integration would yield the identity $I = I$, from which we cannot determine $I$.

</div>
