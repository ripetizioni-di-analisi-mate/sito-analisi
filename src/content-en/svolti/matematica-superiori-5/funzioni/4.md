## Exercise 1

Determine whether the functions $f(x) = \dfrac{x}{x^2 + 1}$, $g(x) = |x| - x^2$, and $h(x) = (x + 1)^2$ are even, odd, or neither.

$$
f(-x) = \frac{-x}{(-x)^2 + 1} = -\frac{x}{x^2 + 1} = -f(x)
$$
$$
g(-x) = |-x| - (-x)^2 = |x| - x^2 = g(x)
$$
$$
h(1) = 4,
\qquad
h(-1) = 0
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The three functions are defined on $\mathbb{R}$, which is symmetric about $0$, so we can test whether they are even or odd. For each function, calculate its value at $-x$ and compare it with its value at $x$.

For $f$, the numerator changes sign and the denominator stays the same because $(-x)^2 = x^2$. Therefore $f(-x) = -f(x)$ and the function $f$ is **odd**. For $g$, we use $|-x| = |x|$, so $g(-x) = g(x)$ and the function $g$ is **even**.

For $h$, one counterexample suffices. Since $h(-1) = 0$ differs from $h(1) = 4$, the function is not even. Since $h(-1) = 0$ also differs from $-h(1) = -4$, the function is not odd. The graph of $h$ is the parabola $y = x^2$ shifted $1$ to the left, and its axis of symmetry is the line $x = -1$, which differs from the $y$-axis.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Find the domain of the function $f(x) = \ln \dfrac{1 + x}{1 - x}$ and determine whether it is even or odd.

$$
\frac{1 + x}{1 - x} > 0
\iff
-1 < x < 1
\qquad
\Longrightarrow
\qquad
D = (-1, 1)
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
f(-x)
&= \ln \frac{1 - x}{1 + x} \\[6pt]
&= \ln \left( \frac{1 + x}{1 - x} \right)^{-1} \\[6pt]
&= -\ln \frac{1 + x}{1 - x} \\[6pt]
&= -f(x)
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The argument of the logarithm must be positive. The numerator $1 + x$ is positive for $x > -1$ and the denominator $1 - x$ is positive for $x < 1$. The two factors have the same sign only for $-1 < x < 1$ because outside this interval one is positive and the other is negative. The domain is therefore the interval $(-1, 1)$, which is symmetric about $0$.

Substituting $-x$ for $x$ changes the fraction into its reciprocal. The logarithm of the reciprocal is the negative of the logarithm by the identity $\ln a^{-1} = -\ln a$, so $f(-x) = -f(x)$ and the function is **odd**. As a check, $f\left( \dfrac{1}{2} \right) = \ln 3$ and $f\left( -\dfrac{1}{2} \right) = \ln \dfrac{1}{3} = -\ln 3$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Find the periods of the functions $f(x) = 3 \sin \left( 4x - \dfrac{\pi}{3} \right)$ and $g(x) = \sin^2 x$.

$$
f(x) = 3 \sin \left( 4x - \frac{\pi}{3} \right),
\qquad
T = \frac{2\pi}{4} = \frac{\pi}{2}
$$
$$
f\left( x + \frac{\pi}{2} \right) = 3 \sin \left( 4x + 2\pi - \frac{\pi}{3} \right) = 3 \sin \left( 4x - \frac{\pi}{3} \right) = f(x)
$$
$$
g(x) = \sin^2 x = \frac{1 - \cos (2x)}{2},
\qquad
T = \frac{2\pi}{2} = \pi
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The factor $3$ multiplies the vertical coordinates, and the term $-\dfrac{\pi}{3}$ shifts the graph horizontally, so neither changes the length of the period. The period therefore depends only on the coefficient $4$ of $x$, and the rule for the **period** of $\sin (kx)$ with $k = 4$ gives $T = \dfrac{2\pi}{4} = \dfrac{\pi}{2}$. To check, adding $\dfrac{\pi}{2}$ to $x$ adds $2\pi$ to the argument of sine, and sine has period $2\pi$.

For $g$, use the half-angle identity $\sin^2 x = \dfrac{1 - \cos (2x)}{2}$. The function $\cos (2x)$ has period $\dfrac{2\pi}{2} = \pi$, and subtracting it from $1$ and dividing by $2$ does not change the period. Therefore $g$ has period $\pi$, half the period of $\sin x$. This can also be checked directly because $\sin (x + \pi) = -\sin x$ and hence $\sin^2 (x + \pi) = (-\sin x)^2 = \sin^2 x$.

</div>

## Exercise 4

Find the period of the function $f(x) = \sin \dfrac{x}{2} + \cos \dfrac{x}{3}$.

$$
T_1 = \frac{2\pi}{\frac{1}{2}} = 4\pi,
\qquad
T_2 = \frac{2\pi}{\frac{1}{3}} = 6\pi
$$
$$
12\pi = 3 \cdot 4\pi = 2 \cdot 6\pi
$$
$$
f(x + 12\pi) = \sin \left( \frac{x}{2} + 6\pi \right) + \cos \left( \frac{x}{3} + 4\pi \right) = f(x)
$$
$$
T = 12\pi
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The function is the sum of two periodic functions. By the rule for the **period** of $\sin (kx)$ and $\cos (kx)$, with $k = \dfrac{1}{2}$ and $k = \dfrac{1}{3}$, the periods of the two terms are $4\pi$ and $6\pi$.

A number $T$ for which $f(x + T) = f(x)$ can be found by taking a common multiple of the two periods, so that both terms return to their original values. The least common multiple of $4\pi$ and $6\pi$ is $12\pi$, just as for the numbers $4$ and $6$, whose least common multiple is $12$. To check, with $x + 12\pi$ the argument of sine increases by $6\pi$ and that of cosine by $4\pi$, both multiples of $2\pi$. The period of $f$ is therefore $12\pi$.

</div>
