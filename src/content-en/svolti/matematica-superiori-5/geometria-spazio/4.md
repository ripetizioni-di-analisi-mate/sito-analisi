## Exercise 1

Write the parametric equations of the line $r$ through $A(1, -2, 0)$ and $B(3, 0, -1)$, and determine whether the points $C(5, 2, -2)$ and $D(-1, -4, 2)$ lie on $r$.

$$
\overrightarrow{AB} = (2, 2, -1)
\qquad
\Longrightarrow
\qquad
r :
\begin{cases}
x = 1 + 2t \\
y = -2 + 2t \\
z = -t
\end{cases}
$$
$$
C : \quad
\begin{cases}
5 = 1 + 2t \\
2 = -2 + 2t \\
-2 = -t
\end{cases}
\qquad
\Longrightarrow
\qquad
t = 2 \ \text{in all three}
\qquad
\Longrightarrow
\qquad
C \in r
$$
$$
D : \quad
\begin{cases}
-1 = 1 + 2t \\
-4 = -2 + 2t \\
2 = -t
\end{cases}
\qquad
\Longrightarrow
\qquad
t = -1, \quad t = -1, \quad t = -2
\qquad
\Longrightarrow
\qquad
D \notin r
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

For the **line through two points**, use $\overrightarrow{AB} = (2, 2, -1)$ as the direction vector and $A$ as the starting point. Obtain the parametric equations by adding to the coordinates of $A$ the components of $\overrightarrow{AB}$ multiplied by $t$.

A point lies on $r$ if *a single* value of $t$ satisfies all three equations simultaneously. For $C$ all three equations give $t = 2$, so $C$ **lies on** $r$. For $D$ the first two equations give $t = -1$ and the third gives $t = -2$. Therefore no value of $t$ satisfies all three, and $D$ **does not lie on** $r$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Write the Cartesian equations of the line
$$
r :
\begin{cases}
x = 2 + t \\
y = 1 - 2t \\
z = 3 + 3t
\end{cases}
$$

$$
t = x - 2,
\qquad
t = \frac{y - 1}{-2},
\qquad
t = \frac{z - 3}{3}
$$
$$
x - 2 = \frac{y - 1}{-2}
\qquad
\Longrightarrow
\qquad
-2x + 4 = y - 1
\qquad
\Longrightarrow
\qquad
2x + y - 5 = 0
$$
$$
x - 2 = \frac{z - 3}{3}
\qquad
\Longrightarrow
\qquad
3x - 6 = z - 3
\qquad
\Longrightarrow
\qquad
3x - z - 3 = 0
$$
$$
r :
\begin{cases}
2x + y - 5 = 0 \\
3x - z - 3 = 0
\end{cases}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The **Cartesian equations** follow by eliminating the parameter. Solve each of the three equations for $t$. The three expressions all equal the same number $t$, so they equal one another.

Equating the first and second expressions and multiplying by $-2$ gives the equation of a plane, $2x + y - 5 = 0$. Equating the first and third and multiplying by $3$ gives the plane $3x - z - 3 = 0$. The line is the intersection of the two planes. As a check, the point $(2, 1, 3)$ on $r$ satisfies both equations, and the direction vector $(1, -2, 3)$ is perpendicular to the two normal vectors $(2, 1, 0)$ and $(3, 0, -1)$ because $2 - 2 + 0 = 0$ and $3 + 0 - 3 = 0$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Write the parametric equations of the line
$$
r :
\begin{cases}
x - y + z - 2 = 0 \\
2x + y - z - 1 = 0
\end{cases}
$$

$$
\vec{n}_1 = (1, -1, 1),
\qquad
\vec{n}_2 = (2, 1, -1)
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
\vec{n}_1 \times \vec{n}_2
&= \big( (-1) \cdot (-1) - 1 \cdot 1,\ \ 1 \cdot 2 - 1 \cdot (-1),\ \ 1 \cdot 1 - (-1) \cdot 2 \big) \\[6pt]
&= (0, 3, 3)
\end{aligned}
$$
$$
\vec{v} = (0, 1, 1)
$$
$$
z = 0:
\qquad
\begin{cases}
x - y = 2 \\
2x + y = 1
\end{cases}
\qquad
\Longrightarrow
\qquad
x = 1, \quad y = -1
$$
$$
r :
\begin{cases}
x = 1 \\
y = -1 + t \\
z = t
\end{cases}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

By the theorem on the **direction vector of a line given as the intersection of two planes**, a direction vector is the cross product of the normal vectors of the two planes. This gives $(0, 3, 3)$, and dividing by $3$ gives another possible direction vector, $\vec{v} = (0, 1, 1)$.

We also need a point on the line. Set $z = 0$ and solve the system for $x$ and $y$. Adding the two equations gives $3x = 3$, so $x = 1$, and the first equation gives $y = x - 2 = -1$. Therefore the line passes through $(1, -1, 0)$.

In the parametric equations the coordinate $x$ always equals $1$, so the line lies in the plane $x = 1$. Adding the two Cartesian equations gives the same result because the terms in $y$ and $z$ cancel, leaving $3x - 3 = 0$.

</div>

## Exercise 4

Write the parametric and Cartesian equations of the line $s$ through $P(1, 2, -1)$ parallel to the line
$$
r :
\begin{cases}
x + y - 1 = 0 \\
y - z - 2 = 0
\end{cases}
$$

$$
\vec{v} = (1, 1, 0) \times (0, 1, -1) = \big( 1 \cdot (-1) - 0 \cdot 1,\ \ 0 \cdot 0 - 1 \cdot (-1),\ \ 1 \cdot 1 - 1 \cdot 0 \big) = (-1, 1, 1)
$$
$$
s :
\begin{cases}
x = 1 - t \\
y = 2 + t \\
z = -1 + t
\end{cases}
$$
$$
x + y = (1 - t) + (2 + t) = 3,
\qquad
y - z = (2 + t) - (-1 + t) = 3
$$
$$
s :
\begin{cases}
x + y - 3 = 0 \\
y - z - 3 = 0
\end{cases}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Two parallel lines have a common direction vector. A direction vector of $r$ is the **cross product** of the normal vectors $(1, 1, 0)$ and $(0, 1, -1)$ of the two planes that define it, which equals $(-1, 1, 1)$. The parametric equations of $s$ can therefore be written using the point $P$ and the vector $(-1, 1, 1)$.

For the Cartesian equations, combine the parametric equations to eliminate $t$. Adding the first two gives $x + y = 3$, and subtracting the third from the second gives $y - z = 3$. The two planes $x + y - 3 = 0$ and $y - z - 3 = 0$ are parallel to the planes that define $r$ because they have the same normal vectors. This confirms that $s$ is **parallel** to $r$.

</div>
