## Exercise 1

Given the line $r : x = 1 + 2t$, $y = -1 + t$, $z = 3 - t$, determine the position of $r$ relative to the planes $\pi_1 : x - y + z - 2 = 0$ and $\pi_2 : x + 2y + z - 5 = 0$, and calculate any points of intersection.

$$
\vec{v} = (2, 1, -1)
$$
$$
\pi_1 : \quad \vec{v} \cdot \vec{n}_1 = 2 - 1 - 1 = 0
$$
$$
(1 + 2t) - (-1 + t) + (3 - t) - 2 = 3 \neq 0
$$
$$
\pi_2 : \quad \vec{v} \cdot \vec{n}_2 = 2 + 2 - 1 = 3 \neq 0
$$
$$
(1 + 2t) + 2\,(-1 + t) + (3 - t) - 5 = 3t - 3 = 0
\qquad
\Longrightarrow
\qquad
t = 1
\qquad
\Longrightarrow
\qquad
(3, 0, 2)
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

By the theorem on the **relative positions of a line and a plane**, calculate the dot product of the direction vector of $r$ and the normal vector of the plane.

For $\pi_1$, with $\vec{n}_1 = (1, -1, 1)$, the dot product is zero, so $r$ is parallel to $\pi_1$. To determine whether the line lies in the plane, substitute the equations of $r$ into the equation of $\pi_1$. The terms in $t$ cancel because $2t - t - t = 0$, leaving $3 = 0$, which is impossible. Therefore the line is **parallel** to $\pi_1$ and has no points in common with it.

For $\pi_2$, with $\vec{n}_2 = (1, 2, 1)$, the dot product is $3$, so $r$ and $\pi_2$ **intersect**. Substituting the equations of $r$ gives $3t - 3 = 0$, or $t = 1$, and the intersection point is $(3, 0, 2)$. It satisfies the equation of $\pi_2$ because $3 + 0 + 2 - 5 = 0$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Given the line $r : x = 1 + t$, $y = 2 - t$, $z = 2t$, determine its position relative to the lines
$$
s_1 : \ x = 3 - 2s, \ \ y = s, \ \ z = 1 - 4s
\qquad \text{and} \qquad
s_2 : \ x = 2 + s, \ \ y = 1 + s, \ \ z = -1 + 3s .
$$

$$
\vec{v} = (1, -1, 2),
\qquad
\vec{w}_1 = (-2, 1, -4) = -2\,\vec{v}
$$
$$
Q(3, 0, 1) \in s_1 :
\qquad
\begin{cases}
3 = 1 + t \\
0 = 2 - t \\
1 = 2t
\end{cases}
\qquad
\Longrightarrow
\qquad
t = 2, \quad t = 2, \quad t = \frac{1}{2}
\qquad
\Longrightarrow
\qquad
Q \notin r
$$
$$
\vec{w}_2 = (1, 1, 3)
$$
$$
\begin{cases}
1 + t = 2 + s \\
2 - t = 1 + s \\
2t = -1 + 3s
\end{cases}
\qquad
\Longrightarrow
\qquad
\begin{cases}
t - s = 1 \\
t + s = 1 \\
2t - 3s = -1
\end{cases}
$$
$$
t = 1, \quad s = 0
\qquad
\Longrightarrow
\qquad
2 \cdot 1 - 3 \cdot 0 = 2 \neq -1
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

To determine the **relative positions of two lines**, first compare their direction vectors. The vector $\vec{w}_1$ of $s_1$ equals $-2\,\vec{v}$, so $r$ and $s_1$ are parallel. To determine whether they coincide, it suffices to check whether a point on $s_1$ lies on $r$. The point $Q(3, 0, 1)$, obtained for $s = 0$, would give $t = 2$ from the first two equations and $t = \dfrac{1}{2}$ from the third, so it does not lie on $r$. Therefore the lines $r$ and $s_1$ are **parallel and distinct**.

The vector $\vec{w}_2 = (1, 1, 3)$ is not proportional to $\vec{v}$ because their first components agree but their second components do not. Therefore $r$ and $s_2$ are not parallel. Equate the corresponding coordinates, using different parameters for the two lines. Adding the first two equations gives $2t = 2$, so $t = 1$ and $s = 0$. These values do not satisfy the third equation, so the system has no solutions. The lines have no points in common and are not parallel, so they are **skew**.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Consider the plane $\pi : 2x + y - z + 1 = 0$ and, for a variable real parameter $k$, the line
$$
r_k : \quad x = 1 + t, \quad y = -t, \quad z = 2 + k t .
$$

1. Find $k$ so that $r_k$ is parallel to $\pi$, and determine whether the line lies in $\pi$ for that value.
2. Determine whether there is a value of $k$ for which $r_k$ is perpendicular to $\pi$.

**Part 1**
$$
\vec{v} \cdot \vec{n} = (1, -1, k) \cdot (2, 1, -1) = 2 - 1 - k = 1 - k = 0
\qquad
\Longrightarrow
\qquad
k = 1
$$
$$
P_0(1, 0, 2) : \quad 2 + 0 - 2 + 1 = 1 \neq 0
\qquad
\Longrightarrow
\qquad
P_0 \notin \pi
$$

**Part 2**
$$
(1, -1, k) = \lambda\,(2, 1, -1)
\qquad
\Longrightarrow
\qquad
\begin{cases}
1 = 2\lambda \\
-1 = \lambda \\
k = -\lambda
\end{cases}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

**Part 1.** By the theorem on the **relative positions of a line and a plane**, $r_k$ is parallel to $\pi$ if and only if the direction vector $(1, -1, k)$ is perpendicular to the normal vector $(2, 1, -1)$. The dot product is $1 - k$, which vanishes for $k = 1$. To determine whether $r_1$ lies in $\pi$, it suffices to check one of its points, such as $P_0(1, 0, 2)$, obtained for $t = 0$. The coordinates of $P_0$ do not satisfy the equation of $\pi$, so $r_1$ is **parallel to but does not lie in** $\pi$.

**Part 2.** By the theorem on a **line perpendicular to a plane**, $r_k$ is perpendicular to $\pi$ if and only if its direction vector is parallel to the normal vector, that is, if there is a scalar $\lambda$ such that $(1, -1, k) = \lambda\,(2, 1, -1)$. The first equation gives $\lambda = \dfrac{1}{2}$ and the second gives $\lambda = -1$, so the system is inconsistent for every $k$. Therefore **there is no** value of $k$ for which $r_k$ is perpendicular to $\pi$.

</div>

## Exercise 4

Given the line $r : x = 2 + t$, $y = -1 + 2t$, $z = -t$ and the point $P(1, 3, 1)$, find the line $s$ that passes through $P$, meets $r$ and is perpendicular to $r$.

$$
H(2 + t,\ -1 + 2t,\ -t) \in r,
\qquad
\overrightarrow{PH} = (1 + t,\ -4 + 2t,\ -1 - t)
$$
$$
\overrightarrow{PH} \cdot \vec{v} = (1 + t) + 2\,(-4 + 2t) - (-1 - t) = 6t - 6 = 0
\qquad
\Longrightarrow
\qquad
t = 1
$$
$$
H(3, 1, -1),
\qquad
\overrightarrow{PH} = (2, -2, -2)
$$
$$
s :
\begin{cases}
x = 1 + u \\
y = 3 - u \\
z = 1 - u
\end{cases}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The line $s$ passes through $P$ and meets $r$ at a point $H$ whose coordinates have the form of a general point on $r$. Therefore write $H$ in terms of $t$ and find the vector $\overrightarrow{PH}$ by subtracting the coordinates of $P$.

The line $s$ is perpendicular to $r$ if $\overrightarrow{PH}$ is perpendicular to the direction vector $\vec{v} = (1, 2, -1)$, that is, if the **dot product** is zero. Expanding the products gives $1 + t - 8 + 4t + 1 + t = 6t - 6$, which vanishes for $t = 1$. Therefore the intersection point is $H(3, 1, -1)$ and the vector $\overrightarrow{PH} = (2, -2, -2)$ gives the direction of $s$.

Dividing by $2$ gives the direction vector $(1, -1, -1)$. Therefore the line $s$ has equations $x = 1 + u$, $y = 3 - u$, $z = 1 - u$, with parameter $u$ to distinguish it from the parameter of $r$. For $u = 2$ we obtain $H(3, 1, -1)$ itself. The point $P$ does not lie on $r$ because the first equation of $r$ would give $t = -1$ and hence $y = -3 \neq 3$. For this reason the line $s$ is unique.

</div>
