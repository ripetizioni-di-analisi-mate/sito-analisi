## Exercise 1

Prove that the equation $x^3 + 2x - 5 = 0$ has exactly one real solution and that it lies between $1$ and $2$.

$$
f(x) = x^3 + 2x - 5
$$
$$
f(1) = 1 + 2 - 5 = -2 < 0,
\qquad
f(2) = 8 + 4 - 5 = 7 > 0
$$
$$
x_1 < x_2
\quad \Longrightarrow \quad
x_1^3 + 2x_1 - 5 < x_2^3 + 2x_2 - 5
$$

The equation has exactly one solution, which lies in $(1, 2)$.

<div class="spiegazione">

The function $f$ is a polynomial, so it is continuous on the closed interval $[1, 2]$. Its values at the endpoints have opposite signs. Therefore, the hypotheses of **Bolzano's theorem** hold, and $f$ is zero at least once in $(1, 2)$.

To prove uniqueness, observe that $x^3$ and $2x - 5$ are strictly increasing on $\mathbb{R}$. If $x_1 < x_2$, then $x_1^3 < x_2^3$ and $2x_1 - 5 < 2x_2 - 5$, and adding the two inequalities gives $f(x_1) < f(x_2)$. Thus $f$ is strictly increasing on $\mathbb{R}$ and attains the value $0$ at most once. Therefore, the equation has **exactly one** real solution, which lies in $(1, 2)$. Its approximate value is $1.33$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Prove that the equation $x^3 - 3x + 1 = 0$ has three real solutions, and find three intervals of length $1$ with integer endpoints, each containing one solution.

| $x$ | $-2$ | $-1$ | $0$ | $1$ | $2$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $f(x) = x^3 - 3x + 1$ | $-1$ | $3$ | $1$ | $-1$ | $3$ |

$$
f(-2) \cdot f(-1) < 0,
\qquad
f(0) \cdot f(1) < 0,
\qquad
f(1) \cdot f(2) < 0
$$

The three solutions lie in $(-2, -1)$, $(0, 1)$ and $(1, 2)$.

<div class="spiegazione">

The function $f$ is a polynomial, so it is continuous on every interval. Evaluate $f$ at the integers from $-2$ to $2$ and look for intervals on which $f$ changes sign. The sign changes between $-2$ and $-1$, between $0$ and $1$, and between $1$ and $2$.

On each of the three closed intervals $[-2, -1]$, $[0, 1]$ and $[1, 2]$, **Bolzano's theorem** applies because $f$ is continuous and its endpoint values have opposite signs. Thus $f$ has at least one zero in each of the three open intervals. These intervals are disjoint, so the three solutions found are distinct. A cubic equation has at most three solutions, so there are **exactly three** real solutions.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Prove that the equation $\cos x = x$ has exactly one solution in $\left[ 0, \dfrac{\pi}{2} \right]$.

$$
g(x) = \cos x - x
$$
$$
g(0) = 1 - 0 = 1 > 0,
\qquad
g\left( \frac{\pi}{2} \right) = 0 - \frac{\pi}{2} = -\frac{\pi}{2} < 0
$$

The equation has exactly one solution in $\left( 0, \dfrac{\pi}{2} \right)$, approximately $0.74$.

<div class="spiegazione">

Moving all terms to the left makes the solutions of the equation the zeros of $g(x) = \cos x - x$. The function $g$ is the difference of two continuous functions, so it is continuous on $\left[ 0, \dfrac{\pi}{2} \right]$. At the endpoints it takes the positive value $1$ and the negative value $-\dfrac{\pi}{2}$. Thus by **Bolzano's theorem** it has at least one zero in the open interval.

On $\left[ 0, \dfrac{\pi}{2} \right]$ the function $\cos x$ is strictly decreasing, and $-x$ is also strictly decreasing. The sum of two strictly decreasing functions is strictly decreasing, so $g$ is zero **exactly once**. The bisection method gives the approximate value of the zero.

</div>

## Exercise 4

Determine whether the extreme value theorem applies to each function on the given interval, and find the maximum and minimum if they exist.

1. $f(x) = x^2 - 2x$ on $[0, 3]$.
2. $g(x) = \dfrac{1}{x}$ on $(0, 2]$.

**Function $f$**
$$
f(x) = (x - 1)^2 - 1,
\qquad
f(1) = -1,
\qquad
f(0) = 0,
\qquad
f(3) = 3
$$

**Function $g$**
$$
\lim_{x \to 0^+} \frac{1}{x} = +\infty,
\qquad
g(2) = \frac{1}{2},
\qquad
\frac{1}{x} \geq \frac{1}{2} \quad \text{for } 0 < x \leq 2
$$

The theorem applies to $f$, and $f$ has minimum $-1$ at $x = 1$ and maximum $3$ at $x = 3$. The theorem does not apply to $g$ because the interval is not closed. The function $g$ has minimum $\dfrac{1}{2}$ at $x = 2$ and has no maximum.

<div class="spiegazione">

**Function $f$.** The function is a polynomial, so it is continuous on the closed, bounded interval $[0, 3]$. The hypotheses of the **extreme value theorem** hold, so the theorem guarantees that the maximum and minimum exist. Completing the square shows that the graph is an upward-opening parabola with vertex $(1, -1)$. The vertex lies in the interval, so the minimum is $f(1) = -1$. The function decreases to the left of the vertex and increases to its right, so the maximum must occur at an endpoint. Since $f(0) = 0$ and $f(3) = 3$, the maximum is $3$, attained at $x = 3$.

**Function $g$.** The function is continuous on $(0, 2]$, but the interval excludes the endpoint $0$, so the extreme value theorem does *not* apply. The theorem gives no conclusion here, so we examine the existence of a maximum and minimum directly. As $x \to 0^+$, the function approaches $+\infty$, so it takes arbitrarily large values and **has no maximum**. The function $\dfrac{1}{x}$ is decreasing for $x > 0$, so the smallest value occurs at the right endpoint of the interval, and the minimum is $g(2) = \dfrac{1}{2}$.

</div>
