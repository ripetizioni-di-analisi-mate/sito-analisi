## Exercise 1

Find and classify the points of discontinuity of $f(x) = \dfrac{x^2 - 1}{x^2 - x}$.

$$
x^2 - x = x\,(x - 1) = 0
\iff
x = 0 \quad \text{or} \quad x = 1,
\qquad
D = \mathbb{R} \setminus \{0, 1\}
$$
$$
f(x) = \frac{(x - 1)(x + 1)}{x\,(x - 1)} = \frac{x + 1}{x}
\qquad \text{for } x \in D
$$
$$
\lim_{x \to 1} f(x) = \lim_{x \to 1} \frac{x + 1}{x} = 2
$$
$$
\lim_{x \to 0^-} \frac{x + 1}{x} = -\infty,
\qquad
\lim_{x \to 0^+} \frac{x + 1}{x} = +\infty
$$

The point $x = 1$ is a discontinuity of the third kind that can be removed by setting $f(1) = 2$. The point $x = 0$ is a discontinuity of the second kind.

::grafico{f="(x+1)/x" punti="1,2,vuoto" asintoti="x=0" xmin=-4 xmax=4 ymin=-4 ymax=6 legenda="y = \dfrac{x^2 - 1}{x^2 - x}" titolo="The graph of $\dfrac{x^2 - 1}{x^2 - x}$ omits the point $(1, 2)$, where the discontinuity is removable, and has vertical asymptote $x = 0$"}

<div class="spiegazione">

The function is rational, so it is continuous on its domain. The possible discontinuities are at the zeros of the denominator, namely $0$ and $1$. To find the limits, factor the numerator and denominator. The numerator is a difference of squares, and the denominator has a common factor of $x$. We can cancel the common factor $x - 1$ because it is different from $0$ throughout the domain.

At $1$ the simplified function $\dfrac{x + 1}{x}$ is continuous, so substitution gives a limit of $2$. The limit is finite but the function is undefined at $1$, so the point is a **discontinuity of the third kind**.

At $0$ the numerator $x + 1$ approaches $1$, and the denominator approaches $0$ through negative values from the left and positive values from the right. Both limits are infinite, so $0$ is a **discontinuity of the second kind**.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Classify the discontinuity of $f(x) = \dfrac{x^2 - 2x}{|x - 2|}$.

$$
D = \mathbb{R} \setminus \{2\},
\qquad
f(x) = \frac{x\,(x - 2)}{|x - 2|}
$$
$$
f(x) =
\begin{cases}
-x & \text{if } x < 2 \\
x & \text{if } x > 2
\end{cases}
$$
$$
\lim_{x \to 2^-} f(x) = \lim_{x \to 2^-} (-x) = -2,
\qquad
\lim_{x \to 2^+} f(x) = \lim_{x \to 2^+} x = 2
$$

The point $x = 2$ is a discontinuity of the first kind with a jump of size $|2 - (-2)| = 4$.

<div class="spiegazione">

The denominator is zero only when $x = 2$, so the only point to examine is $2$. The absolute value $|x - 2|$ equals $x - 2$ for $x > 2$ and $-(x - 2)$ for $x < 2$. For $x > 2$ the factor $x - 2$ cancels, leaving $x$. For $x < 2$ a minus sign remains after cancellation, giving $-x$.

Using $-x$ gives a left-hand limit of $-2$, and using $x$ gives a right-hand limit of $2$. The limits are finite and unequal, so $2$ is a **discontinuity of the first kind**. The size of the jump is the distance between the two limits, namely $4$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Classify the discontinuity of $f(x) = e^{\frac{1}{x - 1}}$.

$$
D = \mathbb{R} \setminus \{1\}
$$
$$
x \to 1^+
\quad \Longrightarrow \quad
\frac{1}{x - 1} \to +\infty
\quad \Longrightarrow \quad
e^{\frac{1}{x - 1}} \to +\infty
$$
$$
x \to 1^-
\quad \Longrightarrow \quad
\frac{1}{x - 1} \to -\infty
\quad \Longrightarrow \quad
e^{\frac{1}{x - 1}} \to 0
$$

The point $x = 1$ is a discontinuity of the second kind.

<div class="spiegazione">

The exponent $\dfrac{1}{x - 1}$ is undefined when $x = 1$, so the point to examine is $1$. We first find the limit of the exponent and then the limit of the exponential function.

As $x \to 1^+$, the denominator $x - 1$ approaches $0$ through positive values. Hence the exponent approaches $+\infty$, and the exponential with base $e > 1$ approaches $+\infty$. As $x \to 1^-$, the denominator is negative, the exponent approaches $-\infty$ and the exponential approaches $0$. The left-hand limit is finite but the right-hand limit is infinite, so $1$ is a **discontinuity of the second kind**. It is enough for *one* of the two limits to be infinite.

</div>

## Exercise 4

Find and classify the discontinuities of $f(x) = \dfrac{\sin x}{x^2 + 3x}$. Where possible, remove a discontinuity by extending the function by continuity.

$$
x^2 + 3x = x\,(x + 3) = 0
\iff
x = 0 \quad \text{or} \quad x = -3
$$
$$
\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sin x}{x\,(x + 3)} = \lim_{x \to 0} \left( \frac{\sin x}{x} \cdot \frac{1}{x + 3} \right) = 1 \cdot \frac{1}{3} = \frac{1}{3}
$$
$$
\sin(-3) \approx -0.14,
\qquad
\lim_{x \to -3^-} f(x) = -\infty,
\qquad
\lim_{x \to -3^+} f(x) = +\infty
$$
$$
g(x) =
\begin{cases}
\dfrac{\sin x}{x^2 + 3x} & \text{if } x \neq 0, \ x \neq -3 \\[8pt]
\dfrac{1}{3} & \text{if } x = 0
\end{cases}
$$

The point $x = 0$ is a discontinuity of the third kind that is removed by the function $g$. The point $x = -3$ is a discontinuity of the second kind.

<div class="spiegazione">

The denominator is zero at $0$ and $-3$. At $0$ the limit has the form $\dfrac{0}{0}$ because $\sin 0 = 0$ as well. Write the fraction as the product of $\dfrac{\sin x}{x}$, which approaches $1$ by the **standard limit**, and $\dfrac{1}{x + 3}$, which approaches $\dfrac{1}{3}$. The limit is finite, so $0$ is a **discontinuity of the third kind**. The function $g$, equal to $f$ away from $0$ and to $\dfrac{1}{3}$ at $0$, is continuous at $0$.

At $-3$ the numerator approaches $\sin(-3)$, a negative number because $-3$ radians lies between $-\pi$ and $-\dfrac{\pi}{2}$. The denominator $x\,(x + 3)$ approaches $0$. For $x$ close to $-3$, the factor $x$ is negative, whereas $x + 3$ is positive to the right of $-3$ and negative to the left. Therefore, the denominator is negative on the right and positive on the left, and the quotient approaches $+\infty$ from the right and $-\infty$ from the left. The limits are infinite, so $-3$ is a **discontinuity of the second kind**.

</div>
