## Exercise 1

Find the asymptotes of $f(x) = \dfrac{x^2 + 3x}{x - 1}$.

$$
D = \mathbb{R} \setminus \{1\},
\qquad
\lim_{x \to 1^-} f(x) = \frac{4}{0^-} = -\infty,
\qquad
\lim_{x \to 1^+} f(x) = \frac{4}{0^+} = +\infty
$$
$$
m = \lim_{x \to \pm\infty} \frac{f(x)}{x} = \lim_{x \to \pm\infty} \frac{x^2 + 3x}{x^2 - x} = 1
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
q
&= \lim_{x \to \pm\infty} \left( \frac{x^2 + 3x}{x - 1} - x \right) \\[6pt]
&= \lim_{x \to \pm\infty} \frac{x^2 + 3x - x^2 + x}{x - 1} \\[6pt]
&= \lim_{x \to \pm\infty} \frac{4x}{x - 1} = 4
\end{aligned}
$$

The line $x = 1$ is a vertical asymptote, and the line $y = x + 4$ is an oblique asymptote.

::grafico{f="(x^2+3*x)/(x-1);x+4" asintoti="x=1" xmin=-8 xmax=10 ymin=-12 ymax=18 legenda="y = \dfrac{x^2 + 3x}{x - 1};y = x + 4" titolo="The graph of $\dfrac{x^2 + 3x}{x - 1}$ has vertical asymptote $x = 1$ and oblique asymptote $y = x + 4$"}

<div class="spiegazione">

At $1$ the numerator equals $1 + 3 = 4$ and the denominator approaches $0$, taking negative values on the left and positive values on the right. Therefore, the two limits are $-\infty$ and $+\infty$, and the line $x = 1$ is a **vertical asymptote**. The numerator has degree $2$ and the denominator has degree $1$, so the function approaches $\pm\infty$ as $x \to \pm\infty$ and there are no horizontal asymptotes.

To find the oblique asymptote, use the formulas for $m$ and $q$. The numerator and denominator of $\dfrac{f(x)}{x}$ both have degree $2$, and both coefficients of $x^2$ equal $1$, so $m = 1$. To find $q$, subtract $x$ and use the common denominator $x - 1$. The terms $x^2$ cancel, leaving $\dfrac{4x}{x - 1}$, which approaches $4$. Both limits are finite and $m \neq 0$, so the line $y = x + 4$ is an **oblique asymptote**. The division $x^2 + 3x = (x - 1)(x + 4) + 4$ gives the same result.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Find the oblique asymptote of $f(x) = \dfrac{2x^3 - x^2 + 1}{x^2 + 1}$ by polynomial division.

$$
2x^3 - x^2 + 1 = \left( x^2 + 1 \right)(2x - 1) + (-2x + 2)
$$
$$
f(x) = 2x - 1 + \frac{-2x + 2}{x^2 + 1}
$$
$$
\lim_{x \to \pm\infty} \frac{-2x + 2}{x^2 + 1} = 0
$$

The line $y = 2x - 1$ is an oblique asymptote. The function has no vertical asymptotes.

<div class="spiegazione">

The denominator $x^2 + 1$ is always positive, so the domain is $\mathbb{R}$ and there are *no* vertical asymptotes. The numerator has degree $3$ and the denominator has degree $2$, so the degree of the numerator is exactly $1$ greater than that of the denominator, and an oblique asymptote exists.

In the division, the first term of the quotient is $\dfrac{2x^3}{x^2} = 2x$. Multiplying $2x$ by $x^2 + 1$ and subtracting gives $-x^2 - 2x + 1$. The second term of the quotient is $-1$. Multiplying $-1$ by $x^2 + 1$ and subtracting leaves $-2x + 2$, whose degree is less than $2$. The remainder divided by $x^2 + 1$ approaches $0$ because the denominator has higher degree. Thus $f(x) - (2x - 1)$ approaches $0$, and the quotient $2x - 1$ gives the equation of the **oblique asymptote**.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Find the asymptotes of $f(x) = \sqrt{x^2 + 4x}$.

$$
x^2 + 4x \geq 0
\iff
x \leq -4 \quad \text{or} \quad x \geq 0
$$

**As $x \to +\infty$**
$$
m = \lim_{x \to +\infty} \frac{\sqrt{x^2 + 4x}}{x} = \lim_{x \to +\infty} \sqrt{1 + \frac{4}{x}} = 1
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
q
&= \lim_{x \to +\infty} \left( \sqrt{x^2 + 4x} - x \right) \\[6pt]
&= \lim_{x \to +\infty} \frac{x^2 + 4x - x^2}{\sqrt{x^2 + 4x} + x} \\[6pt]
&= \lim_{x \to +\infty} \frac{4x}{x\left( \sqrt{1 + \dfrac{4}{x}} + 1 \right)} = \frac{4}{2} = 2
\end{aligned}
$$

**As $x \to -\infty$**
$$
m = \lim_{x \to -\infty} \frac{\sqrt{x^2 + 4x}}{x} = \lim_{x \to -\infty} \frac{-x \sqrt{1 + \dfrac{4}{x}}}{x} = -1
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
q
&= \lim_{x \to -\infty} \left( \sqrt{x^2 + 4x} + x \right) \\[6pt]
&= \lim_{x \to -\infty} \frac{x^2 + 4x - x^2}{\sqrt{x^2 + 4x} - x} \\[6pt]
&= \lim_{x \to -\infty} \frac{4x}{-x\left( \sqrt{1 + \dfrac{4}{x}} + 1 \right)} = \frac{4}{-2} = -2
\end{aligned}
$$

The line $y = x + 2$ is an oblique asymptote to the right, and the line $y = -x - 2$ is an oblique asymptote to the left.

::grafico{f="sqrt(x^2+4*x)" segmenti="0,2,8,10;-12,10,-4,2" xmin=-12 xmax=8 ymin=-2 ymax=10 legenda="y = \sqrt{x^2 + 4x}" titolo="The graph of $\sqrt{x^2 + 4x}$ approaches the line $y = x + 2$ to the right and the line $y = -x - 2$ to the left (dashed)"}

<div class="spiegazione">

The radicand $x^2 + 4x = x\,(x + 4)$ is a trinomial with roots $-4$ and $0$, positive outside the interval between its roots. The domain therefore consists of two rays, and we look for asymptotes separately on the right and left. The function is continuous on its domain and has no vertical asymptotes because the domain includes its endpoints $-4$ and $0$.

To calculate $m$, take $x^2$ outside the square root, using $\sqrt{x^2} = |x|$. For $x > 0$ we have $|x| = x$, and for $x < 0$ we have $|x| = -x$. This is why $m$ equals $1$ on the right and $-1$ on the left.

The limit giving $q$ has the form $\infty - \infty$. Multiply and divide by the sum or difference of the square root and $x$ so that the numerator becomes the difference of squares $x^2 + 4x - x^2 = 4x$. In the denominator, again factor out $|x|$ with the appropriate sign, obtaining $q = 2$ on the right and $q = -2$ on the left. Therefore, the function has two **different oblique asymptotes**, $y = x + 2$ as $x \to +\infty$ and $y = -x - 2$ as $x \to -\infty$.

</div>

## Exercise 4

Find the asymptotes of $f(x) = x\,e^{\frac{1}{x}}$.

$$
D = \mathbb{R} \setminus \{0\}
$$
$$
\lim_{x \to 0^+} x\,e^{\frac{1}{x}} = \lim_{t \to +\infty} \frac{e^{t}}{t} = +\infty
\qquad
\left( t = \frac{1}{x} \right)
$$
$$
\lim_{x \to 0^-} x\,e^{\frac{1}{x}} = 0 \cdot 0 = 0
$$
$$
m = \lim_{x \to \pm\infty} \frac{x\,e^{\frac{1}{x}}}{x} = \lim_{x \to \pm\infty} e^{\frac{1}{x}} = e^{0} = 1
$$
$$
q = \lim_{x \to \pm\infty} \left( x\,e^{\frac{1}{x}} - x \right) = \lim_{x \to \pm\infty} \frac{e^{\frac{1}{x}} - 1}{\dfrac{1}{x}} = \lim_{t \to 0} \frac{e^{t} - 1}{t} = 1
$$

The line $x = 0$ is a vertical asymptote from the right, and the line $y = x + 1$ is an oblique asymptote.

<div class="spiegazione">

As $x \to 0^+$, the limit has the form $0 \cdot \infty$. Substituting $t = \dfrac{1}{x}$ gives $t \to +\infty$ and $x = \dfrac{1}{t}$, so the product becomes $\dfrac{e^{t}}{t}$. The exponential grows faster than $t$, so the quotient approaches $+\infty$ and the line $x = 0$ is a **vertical asymptote** from the right. As $x \to 0^-$, however, the exponent approaches $-\infty$, the exponential approaches $0$ and the product approaches $0$.

As $x \to \pm\infty$, the exponent $\dfrac{1}{x}$ approaches $0$, so $e^{\frac{1}{x}}$ approaches $1$ and $m = 1$. The limit giving $q$ has the form $\infty \cdot 0$ after we factor out $x$. Write $x$ as $\dfrac{1}{\frac{1}{x}}$ and set $t = \dfrac{1}{x}$, which approaches $0$. This gives the **standard limit** for the exponential function, which equals $1$. Both limits are finite, so the line $y = x + 1$ is an **oblique asymptote** to both the right and the left.

</div>
