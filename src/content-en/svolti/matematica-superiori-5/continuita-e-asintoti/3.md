## Exercise 1

Find the asymptotes of $f(x) = \dfrac{2x + 1}{x - 3}$.

$$
D = \mathbb{R} \setminus \{3\}
$$
$$
\lim_{x \to 3^-} \frac{2x + 1}{x - 3} = \frac{7}{0^-} = -\infty,
\qquad
\lim_{x \to 3^+} \frac{2x + 1}{x - 3} = \frac{7}{0^+} = +\infty
$$
$$
\lim_{x \to \pm\infty} \frac{2x + 1}{x - 3} = \lim_{x \to \pm\infty} \frac{2 + \dfrac{1}{x}}{1 - \dfrac{3}{x}} = 2
$$

The line $x = 3$ is a vertical asymptote, and the line $y = 2$ is a horizontal asymptote.

::grafico{f="(2*x+1)/(x-3)" asintoti="x=3;y=2" xmin=-6 xmax=12 ymin=-6 ymax=10 legenda="y = \dfrac{2x + 1}{x - 3}" titolo="The graph of $\dfrac{2x + 1}{x - 3}$ has vertical asymptote $x = 3$ and horizontal asymptote $y = 2$"}

<div class="spiegazione">

The denominator is zero when $x = 3$, where the numerator equals $2 \cdot 3 + 1 = 7$. Thus the numerator approaches a positive number, and the denominator approaches $0$ through negative values to the left of $3$ and positive values to the right. The quotient therefore approaches $-\infty$ from the left and $+\infty$ from the right, and the line $x = 3$ is a **vertical asymptote**.

As $x \to \pm\infty$, the limit has the form $\dfrac{\infty}{\infty}$. After we divide the numerator and denominator by $x$, the fractions $\dfrac{1}{x}$ and $\dfrac{3}{x}$ approach $0$, so the limit is $\dfrac{2}{1} = 2$. The numerator and denominator have the same degree, and the result is the ratio of the coefficients of $x$. The line $y = 2$ is a **horizontal asymptote** to both the right and the left.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Find the asymptotes of $f(x) = \dfrac{x^2 + 1}{x^2 - 4}$.

$$
x^2 - 4 = (x - 2)(x + 2),
\qquad
D = \mathbb{R} \setminus \{-2, 2\}
$$
$$
\lim_{x \to 2^-} f(x) = -\infty,
\qquad
\lim_{x \to 2^+} f(x) = +\infty
$$
$$
\lim_{x \to -2^-} f(x) = +\infty,
\qquad
\lim_{x \to -2^+} f(x) = -\infty
$$
$$
\lim_{x \to \pm\infty} \frac{x^2 + 1}{x^2 - 4} = \lim_{x \to \pm\infty} \frac{1 + \dfrac{1}{x^2}}{1 - \dfrac{4}{x^2}} = 1
$$

The lines $x = 2$ and $x = -2$ are vertical asymptotes, and the line $y = 1$ is a horizontal asymptote.

<div class="spiegazione">

The numerator $x^2 + 1$ is always positive and at $\pm 2$ it equals $5$. Therefore, the sign of the quotient near $\pm 2$ depends only on the sign of the denominator $x^2 - 4$, which is negative for $-2 < x < 2$ and positive for $x < -2$ and for $x > 2$.

As $x \to 2^-$, the point $x$ lies between $-2$ and $2$, so the denominator approaches $0$ through negative values and the quotient approaches $-\infty$. As $x \to 2^+$, the denominator is positive and the quotient approaches $+\infty$. At $-2$ the signs are reversed. To the left of $-2$ the denominator is positive, and to the right it is negative. All these limits are infinite, so the lines $x = \pm 2$ are **vertical asymptotes**. We obtain the same result by observing that $f$ is even because $f(-x) = f(x)$, so its graph is symmetric about the $y$-axis.

The numerator and denominator have degree $2$, and the coefficient of $x^2$ in both is $1$. Thus the limit at infinity is $1$, and the line $y = 1$ is a **horizontal asymptote**.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Find the asymptotes of $f(x) = \dfrac{e^{x}}{e^{x} - 1}$.

$$
e^{x} - 1 = 0
\iff
x = 0,
\qquad
D = \mathbb{R} \setminus \{0\}
$$
$$
\lim_{x \to 0^-} \frac{e^{x}}{e^{x} - 1} = \frac{1}{0^-} = -\infty,
\qquad
\lim_{x \to 0^+} \frac{e^{x}}{e^{x} - 1} = \frac{1}{0^+} = +\infty
$$
$$
\lim_{x \to +\infty} \frac{e^{x}}{e^{x} - 1} = \lim_{x \to +\infty} \frac{1}{1 - e^{-x}} = 1
$$
$$
\lim_{x \to -\infty} \frac{e^{x}}{e^{x} - 1} = \frac{0}{0 - 1} = 0
$$

The line $x = 0$ is a vertical asymptote. The line $y = 1$ is a horizontal asymptote to the right, and the line $y = 0$ is a horizontal asymptote to the left.

::grafico{f="exp(x)/(exp(x)-1)" asintoti="x=0;y=1" xmin=-5 xmax=5 ymin=-4 ymax=5 legenda="y = \dfrac{e^{x}}{e^{x} - 1}" titolo="The graph of $\dfrac{e^{x}}{e^{x} - 1}$ has vertical asymptote $x = 0$, horizontal asymptote $y = 1$ to the right and the $x$-axis as its horizontal asymptote to the left"}

<div class="spiegazione">

The denominator is zero when $e^{x} = 1$, that is, when $x = 0$. Near $0$ the numerator approaches $e^{0} = 1$. The denominator $e^{x} - 1$ is negative for $x < 0$ because $e^{x} < 1$, and positive for $x > 0$. Therefore, the quotient approaches $-\infty$ from the left and $+\infty$ from the right, and the line $x = 0$ is a **vertical asymptote**.

As $x \to +\infty$, the limit has the form $\dfrac{\infty}{\infty}$. Dividing the numerator and denominator by $e^{x}$ gives $\dfrac{1}{1 - e^{-x}}$, and since $e^{-x} \to 0$, the limit is $1$. As $x \to -\infty$, on the other hand, $e^{x} \to 0$, so the numerator approaches $0$ and the denominator approaches $-1$, giving a limit of $0$. The two limits at infinity differ, so the function has two different **horizontal asymptotes**, $y = 1$ to the right and $y = 0$ to the left.

</div>

## Exercise 4

Find the asymptotes of $f(x) = \dfrac{\ln x}{x - 1}$.

$$
D = \{ x > 0, \ x \neq 1 \} = (0, 1) \cup (1, +\infty)
$$
$$
\lim_{x \to 0^+} \frac{\ln x}{x - 1} = \frac{-\infty}{-1} = +\infty
$$
$$
\lim_{x \to 1} \frac{\ln x}{x - 1} = \lim_{t \to 0} \frac{\ln(1 + t)}{t} = 1
\qquad
(x = 1 + t)
$$
$$
\lim_{x \to +\infty} \frac{\ln x}{x - 1} = 0
$$

The line $x = 0$ is a vertical asymptote from the right, and the line $y = 0$ is a horizontal asymptote to the right. The line $x = 1$ is not an asymptote because at $1$ the discontinuity is removable.

<div class="spiegazione">

The logarithm requires $x > 0$, and the denominator requires $x \neq 1$. Thus the vertical lines to examine are $x = 0$, corresponding to an excluded endpoint of the domain, and $x = 1$, corresponding to an excluded point.

As $x \to 0^+$, the logarithm approaches $-\infty$ and the denominator approaches $-1$, so the quotient approaches $+\infty$. The line $x = 0$ is a **vertical asymptote** only from the right because to the left of $0$ the function is undefined.

As $x \to 1$, the limit has the form $\dfrac{0}{0}$. The substitution $x = 1 + t$ gives $t \to 0$, and the limit becomes the standard logarithmic limit, which equals $1$. The limit is finite, so the line $x = 1$ is *not* a vertical asymptote. Finally, as $x \to +\infty$, the logarithm grows more slowly than $x - 1$, so the quotient approaches $0$, and the line $y = 0$ is a **horizontal asymptote** to the right. There is no limit to evaluate as $x \to -\infty$ because the function is undefined for $x < 0$.

</div>
