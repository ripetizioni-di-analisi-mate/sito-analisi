## Exercise 1

Sketch the graph of $f(x) = \dfrac{x^3}{x^2 - 1}$.

$$
D = \mathbb{R} \setminus \{-1, 1\},
\qquad
f(-x) = \frac{-x^3}{x^2 - 1} = -f(x)
$$
$$
f(x) = 0
\iff
x = 0
$$

| | $x < -1$ | $-1 < x < 0$ | $0 < x < 1$ | $x > 1$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $x^3$ | $-$ | $-$ | $+$ | $+$ |
| $x^2 - 1$ | $+$ | $-$ | $-$ | $+$ |
| $f(x)$ | $-$ | $+$ | $-$ | $+$ |

$$
\lim_{x \to 1^-} f(x) = \frac{1}{0^-} = -\infty,
\qquad
\lim_{x \to 1^+} f(x) = \frac{1}{0^+} = +\infty
$$
$$
\lim_{x \to -1^-} f(x) = \frac{-1}{0^+} = -\infty,
\qquad
\lim_{x \to -1^+} f(x) = \frac{-1}{0^-} = +\infty
$$
$$
x^3 = x\left( x^2 - 1 \right) + x
\qquad
\Longrightarrow
\qquad
f(x) = x + \frac{x}{x^2 - 1}
$$

The function is odd, the graph passes through the origin, the lines $x = \pm 1$ are vertical asymptotes, and the line $y = x$ is an oblique asymptote.

::grafico{f="x^3/(x^2-1);x" punti="0,0,pieno" asintoti="x=-1;x=1" xmin=-6 xmax=6 ymin=-8 ymax=8 legenda="y = \dfrac{x^3}{x^2 - 1};y = x" titolo="Sketch of $\dfrac{x^3}{x^2 - 1}$ with vertical asymptotes $x = \pm 1$ and oblique asymptote $y = x$"}

<div class="spiegazione">

The denominator is zero when $x = \pm 1$, so these two points are excluded from the domain. The domain is symmetric about the origin and $f(-x) = -f(x)$, so the function is **odd** and the graph is symmetric about the origin. The numerator is zero only when $x = 0$, so the graph meets the axes only at the origin.

The sign is obtained from the table by considering the numerator and denominator separately. The numerator $x^3$ has the same sign as $x$. The denominator $x^2 - 1$ is negative between $-1$ and $1$ and positive outside that interval.

Near $1$ the numerator approaches $1$, and the sign of the denominator shows that the limit is $-\infty$ from the left and $+\infty$ from the right. Near $-1$ the numerator approaches $-1$ and the signs are reversed. Therefore, the lines $x = \pm 1$ are **vertical asymptotes**. The numerator has degree $3$ and the denominator has degree $2$, and division gives $f(x) = x + \dfrac{x}{x^2 - 1}$. The last fraction approaches $0$ as $x \to \pm\infty$, so the line $y = x$ is an **oblique asymptote**. For $x > 1$ the fraction $\dfrac{x}{x^2 - 1}$ is positive, so the graph lies above the asymptote.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Sketch the graph of $f(x) = \dfrac{e^{x}}{x - 1}$.

$$
D = \mathbb{R} \setminus \{1\},
\qquad
f(0) = \frac{1}{-1} = -1,
\qquad
f(x) > 0 \iff x > 1
$$
$$
\lim_{x \to 1^-} \frac{e^{x}}{x - 1} = \frac{e}{0^-} = -\infty,
\qquad
\lim_{x \to 1^+} \frac{e^{x}}{x - 1} = \frac{e}{0^+} = +\infty
$$
$$
\lim_{x \to -\infty} \frac{e^{x}}{x - 1} = \frac{0}{-\infty} = 0
$$
$$
\lim_{x \to +\infty} \frac{e^{x}}{x - 1} = +\infty,
\qquad
m = \lim_{x \to +\infty} \frac{e^{x}}{x\,(x - 1)} = +\infty
$$

The line $x = 1$ is a vertical asymptote, and the line $y = 0$ is a horizontal asymptote to the left. There are no horizontal or oblique asymptotes to the right.

::grafico{f="exp(x)/(x-1)" punti="0,-1,pieno" asintoti="x=1" xmin=-6 xmax=4 ymin=-6 ymax=14 legenda="y = \dfrac{e^{x}}{x - 1}" titolo="Sketch of $\dfrac{e^{x}}{x - 1}$ with vertical asymptote $x = 1$ and horizontal asymptote $y = 0$ to the left"}

<div class="spiegazione">

The denominator is zero when $x = 1$, so the domain is $\mathbb{R} \setminus \{1\}$, which is not symmetric about the origin. Therefore, the function is neither even nor odd. The numerator $e^{x}$ is always positive, so the graph does *not* meet the $x$-axis, and $f$ has the sign of the denominator. The graph meets the $y$-axis at $(0, -1)$.

Near $1$ the numerator approaches $e$, and the denominator approaches $0$ through negative values on the left and positive values on the right. Thus the line $x = 1$ is a **vertical asymptote**. As $x \to -\infty$, the numerator approaches $0$ and the denominator approaches $-\infty$, so $f(x) \to 0$. The line $y = 0$ is a **horizontal asymptote** to the left, and the graph approaches it from below because $f$ is negative there.

As $x \to +\infty$, the exponential grows faster than $x - 1$, so $f(x) \to +\infty$. For the same reason $\dfrac{f(x)}{x}$ also approaches $+\infty$, so $m$ is not finite and there is no oblique asymptote to the right.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Consider the function
$$
f(x) = \frac{x^2 + a x + b}{x - 1} ,
$$
where $a$ and $b$ are real parameters.

1. Find $a$ and $b$ so that the graph of $f$ passes through $A(0, -2)$ and has the line $y = x + 3$ as an oblique asymptote.
2. Using those values, find the domain, the intercepts and the sign of $f$.
3. Find the asymptotes and determine the intervals on which the graph lies above the oblique asymptote.
4. Sketch the graph of $f$.
5. Prove that the equation $f(x) = x^2$ has at least one solution in $(2, 3)$.

**Part 1**
$$
f(0) = \frac{b}{-1} = -2
\iff
b = 2
$$
$$
m = \lim_{x \to \pm\infty} \frac{x^2 + a x + b}{x^2 - x} = 1
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
q
&= \lim_{x \to \pm\infty} \left( \frac{x^2 + a x + b}{x - 1} - x \right) \\[6pt]
&= \lim_{x \to \pm\infty} \frac{(a + 1)\,x + b}{x - 1} \\[6pt]
&= a + 1
\end{aligned}
$$
$$
a + 1 = 3
\iff
a = 2,
\qquad
f(x) = \frac{x^2 + 2x + 2}{x - 1}
$$

**Part 2**
$$
D = \mathbb{R} \setminus \{1\},
\qquad
x^2 + 2x + 2 = 0 \quad \text{impossible} \quad (\Delta = 4 - 8 < 0)
$$
$$
f(x) > 0
\iff
x > 1,
\qquad
f(x) < 0
\iff
x < 1
$$

**Part 3**
$$
\lim_{x \to 1^-} f(x) = \frac{5}{0^-} = -\infty,
\qquad
\lim_{x \to 1^+} f(x) = \frac{5}{0^+} = +\infty
$$
$$
f(x) - (x + 3) = \frac{x^2 + 2x + 2 - (x + 3)(x - 1)}{x - 1} = \frac{5}{x - 1}
$$

**Part 5**
$$
h(x) = f(x) - x^2,
\qquad
h(2) = \frac{10}{1} - 4 = 6 > 0,
\qquad
h(3) = \frac{17}{2} - 9 = -\frac{1}{2} < 0
$$

The parameters are $a = 2$ and $b = 2$. The graph does not meet the $x$-axis, meets the $y$-axis at $A(0, -2)$, lies above the $x$-axis for $x > 1$ and below it for $x < 1$. The asymptotes are $x = 1$ and $y = x + 3$, and the graph lies above the oblique asymptote for $x > 1$. The equation $f(x) = x^2$ has a solution in $(2, 3)$, approximately $2.92$.

::grafico{f="(x^2+2*x+2)/(x-1);x+3" punti="0,-2,pieno,A" asintoti="x=1" xmin=-8 xmax=10 ymin=-16 ymax=20 legenda="y = \dfrac{x^2 + 2x + 2}{x - 1};y = x + 3" titolo="Sketch of $\dfrac{x^2 + 2x + 2}{x - 1}$ with vertical asymptote $x = 1$ and oblique asymptote $y = x + 3$. The graph lies above the asymptote for $x > 1$ and below it for $x < 1$"}

<div class="spiegazione">

**Part 1.** Passing through $A(0, -2)$ means that $f(0) = -2$. Since $f(0) = \dfrac{b}{-1} = -b$, we get $b = 2$. For the oblique asymptote we use the formulas for $m$ and $q$. The limit of $\dfrac{f(x)}{x}$ is $1$ for all $a$ and $b$ because the numerator and denominator have degree $2$ and both coefficients of $x^2$ equal $1$. This agrees with the line $y = x + 3$, which has slope $1$. To find $q$, use a common denominator. The terms $x^2$ cancel, leaving $\dfrac{(a + 1)\,x + b}{x - 1}$, which approaches the ratio of the coefficients of $x$, namely $a + 1$. The asymptote is $y = x + 3$ if $a + 1 = 3$, so $a = 2$.

**Part 2.** The domain excludes the zero of the denominator. The numerator $x^2 + 2x + 2$ has a negative discriminant and a positive coefficient of $x^2$, so it is always positive. Thus the graph does *not* meet the $x$-axis, and $f$ has the sign of the denominator $x - 1$. Its intersection with the $y$-axis is the point $A(0, -2)$ by the condition in part 1.

**Part 3.** At $1$ the numerator equals $1 + 2 + 2 = 5$, and the denominator approaches $0$ through negative values on the left and positive values on the right. Thus the line $x = 1$ is a **vertical asymptote**. The oblique asymptote is $y = x + 3$ by construction. The function has no horizontal asymptotes because the degree of the numerator exceeds that of the denominator. To compare the graph with the asymptote, calculate the difference $f(x) - (x + 3)$. The product $(x + 3)(x - 1)$ equals $x^2 + 2x - 3$, so the numerator reduces to $5$. The difference $\dfrac{5}{x - 1}$ is positive for $x > 1$ and negative for $x < 1$, so the graph lies **above the asymptote** for $x > 1$ and below it for $x < 1$. In particular, the graph never intersects the oblique asymptote.

**Part 4.** Use the information obtained to sketch the graph. For $x < 1$ the graph lies below the $x$-axis, passes through $A$, approaches the oblique asymptote from below as $x \to -\infty$ and falls toward $-\infty$ as $x \to 1^-$. For $x > 1$ the graph lies above the $x$-axis and above the oblique asymptote. It falls from $+\infty$ near $x = 1$ and then rises as it approaches the asymptote.

**Part 5.** The solutions of the equation are the zeros of $h(x) = f(x) - x^2$. The interval $[2, 3]$ excludes $1$, so $f$ is continuous on $[2, 3]$, and so is $h$. Since $f(2) = \dfrac{4 + 4 + 2}{1} = 10$ and $f(3) = \dfrac{9 + 6 + 2}{2} = \dfrac{17}{2}$, we have $h(2) > 0$ and $h(3) < 0$. The hypotheses of **Bolzano's theorem** hold, so $h$ has at least one zero in $(2, 3)$, and the equation has a solution in that interval.

</div>
