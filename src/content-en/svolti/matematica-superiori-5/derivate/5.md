## Exercise 1

Verify that the function $f(x) = x^3 + 2x - 1$ is invertible on $\mathbb{R}$. Calculate $\left( f^{-1} \right)'(2)$ and write the equation of the tangent to the graph of $f^{-1}$ at the point with horizontal coordinate $2$.

$$
f'(x) = 3x^2 + 2 > 0 \qquad \text{for every } x
$$
$$
f(1) = 1 + 2 - 1 = 2,
\qquad
f'(1) = 3 + 2 = 5
$$
$$
\left( f^{-1} \right)'(2) = \frac{1}{f'(1)} = \frac{1}{5}
$$
$$
y - 1 = \frac{1}{5}\,(x - 2)
\qquad
\Longrightarrow
\qquad
y = \frac{x}{5} + \frac{3}{5}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The derivative $3x^2 + 2$ is positive for every $x$ because it is the sum of a nonnegative term and $2$. Therefore $f$ is **strictly increasing** on $\mathbb{R}$, as shown in the chapter on theorems of differential calculus, and is consequently invertible.

To apply the theorem on the **derivative of an inverse function** at $y_0 = 2$, we need the point $x_0$ such that $f(x_0) = 2$. By inspection, $x_0 = 1$. Since $f'(1) = 5 \neq 0$, the theorem applies and gives $\left( f^{-1} \right)'(2) = \dfrac{1}{5}$.

The graph of $f^{-1}$ passes through $(2, 1)$ because the graph of $f$ passes through $(1, 2)$. The tangent to the graph of $f^{-1}$ at $(2, 1)$ has slope $\dfrac{1}{5}$, so its equation is $y = \dfrac{x}{5} + \dfrac{3}{5}$. We obtained this result without knowing a formula for $f^{-1}$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Calculate the derivative of $f(x) = \arcsin \dfrac{x}{2}$ for $-2 < x < 2$.

$$
f'(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - \left( \dfrac{x}{2} \right)^2}} \cdot \frac{1}{2}
$$
$$
\sqrt{1 - \frac{x^2}{4}} = \sqrt{\frac{4 - x^2}{4}} = \frac{\sqrt{4 - x^2}}{2}
$$
$$
f'(x) = \frac{2}{\sqrt{4 - x^2}} \cdot \frac{1}{2} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{4 - x^2}}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The outer function is inverse sine, with derivative $\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1 - t^2}}$ for $-1 < t < 1$, and the inner function is $\dfrac{x}{2}$, with derivative $\dfrac{1}{2}$. The condition $-2 < x < 2$ ensures that $-1 < \dfrac{x}{2} < 1$, so the **chain rule** applies.

To simplify, write the radicand $1 - \dfrac{x^2}{4}$ over the common denominator $4$ and take $\sqrt{4} = 2$ outside the square root. The reciprocal of $\dfrac{\sqrt{4 - x^2}}{2}$ is $\dfrac{2}{\sqrt{4 - x^2}}$, and multiplying by $\dfrac{1}{2}$ gives $f'(x) = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{4 - x^2}}$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Calculate the derivative of $f(x) = x \arctan x - \dfrac{1}{2} \ln \left( 1 + x^2 \right)$.

$$
(x \arctan x)' = 1 \cdot \arctan x + x \cdot \frac{1}{1 + x^2} = \arctan x + \frac{x}{1 + x^2}
$$
$$
\left( \frac{1}{2} \ln \left( 1 + x^2 \right) \right)' = \frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{2x}{1 + x^2} = \frac{x}{1 + x^2}
$$
$$
f'(x) = \arctan x + \frac{x}{1 + x^2} - \frac{x}{1 + x^2} = \arctan x
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The function is defined on all of $\mathbb{R}$ because $1 + x^2 > 0$. The first term is a product, so differentiate it using the **product rule**, with $(\arctan x)' = \dfrac{1}{1 + x^2}$. The second term contains the logarithm of a function, so differentiate it using the chain rule. The derivative of $\ln \left( 1 + x^2 \right)$ is $\dfrac{2x}{1 + x^2}$, and the factor $\dfrac{1}{2}$ cancels the $2$.

In the difference, the two terms $\dfrac{x}{1 + x^2}$ cancel, so the derivative is $f'(x) = \arctan x$. In other words, $f$ is a function whose derivative is the inverse tangent. This result will appear again in the chapter on indefinite integrals.

</div>
