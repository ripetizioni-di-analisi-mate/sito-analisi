## Exercise 1

Calculate the derivative of $f(x) = 2x^4 - 3x^2 + 4\sqrt{x} - 7$ for $x > 0$.

$$
f'(x) = 2 \cdot 4x^3 - 3 \cdot 2x + 4 \cdot \frac{1}{2\sqrt{x}} - 0
$$
$$
f'(x) = 8x^3 - 6x + \frac{2}{\sqrt{x}}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The function is the sum of four terms, so by the **sum rule** its derivative is the sum of their derivatives. Each constant factor stays in front of the derivative because $(k f)' = k f'$.

By the power rule, $\left( x^4 \right)' = 4x^3$ and $\left( x^2 \right)' = 2x$, and from the table of basic derivatives, $\left( \sqrt{x} \right)' = \dfrac{1}{2\sqrt{x}}$. The derivative of the constant $-7$ is $0$. The third term simplifies because $4 \cdot \dfrac{1}{2\sqrt{x}} = \dfrac{2}{\sqrt{x}}$. We need the condition $x > 0$ because $\sqrt{x}$ is differentiable only for $x > 0$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Calculate the derivative of $f(x) = \left( x^2 + 1 \right) e^{x}$ and write the result in factored form.

$$
f'(x) = 2x \cdot e^{x} + \left( x^2 + 1 \right) \cdot e^{x}
$$
$$
f'(x) = \left( x^2 + 2x + 1 \right) e^{x} = (x + 1)^2\,e^{x}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The function is the product of $x^2 + 1$ and $e^{x}$, so we apply the **product rule** $(f g)' = f' g + f g'$. The derivative of the first factor is $2x$, and that of the second is $e^{x}$.

Both terms contain the common factor $e^{x}$, which we factor out. The other factor is $x^2 + 2x + 1$, the square of the binomial $x + 1$. The factored form allows us to study the sign of the derivative. Since $e^{x} > 0$ and $(x + 1)^2 \geq 0$, the derivative is greater than or equal to $0$ for every $x$ and vanishes only at $x = -1$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Calculate the derivative of $f(x) = x \sin x + \cos x$.

$$
f'(x) = (x \sin x)' + (\cos x)'
$$
$$
(x \sin x)' = 1 \cdot \sin x + x \cos x
$$
$$
f'(x) = \sin x + x \cos x - \sin x = x \cos x
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

By the sum rule, differentiate the two terms separately. The first, $x \sin x$, is a product. By the **product rule**, its derivative is therefore the derivative of $x$, namely $1$, multiplied by $\sin x$, plus $x$ multiplied by the derivative of $\sin x$, namely $\cos x$.

The derivative of the second term is $(\cos x)' = -\sin x$. Adding the results, the terms $\sin x$ and $-\sin x$ cancel, and the derivative is $f'(x) = x \cos x$.

</div>

## Exercise 4

Calculate the derivative of $f(x) = \dfrac{x^2 - 1}{x^2 + 1}$ and write the equation of the tangent line to the graph at the point with horizontal coordinate $1$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
f'(x)
&= \frac{2x \left( x^2 + 1 \right) - \left( x^2 - 1 \right) \cdot 2x}{\left( x^2 + 1 \right)^2} \\[6pt]
&= \frac{2x^3 + 2x - 2x^3 + 2x}{\left( x^2 + 1 \right)^2} \\[6pt]
&= \frac{4x}{\left( x^2 + 1 \right)^2}
\end{aligned}
$$
$$
f(1) = 0,
\qquad
f'(1) = \frac{4}{4} = 1,
\qquad
y = x - 1
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The denominator $x^2 + 1$ is always positive, so the function is defined and differentiable on all of $\mathbb{R}$. Apply the **quotient rule**, taking $f$ to be the numerator $x^2 - 1$ and $g$ to be the denominator $x^2 + 1$. Both derivatives are $2x$.

Expand the two products in the numerator. The cubic terms $2x^3$ and $-2x^3$ cancel, and the linear terms add to give $4x$. At the point with horizontal coordinate $1$, the function equals $0$ and its derivative equals $1$. The tangent is therefore the line through $(1, 0)$ with slope $1$, namely $y = x - 1$.

</div>
