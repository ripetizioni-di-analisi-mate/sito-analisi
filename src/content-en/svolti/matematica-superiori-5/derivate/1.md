## Exercise 1

Use the definition to calculate the derivative of $f(x) = x^2 - 3x$ at $x_0 = 2$ and write the equation of the tangent line to the graph at the point with horizontal coordinate $2$.

$$
f(2) = 4 - 6 = -2
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{f(2 + h) - f(2)}{h}
&= \frac{(2 + h)^2 - 3\,(2 + h) + 2}{h} \\[6pt]
&= \frac{4 + 4h + h^2 - 6 - 3h + 2}{h} \\[6pt]
&= \frac{h + h^2}{h} \\[6pt]
&= 1 + h
\end{aligned}
$$
$$
f'(2) = \lim_{h \to 0} (1 + h) = 1
$$
$$
y - (-2) = 1 \cdot (x - 2)
\qquad
\Longrightarrow
\qquad
y = x - 4
$$

::grafico{f="x^2-3*x;x-4" punti="2,-2,pieno" xmin=-1 xmax=5 ymin=-4 ymax=6 legenda="y = x^2 - 3x;y = x - 4" titolo="The tangent to the graph of $f(x) = x^2 - 3x$ at $(2, -2)$ has slope $f'(2) = 1$"}

<div class="spiegazione">

First calculate $f(2)$, which is needed both in the difference quotient and in the equation of the tangent line. In the difference quotient, substitute $2 + h$ for $x$ and subtract $f(2) = -2$, that is, add $2$. Expanding the square and the product, the terms without $h$ cancel because $4 - 6 + 2 = 0$, and the first-degree terms give $4h - 3h = h$. The numerator is therefore $h + h^2$, which is divided by $h$.

As $h \to 0$, the result $1 + h$ approaches $1$, so the function is **differentiable** at $2$ and $f'(2) = 1$. The tangent line passes through $(2, -2)$ and has slope $1$, so its equation is $y = x - 4$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Use the definition to calculate the derivative of $f(x) = \dfrac{1}{x + 1}$ at $x_0 = 1$.

$$
f(1) = \frac{1}{2},
\qquad
f(1 + h) = \frac{1}{2 + h}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{f(1 + h) - f(1)}{h}
&= \frac{1}{h} \left( \frac{1}{2 + h} - \frac{1}{2} \right) \\[6pt]
&= \frac{1}{h} \cdot \frac{2 - (2 + h)}{2\,(2 + h)} \\[6pt]
&= \frac{1}{h} \cdot \frac{-h}{2\,(2 + h)} \\[6pt]
&= -\frac{1}{2\,(2 + h)}
\end{aligned}
$$
$$
f'(1) = \lim_{h \to 0} \left( -\frac{1}{2\,(2 + h)} \right) = -\frac{1}{4}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The difference quotient contains a difference of two fractions, which we write over the common denominator $2\,(2 + h)$. The numerator becomes $2 - 2 - h = -h$, and this factor cancels with the factor $\dfrac{1}{h}$ in front.

Before cancellation, the limit as $h \to 0$ had the form $\dfrac{0}{0}$. After cancellation, we can substitute $h = 0$ because the expression $-\dfrac{1}{2\,(2 + h)}$ is continuous at $h = 0$, giving $f'(1) = -\dfrac{1}{4}$. The derivative is **negative**, as expected since $\dfrac{1}{x + 1}$ is decreasing for $x > -1$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Use the definition to find the derivative function of $f(x) = \sqrt{x + 3}$ for $x > -3$, and calculate $f'(1)$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\sqrt{x + h + 3} - \sqrt{x + 3}}{h}
&= \frac{\left( \sqrt{x + h + 3} - \sqrt{x + 3} \right)\left( \sqrt{x + h + 3} + \sqrt{x + 3} \right)}{h \left( \sqrt{x + h + 3} + \sqrt{x + 3} \right)} \\[6pt]
&= \frac{(x + h + 3) - (x + 3)}{h \left( \sqrt{x + h + 3} + \sqrt{x + 3} \right)} \\[6pt]
&= \frac{1}{\sqrt{x + h + 3} + \sqrt{x + 3}}
\end{aligned}
$$
$$
f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{1}{\sqrt{x + h + 3} + \sqrt{x + 3}} = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{x + 3}}
$$
$$
f'(1) = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{4}} = \frac{1}{4}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

This time the point $x$ is arbitrary, and the result is the **derivative function**. The numerator of the difference quotient is a difference of square roots, and as $h \to 0$ the quotient has the form $\dfrac{0}{0}$. To eliminate the difference of square roots, multiply the numerator and denominator by the sum $\sqrt{x + h + 3} + \sqrt{x + 3}$. The product of a difference and the sum of the same terms is the difference of their squares, so the numerator reduces to $(x + h + 3) - (x + 3) = h$, which cancels.

As $h \to 0$, the denominator approaches $\sqrt{x + 3} + \sqrt{x + 3} = 2\sqrt{x + 3}$, which is positive because $x > -3$. Thus $f'(x) = \dfrac{1}{2\sqrt{x + 3}}$, and substituting $x = 1$ gives $f'(1) = \dfrac{1}{4}$.

</div>

## Exercise 4

Use the definition to calculate the derivative of $f(x) = e^{2x}$ at $x_0 = 0$.

$$
\frac{f(0 + h) - f(0)}{h} = \frac{e^{2h} - 1}{h} = 2 \cdot \frac{e^{2h} - 1}{2h}
$$
$$
f'(0) = 2 \cdot \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{e^{2h} - 1}{2h} = 2 \cdot 1 = 2
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Since $f(0) = e^{0} = 1$, the difference quotient is $\dfrac{e^{2h} - 1}{h}$, which as $h \to 0$ has the form $\dfrac{0}{0}$. The **standard limit** $\displaystyle\lim_{t \to 0} \dfrac{e^{t} - 1}{t} = 1$ applies when the denominator is the same as the exponent in $e^{t}$. For this reason we multiply and divide by $2$ so that the denominator contains $2h$.

Set $t = 2h$. When $h \to 0$, we also have $t \to 0$, so the quotient $\dfrac{e^{2h} - 1}{2h}$ approaches $1$ and the derivative is $f'(0) = 2$. The chain rule gives the same result, $\left( e^{2x} \right)' = 2e^{2x}$, which at $0$ equals $2$.

</div>
