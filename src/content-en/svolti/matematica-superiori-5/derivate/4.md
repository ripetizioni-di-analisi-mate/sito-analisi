## Exercise 1

Calculate the derivative of $f(x) = \left( 3x^2 - 1 \right)^4$.

$$
f'(x) = 4 \left( 3x^2 - 1 \right)^3 \cdot \left( 3x^2 - 1 \right)'
$$
$$
f'(x) = 4 \left( 3x^2 - 1 \right)^3 \cdot 6x = 24x \left( 3x^2 - 1 \right)^3
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

This is a composite function. The inner function is $g(x) = 3x^2 - 1$ and the outer function is the power function $t^4$. By the **chain rule**, differentiate the power to get $4t^3$, substitute $t = 3x^2 - 1$, and multiply by the derivative of the inner function, which is $6x$.

Multiplying the coefficients $4 \cdot 6$ gives $24$. Expanding the fourth power before differentiating would give the same result with much more work.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Calculate the derivative of $f(x) = \sqrt{x^2 - 4x + 5}$.

$$
f'(x) = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{x^2 - 4x + 5}} \cdot (2x - 4)
$$
$$
f'(x) = \frac{2\,(x - 2)}{2\sqrt{x^2 - 4x + 5}} = \frac{x - 2}{\sqrt{x^2 - 4x + 5}}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The radicand $x^2 - 4x + 5 = (x - 2)^2 + 1$ is always greater than or equal to $1$, so the function is defined on all of $\mathbb{R}$. The square root is differentiable at every point because its radicand never vanishes.

The outer function is the square root, with derivative $\dfrac{1}{2\sqrt{t}}$, and the inner function is $x^2 - 4x + 5$, with derivative $2x - 4$. By the **chain rule**, multiply the two derivatives. Factor out $2$ in the numerator and cancel it with the $2$ in the denominator.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Calculate the derivative of $f(x) = \sin^2 (3x)$ and express it using a single sine.

$$
f(x) = \big( \sin (3x) \big)^2
$$
$$
f'(x) = 2 \sin (3x) \cdot \big( \sin (3x) \big)' = 2 \sin (3x) \cdot \cos (3x) \cdot 3
$$
$$
f'(x) = 3 \cdot 2 \sin (3x) \cos (3x) = 3 \sin (6x)
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The notation $\sin^2 (3x)$ means the square of $\sin (3x)$. The function is therefore a composition of three functions. The innermost is $3x$, followed by sine, and the outermost is the square. Apply the **chain rule** twice. The derivative of the square is $2\sin (3x)$, which must be multiplied by the derivative of $\sin (3x)$. By the same rule, this derivative is $\cos (3x) \cdot 3$.

To express the result using a single sine, use the double-angle formula $2 \sin \alpha \cos \alpha = \sin (2\alpha)$ with $\alpha = 3x$, so $2 \sin (3x) \cos (3x) = \sin (6x)$.

</div>

## Exercise 4

Calculate the derivative of $f(x) = x^{\sin x}$ for $x > 0$.

$$
f(x) = e^{\sin x \cdot \ln x}
$$
$$
\left( \sin x \cdot \ln x \right)' = \cos x \cdot \ln x + \sin x \cdot \frac{1}{x}
$$
$$
f'(x) = e^{\sin x \cdot \ln x} \left( \cos x \ln x + \frac{\sin x}{x} \right) = x^{\sin x} \left( \cos x \ln x + \frac{\sin x}{x} \right)
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The variable occurs in both the base and the exponent, so neither the power rule, which requires a constant exponent, nor the exponential rule, which requires a constant base, applies directly. Since $x > 0$, we have $x = e^{\ln x}$, and the laws of exponents give $x^{\sin x} = e^{\sin x \cdot \ln x}$.

The function is now an exponential with base $e$ and exponent $\sin x \cdot \ln x$. By the **chain rule**, its derivative is the same exponential times the derivative of the exponent. The exponent is a product, so we differentiate it using the product rule. In the last step we return to the notation $x^{\sin x}$.

</div>
