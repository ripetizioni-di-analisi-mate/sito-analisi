## Exercise 1

Solve the initial value problem
$$
\begin{cases}
y' - 3y = 6 \\
y(0) = 1
\end{cases}
$$

$$
a(x) = -3, \qquad b(x) = 6, \qquad A(x) = -3x
$$
$$
\int 6\,e^{-3x}\,dx = -2\,e^{-3x}
$$
$$
y = e^{3x} \left( -2\,e^{-3x} + c \right) = -2 + c\,e^{3x}
$$
$$
y(0) = -2 + c = 1
\qquad
\Longrightarrow
\qquad
c = 3
$$
$$
y = 3\,e^{3x} - 2
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The equation has the form $y' + a(x)\,y = b(x)$ with $a(x) = -3$ and $b(x) = 6$, so it is a first-order **linear** equation. An antiderivative of $a(x)$ is $A(x) = -3x$, so the solution formula contains $e^{A(x)} = e^{-3x}$ and $e^{-A(x)} = e^{3x}$.

An antiderivative of $6\,e^{-3x}$ is $-2\,e^{-3x}$ because the derivative of $-2\,e^{-3x}$ is $-2 \cdot (-3)\,e^{-3x} = 6\,e^{-3x}$. Multiplying by $e^{3x}$ gives the general solution $y = -2 + c\,e^{3x}$. The constant-coefficient formula gives the same result with $a = -3$ and $b = 6$ because $\dfrac{b}{a} = -2$.

The condition $y(0) = 1$ gives $-2 + c = 1$, that is, $c = 3$, and the solution is $y = 3\,e^{3x} - 2$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Solve the initial value problem
$$
\begin{cases}
y' + 2x\,y = 2x \\
y(0) = 3
\end{cases}
$$
and find the limit of the solution as $x \to +\infty$.

$$
a(x) = 2x, \qquad b(x) = 2x, \qquad A(x) = x^2
$$
$$
\int 2x\,e^{x^2}\,dx = e^{x^2}
$$
$$
y = e^{-x^2} \left( e^{x^2} + c \right) = 1 + c\,e^{-x^2}
$$
$$
y(0) = 1 + c = 3
\qquad
\Longrightarrow
\qquad
c = 2
$$
$$
y = 1 + 2\,e^{-x^2},
\qquad
\lim_{x \to +\infty} \left( 1 + 2\,e^{-x^2} \right) = 1
$$

::grafico{f="1+2*exp(-x^2)" punti="0,3,pieno" asintoti="y=1" xmin=-4 xmax=4 ymin=-1 ymax=4 legenda="y = 1 + 2e^{-x^2}" titolo="The solution of $y' + 2xy = 2x$ with $y(0) = 3$ has horizontal asymptote $y = 1$"}

<div class="spiegazione">

The equation is linear with $a(x) = 2x$ and $b(x) = 2x$, both continuous on $\mathbb{R}$. An antiderivative of $2x$ is $A(x) = x^2$. The solution formula requires the integral $\displaystyle\int 2x\,e^{x^2}\,dx$. The function $2x$ is the derivative of the exponent $x^2$, so the integral can be read off directly and equals $e^{x^2}$.

Multiplying by $e^{-x^2}$ gives the **general solution** $y = 1 + c\,e^{-x^2}$. When $x = 0$, the exponential equals $1$, so the condition $y(0) = 3$ gives $c = 2$.

As $x \to +\infty$, the exponent $-x^2$ approaches $-\infty$, so $e^{-x^2}$ approaches $0$ and the solution approaches $1$. The function $y = 1$ is the constant solution of the equation, obtained by setting $c = 0$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Solve the initial value problem
$$
\begin{cases}
y' + \dfrac{y}{x} = 3x \\[6pt]
y(1) = 2
\end{cases}
$$
on the interval $x > 0$.

$$
a(x) = \frac{1}{x}, \qquad A(x) = \ln x, \qquad e^{A(x)} = x, \qquad e^{-A(x)} = \frac{1}{x}
$$
$$
\int 3x \cdot x\,dx = \int 3x^2\,dx = x^3
$$
$$
y = \frac{1}{x} \left( x^3 + c \right) = x^2 + \frac{c}{x}
$$
$$
y(1) = 1 + c = 2
\qquad
\Longrightarrow
\qquad
c = 1
$$
$$
y = x^2 + \frac{1}{x}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The equation is linear with $a(x) = \dfrac{1}{x}$ and $b(x) = 3x$, both continuous on the interval $x > 0$. An antiderivative of $\dfrac{1}{x}$ is $\ln |x|$, which for $x > 0$ equals $\ln x$. Since the exponential and logarithm are inverse functions, $e^{\ln x} = x$ and $e^{-\ln x} = \dfrac{1}{x}$.

In the formula we integrate $b(x)\,e^{A(x)} = 3x \cdot x = 3x^2$, which has antiderivative $x^3$. Dividing by $x$ gives the general solution $y = x^2 + \dfrac{c}{x}$. The condition $y(1) = 2$ gives $1 + c = 2$, so $c = 1$.

As a check, the solution $y = x^2 + \dfrac{1}{x}$ has derivative $y' = 2x - \dfrac{1}{x^2}$, and $\dfrac{y}{x} = x + \dfrac{1}{x^2}$. The sum is $3x$, so the equation is **satisfied**.

</div>

## Exercise 4

Solve the initial value problem
$$
\begin{cases}
y' - y = x\,e^{x} \\
y(0) = 1
\end{cases}
$$

$$
a(x) = -1, \qquad b(x) = x\,e^{x}, \qquad A(x) = -x
$$
$$
\int x\,e^{x} \cdot e^{-x}\,dx = \int x\,dx = \frac{x^2}{2}
$$
$$
y = e^{x} \left( \frac{x^2}{2} + c \right)
$$
$$
y(0) = c = 1
\qquad
\Longrightarrow
\qquad
y = \left( \frac{x^2}{2} + 1 \right) e^{x}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The equation is linear with constant coefficient $a(x) = -1$ and forcing term $b(x) = x\,e^{x}$. An antiderivative of $-1$ is $A(x) = -x$, so in the formula we multiply $b(x)$ by $e^{-x}$. The product $x\,e^{x} \cdot e^{-x}$ equals $x$ because $e^{x} \cdot e^{-x} = e^{0} = 1$, and its antiderivative is $\dfrac{x^2}{2}$.

Multiplying by $e^{-A(x)} = e^{x}$ gives the general solution $y = e^{x} \left( \dfrac{x^2}{2} + c \right)$. For $x = 0$, the factor $e^{x}$ equals $1$ and the factor $\dfrac{x^2}{2} + c$ reduces to $c$, so the condition $y(0) = 1$ gives $c = 1$.

</div>
