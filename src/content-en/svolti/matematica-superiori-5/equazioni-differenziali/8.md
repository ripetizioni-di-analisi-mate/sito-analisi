## Exercise 1

An object of mass $m = 0.2\,\mathrm{kg}$ attached to a spring with spring constant $k = 5\,\mathrm{N/m}$ moves without friction along the $x$-axis, with position measured from equilibrium. At time $t = 0$ the object is at $x = 4\,\mathrm{cm}$ and has velocity $-15\,\mathrm{cm/s}$. Find its position as a function of time, the period, the amplitude, and the maximum velocity.

$$
x'' + \frac{k}{m}\,x = 0,
\qquad
\omega^2 = \frac{5}{0.2} = 25,
\qquad
\omega = 5\,\mathrm{rad/s}
$$
$$
x(t) = c_1 \cos 5t + c_2 \sin 5t,
\qquad
x'(t) = -5c_1 \sin 5t + 5c_2 \cos 5t
$$
$$
x(0) = c_1 = 4,
\qquad
x'(0) = 5c_2 = -15
\qquad
\Longrightarrow
\qquad
c_2 = -3
$$
$$
x(t) = 4 \cos 5t - 3 \sin 5t
$$
$$
T = \frac{2\pi}{5} \approx 1.26\,\mathrm{s},
\qquad
A = \sqrt{4^2 + 3^2} = 5\,\mathrm{cm},
\qquad
v_{\max} = A\,\omega = 25\,\mathrm{cm/s}
$$

::grafico{f="4*cos(5*x)-3*sin(5*x)" punti="0,4,pieno" segmenti="0,5,3,5;0,-5,3,-5" xmin=0 xmax=3 ymin=-6 ymax=6 nomi="t,x" legenda="x = 4\cos 5t - 3\sin 5t" titolo="Position function $x(t) = 4\cos 5t - 3\sin 5t$, with amplitude $5\,\mathrm{cm}$ and period $\dfrac{2\pi}{5}\,\mathrm{s}$"}

<div class="spiegazione">

Newton's second law gives $m\,x'' = -k\,x$, and dividing by $m$ gives the equation of **simple harmonic motion** with $\omega^2 = \dfrac{k}{m} = 25$. The characteristic equation $\lambda^2 + 25 = 0$ has negative discriminant, with $\alpha = 0$ and $\beta = 5$, so position is a linear combination of $\cos 5t$ and $\sin 5t$.

Position is measured in centimeters and time in seconds. For $t = 0$ we have $x(0) = c_1$ and $x'(0) = 5c_2$, so the initial position gives $c_1 = 4$ and the initial velocity gives $5c_2 = -15$, that is, $c_2 = -3$.

The **period** is $T = \dfrac{2\pi}{\omega} = \dfrac{2\pi}{5}\,\mathrm{s}$. The amplitude is $A = \sqrt{c_1^2 + c_2^2} = \sqrt{16 + 9} = 5\,\mathrm{cm}$ because the position function can be written in the form $A \cos(5t + \varphi)$. The velocity is $x'(t) = -5A \sin(5t + \varphi)$, whose maximum value is $5A = 25\,\mathrm{cm/s}$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

An initially uncharged capacitor of capacitance $C = 4\,\mathrm{mF}$ is connected to a source of electromotive force $E = 12\,\mathrm{V}$ through a resistor of resistance $R = 500\,\Omega$. Write the differential equation for the charge $q(t)$, solve it, and find the current, its initial value, and the time when the charge reaches half its final value.

$$
R\,q' + \frac{q}{C} = E
\qquad
\Longrightarrow
\qquad
q' + \frac{1}{RC}\,q = \frac{E}{R},
\qquad
RC = 500 \cdot 0.004 = 2\,\mathrm{s}
$$
$$
q' + \frac{1}{2}\,q = 0.024
\qquad
\Longrightarrow
\qquad
q(t) = 0.048 + c\,e^{-\frac{t}{2}}
$$
$$
q(0) = 0.048 + c = 0
\qquad
\Longrightarrow
\qquad
q(t) = 0.048 \left( 1 - e^{-\frac{t}{2}} \right)\,\mathrm{C} = 48 \left( 1 - e^{-\frac{t}{2}} \right)\,\mathrm{mC}
$$
$$
i(t) = q'(t) = 0.024\,e^{-\frac{t}{2}}\,\mathrm{A},
\qquad
i(0) = 24\,\mathrm{mA}
$$
$$
1 - e^{-\frac{t}{2}} = \frac{1}{2}
\iff
e^{-\frac{t}{2}} = \frac{1}{2}
\iff
t = 2 \ln 2 \approx 1.39\,\mathrm{s}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

By Kirchhoff's loop rule, the electromotive force is the sum of the potential difference $R\,i = R\,q'$ across the resistor and the potential difference $\dfrac{q}{C}$ across the capacitor. Dividing by $R$ gives a linear equation with constant coefficients. With $C = 0.004\,\mathrm{F}$ the time constant is $RC = 2\,\mathrm{s}$, and $\dfrac{E}{R} = \dfrac{12}{500} = 0.024\,\mathrm{A}$.

By the observation on equations with constant coefficients, the general solution is $q = \dfrac{b}{a} + c\,e^{-at}$ with $a = \dfrac{1}{2}$ and $b = 0.024$. We have $\dfrac{b}{a} = 0.048\,\mathrm{C}$, which equals $CE$. The capacitor is initially uncharged, so $q(0) = 0$ and $c = -0.048$.

The **electric current** is the derivative of charge. The derivative of $-e^{-\frac{t}{2}}$ is $\dfrac{1}{2}\,e^{-\frac{t}{2}}$, so $i(t) = 0.048 \cdot \dfrac{1}{2}\,e^{-\frac{t}{2}} = 0.024\,e^{-\frac{t}{2}}$, and at the initial time $i(0) = 0.024\,\mathrm{A} = 24\,\mathrm{mA}$. The charge approaches the final value $48\,\mathrm{mC}$ and reaches half this value when $e^{-\frac{t}{2}} = \dfrac{1}{2}$, that is, after $2 \ln 2 \approx 1.39\,\mathrm{s}$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Consider the differential equation $y'' + a\,y' + y = 0$, where $a$ is a real parameter.

1. Find the values of $a$ for which the general solution has the form $(c_1 + c_2\,x)\,e^{\lambda x}$ and the values for which the solutions contain sine and cosine functions.
2. Set $a = 2$ and find the solution $f(x)$ that satisfies the conditions $f(0) = 0$ and $f'(0) = 4$.
3. Analyze the resulting function $f$ by finding the limits at the ends of its domain, its maxima and minima, and its inflection points. Sketch the graph.
4. Calculate the area of the unbounded region between the graph of $f$ and the positive $x$-axis.

**Part 1**
$$
\lambda^2 + a\,\lambda + 1 = 0,
\qquad
\Delta = a^2 - 4
$$
$$
\Delta = 0
\iff
a = \pm 2,
\qquad
\Delta < 0
\iff
-2 < a < 2
$$

**Part 2**
$$
a = 2:
\quad
\lambda^2 + 2\lambda + 1 = (\lambda + 1)^2 = 0,
\qquad
y = (c_1 + c_2\,x)\,e^{-x}
$$
$$
f(0) = c_1 = 0,
\qquad
f(x) = c_2\,x\,e^{-x},
\qquad
f'(x) = c_2\,(1 - x)\,e^{-x},
\qquad
f'(0) = c_2 = 4
$$
$$
f(x) = 4x\,e^{-x}
$$

**Part 3**
$$
\lim_{x \to -\infty} 4x\,e^{-x} = -\infty,
\qquad
\lim_{x \to +\infty} \frac{4x}{e^{x}} = 0
$$
$$
f'(x) = 4\,(1 - x)\,e^{-x},
\qquad
f'(x) > 0 \iff x < 1,
\qquad
f(1) = \frac{4}{e} \approx 1.47
$$
$$
f''(x) = 4\,(x - 2)\,e^{-x},
\qquad
f''(x) > 0 \iff x > 2,
\qquad
f(2) = \frac{8}{e^2} \approx 1.08
$$

**Part 4**
$$
\int 4x\,e^{-x}\,dx = -4x\,e^{-x} + \int 4\,e^{-x}\,dx = -4\,(x + 1)\,e^{-x} + c
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_0^{+\infty} 4x\,e^{-x}\,dx
&= \lim_{b \to +\infty} \Big[ -4\,(x + 1)\,e^{-x} \Big]_0^b \\[6pt]
&= \lim_{b \to +\infty} \left( -\frac{4\,(b + 1)}{e^{b}} + 4 \right) \\[6pt]
&= 4
\end{aligned}
$$

::grafico{f="4*x*exp(-x)" punti="1,1.47,pieno,M;2,1.08,pieno,F;0,0,pieno" area="4*x*exp(-x);0;0;9" xmin=-1 xmax=9 ymin=-1 ymax=2 legenda="y = 4x\,e^{-x}" titolo="Graph of $f(x) = 4x\,e^{-x}$ with its maximum $M\left(1, \dfrac{4}{e}\right)$, its inflection point $F\left(2, \dfrac{8}{e^2}\right)$, and the region of area $4$ between the graph and the positive $x$-axis"}

<div class="spiegazione">

**Part 1.** The characteristic equation is $\lambda^2 + a\,\lambda + 1 = 0$, with discriminant $a^2 - 4$. The general solution has the form $(c_1 + c_2\,x)\,e^{\lambda x}$ when the characteristic equation has a double root, that is, when $\Delta = 0$, so this occurs for $a = 2$ and $a = -2$. The solutions contain sine and cosine when $\Delta < 0$, that is, when $a^2 < 4$, so this occurs for $-2 < a < 2$.

**Part 2.** For $a = 2$ the discriminant is zero and the double root is $\lambda = -1$. The condition $f(0) = 0$ gives $c_1 = 0$ because $e^{0} = 1$. The derivative of $c_2\,x\,e^{-x}$ follows from the product rule and equals $c_2\,(e^{-x} - x\,e^{-x}) = c_2\,(1 - x)\,e^{-x}$. The condition $f'(0) = 4$ gives $c_2 = 4$, so $f(x) = 4x\,e^{-x}$.

**Part 3.** The function is defined on $\mathbb{R}$, vanishes only at $x = 0$, and is positive for $x > 0$ because $e^{-x} > 0$. As $x \to -\infty$, the factor $4x$ approaches $-\infty$ and the factor $e^{-x}$ approaches $+\infty$, so the product approaches $-\infty$. As $x \to +\infty$, the function can be written as $\dfrac{4x}{e^{x}}$, and the exponential grows faster than $x$, so the limit is $0$ and the line $y = 0$ is a **horizontal asymptote** on the right.

The first derivative is positive for $x < 1$ and negative for $x > 1$, so $f$ increases up to $x = 1$ and then decreases, and the point $M\left( 1, \dfrac{4}{e} \right)$ is the **absolute maximum**. Differentiating $4\,(1 - x)\,e^{-x}$ by the product rule gives the second derivative, $4\,(-e^{-x} - (1 - x)\,e^{-x}) = 4\,(x - 2)\,e^{-x}$. It changes sign at $x = 2$, so the graph is concave down for $x < 2$ and concave up for $x > 2$, and $F\left( 2, \dfrac{8}{e^2} \right)$ is an **inflection point**.

**Part 4.** The region is unbounded, so its area is the improper integral of $f$ from $0$ to $+\infty$, calculated as the limit of the integrals from $0$ to $b$. Find an antiderivative by integration by parts, differentiating the factor $4x$ and integrating the factor $e^{-x}$. At $x = 0$ the antiderivative $-4\,(x + 1)\,e^{-x}$ equals $-4$. As $b \to +\infty$, the quotient $\dfrac{4\,(b + 1)}{e^{b}}$ approaches $0$ because the exponential grows faster than the polynomial. The area of the region is therefore **equal to 4**.

</div>
