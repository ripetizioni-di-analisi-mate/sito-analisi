## Exercise 1

Verify that the function $y = 3e^{2x} - x$ is a solution of the differential equation $y' - 2y = 2x - 1$.

$$
y' = 6e^{2x} - 1
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
y' - 2y
&= 6e^{2x} - 1 - 2\left( 3e^{2x} - x \right) \\[6pt]
&= 6e^{2x} - 1 - 6e^{2x} + 2x \\[6pt]
&= 2x - 1
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

To check that a function is a solution, calculate the derivatives that appear in the equation and substitute them into the left-hand side. By the chain rule, the derivative of $3e^{2x}$ is $3 \cdot 2e^{2x} = 6e^{2x}$, while the derivative of $-x$ is $-1$.

Substitute $y'$ and $y$ into the left-hand side. Multiplying $3e^{2x} - x$ by $-2$ gives $-6e^{2x} + 2x$, and the exponential terms cancel. This leaves $2x - 1$, which is the right-hand side for every real $x$, so the function $y = 3e^{2x} - x$ **is a solution** of the equation on $\mathbb{R}$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Find the values of the real parameter $k$ for which the function $y = e^{kx}$ is a solution of the equation $y'' + y' - 6y = 0$.

$$
y' = k\,e^{kx},
\qquad
y'' = k^2 e^{kx}
$$
$$
k^2 e^{kx} + k\,e^{kx} - 6\,e^{kx} = 0
\iff
e^{kx} \left( k^2 + k - 6 \right) = 0
$$
$$
k^2 + k - 6 = 0
\iff
(k + 3)(k - 2) = 0
\iff
k = -3 \quad \text{or} \quad k = 2
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The derivatives of $e^{kx}$ follow from the chain rule. Each differentiation introduces a factor of $k$, so $y' = k\,e^{kx}$ and $y'' = k^2 e^{kx}$. Substituting into the equation lets us factor out $e^{kx}$.

The factor $e^{kx}$ is positive for every $x$, so the product is zero for every $x$ if and only if $k^2 + k - 6 = 0$. This is the **characteristic equation** of the differential equation. Its roots are $-3$ and $2$, so the functions $e^{-3x}$ and $e^{2x}$ are the solutions we seek.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Find the general solution of the equation $y' = 3x^2 - 4x + 1$ and the solution that satisfies the condition $y(1) = 2$.

$$
y = \int \left( 3x^2 - 4x + 1 \right) dx = x^3 - 2x^2 + x + c,
\qquad
c \in \mathbb{R}
$$
$$
y(1) = 1 - 2 + 1 + c = c = 2
$$
$$
y = x^3 - 2x^2 + x + 2
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The equation has the form $y' = g(x)$ because the right-hand side does not contain $y$. Its solutions are therefore the antiderivatives of $g(x) = 3x^2 - 4x + 1$, and we find the **general solution** by integrating term by term. An antiderivative of $3x^2$ is $x^3$, one of $-4x$ is $-2x^2$, and one of $1$ is $x$.

The condition $y(1) = 2$ requires the integral curve to pass through $(1, 2)$. Substituting $x = 1$ into the general solution, the numerical terms give $1 - 2 + 1 = 0$, so $y(1) = c$ and the condition gives $c = 2$. The solution of the **initial value problem** is therefore $y = x^3 - 2x^2 + x + 2$.

</div>

## Exercise 4

Solve the initial value problem
$$
\begin{cases}
y'' = 6x + e^{-x} \\
y(0) = 1 \\
y'(0) = 0
\end{cases}
$$

$$
y' = \int \left( 6x + e^{-x} \right) dx = 3x^2 - e^{-x} + c_1
$$
$$
y'(0) = 0 - 1 + c_1 = 0
\qquad
\Longrightarrow
\qquad
c_1 = 1
$$
$$
y = \int \left( 3x^2 - e^{-x} + 1 \right) dx = x^3 + e^{-x} + x + c_2
$$
$$
y(0) = 0 + 1 + 0 + c_2 = 1
\qquad
\Longrightarrow
\qquad
c_2 = 0
$$
$$
y = x^3 + x + e^{-x}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The equation is of second order and its right-hand side depends only on $x$, so we integrate twice. The first integration gives the first derivative. An antiderivative of $6x$ is $3x^2$, and an antiderivative of $e^{-x}$ is $-e^{-x}$ because the derivative of $-e^{-x}$ is $e^{-x}$.

Use the condition on the derivative first because $y'$ contains only the constant $c_1$. Since $e^{0} = 1$, we have $y'(0) = -1 + c_1$, and the condition $y'(0) = 0$ gives $c_1 = 1$. The second integration gives $y$. An antiderivative of $-e^{-x}$ is $e^{-x}$, and one of $1$ is $x$.

Finally, the condition $y(0) = 1$ gives $1 + c_2 = 1$, that is, $c_2 = 0$. The solution of the problem is $y = x^3 + x + e^{-x}$. As a check, differentiating twice gives $y'' = 6x + e^{-x}$, the right-hand side of the equation.

</div>
