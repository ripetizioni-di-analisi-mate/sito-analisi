## Exercise 1

A closed cylindrical can must have volume $250\pi\,\mathrm{cm^3}$. Find the base radius and the height that minimize its total surface area, and calculate the minimum surface area.

$$
\pi r^2 h = 250\pi
\qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad
h = \frac{250}{r^2}
$$
$$
S(r) = 2\pi r^2 + 2\pi r h = 2\pi r^2 + \frac{500\pi}{r} ,
\qquad
r > 0
$$
$$
S'(r) = 4\pi r - \frac{500\pi}{r^2} = \frac{4\pi\,\left( r^3 - 125 \right)}{r^2}
$$
$$
S'(r) > 0 \iff r^3 > 125 \iff r > 5
$$
$$
h = \frac{250}{25} = 10\,\mathrm{cm} ,
\qquad
S(5) = 50\pi + 100\pi = 150\pi\,\mathrm{cm^2} \approx 471\,\mathrm{cm^2}
$$

The surface area is smallest when $r = 5\,\mathrm{cm}$ and $h = 10\,\mathrm{cm}$, and its minimum value is $150\pi\,\mathrm{cm^2}$.

<div class="spiegazione">

Take the base radius $r$, in centimeters, as the variable. The total surface area is the sum of the areas of the two bases, each of area $\pi r^2$, and the lateral surface area, which is the area of a rectangle with base $2\pi r$ and height $h$. To express the surface area in terms of $r$ alone, solve the volume condition for $h$ and substitute. The product $2\pi r \cdot \dfrac{250}{r^2}$ simplifies to $\dfrac{500\pi}{r}$. The radius can be any positive number, so the admissible interval is $(0, +\infty)$.

In the derivative, combine the terms over the common denominator $r^2$ and factor out $4\pi$. The denominator is positive, so $S'$ has the sign of $r^3 - 125$, which is negative for $r < 5$ and positive for $r > 5$. The interval is open, so the extreme value theorem does not apply. By the theorem on **absolute extrema on an arbitrary interval**, $r = 5$ is a point of absolute minimum.

The corresponding height is $h = 10\,\mathrm{cm}$, equal to the diameter of the base. Substitution gives the two terms of the minimum surface area, $2\pi \cdot 25 = 50\pi$ and $\dfrac{500\pi}{5} = 100\pi$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Find the point $P$ on the parabola $y = x^2$ closest to the point $A(3, 0)$ and calculate this minimum distance.

$$
P\left( x, x^2 \right) ,
\qquad
\overline{PA}^2 = (x - 3)^2 + x^4
$$
$$
g(x) = (x - 3)^2 + x^4 ,
\qquad
g'(x) = 2\,(x - 3) + 4x^3 = 2\,\left( 2x^3 + x - 3 \right)
$$
$$
2x^3 + x - 3 = (x - 1)\left( 2x^2 + 2x + 3 \right)
$$
$$
g'(x) > 0 \iff x > 1
$$
$$
P(1, 1) ,
\qquad
\overline{PA} = \sqrt{g(1)} = \sqrt{4 + 1} = \sqrt{5}
$$

The required point is $P(1, 1)$, and the minimum distance is $\sqrt{5}$.

::grafico{f="x^2" punti="1,1,pieno,P;3,0,pieno,A" segmenti="1,1,3,0" xmin=-2 xmax=4 ymin=-1 ymax=5 uguale=1 titolo="The point $P(1, 1)$ on the parabola $y = x^2$ is at the minimum distance $\sqrt{5}$ from $A(3, 0)$. The segment $PA$ is perpendicular to the tangent at $P$"}

<div class="spiegazione">

A point on the parabola has coordinates $\left( x, x^2 \right)$. By the distance formula, $\overline{PA} = \sqrt{(x - 3)^2 + \left( x^2 - 0 \right)^2}$. The square root function is increasing, so the distance is smallest where its square $g(x)$ is smallest. Therefore, analyze $g$, which contains no radicals and is easier to differentiate.

The derivative $g'$ contains the polynomial $2x^3 + x - 3$, which vanishes at $x = 1$ because $2 + 1 - 3 = 0$. Synthetic division gives the factorization $(x - 1)\left( 2x^2 + 2x + 3 \right)$. The trinomial $2x^2 + 2x + 3$ has discriminant $4 - 24 < 0$ and is always positive, so $g'$ has the same sign as $x - 1$. The function $g$ decreases for $x < 1$ and increases for $x > 1$, so by the theorem on absolute extrema on an arbitrary interval, $x = 1$ is the point of **absolute minimum**.

As a check, the slope of the tangent to the parabola at $P$ is $2$, and the slope of the line $PA$ is $\dfrac{0 - 1}{3 - 1} = -\dfrac{1}{2}$. The product of their slopes is $-1$, so $PA$ is **perpendicular** to the tangent, as the figure shows.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A lamp of luminous intensity $I = 90\,\mathrm{cd}$ hangs above the center of a round table of radius $1\,\mathrm{m}$ at an adjustable height $h$. The illuminance at a point on the edge of the table is
$$
E = \frac{I \cos \alpha}{d^2} ,
$$
where $d$ is the distance from the lamp to the point and $\alpha$ is the angle between the light ray and the vertical. Find the height that maximizes the illuminance at the edge and calculate this maximum illuminance.

$$
d = \sqrt{h^2 + 1} ,
\qquad
\cos \alpha = \frac{h}{d} = \frac{h}{\sqrt{h^2 + 1}}
$$
$$
E(h) = \frac{90\,h}{\left( h^2 + 1 \right)^{\frac{3}{2}}} ,
\qquad
h > 0
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
E'(h)
&= 90 \cdot \frac{\left( h^2 + 1 \right)^{\frac{3}{2}} - h \cdot \dfrac{3}{2}\left( h^2 + 1 \right)^{\frac{1}{2}} \cdot 2h}{\left( h^2 + 1 \right)^{3}} \\[6pt]
&= 90 \cdot \frac{h^2 + 1 - 3h^2}{\left( h^2 + 1 \right)^{\frac{5}{2}}} \\[6pt]
&= \frac{90\,\left( 1 - 2h^2 \right)}{\left( h^2 + 1 \right)^{\frac{5}{2}}}
\end{aligned}
$$
$$
E'(h) > 0 \iff 1 - 2h^2 > 0 \iff 0 < h < \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}
$$
$$
E\left( \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} \right) = \frac{90 \cdot \dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}}{\left( \dfrac{3}{2} \right)^{\frac{3}{2}}} = 90 \cdot \frac{2\sqrt{3}}{9} = 20\sqrt{3} \approx 34.6\,\mathrm{lx}
$$

The illuminance at the edge is greatest when $h = \dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\,\mathrm{m} \approx 0.71\,\mathrm{m}$, and its maximum value is $20\sqrt{3}\,\mathrm{lx} \approx 34.6\,\mathrm{lx}$.

<div class="spiegazione">

The lamp, the center of the table and a point on the edge form a right triangle with legs $h$ and $1\,\mathrm{m}$. By the Pythagorean theorem, the hypotenuse has length $d = \sqrt{h^2 + 1}$. The angle $\alpha$ is adjacent to the vertical leg $h$, so $\cos \alpha = \dfrac{h}{d}$. Substitution in the formula gives $E = \dfrac{I\,h}{d^3}$, and $d^3 = \left( h^2 + 1 \right)^{\frac{3}{2}}$.

If the lamp is very low, the light hits the edge at a grazing angle and $\cos \alpha$ is small. If the lamp is very high, the distance is large. Thus, there is an optimal intermediate height. Use the **quotient rule** to find the derivative. Factoring out $\left( h^2 + 1 \right)^{\frac{1}{2}}$ in the numerator and canceling it against the denominator leaves $h^2 + 1 - 3h^2 = 1 - 2h^2$.

The denominator is positive, so $E'$ has the same sign as $1 - 2h^2$. The derivative is positive for $h < \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}} = \dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$ and negative for $h > \dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$. By the theorem on absolute extrema on an arbitrary interval, the **absolute maximum** occurs at $h = \dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\,\mathrm{m}$. To calculate its value, use $h^2 + 1 = \dfrac{3}{2}$ and $\left( \dfrac{3}{2} \right)^{\frac{3}{2}} = \dfrac{3}{2}\sqrt{\dfrac{3}{2}} = \dfrac{3\sqrt{6}}{4}$. The quotient $\dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2} \cdot \dfrac{4}{3\sqrt{6}}$ simplifies to $\dfrac{2}{3\sqrt{3}} = \dfrac{2\sqrt{3}}{9}$.

</div>
