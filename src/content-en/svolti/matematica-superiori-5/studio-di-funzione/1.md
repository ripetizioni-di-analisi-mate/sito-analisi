## Exercise 1

Find the intervals on which $f(x) = x^3 - 3x^2 - 9x + 5$ increases or decreases and the points where it has local maxima or minima.

$$
D = \mathbb{R}
$$
$$
f'(x) = 3x^2 - 6x - 9 = 3\,\left( x^2 - 2x - 3 \right) = 3\,(x + 1)(x - 3)
$$

| | $x < -1$ | $-1 < x < 3$ | $x > 3$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $f'(x)$ | $+$ | $-$ | $+$ |
| $f$ | increasing | decreasing | increasing |

$$
f(-1) = -1 - 3 + 9 + 5 = 10 ,
\qquad
f(3) = 27 - 27 - 27 + 5 = -22
$$

The function is increasing on $(-\infty, -1]$ and $[3, +\infty)$ and decreasing on $[-1, 3]$. It has a local maximum at $(-1, 10)$ and a local minimum at $(3, -22)$.

<div class="spiegazione">

The function is a polynomial, so it is defined and differentiable on all of $\mathbb{R}$. By Fermat's theorem, the only candidates for extrema are the stationary points, that is, the solutions of $f'(x) = 0$. Factoring out $3$ leaves the trinomial $x^2 - 2x - 3$, whose roots are $-1$ and $3$ because their product is $-3$ and their sum is $2$.

The coefficient of $x^2$ in the trinomial is positive, so the trinomial is positive outside the interval between its roots and negative between them. This gives the sign chart for $f'$. By the **first derivative test**, the function changes from increasing to decreasing at $x = -1$, which is therefore a point of local maximum, and from decreasing to increasing at $x = 3$, a point of local minimum. Substituting into the function gives the vertical coordinates.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Find the points where $f(x) = x + \dfrac{4}{x}$ attains local maxima and minima, and compare the values of these extrema.

$$
D = (-\infty, 0) \cup (0, +\infty)
$$
$$
f'(x) = 1 - \frac{4}{x^2} = \frac{x^2 - 4}{x^2} = \frac{(x - 2)(x + 2)}{x^2}
$$

| | $x < -2$ | $-2 < x < 0$ | $0 < x < 2$ | $x > 2$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $f'(x)$ | $+$ | $-$ | $-$ | $+$ |
| $f$ | increasing | decreasing | decreasing | increasing |

$$
f(-2) = -2 - 2 = -4 ,
\qquad
f(2) = 2 + 2 = 4
$$

The function has a local maximum at $x = -2$, with $f(-2) = -4$, and a local minimum at $x = 2$, with $f(2) = 4$.

<div class="spiegazione">

The function is defined for $x \neq 0$. The derivative of $\dfrac{4}{x}$ is $-\dfrac{4}{x^2}$, and combining the terms over a common denominator gives a fraction with numerator $x^2 - 4$. The denominator $x^2$ is positive on the domain, so $f'$ has the sign of $x^2 - 4$, which is positive for $|x| > 2$ and negative for $|x| < 2$.

The point $0$ lies outside the domain, so it separates two intervals in the sign chart. By the **first derivative test**, $x = -2$ is a point of local maximum and $x = 2$ is a point of local minimum.

The local maximum value, $-4$, is *less* than the local minimum value, $4$. This agrees with the definitions because a local maximum compares $f(-2)$ only with the values at points near $-2$. The two points lie in different intervals of the domain, separated by the vertical asymptote $x = 0$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Find the intervals on which $f(x) = x^2\,e^{-x}$ increases or decreases and the points where it has local maxima or minima.

$$
D = \mathbb{R}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
f'(x)
&= 2x\,e^{-x} + x^2 \cdot \left( -e^{-x} \right) \\[6pt]
&= e^{-x}\,\left( 2x - x^2 \right) \\[6pt]
&= x\,(2 - x)\,e^{-x}
\end{aligned}
$$

| | $x < 0$ | $0 < x < 2$ | $x > 2$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $f'(x)$ | $-$ | $+$ | $-$ |
| $f$ | decreasing | increasing | decreasing |

$$
f(0) = 0 ,
\qquad
f(2) = 4\,e^{-2} = \frac{4}{e^2} \approx 0.54
$$

The function has a local minimum at $(0, 0)$ and a local maximum at $\left( 2, \dfrac{4}{e^2} \right)$.

::grafico{f="x^2*exp(-x)" punti="0,0,pieno;2,0.541,pieno" segmenti="2,0,2,0.541" xmin=-2 xmax=8 ymin=-1 ymax=3 titolo="The function $f(x) = x^2\,e^{-x}$ has a local minimum at the origin and a local maximum at the point $\left( 2, \dfrac{4}{e^2} \right)$"}

<div class="spiegazione">

The function is the product of a polynomial and an exponential, so it is differentiable on $\mathbb{R}$. Use the **product rule** to differentiate. The derivative of $e^{-x}$ is $-e^{-x}$ by the chain rule. Then factor out $e^{-x}$ and factor the remaining term as $2x - x^2 = x\,(2 - x)$.

The factor $e^{-x}$ is always positive, so $f'$ has the same sign as the product $x\,(2 - x)$. This product is positive between the roots $0$ and $2$ and negative outside that interval. By the first derivative test, $x = 0$ is a point of **local minimum** because $f$ changes from decreasing to increasing there, and $x = 2$ is a point of **local maximum**.

The minimum at $0$ is also absolute because $f(x) = x^2\,e^{-x} \geq 0$ for every $x$ and $f(0) = 0$. The maximum is only local because as $x \to -\infty$ the function tends to $+\infty$.

</div>
