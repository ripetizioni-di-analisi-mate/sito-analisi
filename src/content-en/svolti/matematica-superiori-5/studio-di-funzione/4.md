## Exercise 1

Analyze the function $f(x) = x\,e^{-x}$ and sketch its graph.

**Domain, symmetry, intercepts and sign**
$$
D = \mathbb{R} ,
\qquad
f(-1) = -e ,
\qquad
f(1) = \frac{1}{e}
$$
$$
f(x) = 0 \iff x = 0 ,
\qquad
f(x) > 0 \iff x > 0
$$

**Limits and asymptotes**
$$
\lim_{x \to -\infty} x\,e^{-x} = -\infty ,
\qquad
\lim_{x \to -\infty} \frac{f(x)}{x} = \lim_{x \to -\infty} e^{-x} = +\infty
$$
$$
\lim_{x \to +\infty} x\,e^{-x} = \lim_{x \to +\infty} \frac{x}{e^{x}} = 0
$$

**First derivative**
$$
f'(x) = e^{-x} + x \cdot \left( -e^{-x} \right) = (1 - x)\,e^{-x}
$$
$$
f'(x) > 0 \iff x < 1 ,
\qquad
f(1) = \frac{1}{e} \approx 0.37
$$

**Second derivative**
$$
f''(x) = -e^{-x} + (1 - x) \cdot \left( -e^{-x} \right) = (x - 2)\,e^{-x}
$$
$$
f''(x) > 0 \iff x > 2 ,
\qquad
f(2) = \frac{2}{e^2} \approx 0.27
$$

The function has horizontal asymptote $y = 0$ as $x \to +\infty$, an absolute maximum at $\left( 1, \dfrac{1}{e} \right)$ and an inflection point at $\left( 2, \dfrac{2}{e^2} \right)$.

<div class="spiegazione">

The function is the product of a polynomial and an exponential, so it is defined and differentiable on $\mathbb{R}$. The values $f(-1) = -e$ and $f(1) = \dfrac{1}{e}$ are neither equal nor opposites, so the function is neither even nor odd. The factor $e^{-x}$ is always positive, so $f$ has the same sign as $x$ and the graph meets the axes only at the origin.

As $x \to -\infty$, the factor $x$ tends to $-\infty$ and the factor $e^{-x}$ tends to $+\infty$, so the product tends to $-\infty$. Also, $\dfrac{f(x)}{x} = e^{-x}$ tends to $+\infty$, so there is no oblique asymptote on the left. As $x \to +\infty$, write $f(x) = \dfrac{x}{e^{x}}$. By comparison of powers and exponentials, the exponential grows faster than $x$, so the quotient tends to $0$ and the line $y = 0$ is a **horizontal asymptote** on the right.

Use the product rule to find the first derivative and factor out $e^{-x}$. The derivative has the same sign as $1 - x$, so $f$ increases for $x < 1$ and decreases for $x > 1$. By the first derivative test, $x = 1$ is a point of maximum, and by the theorem on absolute extrema on an arbitrary interval this maximum is **absolute**. The function has no minimum because it tends to $-\infty$ on the left.

To find the second derivative, differentiate $(1 - x)\,e^{-x}$ using the product rule and again factor out $e^{-x}$. The second derivative has the same sign as $x - 2$, so the graph is concave down for $x < 2$ and concave up for $x > 2$. The second derivative changes sign at $x = 2$, which is therefore an **inflection point** with an oblique tangent because $f'(2) = -\dfrac{1}{e^2} \neq 0$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Analyze the function $f(x) = \dfrac{1 + \ln x}{x}$ and sketch its graph.

**Domain, intercepts and sign**
$$
D = (0, +\infty)
$$
$$
f(x) = 0 \iff \ln x = -1 \iff x = \frac{1}{e} ,
\qquad
f(x) > 0 \iff x > \frac{1}{e}
$$

**Limits and asymptotes**
$$
\lim_{x \to 0^+} \frac{1 + \ln x}{x} = -\infty ,
\qquad
\lim_{x \to +\infty} \left( \frac{1}{x} + \frac{\ln x}{x} \right) = 0
$$

**First derivative**
$$
f'(x) = \frac{\dfrac{1}{x} \cdot x - (1 + \ln x) \cdot 1}{x^2} = -\frac{\ln x}{x^2}
$$
$$
f'(x) > 0 \iff \ln x < 0 \iff 0 < x < 1 ,
\qquad
f(1) = 1
$$

**Second derivative**
$$
f''(x) = -\frac{\dfrac{1}{x} \cdot x^2 - \ln x \cdot 2x}{x^4} = \frac{2 \ln x - 1}{x^3}
$$
$$
f''(x) > 0 \iff \ln x > \frac{1}{2} \iff x > \sqrt{e} ,
\qquad
f\left( \sqrt{e} \right) = \frac{1 + \dfrac{1}{2}}{\sqrt{e}} = \frac{3}{2\sqrt{e}} \approx 0.91
$$

The function has vertical asymptote $x = 0$, horizontal asymptote $y = 0$, an absolute maximum at $(1, 1)$ and an inflection point at $\left( \sqrt{e}, \dfrac{3}{2\sqrt{e}} \right)$.

::grafico{f="(1+log(x))/x" punti="1,1,pieno;1.6487,0.9098,vuoto;0.3679,0,pieno" asintoti="x=0;y=0" xmin=-1 xmax=9 ymin=-2 ymax=2 titolo="Graph of $f(x) = \dfrac{1 + \ln x}{x}$ with a zero at $\dfrac{1}{e}$, maximum at $(1, 1)$, an inflection point with horizontal coordinate $\sqrt{e}$ (open circle) and asymptotes $x = 0$ and $y = 0$"}

<div class="spiegazione">

The logarithm is defined for $x > 0$, so the domain is $(0, +\infty)$ and there are no symmetries. The denominator is positive, so $f$ has the same sign as $1 + \ln x$. The numerator vanishes for $\ln x = -1$, that is, for $x = e^{-1} = \dfrac{1}{e}$, and is positive for $x > \dfrac{1}{e}$ because the logarithm is increasing.

As $x \to 0^+$, the numerator tends to $-\infty$ and the denominator tends to $0^+$, so the quotient tends to $-\infty$ and the line $x = 0$ is a **vertical asymptote**. As $x \to +\infty$, split the fraction into two terms. The first, $\dfrac{1}{x}$, tends to $0$. The second tends to $0$ because $x$ grows faster than $\ln x$. Therefore, the line $y = 0$ is a **horizontal asymptote**.

Use the quotient rule to find the first derivative. In the numerator, $\dfrac{1}{x} \cdot x = 1$ and $1 - 1 - \ln x = -\ln x$. The denominator $x^2$ is positive, so $f'$ has the sign of $-\ln x$, which is positive for $0 < x < 1$ and negative for $x > 1$. The function increases on $(0, 1]$ and decreases on $[1, +\infty)$, so $x = 1$ is the point of **absolute maximum**, with $f(1) = \dfrac{1 + 0}{1} = 1$.

To find the second derivative, differentiate $-\dfrac{\ln x}{x^2}$ using the quotient rule. Cancel a factor of $x$ in the numerator and denominator, leaving $-(1 - 2 \ln x) = 2 \ln x - 1$ in the numerator. The denominator $x^3$ is positive on the domain, so $f''$ has the same sign as $2 \ln x - 1$. This expression is positive for $\ln x > \dfrac{1}{2}$, that is, for $x > e^{\frac{1}{2}} = \sqrt{e}$. At $\sqrt{e}$ the second derivative changes sign, so the point is an **inflection point**.

</div>
