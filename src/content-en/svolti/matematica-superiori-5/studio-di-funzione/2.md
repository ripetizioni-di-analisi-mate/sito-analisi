## Exercise 1

Analyze the concavity of $f(x) = x^4 - 4x^3 + 10$, find its inflection points, and write the equation of the tangent at each one.

$$
f'(x) = 4x^3 - 12x^2 = 4x^2\,(x - 3)
$$
$$
f''(x) = 12x^2 - 24x = 12x\,(x - 2)
$$

| | $x < 0$ | $0 < x < 2$ | $x > 2$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $f''(x)$ | $+$ | $-$ | $+$ |
| concavity | up | down | up |

$$
f(0) = 10 , \qquad f'(0) = 0
\qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad
y = 10
$$
$$
f(2) = 16 - 32 + 10 = -6 , \qquad f'(2) = 32 - 48 = -16
$$
$$
y - (-6) = -16\,(x - 2)
\qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad
y = -16x + 26
$$

The graph is concave up on $(-\infty, 0]$ and $[2, +\infty)$ and concave down on $[0, 2]$. The point $(0, 10)$ is an inflection point with horizontal tangent $y = 10$, and the point $(2, -6)$ is an inflection point with oblique tangent $y = -16x + 26$.

<div class="spiegazione">

The function is a polynomial, so it is twice differentiable on $\mathbb{R}$ and its concavity follows from the sign of the second derivative. The trinomial $12x\,(x - 2)$ has roots $0$ and $2$ and is positive outside the interval between its roots and negative between them. By the theorem on the **sign of the second derivative**, the graph is concave up where $f'' > 0$ and concave down where $f'' < 0$.

The second derivative vanishes at $0$ and $2$ and changes sign at both points, so the sufficient condition shows that both are **inflection points**. At $x = 0$, we also have $f'(0) = 0$, so the tangent is horizontal and this is a horizontal inflection point. At $x = 2$, the first derivative equals $-16$, so the inflection point has an oblique tangent, whose equation follows from the formula $y - f(2) = f'(2)\,(x - 2)$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Find the intervals of concavity and the inflection points of $f(x) = \dfrac{1}{1 + x^2}$, and write the equation of the tangent at the inflection point with positive horizontal coordinate.

$$
f'(x) = -\frac{2x}{\left( 1 + x^2 \right)^2}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
f''(x)
&= -\frac{2\,\left( 1 + x^2 \right)^2 - 2x \cdot 2\,\left( 1 + x^2 \right) \cdot 2x}{\left( 1 + x^2 \right)^4} \\[6pt]
&= -\frac{2\,\left( 1 + x^2 \right) - 8x^2}{\left( 1 + x^2 \right)^3} \\[6pt]
&= \frac{6x^2 - 2}{\left( 1 + x^2 \right)^3}
\end{aligned}
$$
$$
f''(x) > 0
\iff
x^2 > \frac{1}{3}
\iff
x < -\frac{\sqrt{3}}{3} \quad \text{or} \quad x > \frac{\sqrt{3}}{3}
$$
$$
f\left( \frac{\sqrt{3}}{3} \right) = \frac{1}{1 + \dfrac{1}{3}} = \frac{3}{4} ,
\qquad
f'\left( \frac{\sqrt{3}}{3} \right) = -\frac{\dfrac{2\sqrt{3}}{3}}{\dfrac{16}{9}} = -\frac{3\sqrt{3}}{8}
$$
$$
y = \frac{3}{4} - \frac{3\sqrt{3}}{8} \left( x - \frac{\sqrt{3}}{3} \right) = -\frac{3\sqrt{3}}{8}\,x + \frac{9}{8}
$$

The graph is concave up for $x \leq -\dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{3}$ and for $x \geq \dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{3}$, and concave down between the two values. The inflection points are $\left( \pm\dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{3}, \dfrac{3}{4} \right)$, and the tangent at the one with positive horizontal coordinate is $y = -\dfrac{3\sqrt{3}}{8}\,x + \dfrac{9}{8}$.

::grafico{f="1/(1+x^2);-0.6495*x+1.125" punti="0.577,0.75,pieno;-0.577,0.75,pieno" xmin=-3 xmax=3 ymin=-1 ymax=2 legenda="y = \dfrac{1}{1 + x^2};y = -\dfrac{3\sqrt{3}}{8}\,x + \dfrac{9}{8}" titolo="The inflection points of $\dfrac{1}{1 + x^2}$ have horizontal coordinates $\pm\dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{3}$ and vertical coordinate $\dfrac{3}{4}$. At the right-hand inflection point the graph crosses its tangent line"}

<div class="spiegazione">

The denominator $1 + x^2$ is always positive, so the domain is $\mathbb{R}$. The formula $\left( \dfrac{1}{g} \right)' = -\dfrac{g'}{g^2}$ with $g(x) = 1 + x^2$ gives the first derivative. Use the **quotient rule** for the second derivative. The derivative of the denominator $\left( 1 + x^2 \right)^2$ is $2\,\left( 1 + x^2 \right) \cdot 2x$ by the chain rule. Factor out $2\,\left( 1 + x^2 \right)$ in the numerator and cancel it with a factor in the denominator. Then $-\left( 2 + 2x^2 - 8x^2 \right) = 6x^2 - 2$.

The denominator $\left( 1 + x^2 \right)^3$ is positive, so $f''$ has the same sign as $6x^2 - 2$. This trinomial vanishes when $x^2 = \dfrac{1}{3}$, that is, when $x = \pm\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{3}} = \pm\dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{3}$, and is positive outside the interval between its roots. The second derivative changes sign at both points, so they are **inflection points**. The function is even, so the two inflection points have the same vertical coordinate.

At the right-hand inflection point, $1 + x^2 = \dfrac{4}{3}$, so $f = \dfrac{3}{4}$ and $\left( 1 + x^2 \right)^2 = \dfrac{16}{9}$. The slope is $-\dfrac{2\sqrt{3}}{3} \cdot \dfrac{9}{16} = -\dfrac{3\sqrt{3}}{8}$. In the tangent line equation, the product $\dfrac{3\sqrt{3}}{8} \cdot \dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{3}$ equals $\dfrac{3}{8}$ and the constant term is $\dfrac{3}{4} + \dfrac{3}{8} = \dfrac{9}{8}$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Find the intervals of concavity and the inflection point of $f(x) = x^2 \ln x$.

$$
D = (0, +\infty)
$$
$$
f'(x) = 2x \ln x + x^2 \cdot \frac{1}{x} = 2x \ln x + x
$$
$$
f''(x) = 2 \ln x + 2x \cdot \frac{1}{x} + 1 = 2 \ln x + 3
$$
$$
f''(x) > 0
\iff
\ln x > -\frac{3}{2}
\iff
x > e^{-\frac{3}{2}}
$$
$$
f\left( e^{-\frac{3}{2}} \right) = e^{-3} \cdot \left( -\frac{3}{2} \right) = -\frac{3}{2e^3}
$$

The graph is concave down on $\left( 0, e^{-\frac{3}{2}} \right]$ and concave up on $\left[ e^{-\frac{3}{2}}, +\infty \right)$, with an inflection point at $\left( e^{-\frac{3}{2}}, -\dfrac{3}{2e^3} \right)$.

<div class="spiegazione">

The logarithm is defined for $x > 0$, so the domain is $(0, +\infty)$. Apply the **product rule** to find the first derivative and simplify the term $x^2 \cdot \dfrac{1}{x}$ to $x$. Differentiating again, the derivative of $2x \ln x$ is $2 \ln x + 2$, again by the product rule, and the derivative of $x$ is $1$, so $f''(x) = 2 \ln x + 3$.

The inequality $2 \ln x + 3 > 0$ is equivalent to $\ln x > -\dfrac{3}{2}$. Since the exponential function is increasing, applying it to both sides gives $x > e^{-\frac{3}{2}}$. The second derivative is therefore negative to the left of $e^{-\frac{3}{2}}$ and positive to the right, so the point is an **inflection point**. To find its vertical coordinate, use $\left( e^{-\frac{3}{2}} \right)^2 = e^{-3}$ and $\ln e^{-\frac{3}{2}} = -\dfrac{3}{2}$. The horizontal coordinate is approximately $0.22$.

</div>
