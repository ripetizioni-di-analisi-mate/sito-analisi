## Exercise 1: Diluting a solution

A $100\,\mathrm{mL}$ portion of a $1.0\,\mathrm{mol/L}$ solution is taken to a final volume of $500\,\mathrm{mL}$ without losing solute. What is the final concentration?

- $0.20\,\mathrm{mol/L}$
- $5.0\,\mathrm{mol/L}$
- $1.0\,\mathrm{mol/L}$
- $0.50\,\mathrm{mol/L}$
- $0.020\,\mathrm{mol/L}$

<div class="spiegazione">

**Answer.** $0.20\,\mathrm{mol/L}$

The initial amount is $1.0\cdot0.100=0.100\,\mathrm{mol}$ and remains constant. The final volume is $0.500\,\mathrm L$, so $c_f=\dfrac{0.100}{0.500}=0.20\,\mathrm{mol/L}$. Volume increases fivefold and concentration falls to one fifth. Five hundred milliliters is the final volume, not the volume of added solvent.

$$
\begin{aligned}n&=1.0\cdot0.100=0.100\,\mathrm{mol},\\[6pt]c_f&=\frac{0.100}{0.500}=0.20\,\mathrm{mol/L}.\end{aligned}
$$

Dilution increases the volume available to the same amount of solute. The first step determines how many moles are in the portion taken; the second distributes them over the final volume. To use liters and moles in the formula, milliliters are divided by one thousand. The curve shows the inverse relationship between concentration and volume at fixed amount: initial and final points lie on the same curve because concentration times volume is constant.

</div>

## Exercise 2: Mixing different concentrations

A $100\,\mathrm{mL}$ portion of a $0.20\,\mathrm{mol/L}$ solution is mixed with $300\,\mathrm{mL}$ of a $0.60\,\mathrm{mol/L}$ solution of the same solute. With no reaction and additive volumes, what is the final concentration?

- $0.50\,\mathrm{mol/L}$
- $0.40\,\mathrm{mol/L}$
- $0.80\,\mathrm{mol/L}$
- $0.20\,\mathrm{mol/L}$
- $2.0\,\mathrm{mol/L}$

<div class="spiegazione">

**Answer.** $0.50\,\mathrm{mol/L}$

The portions contain $0.100\cdot0.20=0.020\,\mathrm{mol}$ and $0.300\cdot0.60=0.180\,\mathrm{mol}$. In total there are $0.200\,\mathrm{mol}$ in $0.400\,\mathrm L$, so $c_f=0.50\,\mathrm{mol/L}$. The simple mean $0.40$ would apply with equal volumes; here the more concentrated solution contributes three times the volume.

$$
\begin{aligned}n_1&=0.20\cdot0.100=0.020\,\mathrm{mol},\\[6pt]n_2&=0.60\cdot0.300=0.180\,\mathrm{mol},\\[6pt]c_f&=\frac{0.020+0.180}{0.100+0.300}=0.50\,\mathrm{mol/L}.\end{aligned}
$$

The larger volume gives the second solution more weight in the result. The final concentration therefore lies between the initial values and is closer to zero point sixty. Adding volumes is authorized by the problem's assumption; in a real situation the final volume might need to be measured. Conservation concerns the moles of the same solute, which are added before forming the new ratio.

</div>

## Exercise 3: Excess after neutralization

A $0.030\,\mathrm{mol}$ amount of hydronium is mixed with $0.020\,\mathrm{mol}$ of hydroxide. After the one-to-one reaction, what amount remains in excess?

- $0.010\,\mathrm{mol}$ of hydronium
- $0.010\,\mathrm{mol}$ of hydroxide
- $0.050\,\mathrm{mol}$ of hydronium
- None, because every acid–base mixture is neutral
- $0.030\,\mathrm{mol}$ of hydroxide

<div class="spiegazione">

**Answer.** $0.010\,\mathrm{mol}$ of hydronium

The reaction $\mathrm{H_3O^++OH^-\to2H_2O}$ consumes equal amounts of the two ions. All hydroxide reacts with $0.020\,\mathrm{mol}$ of hydronium, leaving $0.030-0.020=0.010\,\mathrm{mol}$. Without the final volume, the excess in moles can be determined, but not its concentration or the numerical pH.

$$
n_{\mathrm{H_3O^+,excess}}=0.030-0.020=0.010\,\mathrm{mol}.
$$

The neutralization ratio is set by the ionic equation, so it does not depend on the portions' initial volumes once amounts are known. The less abundant species is fully consumed in the stated reaction model. The difference gives the other species' remaining moles. A result in moles cannot be converted into pH without an additional volume datum; separating these levels avoids attributing information to the problem that it does not contain.

</div>
