## Exercise 1

Find the intersection of the lines $y=2x$ and $y=-x+3$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
2x&=-x+3,\\[4pt]
3x&=3,\\[4pt]
x&=1,\qquad y=2\cdot1=2.
\end{aligned}
\qquad P=(1,2) .
$$

::grafico{f="2*x;-x+3" punti="1,2,pieno,P" xmin=-2 xmax=4 ymin=-2 ymax=6 titolo="The point $P$ satisfies both equations."}

<div class="spiegazione">

A common point has the same input in both rules and must give the same output. Therefore equate $2x$ and $-x+3$. The resulting linear equation has one solution because the coefficient of $x$ after rearranging is $3$. At input $1$, both rules give $2$, since $2\cdot1=2$ and $-1+3=2$.

The system can also be written in standard form as $2x-y=0$ and $x+y=3$. Adding corresponding sides eliminates $y$, which has opposite coefficients. The resulting equation is $3x=3$, just as when the two outputs were set equal. On the graph, to the left of $x=1$ the line $y=2x$ is lower, as at $x=0$, where the outputs are $0$ and $3$. It is higher on the right, for example at $x=2$, where the outputs are $4$ and $1$. This agrees with a unique intersection because the output difference is the linear rule $3x-3$, which vanishes only once. Subtracting the two rules gives the same input. The sign of the difference tells which line is higher at each input, while requiring the difference to be zero identifies the common point.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Determine how many intersections the lines $y=(k+1)x+2$ and $y=3x+2$ have for each $k\in\mathbb R$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
(k+1)x+2&=3x+2\\[4pt]
(k-2)x&=0 .
\end{aligned}
$$

::grafico{f="2;2*x+2;3*x+2" legenda="k=-1;k=1;k=2" xmin=-3 xmax=3 ymin=-4 ymax=7 punti="0,2,pieno,P" titolo="The intercept $P(0,2)$ stays fixed as $k$ varies."}

<div class="spiegazione">

Both lines pass through $(0,2)$. Equating their outputs gives $(k-2)x=0$. If $k\neq2$, division by $k-2$ is allowed and the only common point is $(0,2)$. If $k=2$, the first rule becomes $y=3x+2$, the same as the second. The lines coincide and have infinitely many points in common. Division by $k-2$ is allowed only in the first case.

The value $k$ changes only the slope of the first line. The intercept stays $2$ even at $k=-1$, when the first rule becomes the horizontal line $y=2$. It still meets the second at $(0,2)$. At $k=2$, however, the slopes become equal while the intercepts already agree. Both equations then describe the same entire line. No parameter value gives distinct parallel lines because the common point $(0,2)$ belongs to both for every $k$. Separating the cases before dividing keeps all solutions even when the coefficient of the unknown vanishes. For instance, at $k=2$, the point $(1,5)$ belongs to both lines. At $k=1$, $(1,5)$ no longer belongs to the first, whereas $(0,2)$ still satisfies both equations.

</div>
