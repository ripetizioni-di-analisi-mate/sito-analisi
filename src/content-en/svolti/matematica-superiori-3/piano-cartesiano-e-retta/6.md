## Exercise 1

Find the intersection of the lines $y=2x$ and $y=-x+3$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
2x&=-x+3,\\[4pt]
3x&=3,\\[4pt]
x&=1,\qquad y=2\cdot1=2.
\end{aligned}
\qquad P=(1,2) .
$$

::grafico{f="2*x;-x+3" punti="1,2,pieno,P" xmin=-2 xmax=4 ymin=-2 ymax=6 titolo="The point $P$ satisfies both equations."}

<div class="spiegazione">

A common point has the same input in both rules and must give the same output. Therefore equate $2x$ and $-x+3$. The resulting linear equation has one solution because the coefficient of $x$ after rearranging is $3$. At input $1$, both rules give $2$, since $2\cdot1=2$ and $-1+3=2$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Determine how many intersections the lines $y=(k+1)x+2$ and $y=3x+2$ have for each $k\in\mathbb R$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
(k+1)x+2&=3x+2\\[4pt]
(k-2)x&=0 .
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Both lines pass through $(0,2)$. Equating their outputs gives $(k-2)x=0$. If $k\neq2$, division by $k-2$ is allowed and the only common point is $(0,2)$. If $k=2$, the first rule becomes $y=3x+2$, the same as the second. The lines coincide and have infinitely many points in common. Division by $k-2$ is allowed only in the first case.

</div>
