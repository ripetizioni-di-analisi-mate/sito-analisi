## Exercise 1

Find the perpendicular to $y=2x-3$ through $P(4,1)$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
2m_\perp&=-1,\qquad m_\perp=-\frac{1}{2},\\[4pt]
y-1&=-\frac{1}{2}(x-4),\\[4pt]
y&=-\frac{1}{2}x+3 .
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned} 2x-3&=-\frac12 x+3,\\[4pt] \frac52x&=6,\\[4pt] x&=\frac{12}{5},\qquad y=\frac95 .\end{aligned}
$$

::grafico{f="2*x-3;-0.5*x+3" legenda="y=2x-3;y=-\dfrac12x+3" xmin=-1 xmax=6 ymin=-3 ymax=7 uguale=1 punti="2.4,1.8,pieno,H;4,1,pieno,P" titolo="The intersection $H$ lies on the perpendicular through $P$."}

<div class="spiegazione">

The given line has slope $2$, so a perpendicular slope must be $-\dfrac{1}{2}$ to make the product $-1$. Its output must be $1$ at the horizontal coordinate $4$. This is why the vertical displacement $y-1$ equals the slope times the horizontal displacement $x-4$. Expanding gives an intercept of $3$. Substituting $4$ into the rule returns the output $1$.

Integer displacements can check the right angle. On the given line, moving right by $1$ gives a rise of $2$, so $(1,2)$ gives its direction. On the perpendicular, moving right by $2$ gives a drop of $1$, giving direction $(2,-1)$. Their dot product is $1\cdot2+2(-1)=0$. The lines meet at $(\dfrac{12}{5},\dfrac95)$, obtained by equating $2x-3$ and $-\dfrac12x+3$. The given point $P$ is not the intersection because the original line would give output $5$ there. It fixes which of the lines with the perpendicular direction is required. The intersection also checks the direction on the graph. From $H$ to $P$, the horizontal change is $\dfrac85$ and the vertical change is $-\dfrac45$, again giving the ratio $-\dfrac12$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Find the standard form of the perpendicular to $2x-3y+6=0$ through $P(1,2)$ and verify the coefficient condition.

$$
\begin{aligned}
r&:\ 3x+2y+c=0,\\[4pt]
3\cdot1+2\cdot2+c&=0
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad c=-7,\\[4pt]
2\cdot3+(-3)\cdot2&=0.
\end{aligned}
\qquad r:\ 3x+2y-7=0 .
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The coefficients of the required line must satisfy $2a-3b=0$. The pair $(3,2)$ satisfies this equation and fixes the direction, while the constant is still free. Passing through $P(1,2)$ requires adding $-7$ to $3+4$. The final identity confirms that the coefficients do give perpendicularity. The slopes $\dfrac{2}{3}$ and $-\dfrac{3}{2}$ give the same check.

The standard-form criterion acts on the components of the normal vectors. The original line has normal $(2,-3)$ and the new line has normal $(3,2)$. Their zero dot product shows that the line directions are perpendicular as well. Any nonzero multiple of $(3,2)$ could have been used, provided the constant was scaled by the same factor. For example, coefficients $(6,4)$ would give constant $-14$, so division by two would recover the line already found. The slope check is possible because both coefficients of $y$ are nonzero. The coefficient criterion would still apply in a vertical or horizontal case.

</div>
