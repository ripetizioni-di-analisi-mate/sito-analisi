## Exercise 1

Find the distance from the origin to the line $3x+4y-5=0$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
d&=\frac{|3\cdot0+4\cdot0-5|}{\sqrt{3^2+4^2}}\\[5pt]
&=\frac{5}{\sqrt{25}}=1 .
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned} H&=(3\lambda,4\lambda),\\[4pt] 3(3\lambda)+4(4\lambda)-5=0&\iff\lambda=\frac15,\\[4pt] H&=\left(\frac35,\frac45\right).\end{aligned}
$$

::grafico{f="(5-3*x)/4" legenda="3x+4y-5=0" xmin=-2 xmax=3 ymin=-2 ymax=3 uguale=1 punti="0,0,pieno,O;0.6,0.8,pieno,H" segmenti="0,0,0.6,0.8" titolo="Segment $OH$ follows the normal direction $(3,4)$."}

<div class="spiegazione">

At $P(0,0)$ the coordinate terms are zero, leaving the constant $-5$. Its absolute value is $5$ because the distance is a length. The quantity $\sqrt{3^2+4^2}=5$ is the length of the normal vector $(3,4)$. Dividing by $5$ measures the displacement from the line in units of length. In this case it is $1$.

To construct the foot, start at the origin and move in the normal direction $(3,4)$. After a displacement by factor $\lambda$, the coordinates are $(3\lambda,4\lambda)$. Requiring membership on the line gives $9\lambda+16\lambda=5$, so $\lambda=\dfrac15$. The foot is $\left(\dfrac35,\dfrac45\right)$, and recomputing the distance gives $\sqrt{\left(\dfrac35\right)^2+\left(\dfrac45\right)^2}=1$. This explains why the constant $-5$ does not give a distance of five units. It is the discrepancy in the equation, while the normal direction does not have unit length. Division by its length gives the required geometric quantity. Multiplying the line's equation by $2$ would give numerator $10$ and denominator $10$. The quotient remains $1$, confirming that the distance depends on the line while being independent of the chosen equation.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Let $P(1,4)$ and $r:y=x$. Find the foot $H$ of the perpendicular from $P$ and calculate the distance $PH$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
y-4&=-(x-1),\\[4pt]
x=-x+5&\iff x=\frac{5}{2},\\[4pt]
H&=\left(\frac{5}{2},\frac{5}{2}\right),\\[4pt]
PH&=\sqrt{\left(\frac{3}{2}\right)^2+
                \left(-\frac{3}{2}\right)^2}
=\frac{3\sqrt 2}{2}.
\end{aligned}
$$

::grafico{f="x" segmenti="1,4,2.5,2.5" punti="1,4,pieno,P;2.5,2.5,pieno,H" xmin=-1 xmax=5 ymin=-1 ymax=6 uguale=1 titolo="The segment $PH$ meets $r$ at a right angle."}

<div class="spiegazione">

The line $r$ has slope $1$, so the perpendicular has slope $-1$. Requiring it to pass through $P$ gives $y=-x+5$. The point $H$ also lies on $r$, so its coordinates agree and it can be found by equating $x$ and $-x+5$. From $P$ to $H$ the displacement is $\left(\dfrac{3}{2},-\dfrac{3}{2}\right)$. The Pythagorean theorem then gives $PH=\dfrac{3\sqrt 2}{2}$. The distance formula applied to $x-y=0$ gives the same value, $\dfrac{|1-4|}{\sqrt{2}}$.

Since $r$ is the bisector $y=x$, reflecting $P(1,4)$ across it swaps the coordinates and gives $P'(4,1)$. The midpoint of segment $PP'$ is $\left(\dfrac52,\dfrac52\right)$, providing another way to find $H$. The segment has displacement $(3,-3)$ and therefore meets the direction $(1,1)$ of $r$ at a right angle. Its length is $3\sqrt2$, of which $PH$ is half. To see why this is the minimum distance, take a different point $Q$ on $r$. Triangle $PHQ$ is right-angled at $H$, giving $PQ^2=PH^2+HQ^2>PH^2$ when $Q\neq H$.

</div>
