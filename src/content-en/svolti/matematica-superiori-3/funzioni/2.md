## Exercise 1

Find the real domain of $f(x)=\dfrac{\sqrt{x-1}}{x-4}$.

$$
\begin{cases}
x-1\geq 0\\
x-4\neq 0
\end{cases}
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\begin{cases}
x\geq 1\\
x\neq 4
\end{cases}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad D_f=[1,4)\cup(4,+\infty) .
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The radicand $x-1$ may be zero because the square root is in the numerator. The condition $x\geq 1$ therefore gives the interval $[1,+\infty)$. Remove $4$ from that same interval because it makes the denominator zero. Removing an interior point splits the interval into two pieces that are open at that point. The endpoint $1$ is still included because the numerator is zero there and the denominator is $-3$. In particular, $f(1)=0$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Find the real domain of $g(x)=\dfrac{\sqrt{4-x}}{\sqrt{x^2-1}}$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
4-x\geq 0 &\iff x\leq 4,\\[4pt]
x^2-1>0 &\iff (x-1)(x+1)>0\\[4pt]
&\iff x\in(-\infty,-1)\cup(1,+\infty).
\end{aligned}
\qquad D_g=(-\infty,-1)\cup(1,4] .
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The two square roots have different roles. The numerator only requires $4-x\geq 0$. The square root in the denominator must exist and be nonzero, so impose $x^2-1>0$. The product $(x-1)(x+1)$ is positive when both factors are negative or when both are positive. This yields two intervals, from which we keep only the inputs satisfying $x\leq 4$. The point $4$ is allowed because it makes only the numerator zero, whereas $-1$ and $1$ make the denominator zero.

</div>
