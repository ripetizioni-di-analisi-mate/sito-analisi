## Exercise 1

Find the inverse of $f(x)=2x+1$ on $\mathbb R$ and verify that $(f\circ f^{-1})(y)=y$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
y=2x+1&\iff 2x=y-1\\[4pt]
&\iff x=\frac{y-1}{2},\\[4pt]
f^{-1}(y)&=\frac{y-1}{2},\\[4pt]
f\left(\frac{y-1}{2}\right)&=2\cdot\frac{y-1}{2}+1=y.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Start with the output $y$ and look for the input that produces it. After subtracting $1$ from both sides, divide by $2$, which is nonzero. The result exists and is unique for each $y\in\mathbb R$, so the inverse has domain $\mathbb R$. Substitution of $\dfrac{y-1}{2}$ into the original function returns exactly $y$. On the graphs, $(0,1)$ for $f$ corresponds to $(1,0)$ for $f^{-1}$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Find the inverse of $g(x)=(x-2)^2+1$ with domain $(-\infty,2]$ and evaluate $g^{-1}(10)$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
y=(x-2)^2+1&\iff (x-2)^2=y-1,\\[4pt]
x\leq 2&\Longrightarrow x-2=-\sqrt{y-1},\\[4pt]
g^{-1}(y)&=2-\sqrt{y-1}\qquad(y\geq 1),\\[4pt]
g^{-1}(10)&=2-\sqrt{9}=-1 .
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The quantity $2-x$ is nonnegative on the domain and decreases as $x$ increases. Its square is therefore strictly decreasing, so $g$ has just one preimage for each output it takes. The square can take every nonnegative value on the domain, so the range of $g$ is $[1,+\infty)$, which becomes the domain of the inverse. The negative sign in $x-2=-\sqrt{y-1}$ is required by $x\leq 2$. Indeed, the input $-1$ lies on the required branch and satisfies $g(-1)=10$.

</div>
