## Exercise 1

Find the inverse of $f(x)=2x+1$ on $\mathbb R$ and verify that $(f\circ f^{-1})(y)=y$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
y=2x+1&\iff 2x=y-1\\[4pt]
&\iff x=\frac{y-1}{2},\\[4pt]
f^{-1}(y)&=\frac{y-1}{2},\\[4pt]
f\left(\frac{y-1}{2}\right)&=2\cdot\frac{y-1}{2}+1=y.
\end{aligned}
$$

::grafico{f="2*x+1;(x-1)/2;x" legenda="y=f(x);y=f^{-1}(x);y=x" xmin=-3 xmax=5 ymin=-3 ymax=5 uguale=1 punti="0,1,pieno,A;1,0,pieno,B" segmenti="0,1,1,0" titolo="Swapping coordinates reflects $A(0,1)$ to $B(1,0)$."}

<div class="spiegazione">

Start with the output $y$ and look for the input that produces it. After subtracting $1$ from both sides, divide by $2$, which is nonzero. The result exists and is unique for each $y\in\mathbb R$, so the inverse has domain $\mathbb R$. Substitution of $\dfrac{y-1}{2}$ into the original function returns exactly $y$. On the graphs, $(0,1)$ for $f$ corresponds to $(1,0)$ for $f^{-1}$.

The equality $y=2x+1$ describes the original direction. To recover $x$, undo the two operations in reverse order because adding $1$ is the final operation performed by $f$. Reciprocating the slope alone would give $y=\dfrac x2+1$, which at $x=1$ gives $\dfrac32$ instead of $0$. Subtracting the constant before dividing produces the correct intercept for $f^{-1}$. The other composition can be checked too. Applying the inverse to the output $f(x)$ gives $(f^{-1}\circ f)(x)=\dfrac{(2x+1)-1}{2}=x$ for every real input. The identity shows that moving along either rule and back along the other returns to the starting value.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Find the inverse of $g(x)=(x-2)^2+1$ with domain $(-\infty,2]$ and evaluate $g^{-1}(10)$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
y=(x-2)^2+1&\iff (x-2)^2=y-1,\\[4pt]
x\leq 2&\Longrightarrow x-2=-\sqrt{y-1},\\[4pt]
g^{-1}(y)&=2-\sqrt{y-1}\qquad(y\geq 1),\\[4pt]
g^{-1}(10)&=2-\sqrt{9}=-1 .
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned} g(-1)&=(-1-2)^2+1=10,\\[4pt] 5&\notin(-\infty,2] .\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The quantity $2-x$ is nonnegative on the domain and decreases as $x$ increases. Its square is therefore strictly decreasing, so $g$ has just one preimage for each output it takes. The square can take every nonnegative value on the domain, so the range of $g$ is $[1,+\infty)$, which becomes the domain of the inverse. The negative sign in $x-2=-\sqrt{y-1}$ is required by $x\leq 2$. Indeed, the input $-1$ lies on the required branch and satisfies $g(-1)=10$.

The value $y=10$ gives two algebraic solutions to the unrestricted equation because $y-1=9$, so $x-2$ can be $-3$ or $3$. The inputs are $-1$ and $5$. The second gives the same output for the polynomial on $\mathbb R$, but it is outside the assigned domain. The inverse on the restricted branch therefore cannot return it. At the boundary $y=1$, the two branches meet at the vertex, and the inverse accepts input $1$, returning $2$. Checking the minimum output prevents us from giving the inverse inputs below $1$, for which $y-1$ would be negative and the square root undefined.

</div>
