## Exercise 1

Find the real domain of $f(x)=\dfrac1{\sqrt{x-1}}$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
x-1\geq0,\quad \sqrt{x-1}\neq0
&\iff x-1>0,\\[4pt]
D&=(1,+\infty).
\end{aligned}
$$

::grafico{f="1/sqrt(x-1)" xmin=-0.5 xmax=6 ymin=-1 ymax=5 asintoti="x=1" punti="2,1,pieno" titolo="The graph is defined only to the right of $x=1$"}

<div class="spiegazione">

The square root requires a nonnegative radicand, and the denominator must be nonzero. A zero radicand would make the root zero as well, so the radicand must be strictly positive. This excludes the point $1$.

The inequality $x-1\geq0$ would be sufficient for the root $\sqrt{x-1}$ alone. In the fraction, its value also matters, because we must be able to divide. If $x>1$, the radicand is positive and so is the root, so the reciprocal exists. For example, at $x=2$ the denominator is $\sqrt1=1$. If $x<1$ the radicand is negative and the root is not real. At $x=1$ the root exists but equals zero, so the fraction is undefined. The entire graph is to the right of the vertical line $x=1$. Approaching $1$ from the right means dividing by smaller and smaller positive values, which explains why the ordinate grows.

Comparing $x=2$ and $x=5$ shows how the value changes. The denominators are $1$ and $2$, respectively, so the reciprocal drops from $1$ to $\dfrac12$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Find the real domain of $f(x)=\dfrac{\sqrt{x+1}}{\sqrt{4-x}}$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
x+1\geq0&\iff x\geq-1,\\[4pt]
4-x>0&\iff x<4,\\[4pt]
D&=[-1,+\infty)\cap(-\infty,4)=[-1,4).
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The two roots have different conditions because one is in the numerator and the other is in the denominator. The endpoint $-1$ gives a zero numerator but a positive denominator. The endpoint $4$ would give a zero denominator. Therefore the first is included and the second is excluded.

The first square root is defined on the ray from $-1$ to the right. The second square root is positive on the ray to the left of $4$. On their overlap both expressions exist and division is possible. At $x=-1$ we get $\dfrac{\sqrt0}{\sqrt5}=0$, so the lower boundary belongs to the domain. At $x=4$ the quotient would be $\dfrac{\sqrt5}{\sqrt0}$, which is undefined. The conditions must hold together away from the endpoints too. At $x=-2$ the numerator is not real, and at $x=5$ the denominator is not real. The point $x=0$ instead gives $\dfrac{1}{2}$ and checks an interior value.

The domain can also be read as the intersection of two rays with different boundaries. Any point to the left of the first boundary or to the right of the second fails at least one necessary condition.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Find the real domain of $f(x)=\dfrac1{\sqrt{\dfrac{x-1}{x+2}}}$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{x-1}{x+2}>0&\iff
x<-2\ \lor\ x>1,\\[4pt]
D&=(-\infty,-2)\cup(1,+\infty).
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The inner denominator excludes $-2$. Since the root itself is in a denominator, the radicand must be strictly positive. The quotient is positive when numerator and denominator are both negative or both positive. At $x=1$ the quotient is zero, so this endpoint is excluded as well.

On the interval $-2<x<1$, the numerator is negative and the denominator is positive. For example, the radicand at $x=0$ is $-\dfrac12$ and has no real square root. For $x<-2$, both terms in the fraction are negative, as at $x=-3$, where the ratio is $4$. For $x>1$ both are positive, as at $x=2$, where the ratio is $\dfrac14$. The domain therefore contains both exterior intervals. The two boundaries are excluded for different reasons. At $-2$ the ratio itself is undefined, while at $1$ the ratio exists and is zero but taking the root would leave division by zero.

For the square root of the quotient alone, the last boundary would be included. Here there is also a reciprocal, so a domain condition becomes a strict inequality.

</div>
