## Exercise 1

Solve $\sqrt{x-1}\leq3$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
0\leq x-1\leq9 &\iff 1\leq x\leq10,\\[4pt]
S&=[1,10].
\end{aligned}
$$

::grafico{f="sqrt(x-1);3" xmin=-0.5 xmax=13 ymin=-0.5 ymax=4.5 punti="1,0,pieno;10,3,pieno" titolo="The allowed region starts at $x=1$ and ends at height $3$"}

<div class="spiegazione">

The lower bound $x\geq1$ comes from the domain. The upper bound comes from $x-1\leq9$. The point $1$ gives a zero square root, while at $10$ we have $\sqrt9=3$. Both satisfy the comparison.

The square root on the left is nonnegative wherever it is defined, and the threshold $3$ is positive as well. Squaring therefore preserves the comparison. The condition $x-1\leq9$ gives $x\leq10$. However, the constraint $x\geq1$ must still hold. For example, $x=0$ passes the squared comparison, but the original radicand is $-1$, so that value is excluded. At $x=11$ the square root exists but is $\sqrt{10}>3$, so the second constraint fails. Every value in between satisfies both. In particular, at $x=5$ the radicand is $4$ and its square root is $2$. The domain determines the lower boundary and the height $3$ determines the upper one.

The square root increases with its radicand on the nonnegative numbers. Thus each $x$ between $1$ and $10$ gives a y-coordinate between $0$ and $3$, as shown by the relevant portion.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Solve $\sqrt{x^2-1}\leq\sqrt3$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
0\leq x^2-1\leq3 &\iff 1\leq x^2\leq4,\\[4pt]
S&=[-2,-1]\cup[1,2].
\end{aligned}
$$

::grafico{f="sqrt(x^2-1);sqrt(3)" xmin=-3.3 xmax=3.3 ymin=-0.8 ymax=3.5 punti="-2,1.7320508076,pieno;-1,0,pieno;1,0,pieno;2,1.7320508076,pieno" titolo="The two allowed portions extend from the domain endpoints to the threshold $\sqrt3$"}

<div class="spiegazione">

With both sides nonnegative, the upper bound is equivalent to $x^2-1\leq3$. Existence of the root on the left also requires $x^2-1\geq0$. Thus the allowed values have distance from zero between $1$ and $2$. The inner endpoints give a zero radicand and remain included.

The condition $x^2\geq1$ gives the two alternatives $x\leq-1$ and $x\geq1$. The condition $x^2\leq4$ gives $-2\leq x\leq2$. Intersecting each branch of the domain with this interval gives the two closed portions. For example, at the origin the upper comparison on the squares would hold, but $x^2-1=-1$ rules out a real root. At $x=3$ the square root exists but is $\sqrt8>\sqrt3$. Equality holds at the outer endpoints $x=\pm2$, because $x^2-1=3$. The inner endpoints $x=\pm1$ give $0\leq\sqrt3$. The graph shows both portions and the middle region in which there is no curve.

With a strict threshold, the inner endpoints would remain allowed because their ordinate is zero. The outer endpoints would be excluded because their ordinate equals $\sqrt3$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Solve $\sqrt{2x+3}>x$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
D&=\left[-\frac32,+\infty\right),\\[4pt]
x\in\left[-\frac32,0\right)&\implies \sqrt{2x+3}>x,\\[4pt]
x\geq0,\quad \sqrt{2x+3}>x &\iff 2x+3>x^2,\\[4pt]
(x+1)(x-3)&<0\implies x\in[0,3),\\[4pt]
S&=\left[-\frac32,3\right).
\end{aligned}
$$

::grafico{f="sqrt(2*x+3);x" xmin=-2.5 xmax=5 ymin=-2 ymax=5 punti="-1.5,0,pieno;3,3,pieno" titolo="The root lies above the line from $-\dfrac32$, included, to $3$, excluded"}

<div class="spiegazione">

For $x<0$, any real square root is greater than $x$, so the domain is sufficient. For $x\geq0$, the squares can be compared. The product $(x+1)(x-3)$ is negative on $(-1,3)$, which becomes $[0,3)$ in the second case. At $3$ the root equals the right-hand side, so $3$ is excluded.

The domain starts at $x=-\dfrac32$, where the radicand $2x+3$ equals zero. The inequality holds there because $0>-\dfrac32$. Before the zero of the right-hand side, the signs alone establish the inequality. From $x=0$ onward, subtracting $2x+3$ from $x^2$ gives $x^2-2x-3=(x+1)(x-3)$. The first factor is positive for $x\geq0$, so the sign depends on $x-3$. At $x=0$ the two parts of the reasoning agree, since $\sqrt3>0$. The squared equality also gives $-1$, but the right-hand side is negative there, so this value belongs to the first branch of the reasoning. It is not an equality point of the original inequality.

The graph therefore has a single intersection, $(3,3)$. To its right, for example at $x=5$, the root is $\sqrt{13}<5$, so the curve lies below the line.

</div>
