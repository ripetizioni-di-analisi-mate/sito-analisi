## Exercise 1

Solve $\sqrt{x+2}=x$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\sqrt{x+2}=x &\iff \begin{cases}x\geq0,\\ x^2=x+2.\end{cases}\\[4pt]
x^2-x-2&=(x-2)(x+1),\\[4pt]
S&=\{-1,2\}\cap[0,+\infty)=\{2\}.
\end{aligned}
$$

::grafico{f="sqrt(x+2);x" xmin=-3 xmax=4 ymin=-2 ymax=4 punti="-1,1,pieno;-1,-1,pieno;2,2,pieno" segmenti="-1,-1,-1,1" titolo="At $x=-1$ the ordinates are opposites, and at $x=2$ they agree"}

<div class="spiegazione">

Squaring does not preserve the sign. At $-1$, the square root equals $\sqrt1=1$, while the right-hand side equals $-1$. The condition $x\geq0$ therefore removes that candidate. At $2$, both sides equal $2$.

The square root already exists for $x\geq-2$. The constraint $x\geq0$ is stronger because the right-hand side must have the correct sign. Solve the polynomial equation and intersect the result with $[0,+\infty)$. The candidate $-1$ belongs to the square-root domain but not to this smaller set. To verify $2$, calculate $\sqrt{2+2}=\sqrt4=2$. The graphs of $y=\sqrt{x+2}$ and $y=x$ show the same situation. At $x=-1$ the ordinates are opposites, and at $x=2$ they agree. The equation requires matching ordinates, whereas merely matching squares also allows the first case.

The reverse direction can also be checked algebraically. If $x$ satisfies both $x^2=x+2$ and $x\geq0$, then $x$ is exactly the nonnegative number whose square equals $x+2$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Solve $\sqrt{3x+1}=|x-3|$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
3x+1&=(x-3)^2,\\[4pt]
x^2-9x+8&=(x-1)(x-8)=0,\\[4pt]
S&=\{1,8\}.
\end{aligned}
$$

::grafico{f="sqrt(3*x+1);abs(x-3)" xmin=-0.8 xmax=9 ymin=-1 ymax=7 punti="1,2,pieno;8,5,pieno;3,0,pieno" titolo="Each branch of the absolute value gives one intersection"}

<div class="spiegazione">

The absolute value on the right is nonnegative for every real $x$, so once the domain has been imposed we can square both sides. Both candidates satisfy $3x+1\geq0$. At $1$, both sides equal $2$. At $8$, they both equal $5$.

Expand the square as $(x-3)^2=x^2-6x+9$. Moving the radicand to the right gives $x^2-6x+9-3x-1=x^2-9x+8$. The numbers $1$ and $8$ have sum $9$ and product $8$, so they give the two factors. The original domain is $x\geq-\dfrac13$, which both satisfy. The point $x=1$ lies on the branch where $x-3$ is negative, so $|1-3|=2$. The point $x=8$ lies on the opposite branch and $|8-3|=5$. Thus there are two intersections on different branches of the absolute value graph. In this case both branches have nonnegative y-coordinates, unlike a right-hand side without the absolute value.

The factorization identifies every candidate because a product of two linear factors is zero only where at least one factor is zero. Once both are confirmed to be in the domain, the solution set is complete.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Solve $\sqrt{x+9}-\sqrt x=1$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
x&\geq0,\\[4pt]
\sqrt{x+9}&=1+\sqrt x,\\[4pt]
x+9&=1+2\sqrt x+x,\\[4pt]
\sqrt x&=4\iff x=16.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The common domain is $x\geq0$. After moving $\sqrt x$ to the right, both sides are nonnegative. Squaring the right-hand side gives the cross term $2\sqrt x$, and the terms in $x$ cancel. Substitution gives $\sqrt{25}-\sqrt{16}=5-4=1$, so the solution is $16$.

After the first squaring, $x+9=1+2\sqrt x+x$ remains. Subtracting $x+1$ from both sides gives $8=2\sqrt x$, so $\sqrt x=4$. The right-hand side is positive at this step too, so the second squaring is reversible. Rationalizing the difference gives another check. For $x\geq0$, we have $\sqrt{x+9}-\sqrt x=\dfrac{9}{\sqrt{x+9}+\sqrt x}$. The denominator is positive because the first square root is at least $3$. At $x=16$ the denominator is $5+4=9$, so the fraction gives $1$. Comparing the rationalized form with the required value shows the denominator must equal $9$.

In the rationalized form, equality requires $\sqrt{x+9}+\sqrt x=9$. Alongside the difference equal to $1$, subtracting gives $2\sqrt x=8$, recovering $x=16$.

</div>
