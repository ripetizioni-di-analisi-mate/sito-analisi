## Exercise 1

Solve $|x-1|+|x-4|=5$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
x<1&\implies 5-2x=5 \iff x=0,\\[4pt]
1\leq x\leq4&\implies 3=5,\\[4pt]
x>4&\implies 2x-5=5 \iff x=5,\\[4pt]
S&=\{0,5\}.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Between $1$ and $4$, the distances add to the length of the segment, which is $3$. To reach $5$, two additional units are needed. Moving one unit to the left or right of the segment adds those two units. The calculation on the outer branches gives exactly $0$ and $5$.

For $x<1$, the two absolute values are $1-x$ and $4-x$. Their sum is $5-2x$. On the middle interval they are $x-1$ and $4-x$, so the sum is constant. For $x>4$ they are instead $x-1$ and $x-4$, giving $2x-5$. The value $0$ from the first branch satisfies $0<1$, while the value $5$ from the last satisfies $5>4$. Substituting in the problem gives $|0-1|+|0-4|=1+4=5$ and $|5-1|+|5-4|=4+1=5$. The endpoints $1$ and $4$ lie on the portion where the sum is $3$, so they are excluded.

Each solution is $1$ unit from the nearer endpoint of the segment. The sum rises by two units because both distances increase by one unit when moving away from the segment.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Solve $|x+2|-|x-2|=1$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
x<-2&\implies -4=1,\\[4pt]
-2\leq x\leq2&\implies 2x=1 \iff x=\frac12,\\[4pt]
x>2&\implies 4=1,\\[4pt]
S&=\left\{\frac12\right\}.
\end{aligned}
$$

::grafico{f="abs(x+2)-abs(x-2);1" xmin=-4 xmax=4 ymin=-5 ymax=5 punti="-2,-4,pieno;0.5,1,pieno;2,4,pieno" titolo="Only the increasing branch meets the line at height $1$"}

<div class="spiegazione">

To the left of $-2$, both arguments are negative and the difference between their absolute values is always $-4$. To the right of $2$, both are positive and the difference is always $4$. Between $-2$ and $2$ we have $(x+2)-(2-x)=2x$. The candidate $\dfrac12$ belongs to the branch on which it was found.

On the left branch the expression is $(-x-2)-(2-x)=-4$. On the right branch it is $(x+2)-(x-2)=4$. Neither constant height can equal $1$. Between the two breakpoints, removing the absolute values gives $2x$, whose graph is the sloped segment. Finding the solution amounts to intersecting it with height $1$. At $x=\dfrac12$ the two absolute values are $\dfrac52$ and $\dfrac32$, so their difference really is $1$. At the endpoints $-2$ and $2$, the branches agree with the segment, since $2(-2)=-4$ and $2\cdot2=4$. The only intersection remains in the interior.

Because the middle portion is strictly increasing and takes values from $-4$ to $4$, each threshold strictly between those values is reached exactly once. Height $1$ is inside that interval.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Solve $|x-1|+2|x-3|\leq4$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
x<1&\implies 7-3x\leq4\iff x\geq1,\\[4pt]
1\leq x\leq3&\implies 5-x\leq4\iff x\geq1,\\[4pt]
x>3&\implies 3x-7\leq4\iff x\leq\frac{11}{3},\\[4pt]
S&=\left[1,\frac{11}{3}\right].
\end{aligned}
$$

::grafico{f="abs(x-1)+2*abs(x-3);4" xmin=-0.5 xmax=5 ymin=-1 ymax=10 punti="1,4,pieno;3,2,pieno;3.6666666667,4,pieno" titolo="The portion at or below the threshold runs from $1$ to $\dfrac{11}{3}$, including both endpoints"}

<div class="spiegazione">

The expressions inside the absolute values change sign at $1$ and $3$. The first branch is $x<1$ and its condition $x\geq1$ is incompatible. The middle branch satisfies the inequality throughout $[1,3]$. On the right branch, intersecting with $x>3$ gives $\left(3,\dfrac{11}{3}\right]$. The two allowed pieces form the stated interval.

For the first branch, add $1-x$ and $2(3-x)$ to obtain $7-3x$. In the middle branch the first absolute value is $x-1$ and the second still contributes $2(3-x)$, so the result is $5-x$. To the right it is $(x-1)+2(x-3)=3x-7$. The three branches therefore have slopes $-3$, $-1$, and $3$. At $x=1$ the expression equals $0+2\cdot2=4$, while at $x=3$ it equals $2+0=2$. At the right endpoint $x=\dfrac{11}{3}$ we have $3\cdot\dfrac{11}{3}-7=4$, so the endpoint is included. For comparison, $x=4$ gives $5>4$. Only the portion between the points at height $4$ is allowed.

For completeness, at $x=0$ the sum is $1+6=7$. This confirms that the first branch lies above the threshold before reaching its boundary at $1$.

</div>
