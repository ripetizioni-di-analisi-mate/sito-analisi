## Exercise 1

Find the tangent to $y=x^2$ at the point with x-coordinate $1$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
x^2-mx-q&=(x-1)^2=x^2-2x+1,\\[4pt]
m&=2,\qquad q=-1,\\
y&=2x-1 .
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The tangent must give the double root $1$ in the intersection equation. Since the coefficient of $x^2$ is $1$, the difference between parabola and line must be $(x-1)^2$. Equating coefficients gives $m=2$ from the linear term and $q=-1$ from the constant term. Substitution of $x=1$ gives $y=1$ on the line, the same as on the parabola.

One can also start by requiring the line to pass through $(1,1)$. In $y=mx+q$, this gives $q=1-m$. Substituting into the intersection equation with the parabola yields
$$
x^2-mx+m-1=0,
\qquad
\Delta=m^2-4(m-1)=(m-2)^2 .
$$
Tangency requires the discriminant to be zero, so $m=2$ and $q=1-2=-1$. This method shows why passing through $(1,1)$ is insufficient by itself. For example, the line $y=x$ passes through the same point but also meets the parabola at the origin. The tangent makes the two roots of the intersection polynomial coincide at $x=1$.

The curves' relative positions follow from $x^2-(2x-1)=(x-1)^2$. For example, at $x=0$ the parabola has ordinate zero and the line has ordinate $-1$. The square guarantees the same order on the other side of the contact.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Find the tangent to $y=-x^2+4x$ parallel to $y=2x+5$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
2&=2(-1)t+4,\qquad t=1,\\[4pt]
y(1)&=-1+4=3,\\[4pt]
y-3&=2(x-1),\\
y&=2x+1 .
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Parallelism fixes the slope at $2$. The slope of the tangent at x-coordinate $t$ is $-2t+4$, so $t=1$. Substituting into the parabola gives the point of tangency $(1,3)$. An algebraic check subtracts $2x+1$ from $-x^2+4x$, giving $-(x-1)^2$.

To check the intercept without using the tangent formula, set the slope to $2$ and keep $q$ free. The difference between the parabola and $y=2x+q$ is
$$
-x^2+4x-(2x+q)=1-q-(x-1)^2 .
$$
The square vanishes at $x=1$. A double root requires the constant part $1-q$ to vanish too, giving $q=1$. This calculation distinguishes parallelism from tangency. The given line has $q=5$ and misses the parabola because the difference is always at most $-4$. The tangent retains the given slope and has a different intercept, chosen to produce one common point.

Lowering the line to $q=0$ would give two solutions because $(x-1)^2=1$. The x-coordinates $0$ and $2$ both give intersections, showing how tangency separates a secant from a line that misses the parabola.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Find the tangents to $y=x^2$ through $P(1,-3)$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
-3&=2t\cdot1-t^2,\\
t^2-2t-3&=(t-3)(t+1)=0,\\
t&=3,\qquad t=-1,\\[4pt]
y&=6x-9,\qquad y=-2x-1 .
\end{aligned}
$$

::grafico{f="x^2;6*x-9;-2*x-1" xmin=-2.3 xmax=3.8 ymin=-6 ymax=12 punti="1,-3,pieno,P;3,9,pieno,A;-1,1,pieno,B" titolo="P lies on both lines. A and B are the points where they touch the parabola"}

<div class="spiegazione">

The x-coordinate $1$ of the given point is used to impose the condition of passing through $P$. The x-coordinate of tangency is the unknown $t$ in $y=2tx-t^2$. The point condition gives two values of $t$, hence two lines. Substituting $x=1$ into each gives $-3$. Subtracting the lines from $x^2$ gives $(x-3)^2$ and $(x+1)^2$, respectively.

The points of tangency are $(3,9)$ and $(-1,1)$, with x-coordinates different from that of $P$. The given point is on both lines but off the curve because at $x=1$ the parabola has ordinate $1$ and $P$ has ordinate $-3$. The difference in heights is $4$. Rewriting the point condition as $(t-1)^2=4$ gives the square that controls the number of contacts. Both x-coordinates of tangency are therefore $2$ units from $1$.

The slopes are $6$ and $-2$, respectively, so the two lines are distinct. The curve stays above each line because the differences between their ordinates are nonnegative squares.

</div>
