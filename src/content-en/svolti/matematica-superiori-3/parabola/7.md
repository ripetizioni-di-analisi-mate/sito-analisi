## Exercise 1

Find the vertex of $x=(y-2)^2-1$ and its intersections with $x=3$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
V&=(-1,2),\\
(y-2)^2&=3+1=4,\\
y&=2\pm2,\\
A&=(3,0),\qquad B=(3,4) .
\end{aligned}
$$

::grafico{curve="(t-2)^2-1,t,-1,5" legenda="x=(y-2)^2-1" xmin=-2.5 xmax=6 ymin=-1.2 ymax=5.3 segmenti="3,0,3,4;-2,2,5.5,2" punti="-1,2,pieno,V;3,0,pieno,A;3,4,pieno,B" titolo="A and B have x-coordinate 3 and heights symmetric about the axis"}

<div class="spiegazione">

The square is zero at $y=2$, so the smallest x-coordinate is $-1$ at ordinate $2$. Substituting $x=3$ fixes the distance of $y$ from $2$ to two units. The points share an x-coordinate, while their ordinates are symmetric about the horizontal axis $y=2$.

Check the two coordinate pairs directly. At $y=0$, $(0-2)^2-1=3$. At $y=4$, $(4-2)^2-1=3$ again. The two heights lie one above and one below $y=2$, as their average $\dfrac{0+4}{2}=2$ confirms.

There are two points because the prescribed x-coordinate is to the right of the vertex. More generally, the line $x=c$ gives $(y-2)^2=c+1$. If $c>-1$, there are two distinct ordinates. If $c=-1$, the square is zero and the only ordinate is $2$. For $c<-1$, the right-hand side would be negative. The minimum x-coordinate is the threshold between these cases.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Find the focus and directrix of $(y+1)^2=-8(x-3)$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
V&=(3,-1),\\
4p&=-8,\qquad p=-2,\\
F&=(3-2,-1)=(1,-1),\\
d&:x=3-(-2)=5 .
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The squared variable is $y+1$, so the axis is horizontal and passes through ordinate $-1$. The coefficient $4p$ is negative, which puts the focus to the left of the vertex and the vertical directrix two units to the right. The equation also gives $x\le3$, consistent with opening to the left.

To verify the relative positions of focus and directrix, note that both are $2$ units from the vertex along the axis $y=-1$. The perpendicular from a point to the directrix is horizontal, so here the distance to the line depends on the x-coordinate.

For a numerical check, take $Q(1,3)$. Here
$$
(3+1)^2=16=-8(1-3) ,
$$
so $Q$ is on the curve. Its distance from $F(1,-1)$ is $3-(-1)=4$ vertically, and its distance from $x=5$ is $5-1=4$ horizontally. The comparison confirms the vertical directrix at $x=5$. Choosing $x=1$ instead would make the line distance zero for $Q$, contrary to the definition.

The opening direction also follows from the signs in the equation. The square is nonnegative, so $-8(x-3)\ge0$ and hence $x\le3$. The curve extends to the left of the vertical line through the vertex.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Express the two branches of $x=-2(y-1)^2+5$ as functions of $x$ and specify their domain.

$$
\begin{aligned}
(y-1)^2&=\frac{5-x}{2},\\[4pt]
\frac{5-x}{2}&\ge0\quad\iff\quad x\le5,\\[6pt]
y_+(x)&=1+\sqrt{\frac{5-x}{2}},\\[4pt]
y_-(x)&=1-\sqrt{\frac{5-x}{2}} .
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Isolating the square shows that the right-hand side must be nonnegative. This gives the common domain $(-\infty,5]$. For $x<5$ the root is positive, so one branch is above $y=1$ and the other below it. At $x=5$ the root is zero and the branches meet at the same vertex $(5,1)$.

The radicand comes from dividing by $-2$. That gives $\dfrac{5-x}{2}$ and the domain is expressed as $x\le5$. The two functions can also be distinguished by restricting the ordinate in the original parabola. If $y\ge1$, then $y-1$ is nonnegative and the result is $y_+$. The restriction $y\le1$ gives $y_-$. Each restriction gives one ordinate for every permitted $x$.

For example, at $x=3$ the radicand is $1$ and the two ordinates are $2$ and $0$. Substituting them into the original equation gives $x=5-2=3$ in both cases. The two points on that vertical line are $2$ units apart. That distance decreases to zero as $x$ reaches the vertex.

</div>
