## Exercise 1

Find the roots of $y=(x-2)^2-4$ and determine where $y<0$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
(x-2)^2&=4\\
x&=2\pm2\\
x_1&=0,\qquad x_2=4,\\[4pt]
(x-2)^2-4&=x(x-4)<0\quad\iff\quad x\in(0,4).
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The square equals $4$ when the distance from $x$ to $2$ is $2$. This gives two roots, $0$ and $4$. Between the roots the first factor is positive and the second negative, so the product is negative. Outside the roots both factors have the same sign. Since the inequality is strict, the roots are excluded.

The same condition can be read without factoring the polynomial. A negative ordinate means that the square is less than $4$. Thus
$$
(x-2)^2<4
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
|x-2|<2
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
0<x<4 .
$$
The distance from the axis must be *strictly* less than two units. The test value $x=2$ gives $-4$, consistent with the interval, while $x=5$ gives $5$. At $x=0$ and $x=4$ the square is exactly $4$ and the function is zero. Those endpoints would solve an inequality with $\le0$, but are excluded when only the points below the axis are required.

The lowest ordinate on the negative portion is $-4$ because the square is zero at its centre. For example, $x=1$ and $x=3$ both give $-3$, confirming that the portion is symmetric.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Solve $-2x^2+4x-2\ge0$ and describe the intersection with the x-axis.

$$
\begin{aligned}
-2x^2+4x-2&=-2(x^2-2x+1)\\
&=-2(x-1)^2\le0,\\[4pt]
-2(x-1)^2\ge0&\quad\iff\quad x=1 .
\end{aligned}
$$

::grafico{f="-2*(x-1)^2" xmin=-1.5 xmax=3.5 ymin=-8 ymax=1.5 punti="1,0,pieno,V" titolo="The graph touches the x-axis at the vertex"}

<div class="spiegazione">

The square $(x-1)^2$ is positive to the left and right of $1$. Multiplying by $-2$ gives negative ordinates on both sides. The inequality therefore holds only where the square is zero. The parabola touches the axis at the vertex $(1,0)$ and stays below the axis elsewhere.

The inequality can also be divided by the negative coefficient $-2$, provided the inequality sign is reversed. This gives $(x-1)^2\le0$. Since a real square is always nonnegative, equality is the only possibility and $x=1$. Substituting in the quadratic gives $-2+4-2=0$.

The discriminant is $4^2-4(-2)(-2)=0$. The double root is therefore exactly the x-coordinate of the vertex, as the factored form also shows. The nearby x-coordinates $0$ and $2$ both give ordinate $-2$. This distinguishes a touch from a crossing. Had the question asked for $y>0$, even $1$ would have been excluded because it gives equality.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Determine which values of $q$ make $y=2x^2-8x+q$ lie strictly above the x-axis.

$$
\begin{aligned}
2x^2-8x+q
&=2(x-2)^2+q-8,\\[4pt]
\min y&=q-8,\\[4pt]
q-8>0&\quad\iff\quad q>8 .
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The term $2(x-2)^2$ is always nonnegative and equals zero at $x=2$, so $q-8$ is the minimum actually attained. For every ordinate to be positive, that minimum must be positive. When $q=8$, the graph touches the axis, while for $q<8$ the vertex is below the axis.

The parameter $q$ is the constant term and translates the graph vertically without moving the axis $x=2$. For instance, $q=9$ makes every ordinate at least $1$. In contrast, when $q=7$ the vertex has ordinate $-1$, already giving a counterexample to the requirement.

The discriminant gives the same threshold.
$$
\Delta=(-8)^2-4\cdot 2q=64-8q .
$$
If $q>8$, the discriminant is negative and the quadratic coefficient is positive, so the graph lies entirely above the axis. At $q=8$ the discriminant is zero, giving a point of tangency at height zero. For $q<8$ there are two roots and the ordinate is negative between them. The boundary value $8$ is excluded because the requirement is strict.

</div>
