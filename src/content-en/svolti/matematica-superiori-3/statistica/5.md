## Exercise 1

A scale overestimates each mass by $2\,\mathrm{g}$. Its readings have mean $48\,\mathrm{g}$ and standard deviation $3\,\mathrm{g}$. Find both measures after correction.

$$
\begin{aligned}
y_i&=x_i-2,\\
\bar y&=48-2=46\,\mathrm{g},\\
\sigma_y&=|1|\cdot 3=3\,\mathrm{g}.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The correction subtracts $2\,\mathrm{g}$ from both each reading and the mean. The difference between a reading and the mean stays the same, so the standard deviation does as well.

For instance, a reading of $51\,\mathrm{g}$ lies $3\,\mathrm{g}$ above the original mean. After correction it becomes $49\,\mathrm{g}$, still $3\,\mathrm{g}$ above the new mean. A reading of $45\,\mathrm{g}$ would similarly shift to $43\,\mathrm{g}$ and keep its deviation of $-3\,\mathrm{g}$. These are illustrative readings, not additional observations supplied by the problem. In general, $y_i-\bar y=(x_i-2)-(48-2)=x_i-48$. Each deviation keeps its sign and magnitude, so averaging the squares also leaves the variance unchanged. The correction removes a common overestimate while leaving the differences between individual readings intact.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A sensor transforms its measurements according to $y=-2x+10$. The original data have $\bar x=3$ and $\sigma_x^2=4$. Find the mean, variance and standard deviation of $y$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\bar y&=-2\cdot3+10=4,\\
\sigma_y^2&=(-2)^2\cdot4=16,\\
\sigma_y&=\sqrt{16}=4.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The negative factor reverses the order of the observations. Their distances from the new mean double because they depend on the absolute value of the factor. The squared distances quadruple, and the term $10$ cancels when the deviation is calculated.

Rewriting the transformation around the mean gives $y_i-4=-2(x_i-3)$. Thus an input $x_i=5$, two units above the original centre, would give $y_i=0$, four units below the new centre. The sign changes, while the distance doubles. To find the variance, square the whole relation to get $(y_i-4)^2=4(x_i-3)^2$. The mean of these squares is $4\cdot4=16$. We can check the standard deviation directly. Its original value was $\sqrt4=2$ and the scale factor for distances has magnitude $2$, so the new value is $2\cdot2=4$. The term $10$ helps determine the location of the centre, but disappears when each observation is compared with that centre.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Apply $y=ax+b$ with $a>0$. The means are $\bar x=10$ and $\bar y=35$, and the standard deviations are $2$ and $6$. Find $a$ and $b$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
6&=a\cdot2, & a&=3,\\
35&=3\cdot10+b, & b&=5.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The standard deviation depends only on the scale factor, so find $a$ first. The condition $a>0$ means that $|a|$ can be replaced by $a$. Once the scale is known, use the mean to find the shift.

The two pieces of information identify different effects of the transformation. The ratio of standard deviations determines how distances from the respective centres scale. Comparing the means then determines the added shift. To check the result, recover both measures using $y=3x+5$. For the means we have $3\cdot10+5=35$. For the deviations, $y_i-35=3(x_i-10)$ gives $\sigma_y=3\cdot2=6$. If the sign of $a$ were unspecified, the ratio of magnitudes would also allow $a=-3$. The mean equation would then be $35=-30+b$, giving $b=65$. The assumption of a positive slope therefore makes the requested pair unique.

</div>
