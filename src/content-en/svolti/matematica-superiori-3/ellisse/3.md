## Exercise 1

Find the eccentricity of $\dfrac{x^2}{16}+\dfrac{y^2}{25}=1$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
a&=5,\qquad b=4,\\
c&=\sqrt{25-16}=3,\\
e&=\frac{c}{a}=\frac35.
\end{aligned}
$$

::grafico{curve="4*cos(t),5*sin(t),0,6.283185307" xmin=-6 xmax=6 ymin=-6 ymax=6 uguale=1 punti="0,3,pieno,F_2;0,-3,pieno,F_1;0,5,pieno,V_2;0,-5,pieno,V_1;4,0,pieno,P;0,0,pieno,C" segmenti="4,0,0,3;4,0,0,-3" legenda="\dfrac{x^2}{16}+\dfrac{y^2}{25}=1" titolo="Both the focal separation $6$ and the major-axis length $10$ are measured vertically."}

<div class="spiegazione">

The semi-major axis is vertical because the denominator $25$ is under $y^2$. The letter $a$ therefore denotes $5$. Find $c=3$ from the difference of the squares and divide it by $a$. The ratio has no units, so it describes the shape independently of the unit of length. Rotating the ellipse by a right angle would leave its semi-axes at $5$ and $4$, with the same eccentricity.

The geometric comparison can be made in the vertical direction, where the vertices are $(0,\pm5)$ and the foci $(0,\pm3)$. The focal separation is $6$ and the major axis measures $10$. The ratio of these full lengths is also $\dfrac6{10}=\dfrac35$, because the factors of $2$ cancel. Using the horizontal semi-axis $4$ as the denominator would give a different ratio referring to the minor direction. As a check, from $(4,0)$ the distances to both foci are $\sqrt{16+9}=5$, exactly the semi-major axis. This reconstructs the triangle behind $a^2=b^2+c^2$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Two horizontal ellipses have the same semi-minor axis $b=4$ and eccentricities $\dfrac35$ and $\dfrac{\sqrt3}{2}$. Find their horizontal widths.

$$
\begin{aligned}
b^2&=a^2(1-e^2),\\
a_1^2&=\frac{16}{1-\frac{9}{25}}=25,\\[4pt]
a_2^2&=\frac{16}{1-\frac34}=64.
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
2a_1=10,\qquad 2a_2=16.
$$

::grafico{curve="5*cos(t),4*sin(t),0,6.283185307;8*cos(t),4*sin(t),0,6.283185307" xmin=-9 xmax=9 ymin=-5 ymax=5 uguale=1 punti="-8,0;-5,0;5,0;8,0;0,-4;0,4" legenda="a_1=5,\ b=4;a_2=8,\ b=4" titolo="The two ellipses have the same vertical extremes and different widths."}

<div class="spiegazione">

Since the semi-minor axis is fixed, isolate $a^2$ in the relation $b^2=a^2(1-e^2)$. The factor $1-e^2$ is positive in both cases, so we can divide by it. For the first ellipse it is $\dfrac{16}{25}$ and for the second it is $\dfrac14$. A smaller factor requires a larger semi-major axis to preserve the height. The second ellipse is $16$ units wide and $8$ high, while the first is $10$ units wide and $8$ high.

To check the second width, start with $a_2=8$ and find $c_2=\sqrt{64-16}=4\sqrt3$. The ratio of $c_2$ to $a_2$ is $\dfrac{\sqrt3}{2}$, as required. For the first ellipse the same check gives $c_1=\sqrt{25-16}=3$, hence $e_1=\dfrac35$. In the diagram the curves share only the minor-axis endpoints $(0,\pm4)$. For a vertical coordinate between them, the quantity $1-\dfrac{y^2}{16}$ is positive. Multiplying it by $25$ or $64$ gives different values of $x^2$, and the horizontal endpoints remain distinct.

</div>
