## Exercise 1

Find the $y$-coordinates of the points on $\dfrac{x^2}{25}+\dfrac{y^2}{9}=1$ with $x$-coordinate $3$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{y^2}{9}&=1-\frac{3^2}{25}=\frac{16}{25},\\[4pt]
y^2&=\frac{144}{25},\\[4pt]
y&=\pm\frac{12}{5}.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Substituting $x=3$, the horizontal term accounts for $\dfrac9{25}$ of the total $1$. This leaves $\dfrac{16}{25}$ for the vertical term. Multiply by $9$ before taking the square root to obtain $y^2$. Since that value is positive, there are two opposite $y$-coordinates. The vertical line meets both the upper and the lower half, and the two points are symmetric about the $x$-axis.

Both solutions can be checked in the original equation. The values $\pm\dfrac{12}{5}$ both square to $\dfrac{144}{25}$ and so contribute $\dfrac{16}{25}$ on the left. The horizontal coordinate $3$ contributes $\dfrac9{25}$, giving a sum of $1$. This spells out the role of the vertical denominator $9$. Taking the square root of $\dfrac{16}{25}$ directly gives $\dfrac45$, the ratio of the distance from the horizontal axis to the vertical semi-axis. Multiplying that ratio by $3$ gives the vertical coordinate. The vertical chord therefore has midpoint $(3,0)$ and length $\dfrac{24}{5}$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

For every $t\in\mathbb R$, classify the intersection of the line $y=t$ and the ellipse $\dfrac{x^2}{9}+\dfrac{y^2}{4}=1$.

$$
x^2=9\left(1-\frac{t^2}{4}\right).
$$

- $|t|<2 \Longrightarrow x=\pm3\sqrt{1-\dfrac{t^2}{4}}$.
- $|t|=2 \Longrightarrow x=0$.
- $|t|>2 \Longrightarrow \varnothing$.

::grafico{f="1;2;3" curve="3*cos(t),2*sin(t),0,6.283185307" xmin=-5 xmax=5 ymin=-3 ymax=4 uguale=1 punti="-2.598076211,1,pieno,A;2.598076211,1,pieno,B;0,2,pieno,T" legenda="y=1;y=2;y=3;\dfrac{x^2}{9}+\dfrac{y^2}{4}=1" titolo="The section at height $1$ contains two points, the one at height $2$ touches the curve at $T$, and the one at height $3$ misses it."}

<div class="spiegazione">

The $y$-coordinate is now fixed, and it is the square of the $x$-coordinate that we need. For $|t|<2$, the right-hand side is positive and there are two points on the horizontal line. For $|t|=2$, the value of $x^2$ is zero, so the line meets only one endpoint of the vertical axis. For $|t|>2$, the right-hand side is negative. The line is above or below the entire ellipse, whose vertical semi-axis measures $2$.

Three sections verify the transition from two intersections to one. At $t=1$ the reduced equation is $x^2=\dfrac{27}{4}$, giving horizontal coordinates $\pm\dfrac{3\sqrt3}{2}$. At $t=2$ only $x^2=0$ remains, hence the single point $(0,2)$. At $t=3$ we get $x^2=-\dfrac{45}{4}$, so no point is admissible. The same intersection counts hold at the opposite heights since the equation uses $t^2$. Absolute value therefore combines the cases above and below the horizontal axis in one condition.

</div>
