## Exercise 1

Find the $y$-coordinates of the points on $\dfrac{x^2}{25}+\dfrac{y^2}{9}=1$ with $x$-coordinate $3$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{y^2}{9}&=1-\frac{3^2}{25}=\frac{16}{25},\\[4pt]
y^2&=\frac{144}{25},\\[4pt]
y&=\pm\frac{12}{5}.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Substituting $x=3$, the horizontal term accounts for $\dfrac9{25}$ of the total $1$. This leaves $\dfrac{16}{25}$ for the vertical term. Multiply by $9$ before taking the square root to obtain $y^2$. Since that value is positive, there are two opposite $y$-coordinates. The vertical line meets both the upper and the lower half, and the two points are symmetric about the $x$-axis.

</div>

## Exercise 2

For every $t\in\mathbb R$, classify the intersection of the line $y=t$ and the ellipse $\dfrac{x^2}{9}+\dfrac{y^2}{4}=1$.

$$
x^2=9\left(1-\frac{t^2}{4}\right).
$$

- $|t|<2 \Longrightarrow x=\pm3\sqrt{1-\dfrac{t^2}{4}}$.
- $|t|=2 \Longrightarrow x=0$.
- $|t|>2 \Longrightarrow \varnothing$.

<div class="spiegazione">

The $y$-coordinate is now fixed, and it is the square of the $x$-coordinate that we need. For $|t|<2$, the right-hand side is positive and there are two points on the horizontal line. For $|t|=2$, the value of $x^2$ is zero, so the line meets only one endpoint of the vertical axis. For $|t|>2$, the right-hand side is negative. The line is above or below the entire ellipse, whose vertical semi-axis measures $2$.

</div>
