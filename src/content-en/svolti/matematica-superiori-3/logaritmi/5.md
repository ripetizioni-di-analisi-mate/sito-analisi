## Exercise 1

Solve $\log_3(2x-1)=2$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
2x-1>0 &\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad x>\frac12,\\[6pt]
\log_3(2x-1)=2 &\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad 2x-1=3^2,\\[4pt]
2x&=10\\[4pt]
x&=5.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The logarithm is defined for $x>\dfrac12$. On this interval the definition turns the equation into an equality between the argument and a power. The value $2$ is the exponent of base $3$, so the argument must be $9$. The only candidate is $5$, which lies in the domain and satisfies $\log_3(2\cdot5-1)=\log_3 9=2$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Solve $\log_2(x-2)+\log_2(x+2)=\log_2 12$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\begin{cases}x-2>0,\\x+2>0\end{cases}
 &\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad x>2,\\[6pt]
\log_2\bigl((x-2)(x+2)\bigr)&=\log_2 12,\\[4pt]
(x-2)(x+2)&=12,\\[4pt]
x^2&=16.
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\{-4,4\}\cap(2,+\infty)=\{4\}.
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Set the domain constraints on the two arguments separately. The sum can be combined into the logarithm of a product only on that domain. Because the logarithms have the same base, equality requires equal arguments, giving two candidates. At $x=-4$ the product is $12$ but both factors are negative, so $-4$ is discarded. At $x=4$ the factors are $2$ and $6$, so the original equation is satisfied.

</div>
