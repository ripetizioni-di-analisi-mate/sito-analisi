## Exercise 1

Find the domain of $f(x)=\log_2\dfrac{x-3}{x+1}$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{x-3}{x+1}>0
&\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad x<-1 \ \text{or}\ x>3.
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\left.\frac{x-3}{x+1}\right|_{x=-2}&=5,\\[4pt]
\left.\frac{x-3}{x+1}\right|_{x=0}&=-3,\\[4pt]
\left.\frac{x-3}{x+1}\right|_{x=4}&=\frac15.
\end{aligned}
$$

::grafico{f="(x-3)/(x+1)" xmin=-5 xmax=7 ymin=-5 ymax=5.5 asintoti="x=-1" punti="3,0,vuoto;0,-3;4,0.2" legenda="\dfrac{x-3}{x+1}" titolo="The quotient is positive on the two outer intervals. The point at height zero is excluded from the logarithmic domain"}

<div class="spiegazione">

Base $2$ is admissible for every $x$. The domain question is where the quotient is positive. The numerator $x-3$ changes sign at $3$, and the denominator $x+1$ changes sign at $-1$. This gives three intervals to check. For instance, $x=-2$ makes both factors negative, while $x=0$ makes only the numerator negative. At $x=4$ both are positive.

Positive quotients occur in the two outer intervals. At $x=-1$ the fraction is undefined. At $x=3$ the fraction exists and equals zero, so it is the logarithm that is undefined. The two points are excluded for different reasons.

The figure shows the quotient before taking the logarithm. Keep only inputs where the curve is strictly above the horizontal axis, so the domain is $(-\infty,-1)\cup(3,+\infty)$. The signs remain the same throughout the three intervals because the two linear factors can change sign only at their respective zeros.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Find the domain of $f(x)=\log_{x-1}(4-x)$.

$$
\begin{cases}
x-1>0,\\
x-1\ne1,\\
4-x>0
\end{cases}
\Longleftrightarrow
\begin{cases}
x>1,\\
x\ne2,\\
x<4.
\end{cases}
$$

$$
D=(1,2)\cup(2,4).
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
x=\frac32&\quad\Longrightarrow\quad (x-1,4-x)=\left(\frac12,\frac52\right),\\[4pt]
x=3&\quad\Longrightarrow\quad (x-1,4-x)=(2,1),\\[4pt]
x=2&\quad\Longrightarrow\quad (x-1,4-x)=(1,2).
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Both the base and the argument depend on $x$. The base $x-1$ is positive for $x>1$. It equals $1$ at $x=2$, which must be excluded because powers of $1$ are constant. The argument $4-x$ is positive for $x<4$. All three conditions apply at once.

The positivity conditions first give $1<x<4$. The point $2$ lies in this interval and makes the argument $2$, which is allowed. It is the base that prevents including $2$. Removing that point splits the domain into $(1,2)$ and $(2,4)$.

The first two examples show that admissible bases can lie on either side of $1$. The second also gives logarithm zero, which is allowed because the positivity constraint concerns the argument, here equal to $1$. Solving only $4-x>0$ would also allow inputs such as $x=0$, where the base is $-1$. The domain of the argument is therefore insufficient when the base also depends on the unknown.

</div>
