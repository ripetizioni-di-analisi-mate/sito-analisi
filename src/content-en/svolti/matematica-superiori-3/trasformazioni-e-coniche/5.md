## Exercise 1

Classify the locus $-x^2-y^2+4x-6y-9=0$ and find its center and radius if they exist.

$$
\begin{aligned}
x^2+y^2-4x+6y+9&=0\\
(x-2)^2-4+(y+3)^2-9+9&=0\\
(x-2)^2+(y+3)^2&=4 .
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
C=(2,-3),\qquad r=2 .
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Both quadratic coefficients are negative. Multiplying the entire equation by $-1$ makes both terms positive without changing the solutions. Since the two squares have the same coefficient, the locus is determined by the sum of the squared coordinate offsets from the center. The right-hand side $4$ is positive, so a real circle exists. The value $4$ is the squared radius and gives radius $2$. One point on the curve is $(4,-3)$, because a horizontal offset of $2$ and a vertical offset of zero give the required distance.

The sign reversal can be checked at the center. The original polynomial at $(2,-3)$ is $-4-9+8+18-9=4$. In the rewritten form the squared terms vanish at the center, so the sign-reversed polynomial must give $-4$, consistent with $(x-2)^2+(y+3)^2-4=0$. Another curve point is $(2,-1)$, which is $2$ units from the center and again gives zero by substitution. The step from $4$ to $2$ follows from the distance formula, since the sum of squares is the square of the radius.

</div>

## Exercise 2

For every $t\in\mathbb R$, classify $x^2+y^2-6x-2y+t=0$. Give the center and radius in the circle cases.

$$
\begin{aligned}
(x-3)^2-9+(y-1)^2-1+t&=0\\
(x-3)^2+(y-1)^2&=10-t .
\end{aligned}
$$

- If $t<10$, a circle centered at $(3,1)$ with radius $\sqrt{10-t}$.
- If $t=10$, just the point $(3,1)$.
- If $t>10$, the empty locus.

::grafico{curve="3+2*cos(t),1+2*sin(t),0,6.283185307;3+cos(t),1+sin(t),0,6.283185307" xmin=-1 xmax=7 ymin=-3 ymax=5 uguale=1 punti="3,1,pieno,C;5,1;4,1" legenda="(x-3)^2+(y-1)^2=4;(x-3)^2+(y-1)^2=1" titolo="The outer circle comes from $t=6$ and the inner circle from $t=9$. At $t=10$, only $C$ remains."}

<div class="spiegazione">

The linear terms are independent of the parameter, so all real members of the family with positive radius have the same center. The right-hand side $10-t$ determines whether the radius exists. For instance, when $t=6$ it equals $4$ and the radius is $2$. When $t=10$ the sum of squares is zero, so each square must vanish. Finally, when $t>10$, the right-hand side is negative, while any sum of real squares is nonnegative. In that case there are no real solutions.

The threshold can also be recovered by evaluating the polynomial at $(3,1)$. This gives $9+1-18-2+t=t-10$, its minimum because every other point adds a sum of nonnegative squares. If the minimum is zero, the center is the only solution. If it is positive, there are no zeros. If it is negative, the zeros lie at the distance from the center needed to offset $t-10$. For example, $t=9$ gives radius $1$ and $t=6$ gives radius $2$. The two circles are concentric.

</div>
