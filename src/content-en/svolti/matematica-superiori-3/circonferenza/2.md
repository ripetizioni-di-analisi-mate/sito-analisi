## Exercise 1

Identify the locus represented by $x^2+y^2+4x-6y+13=0$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
x^2+4x+y^2-6y&=-13,\\
(x+2)^2+(y-3)^2&=-13+4+9=0.
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
(x,y)=(-2,3).
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Complete the square in $x$ with $4$ and in $y$ with $9$. These two additions exactly balance the term $-13$. The sum of the squares is zero only when both are zero, so the locus consists solely of $(-2,3)$. A circle as a locus at fixed distance requires a positive radius. Here the fixed distance is zero and every point of the locus coincides with the centre.

The degenerate case can also be read from sections. At $x=-2$, where the first square vanishes, the second still has minimum value $0$ and attains it only at $y=3$. For any $x\ne-2$, the first square is already positive and no vertical coordinate can offset it. Substituting the point into the original equation shows that it is indeed a solution. The argument using squares also proves it is the only one.

This case differs from having a negative right-hand side, since here the sum can attain the required value. For example, replacing $13$ by $14$ would keep the two binomials but require $(x+2)^2+(y-3)^2=-1$.

$$
(-2)^2+3^2+4(-2)-6\cdot3+13=4+9-8-18+13=0.
$$

</div>

## Exercise 2

For which $m\in\mathbb R$ does $x^2+y^2-6x+2y+m=0$ represent a circle with radius at least $2$?

$$
\begin{aligned}
(x-3)^2+(y+1)^2&=9+1-m=10-m,\\
r\geq 2
&\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad 10-m\geq 4,\\
&\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad m\leq 6.
\end{aligned}
$$

::grafico{curve="3+3*cos(t),-1+3*sin(t),0,6.283185307;3+2*cos(t),-1+2*sin(t),0,6.283185307" xmin=-1 xmax=7 ymin=-5 ymax=3 uguale=1 punti="3,-1,pieno,C;5,-1;6,-1" segmenti="3,-1,6,-1" legenda="m=1;m=6" titolo="The centre is common to both. The circle of radius $2$ corresponds to the threshold $m=6$."}

<div class="spiegazione">

The linear terms stay fixed, so the centre is always $(3,-1)$ whenever the curve exists. The parameter changes the right-hand side. A radius of at least $2$ requires a squared radius of at least $4$. This also guarantees that the radius is positive. At $m=6$ the radius is exactly $2$, so the boundary value is included. For $6<m<10$ there is still a circle, but its radius is less than $2$.

Completing the squares yields the threshold without evaluating a square root before comparing the radii. As $m$ varies, the only change is the distance of the points from the fixed pair $(3,-1)$. At $m=1$ the radius is $3$ and the circle encloses the one for $m=6$. At $m=10$ the locus has one point. Above $10$ the right-hand side is negative and the locus is empty.

The threshold can be checked geometrically at $P(5,-1)$, which is $2$ units from the centre. Substituting it into the original left-hand side gives $25+1-30-2+m=m-6$. Thus $P$ belongs to the circle exactly at $m=6$.

</div>
