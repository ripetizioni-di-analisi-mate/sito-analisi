## Exercise 1

Find the common points of $x^2+y^2=25$ and $(x-6)^2+y^2=25$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
(x-6)^2-x^2&=0,\\
-12x+36&=0,\\
x&=3,\\
y^2&=25-3^2=16.
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
P=(3,4),\qquad Q=(3,-4).
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The polynomials on the left of the two equations must have the same value at every common point. Subtracting them cancels the quadratic terms and leaves the line $x=3$. The centres are $6$ units apart and the radii are equal, so this is also the perpendicular bisector of the segment joining the centres. The condition $x=3$ alone leaves infinitely many points. Requiring membership of the first circle gives the two $y$-coordinates, and both satisfy $(3-6)^2+4^2=25$ in the second equation.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Use the radical axis to determine whether $x^2+y^2=9$ and $(x-5)^2+y^2=1$ have common points.

$$
\begin{aligned}
(x-5)^2-x^2&=1-9=-8,\\
-10x+25&=-8,\\
x&=\frac{33}{10},\\[4pt]
y^2&=9-\left(\frac{33}{10}\right)^2=-\frac{189}{100}.
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\varnothing
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Subtraction still gives a line because the centres are distinct. Any common point would have to have $x$-coordinate $\dfrac{33}{10}$. Substituting into the first circle makes the square of the $y$-coordinate negative, so there are no common points. The geometric data give the same result. The radii sum to $3+1=4$ and the centres are $5$ units apart, so the circles lie outside one another.

</div>
