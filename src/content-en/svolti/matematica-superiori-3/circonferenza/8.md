## Exercise 1

Find the common points of $x^2+y^2=25$ and $(x-6)^2+y^2=25$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
(x-6)^2-x^2&=0,\\
-12x+36&=0,\\
x&=3,\\
y^2&=25-3^2=16.
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
P=(3,4),\qquad Q=(3,-4).
$$

::grafico{curve="5*cos(t),5*sin(t),0,6.283185307;6+5*cos(t),5*sin(t),0,6.283185307;3,t,-6,6" xmin=-6 xmax=12 ymin=-6 ymax=6 uguale=1 punti="0,0,pieno,C_1;6,0,pieno,C_2;3,4,pieno,P;3,-4,pieno,Q" legenda="x^2+y^2=25;(x-6)^2+y^2=25;x=3" titolo="Only $P$ and $Q$ on the radical axis belong to both circles."}

<div class="spiegazione">

The polynomials on the left of the two equations must have the same value at every common point. Subtracting them cancels the quadratic terms and leaves the line $x=3$. The centres are $6$ units apart and the radii are equal, so this is also the perpendicular bisector of the segment joining the centres. The condition $x=3$ alone leaves infinitely many points. Requiring membership of the first circle gives the two $y$-coordinates, and both satisfy $(3-6)^2+4^2=25$ in the second equation.

The condition $x=3$ can also be obtained from equal distances to the centres $(0,0)$ and $(6,0)$. It reduces a system in two unknowns to an equation in the vertical coordinate alone. For example, $(3,0)$ is equidistant from the centres and therefore lies on the radical axis, but both distances are $3$ instead of $5$. Checking membership of one circle is thus essential even after finding the radical axis.

The number of intersections could be predicted before calculating. The separation $6$ of the centres is strictly greater than the radius difference $0$ and strictly less than their sum $10$. The radical axis must therefore cut both circles.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Use the radical axis to determine whether $x^2+y^2=9$ and $(x-5)^2+y^2=1$ have common points.

$$
\begin{aligned}
(x-5)^2-x^2&=1-9=-8,\\
-10x+25&=-8,\\
x&=\frac{33}{10},\\[4pt]
y^2&=9-\left(\frac{33}{10}\right)^2=-\frac{189}{100}.
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\varnothing
$$

::grafico{curve="3*cos(t),3*sin(t),0,6.283185307;5+cos(t),sin(t),0,6.283185307;3.3,t,-4,4" xmin=-4 xmax=7 ymin=-4 ymax=4 uguale=1 punti="0,0,pieno,C_1;5,0,pieno,C_2;3,0;4,0" legenda="x^2+y^2=9;(x-5)^2+y^2=1;x=\dfrac{33}{10}" titolo="The radical axis runs through the gap between the circles and meets neither one."}

<div class="spiegazione">

Subtraction still gives a line because the centres are distinct. Any common point would have to have $x$-coordinate $\dfrac{33}{10}$. Substituting into the first circle makes the square of the $y$-coordinate negative, so there are no common points. The geometric data give the same result. The radii sum to $3+1=4$ and the centres are $5$ units apart, so the circles lie outside one another.

The radical axis can lie entirely outside both circles. Here the rightmost horizontal coordinate of the first circle is $3$, and the leftmost one of the second is $4$. The coordinate $\dfrac{33}{10}$ lies between them. This checks the system without using any vertical coordinates. It also explains why the existence of the line from subtracting the equations does not imply that the circles share a point.

Substituting the same horizontal coordinate into the other circle gives the same conclusion. The remaining square is $1-\left(\dfrac{33}{10}-5\right)^2=1-\dfrac{289}{100}=-\dfrac{189}{100}$. Along the radical axis, the two conditions are equivalent and fail together.

</div>
