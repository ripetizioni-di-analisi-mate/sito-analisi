## Exercise 1

Find the centre and radius of $x^2-2x+y^2-8=0$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
x^2-2x+y^2 &=8,\\
(x^2-2x+1)+y^2 &=8+1,\\
(x-1)^2+y^2 &=9.
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
C=(1,0), \qquad r=3.
$$

::grafico{curve="1+3*cos(t),3*sin(t),0,6.283185307" xmin=-4 xmax=6 ymin=-4 ymax=4 uguale=1 punti="1,0,pieno,C;-2,0;4,0" segmenti="-2,0,4,0" legenda="(x-1)^2+y^2=9" titolo="The horizontal diameter has endpoints $(-2,0)$ and $(4,0)$."}

<div class="spiegazione">

The term $-2x$ is twice the product of $x$ and $-1$, so the required square is $(x-1)^2$. To preserve the equation, add $1$ to the right-hand side as well. The term $y^2$ is already a square, so the centre lies on the $x$-axis. The $x$-coordinate $1$ makes $x-1$ zero, and the right-hand side is the square of the radius. The points $(4,0)$ and $(-2,0)$ both lie $3$ units from the centre and satisfy the equation.

Completing the square gives more information than the formula for the centre's horizontal coordinate alone. Letting $y$ vary at $x=1$ also gives the two vertical endpoints $(1,-3)$ and $(1,3)$. At either point the horizontal square vanishes and the vertical square equals $9$. The vertical line $x=1$ thus contains a diameter, as does the line $y=0$ in the figure. As an algebraic check, expand the centred equation and bring the right-hand term back to the left.

Here $y$ appears squared without a linear term. Reflection $(x,y)\mapsto(x,-y)$ therefore preserves the curve, as do the locations of both vertical endpoints and the centre.

$$
(x-1)^2+y^2-9=x^2-2x+y^2-8.
$$

</div>

## Exercise 2

Find the centre and radius of $2x^2+2y^2+6x-8y-12=0$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
x^2+3x+y^2-4y &=6,\\[4pt]
\left(x+\frac32\right)^2+(y-2)^2
&=6+\frac94+4=\frac{49}{4}.
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
C=\left(-\frac32,2\right), \qquad r=\frac72.
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Divide the entire equation by $2$ to make both squared terms have coefficient one. The linear coefficient in $x$ becomes $3$, so the binomial contains $\dfrac32$ and we add $\dfrac94$. The linear coefficient in $y$ becomes $-4$, so the second binomial is $y-2$ and we add $4$. Setting both binomials equal to zero gives the centre. The positive square root of $\dfrac{49}{4}$ is $\dfrac72$. For example, $P(2,2)$ has the same $y$-coordinate as the centre and is $\dfrac72$ units from it, so it lies on the curve.

The factor $2$ multiplies every term. Completing the square directly from $6x$ before normalising would put $3$ in the square in $x$ in place of $\dfrac32$. A second check substitutes $P$ into the original equation, where the contribution of its vertical coordinate is $8-16$. The same distance from the centre is attained on the left at $x=-5$, because $\dfrac72$ is half the length of the horizontal interval $[-5,2]$.

The vertical endpoints instead have coordinates $2\pm\dfrac72$ measured vertically, with horizontal coordinate $-\dfrac32$. A drawing would confirm that the translated circle has horizontal and vertical diameters of the same length.

$$
2\cdot2^2+2\cdot2^2+6\cdot2-8\cdot2-12=0.
$$

</div>
