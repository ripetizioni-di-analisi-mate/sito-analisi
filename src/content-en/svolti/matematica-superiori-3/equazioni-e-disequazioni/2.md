## Exercise 1

Solve $x^2-1=0$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
x^2-1=0 &\iff (x-1)(x+1)=0 \\[6pt]
&\iff x=1\ \lor\ x=-1,\qquad S=\{-1,1\}.
\end{aligned}
$$

::grafico{f="x^2;1" xmin=-2.3 xmax=2.3 ymin=-1 ymax=5 punti="-1,1,pieno;1,1,pieno" titolo="The line at height $1$ meets the parabola at two points"}

<div class="spiegazione">

Use $u^2-v^2=(u-v)(u+v)$ with $u=x$ and $v=1$. The term $1$ is a square, so the two factors have opposite constant terms. Both values found have square equal to $1$.

The equation can also be rearranged directly to $x^2=1$. On the number line, this asks which points are $1$ unit from the origin, because opposite numbers have the same square. Hence there is one point to the left and one to the right. The graph compares $y=x^2$ and $y=1$. Their y-coordinates agree at $(-1,1)$ and $(1,1)$. The solutions of the equation are their x-coordinates. If we write $\sqrt{x^2}=1$, the left side is $|x|$, because the square root returns a nonnegative number.

Between the two intersections, for example at the origin, $x^2<1$. Outside, for example at $x=2$, $x^2>1$. The equation requires the same height, so only the two crossing points are allowed.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Solve $(2x-3)^2=7$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
(2x-3)^2=7&\iff 2x-3=\pm\sqrt7 \\[6pt]
&\iff x=\frac{3\pm\sqrt7}{2}.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The square is already isolated. The two real numbers with square equal to $7$ are $\sqrt7$ and $-\sqrt7$. In each case, add $3$ and then divide by $2$. The two values of $x$ are distinct because $\sqrt7>0$.

The expression $2x-3$ is zero at $x=\dfrac32$. The two solutions are therefore symmetric about that value. Indeed, they can be written as $x=\dfrac32\pm\dfrac{\sqrt7}{2}$, and their deviation from $\dfrac32$ is half of $\sqrt7$ because the variable has coefficient $2$. Check the larger value by calculating $2\cdot\dfrac{3+\sqrt7}{2}-3=\sqrt7$. For the smaller value the same substitution gives $-\sqrt7$. Squaring then yields $7$ in both cases.

Applying the formula to the expanded trinomial $4x^2-12x+2=0$ would give the same two roots. The original form gives them through two linear equations.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Solve $2x^2-8x+9=0$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
2x^2-8x+9&=2(x^2-4x+4)+1 \\[6pt]
&=2(x-2)^2+1\geq1,\qquad S=\varnothing.
\end{aligned}
$$

::grafico{f="2*(x-2)^2+1" xmin=-0.5 xmax=4.5 ymin=-1 ymax=10 punti="2,1,pieno" titolo="The lowest point has y-coordinate $1$"}

<div class="spiegazione">

After factoring $2$ from the $x^2$ and $x$ terms, add $4$ inside to complete the square. This addition contributes $8$ to the original expression, so $1$ of the constant term $9$ remains outside. Even at $x=2$, where the square becomes zero, the trinomial is positive. Therefore it has no real roots.

The transformation is an identity, so it gives the same value as the trinomial for every $x$. Expand to check that $2(x-2)^2+1=2(x^2-4x+4)+1=2x^2-8x+9$. The minimum $1$ is reached at $x=2$. At $x=1$ and $x=3$, equally far from the vertex, the value is $3$. This explains why both branches of the graph lie above the horizontal axis. Isolating the square would give $(x-2)^2=-\dfrac12$. A square cannot be negative, which is the same fact that rules out every intersection with the axis.

The discriminant confirms the result, since $(-8)^2-4\cdot2\cdot9=64-72=-8$. Completing the square also gives information not contained in the sign of the discriminant alone, namely the exact minimum.

</div>
