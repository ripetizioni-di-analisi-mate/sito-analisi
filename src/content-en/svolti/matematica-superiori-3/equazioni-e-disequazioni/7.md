## Exercise 1

Find $m$ so that $x^2-2x+m=0$ has a double root.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta&=(-2)^2-4m=4-4m, \\[6pt]
\Delta=0&\iff m=1,\\[6pt]
x^2-2x+1&=(x-1)^2.
\end{aligned}
$$

::grafico{f="x^2-2*x;x^2-2*x+1;x^2-2*x+2" xmin=-2 xmax=4 ymin=-2 ymax=7 punti="0,0,pieno;1,0,pieno;2,0,pieno" titolo="Each value of $m$ gives a different height of the vertex"}

<div class="spiegazione">

The coefficient of $x^2$ is always $1$, so the equation is quadratic for every $m$. Its single root is double when the discriminant is zero. With $m=1$, factoring directly shows that the root is $1$.

The same calculation can be read in the form $x^2-2x+m=(x-1)^2+(m-1)$. The term $m-1$ is the lowest y-coordinate of the graph, since the square is minimized at $x=1$. To share just one point with the $x$-axis, that minimum must be zero. If $m=0$, the minimum is $-1$ and there are two intersections, at $0$ and $2$. If $m=2$, the minimum is $1$ and the curve remains above the axis. The intermediate case $m=1$ therefore gives tangency and explains why the two roots in the quadratic formula coincide.

The formula gives the double root $-\dfrac{-2}{2\cdot1}=1$. Factoring confirms that the root occurs twice, even though only one distinct value solves the equation.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Determine the number of real solutions of $(m-2)x=m+1$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
m=2&\implies 0\cdot x=3,&& S=\varnothing, \\[6pt]
m\neq2&\implies x=\frac{m+1}{m-2},&& |S|=1.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Division by $m-2$ is possible when $m\neq2$. At $m=2$, the coefficient of the unknown is zero but the right-hand side equals $3$. No value of $x$ makes the equality true. For all other parameter values, dividing determines exactly one solution.

For example, at $m=1$ the equation is $-x=2$, so $x=-2$. At $m=3$ it becomes $x=4$. In both cases the coefficient of the unknown is nonzero, and the result can be checked by substitution. To interpret $m=2$, consider the graphs $y=(m-2)x$ and $y=m+1$. The first coincides with the $x$-axis, and the second is the horizontal line at height $3$. These are distinct parallel lines, so they have no points in common. If the right-hand side were also zero, the lines would coincide and every x-coordinate would be a solution.

Dividing gives $x=\dfrac{m+1}{m-2}$. Substituting into $(m-2)x$, the factor $m-2$ cancels and recovers $m+1$. This also shows why the check requires $m\neq2$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Determine the number of real solutions of $mx^2-4x+1=0$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
m=0&\implies -4x+1=0\implies x=\frac14, \\[6pt]
m\neq0&\implies \Delta=16-4m=4(4-m), \\[6pt]
m=4&\implies 4x^2-4x+1=(2x-1)^2.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

For $m=0$, the degree drops to one, so there is one simple solution. For $m<4$ with $m\neq0$, the discriminant is positive and there are two solutions. At $m=4$, the discriminant is zero and there is a double root, $\dfrac12$. For $m>4$, the discriminant is negative and the solution set is empty. Both parameter values $0$ and $4$ give one solution, for different reasons.

Substitute the parameter into the equation to see why the two one-solution cases must be distinguished. With $m=0$, the polynomial is $1-4x$, whose graph is a line crossing the axis at $\dfrac14$. With $m=4$, it is $(2x-1)^2$, a parabola touching the axis at $\dfrac12$. The latter zero is double because its linear factor occurs twice. For a parameter between $0$ and $4$, such as $m=1$, the discriminant is $12>0$. There are then two intersections, as the classification predicts. The quadratic formula explicitly requires $m\neq0$.

For a check on the branch with no solutions, at $m=5$ the discriminant is $\Delta=16-20=-4$. The polynomial retains degree two and has no real zeros.

</div>
