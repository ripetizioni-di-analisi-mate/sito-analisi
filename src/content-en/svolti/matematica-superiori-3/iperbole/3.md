## Exercise 1

Find the asymptote lines of $\dfrac{x^2}{9}-\dfrac{y^2}{16}=1$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
a&=3,\qquad b=4\\
m_1&=\frac{4}{3},\qquad m_2=-\frac{4}{3}.
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
y=\frac43 x,\qquad y=-\frac43 x .
$$

::grafico{f="4*sqrt(x^2-9)/3;-4*sqrt(x^2-9)/3;4*x/3;-4*x/3" xmin=-7 xmax=7 ymin=-8 ymax=8 uguale=1 punti="3,4,pieno,R" segmenti="-3,-4,3,-4;3,-4,3,4;3,4,-3,4;-3,4,-3,-4" legenda="y=\dfrac43\sqrt{x^2-9};y=-\dfrac43\sqrt{x^2-9};y=\dfrac43x;y=-\dfrac43x" titolo="The corner $R=(3,4)$ of the rectangle lies on the rising asymptote."}

<div class="spiegazione">

The denominator of $x^2$ tells us that the rectangle extends $3$ units to the right and left of the center. The denominator of $y^2$ gives $4$ units above and below it. The slope of the rising diagonal is therefore the rise $4$ divided by the horizontal run $3$. The falling diagonal goes toward $(3,-4)$ and has the opposite slope. Both lines go through the origin, so both have zero intercept.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Find the equation of the horizontal hyperbola centered at $(2,-1)$ with right vertex $(6,-1)$ and falling asymptote $y+1=-\dfrac34(x-2)$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
a&=6-2=4\\
-\frac{b}{a}&=-\frac34\\
b&=\frac34\cdot4=3 .
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\frac{(x-2)^2}{16}-\frac{(y+1)^2}{9}=1 .
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The vertex abscissa exceeds the center abscissa by $4$, so the transverse semiaxis is $a=4$. To find $b$, read the slope of the falling line. The negative sign indicates the direction of the line, while the ratio of lengths is positive. This gives $b=3$. The equation then uses coordinates relative to the center, $x-2$ and $y+1$. Checking the vertex gives $(x-2)^2=16$ and $(y+1)^2=0$, and the left-hand side is $1$ as required.

</div>
