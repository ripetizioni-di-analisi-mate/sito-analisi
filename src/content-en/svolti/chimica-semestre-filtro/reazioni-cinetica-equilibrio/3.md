## Exercise 1

At constant volume, hydrogen peroxide concentration falls from $0.120$ to $0.080\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$ in $20.0\,\mathrm{s}$. Only $2\mathrm{H_2O_2}\longrightarrow2\mathrm{H_2O}+\mathrm{O_2}$ occurs. Calculate the average peroxide disappearance rate, normalised reaction rate and oxygen formation rate.

$$
-\dfrac{\Delta[\mathrm{H_2O_2}]}{\Delta t}=\dfrac{0.120-0.080}{20.0}=2.00\cdot10^{-3}\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}\,s^{-1}}.
$$
$$
\overline v=\dfrac{1}{2}\left(2.00\cdot10^{-3}\right)=1.00\cdot10^{-3}\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}\,s^{-1}},
$$
$$
\boxed{\dfrac{\Delta[\mathrm{O_2}]}{\Delta t}=\overline v}.
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Peroxide consumption is positive when its concentration change is negated. Dividing by coefficient two gives reaction rate; oxygen has coefficient one. The result is an interval average, so it neither establishes a constant rate nor automatically gives the final instantaneous rate. Constant volume allows concentration change to be attributed to chemistry. Comparing the two experimental signals can also test whether their stoichiometric ratio is consistent with a single transformation.

</div>

## Exercise 2

At constant temperature, the following initial rates are measured for a law $v=k[A]^\alpha[B]^\beta$. Determine orders and rate coefficient; discuss whether the data establish molecularity.

| Trial | $[A]$ in $\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$ | $[B]$ in $\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$ | $v$ in $\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}\,min^{-1}}$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | $0.10$ | $0.20$ | $0.0010$ |
| 2 | $0.20$ | $0.20$ | $0.0040$ |
| 3 | $0.20$ | $0.40$ | $0.0080$ |

$$
\dfrac{v_2}{v_1}=4=2^\alpha\Rightarrow\alpha=2,\qquad
\dfrac{v_3}{v_2}=2=2^\beta\Rightarrow\beta=1.
$$
$$
\boxed{k=\dfrac{0.0010}{(0.10)^2\cdot0.20}=0.50\,\mathrm{L^2\,mol^{-2}\,min^{-1}}}.
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The first comparison keeps B unchanged and isolates dependence on A. The second keeps A unchanged and isolates dependence on B. Overall order is three; coefficient units must convert concentration cubed into concentration per time. Recalculating $k$ from trials two and three gives the same value, consistent with the model. Molecularity instead concerns an elementary event: a third-order law may also arise through several steps. Mechanistic information is needed to assign three particles to one event.

</div>
