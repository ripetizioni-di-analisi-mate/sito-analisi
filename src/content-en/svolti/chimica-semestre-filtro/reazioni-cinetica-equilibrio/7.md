## Exercise 1

An ideal mixture of $\mathrm{N_2O_4(g)}\rightleftharpoons2\mathrm{NO_2(g)}$ is at equilibrium. At constant temperature, compare halving volume; adding inert gas at constant volume; and adding inert gas at constant total pressure so that volume doubles. Evaluate $Q$ immediately after intervention, before chemical response.

$$
Q=\dfrac{\left(\dfrac{p_{\mathrm{NO_2}}}{p^\circ}\right)^2}{\dfrac{p_{\mathrm{N_2O_4}}}{p^\circ}},\qquad\Delta\nu=2-1=1.
$$
If every partial pressure is multiplied by $\lambda$, then $Q'=\lambda Q$.

| Intervention | $\lambda$ | $Q'$ | Initial evolution |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Volume halved | $2$ | $2K$ | Towards $\mathrm{N_2O_4}$ |
| Inert gas at constant volume | $1$ | $K$ | No shift |
| Inert gas, volume doubled | $\dfrac12$ | $\dfrac{K}{2}$ | Towards $\mathrm{NO_2}$ |

![Three perturbations of the same mixture affect partial pressures and the quotient differently.](/figure/chimica-semestre-filtro/reazioni-cinetica-equilibrio/gas-inerte-en.svg)

<div class="spiegazione">

The first intervention doubles both partial pressures: the numerator increases fourfold and the denominator twofold. In the second, reacting mole numbers and volume are unchanged, so their partial pressures also stay unchanged. The third dilutes reacting gases through expansion. Total pressure alone therefore cannot predict the shift. Every intervention preserves temperature, so $K$ stays identical; the instantaneous quotient may change. The reasoning uses the stated ideality and absence of reactions involving the inert gas.

</div>

## Exercise 2

For an endothermic reaction, $K_1=4.00$ at $300\,\mathrm{K}$ and $\Delta_rH^\circ=40.0\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$. Assume constant enthalpy and estimate $K_2$ at $330\,\mathrm{K}$, with $R=8.314\,\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}}$.

$$
\ln\dfrac{K_2}{4.00}=-\dfrac{40000}{8.314}\left(\dfrac{1}{330}-\dfrac{1}{300}\right)\simeq1.458.
$$
$$
\boxed{K_2=4.00e^{1.458}\simeq17.2}.
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Heating relatively favours products in the endothermic direction, so a larger constant is expected before calculation. Both sign and result agree with that prediction. The calculation uses reaction enthalpy, whereas estimating a ratio of rate constants would require activation energy. The two energies answer different questions. The estimate also assumes no change of phase, reference reaction or standard state over the interval and negligible temperature dependence of enthalpy.

</div>
