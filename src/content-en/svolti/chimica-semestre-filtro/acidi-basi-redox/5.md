## Exercise 1

A diprotic acid $\mathrm{H_2A}$ has $C=0.0100\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$, $K_{a1}=1.00\cdot10^{-3}$ and $K_{a2}=1.00\cdot10^{-8}$ at $25\,^\circ\mathrm C$. Estimate pH using the first step only and check that the second is negligible. Assume an ideal dilute solution.

The separation of constants suggests a two-stage calculation, not permission to ignore the second step without checking. Solve the first equilibrium without approximating acid consumption. Then use the resulting hydronium to estimate the ratio of the second-step species and check the initial assumption.

$$
\dfrac{x^2}{0.0100-x}=1.00\cdot10^{-3}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
x&=\dfrac{-0.00100+\sqrt{(0.00100)^2+4(0.00100)(0.0100)}}{2}\\
&=2.702\cdot10^{-3}\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}},\\
\mathrm{pH}&=2.568\simeq2.57.
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\dfrac{[\mathrm{A^{2-}}]}{[\mathrm{HA^-}]}\simeq\dfrac{K_{a2}}{[\mathrm{H_3O^+}]}=
\dfrac{10^{-8}}{2.702\cdot10^{-3}}=3.70\cdot10^{-6}.
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The first constant is much larger than the second. Solve the first dissociation, then use the hydronium result to evaluate the ratio of the second-step species. This ratio is about $3.70\cdot10^{-6}$, making $\mathrm{A^{2-}}$ negligible compared with $\mathrm{HA^-}$. The **estimated pH is $2.57$**. Hydronium is also much greater than $10^{-7}\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$, so autoprotolysis has negligible effect.

Having two dissociable protons does not justify doubling initial concentration to obtain hydronium. That shortcut would assume two nearly complete dissociations, whereas the constants here describe very different equilibria.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Estimate the pH of $0.10\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$ sodium bicarbonate in a closed system with negligible gas phase at $25\,^\circ\mathrm C$. Assume ideal dilute behaviour and use $K_{a1}=4.3\cdot10^{-7}$ and $K_{a2}=4.7\cdot10^{-11}$ for the carbonic pair of steps. The first value is apparent and refers the acidic component to the sum of $\mathrm{CO_2(aq)}$ and $\mathrm{H_2CO_3}$. Apply the amphiprotic approximation.

The starting species is intermediate: it can accept or lose a proton. Treating it automatically as a strong base because its salt contains sodium would be incorrect. The given conditions allow the amphiprotic approximation using both adjacent equilibria; first calculate their logarithmic constants.

$$
\begin{aligned}
pK_{a1}&=-\log_{10}(4.3\cdot10^{-7})=6.367,\\
pK_{a2}&=-\log_{10}(4.7\cdot10^{-11})=10.328,\\
\mathrm{pH}&\simeq\dfrac{6.367+10.328}{2}=8.347\simeq8.35.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Bicarbonate can accept a proton to form the carbonic acid component or donate one to form carbonate. For a sufficiently concentrated solution of the intermediate species, adjacent equilibria lead to the mean of the two $pK_a$ values. Thus **$\mathrm{pH}\simeq8.35$**. The closed-system assumption excludes an externally imposed $\mathrm{CO_2}$ pressure; blood buffering instead needs an open-system model and constants for physiological conditions.

The required mean concerns logarithmic constants, not the arithmetic mean of the constants themselves. The basic result is consistent with bicarbonate's predominant basic behaviour under the stated conditions, without removing its ability to act as an acid.

</div>
