## Exercise 1

Mix $20.0\,\mathrm{mL}$ of $0.0500\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$ HCl and $15.0\,\mathrm{mL}$ of $0.0400\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$ NaOH. Calculate pH at $25\,^\circ\mathrm C$, assuming additive volumes and ideal dilute behaviour.

The volumes differ, so directly subtracting concentrations would not give the remaining amount. Convert volumes to litres and calculate both mole amounts. Stoichiometry identifies the limiting reagent and excess; only afterwards use final volume to find residual concentration.

$$
\begin{aligned}
n_{\mathrm{H^+}}&=0.0200\cdot0.0500=1.00\cdot10^{-3}\,\mathrm{mol},\\
n_{\mathrm{OH^-}}&=0.0150\cdot0.0400=6.00\cdot10^{-4}\,\mathrm{mol},\\
n_{\mathrm{H^+},\mathrm{exc}}&=4.00\cdot10^{-4}\,\mathrm{mol},\\
[\mathrm{H_3O^+}]&=\dfrac{4.00\cdot10^{-4}}{0.0350}=0.01143\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}},\\
\mathrm{pH}&=1.942\simeq1.94.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Neutralisation consumes equal hydronium and hydroxide amounts. Since initial acid moles are greater, subtract base moles and divide the excess by the total $35.0\,\mathrm{mL}$ volume. The logarithm of reduced concentration gives **$\mathrm{pH}=1.94$**. The subtraction must use moles because initial volumes differ.

Acid excess is consistent with pH below neutrality. Averaging initial pH values would ignore both the logarithmic scale and the chemical reaction. The subscript exc denotes excess remaining after neutralisation.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Titrate $25.0\,\mathrm{mL}$ of $0.100\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$ monoprotic HA with $0.100\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$ NaOH. The acid has $pK_a$ equal to $4.76$. Find the equivalence volume and pH after $12.5\,\mathrm{mL}$ of base. Assume ideal dilute behaviour and use Henderson–Hasselbalch while both components are present.

First determine how much base consumes all the acid, establishing the titration reference. Compare the actual added volume with that reference: it is half as much, so the balance gives equal residual acid and conjugate base under the buffer approximation.

![Half-equivalence and equivalence during titration](/figure/chimica-semestre-filtro/acidi-basi-redox/titolazione-en.svg)

$$
\begin{aligned}
n_{\mathrm{HA},0}&=0.0250\cdot0.100=2.50\cdot10^{-3}\,\mathrm{mol},\\
V_{\mathrm{eq}}&=\dfrac{2.50\cdot10^{-3}}{0.100}=0.0250\,\mathrm L=25.0\,\mathrm{mL}.
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
n_{\mathrm{OH^-}}&=0.0125\cdot0.100=1.25\cdot10^{-3}\,\mathrm{mol},\\
n_{\mathrm{HA}}&=n_{\mathrm{A^-}}=1.25\cdot10^{-3}\,\mathrm{mol},\\
\mathrm{pH}&=4.76+\log_{10}1=4.76.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The acid is monoprotic, so equivalence requires one mole of hydroxide per initial mole of HA. At $12.5\,\mathrm{mL}$, half the required base has been added and half the acid converted to conjugate base. HA and $\mathrm{A^-}$ amounts are equal, making the buffer ratio one. Thus **at half-equivalence pH equals $pK_a$** in the adopted model.

Do not confuse half-equivalence with equivalence. At the former, both buffer forms remain; at the latter, conjugate-base salt predominates and hydrolysis determines pH. Titrant concentration is essential to convert the required moles to a volume.

</div>
