## Exercise 1

$8.8\,\mathrm g$ of propane undergoes complete combustion with sufficient oxygen. Use approximate molar masses $M(\mathrm{C_3H_8})=44$, $M(\mathrm{O_2})=32$, $M(\mathrm{CO_2})=44$ and $M(\mathrm{H_2O})=18\,\mathrm{g/mol}$. Calculate minimum oxygen requirement and product amounts; check mass conservation.

The balance is $\mathrm{C_3H_8+5O_2\longrightarrow3CO_2+4H_2O}$.
$$\begin{aligned}n(\mathrm{C_3H_8})&=\dfrac{8.8}{44}=0.20\,\mathrm{mol},\\n(\mathrm{O_2})&=1.0\,\mathrm{mol},\quad m(\mathrm{O_2})=32\,\mathrm g,\\n(\mathrm{CO_2})&=0.60\,\mathrm{mol},\quad m(\mathrm{CO_2})=26.4\,\mathrm g,\\n(\mathrm{H_2O})&=0.80\,\mathrm{mol},\quad m(\mathrm{H_2O})=14.4\,\mathrm g.\end{aligned}$$
The check is $8.8+32=26.4+14.4=40.8\,\mathrm g$.

<div class="spiegazione">

Stoichiometric coefficients multiply mole amounts, so fuel mass is converted first. Calculated oxygen is the minimum stoichiometric consumption for complete combustion; any initial excess remains distinct from the amount reacting. Product masses use the stated approximations, making the final check consistent. Temperature and pressure are unnecessary because gas volumes are not requested. Calculating volumes would require state conditions and an appropriate model. Incomplete combustion could form other products, so the chosen balance alone would no longer describe the transformation.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Compare HBr addition to propene in two models: ionic addition without peroxides and radical addition under suitable peroxide conditions. Identify the predominant constitutional products and the intermediate explaining each orientation.

In the ionic pathway, protonation producing a secondary carbocation is favoured, and bromide forms **2-bromopropane**. In the radical pathway, bromine-radical addition to the terminal carbon leaves a secondary radical at the central carbon; subsequent H abstraction from HBr gives **1-bromopropane**.

<div class="spiegazione">

Both balances add one hydrogen and one bromine across the double bond, but bond-formation order and intermediate identity differ. The first pathway involves a carbocation, the second a carbon radical. Placing the more stable centre along the chain consequently gives opposite bromine orientations in the product. Merely writing “Markovnikov” without specifying conditions would leave the prediction incomplete. The radical peroxide effect is the classical HBr case and must not automatically be extended to other hydrogen halides. The reasoning compares specified mechanisms rather than claiming every peroxide-containing mixture gives a single pure product.

</div>
