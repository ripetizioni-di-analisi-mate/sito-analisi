## Exercise 1

The two but-2-ene structures in the figure differ in methyl-group positions. Assign E/Z and classify their relationship. Then compare with propene.

At each double-bond carbon, methyl outranks H by CIP. Methyls on the same side give $Z$; opposite methyls give $E$.

The forms are **diastereoisomers**. Propene, $\mathrm{CH_2{=}CHCH_3}$, does not admit E/Z because the terminal carbon bears two identical hydrogens.

![Methyl groups on the same side in Z and opposite sides in E](/figure/chimica-semestre-filtro/carbonio-idrocarburi-stereochimica/ez-en.svg)

<div class="spiegazione">

Comparison is performed separately at the two double-bond ends. In but-2-ene, the higher-priority group is the methyl used by cis/trans terminology, so cis corresponds to Z. This cannot automatically be extended to differently substituted alkenes, where CIP rankings matter. E and Z preserve connectivity and are not a nonsuperposable mirror pair. Propene lacks a distinguishable choice at CH₂: exchanging its two hydrogens leaves the structure identical. Merely drawing groups on two sides does not create configurational isomerism.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Classify three comparisons: butane and 2-methylpropane; staggered and eclipsed ethane; the two butan-2-ol enantiomers. State what changes and what remains constant in each case.

Butane and 2-methylpropane are **constitutional isomers**: equal formula, different connections. The ethane forms are **conformations** of the same connectivity, interconverted by C–C rotation. The butan-2-ol forms are **enantiomers**, preserving connectivity but having nonsuperposable mirror configurations at the stereogenic centre.

<div class="spiegazione">

The first check is atomic connectivity, before looking for a three-dimensional difference. If connectivity changes, rotating the molecule cannot generate the other structure. In the second comparison, bond rotation changes only the dihedral angle and torsional cost; ethane interconverts rapidly under ordinary conditions. In the third, rotating the whole molecule or a bond does not superpose the four group directions at the centre. This distinction also matters experimentally: a different drawing can represent the same compound, an accessible conformation or a distinct configuration. Equality of molecular formulas alone does not distinguish these possibilities.

</div>
