## Exercise 1

A sample contains $9.00\,\mathrm g$ of pure water. Use $M=18.0\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}$ and $N_A=6.022\cdot10^{23}\,\mathrm{mol^{-1}}$. Calculate moles of water, molecules and the hydrogen atom count.

$$\begin{aligned}n(\mathrm{H_2O})&=\dfrac{9.00}{18.0}=0.500\,\mathrm{mol},\\[5pt]N(\mathrm{H_2O})&=0.500\cdot6.022\cdot10^{23}\simeq3.01\cdot10^{23},\\[5pt]N(\mathrm H)&=2N(\mathrm{H_2O})\simeq6.02\cdot10^{23}.\end{aligned}$$

The diagram shows the ratios for one mole of water; all counts are halved in this sample.

![Ratio between water molecules and their constituent H and O atoms in the sample](/figure/chimica-semestre-filtro/atomo-legami-termodinamica/entita-en.svg)

The sample contains **half a mole of molecules and one mole of H atoms**.

<div class="spiegazione">

Molar mass converts grams into moles of specified entities, here water molecules. The Avogadro constant then converts those moles into a molecule count. Hydrogen’s subscript 2 introduces another step: every molecule contains two H atoms, so the count doubles. No dissociation of water is assumed; the calculation counts atoms already within the molecules. At three significant figures, the final values are 3.01 and 6.02 times ten to the twenty-third. Checking these ratios avoids confusing one mole of water with one mole of all its constituent atoms.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A pure sample contains $26.4\,\mathrm g$ of ammonium sulfate, $\mathrm{(NH_4)_2SO_4}$. Assume $M=132\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}$. Find the moles of formula units and of ammonium groups, N atoms and H atoms within the solid.

$$n_f=\dfrac{26.4}{132}=0.200\,\mathrm{mol}.$$

Each formula unit contains two ammonium groups, two N atoms and eight H atoms.

$$n(\mathrm{NH_4^+})=0.400\,\mathrm{mol},\quad n(\mathrm N)=0.400\,\mathrm{mol},\quad n(\mathrm H)=1.60\,\mathrm{mol}.$$

The outer subscript multiplies the **entire NH₄ group**.

<div class="spiegazione">

The parentheses identify the polyatomic ion occurring twice in each formula unit. Each ammonium ion contains one N and four H atoms: doubling nitrogen alone would miss four hydrogen atoms per formula unit. The solid is ionic, so formula unit is the appropriate term, without assuming isolated salt molecules. This is a composition count and does not require dissolving the sample. As a formula check, two singly positive ammonium cations balance one doubly negative sulfate anion.

</div>
