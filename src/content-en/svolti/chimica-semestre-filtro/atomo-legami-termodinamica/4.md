## Exercise 1

Compare $\mathrm{NH_3}$ and $\mathrm{NH_4^+}$: count valence electrons, assign nitrogen bonds and lone pairs, and derive domain arrangement and molecular geometry. N contributes 5 valence electrons and H contributes 1.

$$N_v(\mathrm{NH_3})=5+3=8,\qquad N_v(\mathrm{NH_4^+})=5+4-1=8.$$

Both species have a tetrahedral electron-domain arrangement.

In NH₃, three pairs form bonds and one remains unshared: four domains and **trigonal pyramidal** molecular geometry. In NH₄⁺, all four pairs form bonds: four domains and **tetrahedral** molecular geometry.

<div class="spiegazione">

The positive charge requires subtracting one electron from ammonium’s count. The species thus have the same valence-electron total but allocate it differently. VSEPR includes both bonds and lone pairs when arranging the four domains; molecular geometry considers only atom positions. When ammonia is protonated, its available lone pair is shared with the proton. The initial origin of both electrons describes coordinate bonding, whereas the final ion has four equivalent N-H bonds. Domain count alone therefore does not distinguish the two molecular shapes.

</div>

## Exercise 2

The C-O bonds in $\mathrm{CO_2}$ and O-H bonds in $\mathrm{H_2O}$ are polar. Use their geometries to explain why only water has a nonzero permanent dipole moment.

![Opposing dipoles in CO2 and non-opposing dipoles in bent water](/figure/chimica-semestre-filtro/atomo-legami-termodinamica/geometria-en.svg)

CO₂ is linear: two equal and opposite contributions cancel. H₂O is bent: transverse components cancel, while components along the bisector add. **CO₂ is nonpolar and H₂O is polar.**

<div class="spiegazione">

Electronegativity describes charge distribution within an individual bond. Inferring molecular polarity also requires spatial arrangement because dipole moment is a vector. Carbon dioxide’s symmetry makes its two oxygen contributions equal and opposite. In water, oxygen’s lone pairs accompany a bent geometry, so the two contributions do not point in opposite directions. The figure indicates electron-density displacement towards O. The result explains why a polar bond alone does not guarantee a polar molecule and why water can orient around ions.

</div>
