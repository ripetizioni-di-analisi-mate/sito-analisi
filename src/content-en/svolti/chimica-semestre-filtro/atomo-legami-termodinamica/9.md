## Exercise 1

A sample of solid CO₂ is heated slowly at a constant pressure of $1.00\,\mathrm{bar}$. Its triple-point pressure is approximately $5.185\,\mathrm{bar}$. Determine whether a stable liquid phase appears and compare the path with water ice at the same pressure.

![Qualitative phase diagrams of water and CO2 showing the relative position of a one-bar isobar](/figure/chimica-semestre-filtro/atomo-legami-termodinamica/fasi-en.svg)

For CO₂, the isobar lies below the triple point and crosses the **solid–gas** boundary, giving sublimation. For water, one bar is above triple-point pressure: heating crosses the **solid–liquid** boundary and eventually the liquid–vapour boundary.

<div class="spiegazione">

Pressure determines which phase regions can be reached during isobaric heating. Temperature changes while pressure remains fixed. For CO₂, the liquid region does not intersect the one-bar path, so raising temperature alone cannot produce stable liquid. Ordinary water ice can instead melt. The different melting-curve slope provides another comparison: increasing pressure near usual conditions favours liquid water, which is denser than ice. The figure’s curves are qualitative; precise values require experimental data.

</div>

## Exercise 2

In a thermoregulation model, $12.0\,\mathrm g$ of water evaporates with constant assumed vaporisation heat $L=2.40\,\mathrm{kJ\,g^{-1}}$. All energy comes from the body. Calculate the heat removed and explain the change if half the water drips away without evaporating.

For complete evaporation,

$$Q=mL=12.0\cdot2.40=28.8\,\mathrm{kJ}.$$

The body releases that energy, so its heat contribution is $q=-28.8\,\mathrm{kJ}$. If only half the mass evaporates,

$$Q'=6.00\cdot2.40=14.4\,\mathrm{kJ}.$$

Evaporative cooling depends on **mass actually evaporated**.

<div class="spiegazione">

Converting liquid into vapour requires energy to change the molecular state. In this model, that energy is drawn from the body, so its heat exchange is negative. Grams multiplied by kilojoules per gram correctly give kilojoules. Dripping water can transfer heat by other mechanisms but does not provide the latent-evaporation contribution calculated here. The comparison explains why sweat production does not automatically equal evaporative cooling. The calculation also excludes energy used to warm the water and any environmental exchange other than the one specified.

</div>
