## Exercise 1

Compare $\mathrm{CH_3COOCH_3}$ and $\mathrm{CH_3CONHCH_3}$. Identify their groups, explain the amide's relative resistance to hydrolysis under customary conditions, and identify product forms after its complete hydrolysis in two separate media: strongly acidic and strongly basic, both with suitable heating.

The first is an ester and the second an N-substituted amide. For the amide:

| Final medium | Acyl portion | Nitrogen portion |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Acidic | Acetic acid | Methylammonium |
| Basic | Acetate | Methylamine |

![The group directly attached to the acyl group distinguishes esters, thioesters, amides and acyl phosphates](/figure/chimica-semestre-filtro/gruppi-funzionali/acili-en.svg)

<div class="spiegazione">

Both molecules contain the CH3CO acyl group, but the atom attached to carbonyl carbon is O in the ester and N in the amide. The N pair contributes effectively to delocalization, reducing electrophilicity and giving C–N partial double-bond character. The unactivated amide's leaving group is also unfavorable. These factors explain relative resistance without making an amide absolutely inert.

Hydrolysis separates the acyl and nitrogen portions while retaining the methyl attached to N. Medium acidity then determines product states: amine protonation gives methylammonium; in base the carboxyl group remains carboxylate. The acid from one condition should not be combined with the neutral amine from the other as though pH were irrelevant.

The table assumes complete hydrolysis achieved under the stated conditions, rather than instantaneous conversion upon simply adding water.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A system contains $0.150\,\mathrm{mol}$ of ethyl ethanoate and $0.100\,\mathrm{mol}$ of OH− in water. Assume quantitative saponification relative to the limiting reagent, no side reactions and no final acid work-up. Calculate remaining ester, carboxylate and alcohol produced.

$$
\mathrm{CH_3COOCH_2CH_3}+\mathrm{OH^-}
\longrightarrow\mathrm{CH_3COO^-}+\mathrm{CH_3CH_2OH}.
$$

The reaction consumes $0.100\,\mathrm{mol}$ of ester, leaving $0.050\,\mathrm{mol}$. It produces $0.100\,\mathrm{mol}$ of acetate and $0.100\,\mathrm{mol}$ of ethanol.

<div class="spiegazione">

The balance requires one OH− per hydrolyzed ester, so base imposes the limit. Treating it as a catalyst would incorrectly consume all 0.150 mol of ester. Reconstruct the product pair by separating CH3CO from ethoxy: the latter supplies ethanol, not methanol.

Mechanistically, OH− attacks the carbonyl and a tetrahedral intermediate forms. Collapse restores C=O; proton transfers ultimately give carboxylate and alcohol. The carboxylate product is not the neutral acid and does not readily re-esterify with alcohol under the same basic conditions.

Subsequent acid work-up would change the carboxyl group's form, but is not included. Moles are not concentrations: without a final volume, inventing molar concentrations is unjustified even though stoichiometry is already fully determined.

</div>
