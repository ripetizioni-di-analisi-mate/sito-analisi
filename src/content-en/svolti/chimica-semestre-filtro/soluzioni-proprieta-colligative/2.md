## Exercise 1

![Equal percentages with different numerators: grams and millilitres](/figure/chimica-semestre-filtro/soluzioni-proprieta-colligative/percentuali-en.svg)

Dissolve $4.0\,\mathrm g$ of solute in $96.0\,\mathrm g$ of water without reactions or losses. Calculate solution mass percentage.

$$
m_{\mathrm{tot}}=4.0+96.0=100.0\,\mathrm g
$$

$$
\%\,(m/m)=100\dfrac{4.0}{100.0}=4.0\%
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Mass percentage compares solute mass with total solution mass. With no reactions or losses, final mass is the sum of both initial masses, so the denominator is $100.0\,\mathrm g$, including solute as well as water. Each $100\,\mathrm g$ of solution contains $4.0\,\mathrm g$ of solute. Dividing by water mass alone would produce a solute-to-solvent mass ratio instead. No volume or density is required because both numerator and denominator are masses. Conversely, this answer cannot automatically be labelled a mass–volume percentage: that conversion would require the mass corresponding to a specified final solution volume. A further check is that solute mass is smaller than total solution mass.

</div>

## Exercise 2

One solution contains $7.5\,\mathrm g$ glucose in a final volume of $250\,\mathrm{mL}$. Another contains $6.0\,\mathrm{mL}$ ethanol brought to a final volume of $200\,\mathrm{mL}$. Calculate their mass–volume and volume percentages respectively.

$$
m_{100}=\dfrac{7.5\,\mathrm g}{250\,\mathrm{mL}}\cdot100\,\mathrm{mL}
=3.0\,\mathrm g
$$

$$
\%\,(V/V)=100\dfrac{6.0\,\mathrm{mL}}{200\,\mathrm{mL}}=3.0\%
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

For the first solution, scale the mass to a final solution volume of $100\,\mathrm{mL}$ using the factor $\dfrac{100}{250}$; the result is $3.0\%$ mass–volume. For the second, compare component volume with final solution volume in matching units; the dimensionless ratio gives $3.0\%$ by volume. Equal numerical percentages therefore do not describe equal physical quantities. Both final volumes are supplied, so additive water and ethanol volumes are unnecessary. Preparing the ethanol sample means reaching the final volume mark, not assuming that a separately measured water volume must exactly equal the difference between final and component volumes. This distinction matters when interactions between liquids cause contraction or expansion of the combined volume.

</div>
