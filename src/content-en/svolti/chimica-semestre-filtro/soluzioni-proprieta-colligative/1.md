## Exercise 1

![Comparing molecular, colloidal and settled dispersions](/figure/chimica-semestre-filtro/soluzioni-proprieta-colligative/miscele-en.svg)

Consider dust-free dry air, water with completely dissolved sugar, and water with settled sand. Classify each as homogeneous or heterogeneous and identify distinguishable phases.

| Sample | Distinguishable phases | Classification |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Dry air | One gas phase | Homogeneous mixture |
| Dissolved sugar in water | One liquid phase | Homogeneous solution |
| Water and sand | Liquid and solid | Heterogeneous mixture |

<div class="spiegazione">

A phase has uniform intensive properties and is separated from others by an interface. Dry air contains several gases distributed throughout the same gas phase; chemical diversity does not require multiple phases. Dissolved sugar is dispersed molecularly, so the liquid sample is one homogeneous solution. Sand remains separate and settles, giving at least liquid and solid phases. The observations and assumptions determine the classification: adding dust to air or excess undissolved sugar to water could change it. Merely counting chemical substances would therefore be the wrong procedure. First identify whether separate regions and interfaces persist, then describe the components within each region. Complete dissolution specifically excludes a residual solid phase in the sugar sample.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Classify milk, fog and a copper–nickel alloy prepared as a single solid phase. For the first two, specify the continuous medium and dispersed component.

| Sample | Medium and dispersed component | Classification |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Milk | Lipid droplets in water | Colloidal emulsion |
| Fog | Water droplets in air | Aerosol |
| Single-phase copper–nickel alloy | One uniform solid phase | Solid solution |

<div class="spiegazione">

Milk contains lipid droplets dispersed in an aqueous continuous medium, making it a colloidal emulsion. Fog has liquid droplets in a gas and is an aerosol; reversing these phases would describe a foam. The alloy is explicitly single-phase and compositionally uniform at the observation scale, so it is a solid solution. Different chemical elements do not by themselves imply different phases. Nor should the alloy example be generalised to every alloy: other compositions or preparation conditions can produce multiple phases. The useful sequence is to identify the physical state of the medium, the dispersed component and the scale at which heterogeneity appears. Absence of immediate settling alone does not establish that a sample is a molecular solution.

</div>
