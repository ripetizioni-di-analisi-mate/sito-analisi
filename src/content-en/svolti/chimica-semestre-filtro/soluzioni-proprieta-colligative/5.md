## Exercise 1

A solution contains $46.0\,\mathrm g$ ethanol and $90.0\,\mathrm g$ water. Use approximate molar masses $46.0$ and $18.0\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}$. Calculate both mole fractions.

$$
n_{e}=\dfrac{46.0}{46.0}=1.00\,\mathrm{mol},\qquad
n_{w}=\dfrac{90.0}{18.0}=5.00\,\mathrm{mol}
$$

$$
x_{e}=\dfrac{1.00}{6.00}=\dfrac16\simeq0.167,\qquad
x_{w}=\dfrac56\simeq0.833
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Mole fractions compare amounts of substance, so both masses must first be converted into moles. Total amount is $6.00$ moles, including ethanol and water; dividing by water alone would give a component ratio rather than a fraction of the whole mixture. The fractions are dimensionless and sum to one, providing an immediate balance check. Their values differ from mass fractions because the two components have different molar masses. No density or solution-volume measurement is required. These fractions could be used in an explicitly ideal vapour-pressure model, but calculating them does not itself establish that the real ethanol–water mixture behaves ideally.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A completely dissociated $\mathrm{CaCl_2}$ solution has formal concentration $0.0500\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$. Calculate calcium concentration in $\mathrm{mEq\,L^{-1}}$. Using $a=\gamma c/c^\circ$, $c^\circ=1\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$ and an assigned calcium coefficient $\gamma=0.60$, determine calcium activity.

$$
c_{\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}}=0.0500\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}
$$

$$
c_{\mathrm{eq}}=2c_{\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}}=0.100\,\mathrm{Eq\,L^{-1}}
=100\,\mathrm{mEq\,L^{-1}}
$$

$$
a_{\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}}=0.60\dfrac{0.0500}{1}=0.030
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Each formula unit supplies one calcium ion, so calcium amount concentration equals formal salt concentration. Each calcium ion carries two positive charges, hence charge equivalents are twice the molar amount. Activity is a different quantity: multiply relative concentration by the assigned coefficient, yielding a dimensionless result. Individual-ion activity is interpreted within the stated convention. Neither the charge multiplier nor the coefficient should be used as the three-particle dissociation count of calcium chloride. Chloride contributes its own charge and particle amounts; the present calculation asks specifically about calcium, so the scope of each quantity must remain explicit. Charge equivalents use the magnitude of ionic charge; a redox-equivalent calculation would instead require a specified reaction.

</div>
