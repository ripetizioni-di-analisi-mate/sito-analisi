## Exercise 1

![Qualitative cell responses to retained solutes and permeating urea](/figure/chimica-semestre-filtro/soluzioni-proprieta-colligative/tonicita-en.svg)

In an ideal model, a cell with a water-permeable membrane initially contains effectively non-permeating solutes at $0.300\,\mathrm{Osm\,L^{-1}}$. It is placed in a solution of non-permeating solutes at $0.200\,\mathrm{Osm\,L^{-1}}$, at the same temperature and pressure. State initial water movement. Then assess whether an external solution containing only permeating urea at $0.300\,\mathrm{Osm\,L^{-1}}$ ensures sustained isotonicity.

$$c_{\mathrm{osm,ext}}<c_{\mathrm{osm,int}}.$$

Water initially enters the cell. Permeating urea does not ensure constant volume.

<div class="spiegazione">

In the first case solutes are retained on both sides, so initial water movement is into the cell, where particle concentration is higher. The external medium is hypotonic relative to that cell. In the second case equal total initial osmolarities do not ensure sustained volume constancy: urea can enter and distribute while internal non-permeating solutes remain. Water entry can then cause swelling. No final volume can be uniquely calculated from the statement alone, because reservoir size, non-osmotic volume and mechanical constraints are unspecified. The comparison illustrates why total particle concentration and membrane-dependent tonicity must be distinguished rather than treated as synonyms. Urea remains a measurable osmotic particle even after its contribution balances on both sides: permeability does not mean disappearance of the substance or absence of initial osmolarity.

</div>

## Exercise 2

For a neutral solute in a one-dimensional diffusion model, $D=2.0\cdot10^{-9}\,\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}$ and constant gradient $\dfrac{\mathrm dc}{\mathrm dx}=1.0\cdot10^3\,\mathrm{mol\,m^{-4}}$. Area is $A=1.0\cdot10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m^2}$. Calculate Fick flux and molar transfer rate, signed relative to the $x$-axis.

$$
J=-D\dfrac{\mathrm dc}{\mathrm dx}
=-2.0\cdot10^{-9}\cdot1.0\cdot10^3
=-2.0\cdot10^{-6}\,\mathrm{mol\,m^{-2}\,s^{-1}}
$$

$$
\dot n=JA=-2.0\cdot10^{-6}\cdot1.0\cdot10^{-4}
=-2.0\cdot10^{-10}\,\mathrm{mol\,s^{-1}}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Concentration rises with $x$, so the specified gradient is positive. The minus sign in Fick's law makes flux negative, towards smaller $x$ where concentration is lower. This sign indicates direction, not negative matter. Multiplying flux per square metre by total area gives molar transfer rate across the whole surface. Units provide an independent check: diffusion coefficient times concentration gradient gives amount per area per time, and the area factor then cancels. The model assumes a neutral species without convection or other driving forces; charged-ion transport may additionally require an electrical-potential contribution.

</div>
