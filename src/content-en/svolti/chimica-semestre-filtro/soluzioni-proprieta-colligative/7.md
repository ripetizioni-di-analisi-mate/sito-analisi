## Exercise 1

![Relative solvent pressure in the ideal non-volatile-solute model](/figure/chimica-semestre-filtro/soluzioni-proprieta-colligative/raoult-en.svg)

Dissolve $9.00\,\mathrm g$ glucose in $90.0\,\mathrm g$ water. Use $M_{g}=180\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}$ and $M_{w}=18.0\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}$. At the stated temperature pure water has vapour pressure $23.76\,\mathrm{mmHg}$. Assume ideal solution and non-volatile glucose; calculate vapour pressure and its lowering.

$$
n_s=\dfrac{9.00}{180}=0.0500\,\mathrm{mol},\qquad
n_w=\dfrac{90.0}{18.0}=5.00\,\mathrm{mol}
$$

$$
x_w=\dfrac{5.00}{5.05}=\dfrac{100}{101}
$$

$$
p=\dfrac{100}{101}\cdot23.76\simeq23.525\,\mathrm{mmHg}
$$

$$
\Delta p=23.76-p\simeq0.235\,\mathrm{mmHg}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Non-volatile glucose contributes negligibly to vapour, so water supplies total vapour pressure. Raoult's law uses the water mole fraction in the liquid, calculated after converting both masses into amounts. The denominator includes glucose as well as water. Because the fraction is slightly below one, the final pressure must be slightly below pure-water pressure. Lowering is the positive difference between the two pressures and can also be checked using solute mole fraction times pure-solvent pressure. The calculation is an ideal-solution model; it does not treat solute as a mechanical layer blocking the surface or establish exact real-solution behaviour.

</div>

## Exercise 2

An ideal liquid mixture contains volatile A and B with $x_A=0.400$ and $x_B=0.600$. At the same temperature, pure-component vapour pressures are $p_A^\ast=80.0\,\mathrm{kPa}$ and $p_B^\ast=30.0\,\mathrm{kPa}$. Calculate total pressure and the mole fraction of A in ideal vapour.

$$
p_A=0.400\cdot80.0=32.0\,\mathrm{kPa},\qquad
p_B=0.600\cdot30.0=18.0\,\mathrm{kPa}
$$

$$
p_{\mathrm{tot}}=32.0+18.0=50.0\,\mathrm{kPa}
$$

$$
y_A=\dfrac{p_A}{p_{\mathrm{tot}}}=\dfrac{32.0}{50.0}=0.640
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Both substances are volatile, so each contributes its own Raoult partial pressure. Dalton's law adds those contributions and relates each to vapour composition. A is more volatile because its pure-component vapour pressure is higher at the same temperature; vapour is therefore enriched in A: its fraction $0.640$ exceeds the equilibrium liquid fraction $0.400$. Liquid fractions and vapour fractions must not be equated. A useful check is that total pressure lies between the two pure-component pressures in this ideal binary model. Both phase idealities are stated: liquid ideality supports Raoult, while vapour ideality supports the simple partial-pressure composition relation.

</div>
