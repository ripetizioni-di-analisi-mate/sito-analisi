## Exercise 1

Dissolve $5.85\,\mathrm g$ of $\mathrm{NaCl}$ and bring the solution to a final volume of $500\,\mathrm{mL}$. Use $M_{\mathrm{NaCl}}=58.5\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}$ and calculate the salt’s formal molarity.

$$
n=\dfrac{5.85\,\mathrm g}{58.5\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}}=0.100\,\mathrm{mol}
$$

$$
c=\dfrac{0.100\,\mathrm{mol}}{0.500\,\mathrm L}=0.200\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Molar mass converts reagent mass into $0.100\,\mathrm{mol}$ of sodium chloride formula units. Molarity divides by the final volume of $0.500\,\mathrm L$, giving $0.200\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$. Dissociation is not an extra multiplier in this calculation: formal salt molarity records how many formula units were introduced per solution volume. Ionic concentrations and osmolarity are separate questions. With complete dissociation, sodium and chloride each have the same amount concentration as the salt introduced, while total ideal particle concentration is twice that value. Confusing these levels of counting would incorrectly double the requested formal molarity. The volume is final solution volume, not the amount of water initially used.

</div>

## Exercise 2

![For the second exercise: same solute amount, different solvent mass and final volume](/figure/chimica-semestre-filtro/soluzioni-proprieta-colligative/denominatori-en.svg)

Dissolve $18.0\,\mathrm g$ glucose, with molar mass $180\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}$, in $200\,\mathrm g$ water. Measured final volume is $210\,\mathrm{mL}$. Calculate molality and molarity.

$$
n=\dfrac{18.0}{180}\,\mathrm{mol}=0.100\,\mathrm{mol}
$$

$$
b=\dfrac{0.100}{0.200}\,\mathrm{mol\,kg^{-1}}=0.500\,\mathrm{mol\,kg^{-1}}
$$

$$
c=\dfrac{0.100}{0.210}\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}\simeq0.476\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Both calculations start from the same glucose amount. Molality divides by solvent mass, $0.200\,\mathrm{kg}$, whereas molarity divides by measured final solution volume, $0.210\,\mathrm L$. Solute mass must not be added to the molality denominator: doing so would define a different quantity and underestimate molality. Likewise, treating the water mass as a final solution volume would disregard the supplied volume measurement. Their numerical difference is expected rather than an inconsistency. If the closed solution expands on warming without reaction or phase change, molarity changes while molality remains constant because its mass denominator is conserved.

</div>
