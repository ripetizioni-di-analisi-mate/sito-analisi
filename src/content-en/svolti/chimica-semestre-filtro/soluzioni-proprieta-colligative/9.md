## Exercise 1

![Particles per introduced unit: glucose, NaCl and CaCl2](/figure/chimica-semestre-filtro/soluzioni-proprieta-colligative/particelle-en.svg)

An ideal dilute glucose solution has concentration $0.100\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$ at $300\,\mathrm K$. Calculate osmotic pressure against pure water through a glucose-retaining membrane. Use $R=0.082057\,\mathrm{L\,atm\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}}$.

$$
i=1,\qquad c_{\mathrm{osm}}=0.100\,\mathrm{Osm\,L^{-1}}
$$

$$
\pi=icRT=0.100\cdot0.082057\cdot300
=2.46171\,\mathrm{atm}\simeq2.46\,\mathrm{atm}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Glucose remains molecular, so one mole of solute ideally contributes one mole of particles. Since the membrane retains glucose, the van’t Hoff relation gives the pressure excess required to balance osmosis against pure water. Temperature is absolute and the chosen gas constant matches litres and atmospheres, so units cancel to pressure. This is an equilibrium comparison, not a requirement that a cell exposed to glucose must have this hydrostatic pressure. If the membrane passed glucose freely, its distribution could change and this initial comparison would not by itself predict sustained cell volume. Additional membrane and time information would be needed. Using Celsius temperature or concentration units inconsistent with the gas constant would alter the result.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Compare ideal dilute $\mathrm{CaCl_2}$ at $0.0500\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$ with $i=3$ and glucose at $0.150\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$ with $i=1$, both at $300\,\mathrm K$. Calculate osmolarities and osmotic pressures against pure water through membranes retaining the respective solutes. Use $R=0.082057\,\mathrm{L\,atm\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}}$.

$$
c_{\mathrm{osm,CaCl_2}}=3\cdot0.0500=0.150\,\mathrm{Osm\,L^{-1}}
$$

$$
c_{\mathrm{osm,g}}=1\cdot0.150=0.150\,\mathrm{Osm\,L^{-1}}
$$

$$
\pi_{\mathrm{CaCl_2}}=\pi_{g}
=0.150\cdot0.082057\cdot300
\simeq3.69\,\mathrm{atm}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Calcium chloride gives three particles per formula unit in the assigned model, while glucose gives one. Different formal molarities therefore produce equal total particle concentrations, making the solutions isoosmotic. Equal temperature then gives equal ideal osmotic pressures against pure water through the specified retaining membranes. The particle factor has already been included in osmolarity and must not be applied again. A biological tonicity comparison would require a particular cell membrane and information on transport and metabolism. Neither calcium's divalent charge nor the formal salt concentration alone gives the correct total-particle count: the factor of three comes from one cation and two anions.

</div>
