## Exercise 1

An ideal dilute aqueous solution of a non-volatile, non-associating non-electrolyte has molality $0.200\,\mathrm{mol\,kg^{-1}}$. Use $K_f=1.86\,\mathrm{K\,kg\,mol^{-1}}$ and $K_b=0.512\,\mathrm{K\,kg\,mol^{-1}}$. Calculate temperature changes and final values relative to pure water freezing at $0\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$ and boiling at $100\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$ at the stated pressure; assume pure ice crystallises.

$$
\Delta T_f=1.86\cdot0.200=0.372\,\mathrm K,
\qquad T_f=-0.372\,{}^\circ\mathrm C
$$

$$
\Delta T_b=0.512\cdot0.200=0.1024\,\mathrm K,
\qquad T_b=100.1024\,{}^\circ\mathrm C
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

An undissociated, non-associating non-electrolyte has ideal particle factor one. Multiply the same solute molality by each solvent constant to obtain positive change magnitudes. Freezing depression is subtracted from the pure-solvent freezing point, whereas boiling elevation is added to the pure-solvent boiling point. Kelvin and Celsius intervals have equal numerical values, so these differences can be combined with the specified Celsius references. The temperatures must be compared at the same external pressure; changing pressure would introduce a separate effect. Non-volatility and pure-solvent crystallisation are part of the model. The displayed extra digits show arithmetic and should not be mistaken for experimental precision. At equal particle molality, another ideal non-electrolyte would give the same changes even if its molar mass differed.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Dissolve $1.80\,\mathrm g$ of a non-volatile non-electrolyte in $100\,\mathrm g$ water. In the ideal dilute model, measure $\Delta T_f=0.186\,\mathrm K$. Use $K_f=1.86\,\mathrm{K\,kg\,mol^{-1}}$ to determine molar mass, assuming no association and pure ice formation.

$$
b=\dfrac{\Delta T_f}{K_f}=\dfrac{0.186}{1.86}=0.100\,\mathrm{mol\,kg^{-1}}
$$

$$
n=bm_{w}=0.100\cdot0.100=0.0100\,\mathrm{mol}
$$

$$
M=\dfrac{1.80\,\mathrm g}{0.0100\,\mathrm{mol}}=180\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Cryoscopy determines particle molality. Because the solute is undissociated and unassociated, that equals solute molality. Multiplying by water mass, $0.100\,\mathrm{kg}$, gives $0.0100$ moles of solute; dividing the introduced mass by those moles yields molar mass. Each step uses a different quantity, so keeping units visible prevents treating grams of water as kilograms or using solution mass instead of solvent mass. Unrecognised dissociation would increase particle number and lead to an underestimated apparent molar mass. Association would have the opposite tendency. The inferred value therefore depends on the declared particle model as well as on the measured depression.

</div>
