## Exercise 1

Two points in oil of density $800\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}$ differ by $24\,\mathrm{kPa}$ in pressure. With $g=10\,\mathrm{N/kg}$, what is their vertical separation?

::grafico{f="8*x" punti="2,16,pieno,A;5,40,pieno,B" nomi="h (m),Δp (kPa)" xmin=0 xmax=6 ymin=0 ymax=50 titolo="In oil the slope is 8 kPa per metre. From A to B pressure increases by 24 kPa"}

$$
\Delta h=\frac{24000\,\mathrm{Pa}}{800\cdot10\,\mathrm{Pa/m}}=3\,\mathrm m.
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Subtracting the pressures removes their common surface value. Each metre adds eight kilopascals, so twenty-four kilopascals correspond to three metres. The graph shows one pair of depths separated by this amount. The measurement does not specify the individual depths.

On the graph the points are at two and five metres. Another pair with the same vertical separation would give the same pressure difference, provided both points were in the uniform oil. Two measured absolute depths would also let us infer the surface pressure from the intercept if the absolute pressures were known.

</div>

## Exercise 2

In a sealed tank the absolute pressure is $128\,\mathrm{kPa}$ at $2.2\,\mathrm m$ below the water surface. With $\rho=1000\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}$ and $g=10\,\mathrm{N/kg}$, what is the surface pressure?

$$
p_0=128\,\mathrm{kPa}-\frac{1000\cdot10\cdot2.2}{1000}\,\mathrm{kPa}=106\,\mathrm{kPa}.
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Moving from the sensor up to the surface removes the contribution of the two-point-two-metre column. The surface is sealed under a gas, so its pressure follows from the data and may differ from the atmosphere. The result is below the deeper pressure as the hydrostatic law requires.

The column adds $22\,\mathrm{kPa}$. As a check, start from the calculated surface pressure and move down to the sensor again, obtaining $(106+22)\,\mathrm{kPa}=128\,\mathrm{kPa}$. To compare the gas in the tank with the surroundings, a separate reading of the atmosphere would be needed.

</div>

## Exercise 3

The absolute pressure graph against depth has slope $7.5\,\mathrm{kPa/m}$. If $g=10\,\mathrm{N/kg}$, what is the density of the uniform liquid?

$$
\rho=\frac{7500\,\mathrm{Pa/m}}{10\,\mathrm{N/kg}}=750\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}.
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The slope is the increase in pressure per metre, equal to $\rho g$. Convert kilopascals into pascals before dividing. A one-metre depth check recovers exactly the change shown by the graph.

Across two metres of depth the liquid pressure rises by $15\,\mathrm{kPa}$, whatever the surface pressure. If another run changed the pressure of the gas above the liquid while keeping density fixed, the line would move upwards or downwards with its gradient unchanged.

</div>
