## Exercise 1

A rectangle's base and height have relative uncertainties $7\%$ and $8\%$. Using conservative linear propagation, what is the relative uncertainty of its area?

$$
\varepsilon_A\simeq7\%+8\% = 15\%
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Area is the product of two positive dimensions. In the conservative first-order model, each relative deviation enters as an addend. The estimate leaves out the product of the two relative deviations. Exact bounds would instead use products of the corresponding length endpoints. Therefore $\varepsilon_A\simeq7\%+8\%=15\%$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Mass and volume have relative uncertainties $7\%$ and $9\%$. Under the conservative first-order rule for $\rho=m/V$, what is the relative uncertainty of density?

$$
\varepsilon_\rho\simeq7\%+9\% = 16\%
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The ratio grows if the numerator increases and the denominator decreases. In the conservative case the two deviations therefore act in the same direction on the result. The linear approximation adds the relative uncertainties. With large deviations, dividing the appropriate endpoints gives the bounds directly. Therefore $\varepsilon_\rho\simeq7\%+9\%=16\%$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A square's side has relative uncertainty $7\%$. What is the first-order relative uncertainty of its area?

::grafico{f="x^2" xmin=0.9 xmax=1.1 ymin=0.82 ymax=1.21 nomi="l/l₀,A/A₀" punti="0.93,0.8649,pieno;1,1,pieno;1.07,1.1449,pieno" segmenti="1,0.86,1,1;1.07,0.86,1.07,1.1449" titolo="A 7% relative increase in side length affects both factors in the square"}

$$
\varepsilon_A\simeq2\varepsilon_l = 14\%
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Area is the side squared. If the side is multiplied by $1+\varepsilon_l$, the area is multiplied by $(1+\varepsilon_l)^2$. Expansion gives two equal linear terms and a quadratic term. The first-order estimate keeps only twice the relative uncertainty in the side. The power rule gives $\varepsilon_A\simeq2\varepsilon_l=14\%$.

</div>
