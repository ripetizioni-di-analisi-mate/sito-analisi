## Exercise 1

A test spring follows $F=kx$ with $k=80\,\mathrm{N/m}$. What extension does a force $16\,\mathrm N$ produce?

::grafico{f="80*x" xmin=0 xmax=0.36 ymin=0 ymax=32 nomi="x (m),F (N)" punti="0.2,16,pieno;0.2,0,pieno;0,16,pieno" segmenti="0,16,0.2,16;0.2,0,0.2,16" etichette="0.209,15.4,P;0.19,-1.8,0.2" titolo="The point with vertical value 16 N has horizontal value 0.2 m"}

$$
\begin{aligned}
x &= \dfrac{F}{k}\\[6pt]
&= \dfrac{16}{80}\\[6pt]
&= 0.2\,\mathrm m
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The spring law is supplied for its proportional range, with coefficient force per unit length. Extension is the quantity multiplied by that coefficient under these conditions. Divide the force by the coefficient to isolate it; metres remain when the units cancel. Substituting the extension recovers the stated force. It follows that $x=\dfrac{F}{k}=\dfrac{16}{80}=0.2\,\mathrm m$.

The given force is one fifth of eighty newtons. Since the coefficient is eighty newtons per metre, that force produces one fifth of a metre. To read the graph, move horizontally from sixteen to the line, then down to the axis. Multiplying by eighty again checks the numerical result.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A fixed volume of material covers $24\,\mathrm{cm^2}$ at a uniform thickness of $3\,\mathrm{cm}$. The same base and an extra section are to be covered using all the material at a thickness of $2\,\mathrm{cm}$. What additional area can be covered?

$$
\begin{aligned}
V&=24\,\mathrm{cm^2}\cdot3\,\mathrm{cm}=72\,\mathrm{cm^3},\\[4pt]
A_2&=\dfrac{72\,\mathrm{cm^3}}{2\,\mathrm{cm}}=36\,\mathrm{cm^2},\\[4pt]
\Delta A&=36\,\mathrm{cm^2}-24\,\mathrm{cm^2}=12\,\mathrm{cm^2}.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Area times thickness is the volume, which stays constant. Reducing thickness therefore increases the covered area. First calculate the entire final area and then subtract the original covered section. Alternatively, lowering the thickness on the old area by one centimetre frees $24\,\mathrm{cm^3}$ of material. At a thickness of two centimetres, this covers twelve extra square centimetres. This second method checks the result without first calculating the total area. Thirty-six would answer a question about the final area, which includes the original twenty-four square centimetres.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A vessel contains $6\,\mathrm L$. A pump delivers $4\,\mathrm{L/min}$ for the first two minutes. It then stops until minute four and restarts at $3\,\mathrm{L/min}$. There are no leaks and the vessel holds at least $23\,\mathrm L$. At what time, measured from the start, does the volume reach $23\,\mathrm L$?

$$
\begin{aligned}
V(2)&=6\,\mathrm L+4\,\mathrm{L/min}\cdot2\,\mathrm{min}=14\,\mathrm L,\\[4pt]
V(4)&=14\,\mathrm L,\\[4pt]
t&=4\,\mathrm{min}+\dfrac{23\,\mathrm L-14\,\mathrm L}{3\,\mathrm{L/min}}
=7\,\mathrm{min}.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The flow rate changes, so one line does not represent the whole filling process. The first segment ends with fourteen litres. During the pause the volume stays at that level, so it has not yet reached the target of twenty-three litres. At the restart nine litres are still needed, which take three minutes at the rate of three. Those three minutes start at minute four on the same clock. The result is therefore minute seven. Counting only when the pump is on would instead give five minutes, omitting the intervening pause.

</div>
