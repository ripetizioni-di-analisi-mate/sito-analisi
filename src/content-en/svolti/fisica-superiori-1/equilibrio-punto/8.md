## Exercise 1

A vertical wall must support a $16\,\mathrm N$ weight by static friction. There are no other vertical forces and $\mu_s=0.5$. What minimum normal load makes equilibrium possible?

::grafico{f="0.5*x;16" punti="32,16,pieno,A" nomi="N (N),f (N)" xmin=0 xmax=42 ymin=0 ymax=24 titolo="A marks the smallest normal reaction that can balance the 16 N weight"}

$$
0.5N\geq16\,\mathrm N\quad\Longrightarrow\quad N_{\min}=32\,\mathrm N.
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Find the normal reaction that lets maximum friction reach the weight. Since the coefficient is one half, available friction is half the normal load. The threshold is therefore twice the weight, shown by the intersection in the graph.

For example, with $N=40\,\mathrm N$, the limit would rise to $20\,\mathrm N$. The weight would still require $16\,\mathrm N$ of upward friction. On the graph, compare the same weight level with a higher point on the limit line.

With a coefficient of $0.4$, a normal force of $40\,\mathrm N$ would be needed to support the same weight at the limit. It rises from $32\,\mathrm N$ to $40\,\mathrm N$ because the new contact supplies less available friction per newton of normal force.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A point is on a non-adhesive horizontal support, and the applied upward force exceeds its weight. If weight, that force, and the normal reaction are the only actions, can it stay at rest in contact?

$$
N=P-T<0.
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The vertical sum vanishes only if the normal reaction equals weight minus pull. A simple support can push upward. A negative value would represent a downward force, which is incompatible with the stated support.

Even if the normal force were set to its smallest physically admissible value, zero, the upward force would still exceed the weight. A positive normal force would increase that imbalance. Equilibrium would require a restraint that could exert a downward force.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A body is assumed at rest on a ramp with $P_\parallel=20\,\mathrm N$, $N=30\,\mathrm N$, and $\mu_s=0.5$. An applied $4\,\mathrm N$ force acts uphill. Is rest admissible?

$$
\begin{aligned}f_{\mathrm{rich}}&=(20-4)\,\mathrm N=16\,\mathrm N\\[6pt]f_{\max}&=0.5\cdot30\,\mathrm N=15\,\mathrm N.\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The uphill force cancels part of the downslope weight. This still leaves sixteen newtons for friction to balance. The available limit is $15\,\mathrm N$, one newton less than required. Even with this small difference, the stated exact values fail the inequality required for the static model.

With the same normal force, increasing the uphill pull from $4\,\mathrm N$ to $5\,\mathrm N$ would reduce the requirement to $15\,\mathrm N$. In that new case, the block would be exactly at the threshold.

</div>
