## Exercise 1

A jug contains $4\,\mathrm L$. What is the volume in cubic metres?

::grafico{f="0.001*x" xmin=-0.8 xmax=6 ymin=0 ymax=0.006 nomi="V (L),V (m³)" punti="4,0.004,pieno,A" segmenti="0,0.004,4,0.004;4,0,4,0.004" etichette="-0.7,0.00394,0.004" titolo="Point A shows that 4 litres correspond to 0.004 cubic metres"}

$$
V = 0.004\,\mathrm m^3
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

A litre equals one cubic decimetre. A decimetre is one tenth of a metre, so the product of the three dimensions gives $(10^{-1})^3=10^{-3}$. Each litre is counted as one thousandth of a cubic metre. The result should be far below one cubic metre, which has a capacity of a thousand litres. From $1\,\mathrm L=10^{-3}\,\mathrm m^3$ it follows that $V=0.004\,\mathrm m^3$.

A cubic decimetre contains $10\cdot10\cdot10=1000$ cubic centimetres. The same jug therefore holds $4000\,\mathrm{cm}^3$. Converting those cubic centimetres with $10^{-6}\,\mathrm m^3$ each gives the same result. The two paths also agree on the units, because a litre measures a volume and represents one thousand cubic centimetres.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A liquid dose has volume $7\,\mathrm{{mL}}$. How many litres does it contain?

$$
\begin{aligned}
V &= 7\cdot10^{-3}\\[6pt]
&= 0.007\,\mathrm L
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The prefix milli modifies the unit that follows, which here is the litre. One millilitre is one thousandth of a litre, so the number of millilitres is multiplied by $10^{-3}$. The new numerical value is less than one because the dose is only a fraction of a one-litre container's capacity. The dose has volume $V=7\cdot10^{-3}=0.007\,\mathrm L$.

On a millilitre scale a litre corresponds to $1000$ units. For example, removing the dose from an initially full one-litre bottle leaves $1000-7=993\,\mathrm{mL}$. In litres, subtract $0.007$ from $1$ to get $0.993$. The two balances confirm that both expressions describe the same dose.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A container of constant cross-sectional area $180\,\mathrm{cm}^2$ has a mark for $0.90\,\mathrm L$. Its current level is $4.6\,\mathrm{cm}$ above the bottom. How many millilitres are needed to reach the mark? Treat the stated nominal values as exact.

$$
\begin{aligned}
V_{\mathrm{letto}}&=(180\,\mathrm{cm}^2)(4.6\,\mathrm{cm})
                  =828\,\mathrm{cm}^3,\\
V_{\mathrm{tacca}}&=0.90\,\mathrm L=900\,\mathrm{cm}^3,\\
V_{\mathrm{mancante}}&=900\,\mathrm{cm}^3-828\,\mathrm{cm}^3
                       =72\,\mathrm{cm}^3=72\,\mathrm{mL}.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The level is a length measured from the bottom. Its horizontal cross-section is the same at every height, so the liquid occupies a volume equal to the cross-sectional area times the height. Multiplying the measurements already in centimetres and square centimetres gives cubic centimetres directly. The mark gives the capacity in litres and must be converted to the same unit before subtracting.

Check the result by first finding the height of the mark. Dividing $900\,\mathrm{cm}^3$ by $180\,\mathrm{cm}^2$ gives $5\,\mathrm{cm}$. There are $0.4\,\mathrm{cm}$ of height still to fill. That layer has volume $180\cdot0.4=72\,\mathrm{cm}^3$, confirming the $72\,\mathrm{mL}$.

</div>
