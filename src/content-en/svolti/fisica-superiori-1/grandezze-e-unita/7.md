## Exercise 1

A calculation models machining time as $t=a\ell^2+b\ell$, with $\ell$ in metres and $t$ in seconds. What units must the coefficients have? Then calculate $t$ for $\ell=2\,\mathrm m$ when $a$ and $b$ are $0.40$ and $1.0$, respectively, in the units you have found.

$$
[a]=\mathrm{s/m^2},\qquad [b]=\mathrm{s/m}.
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
t&=(0.40\,\mathrm{s/m^2})(2\,\mathrm m)^2
      +(1.0\,\mathrm{s/m})(2\,\mathrm m)\\
 &=1.6\,\mathrm s+2.0\,\mathrm s
 =3.6\,\mathrm s.
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Both terms of the sum have to be durations. The coefficient $a$ multiplies a length squared, so its seconds must be accompanied by the reciprocal of two metre factors. The coefficient $b$ multiplies a single length and must cancel only one metre. The square brackets above denote the units of the coefficients. With the units included, the separate contributions can be added because they are both in seconds.

Doubling the machining length multiplies the first contribution by four and the second by two. The total duration can therefore change by a factor other than two, even though each term still has the same time unit.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A rectangle has base $8\,\mathrm m$. Which expression has the dimensions of an area?

- $(8\,\mathrm m)(2\,\mathrm m)$
- $(8\,\mathrm m)+(2\,\mathrm m)$
- $(8\,\mathrm m)\div(2\,\mathrm m)$
- $(8\,\mathrm m)(2\,\mathrm s)$

$$
(8\,\mathrm m)(2\,\mathrm m)=16\,\mathrm m^2
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The area of a rectangle requires a base and a height, both lengths. Multiplying two factors measured in metres gives square metres. Adding two lengths gives metres, while taking their ratio cancels the length units. Multiplying a length by a time would give a quantity with dimensions different from area. The product of the units is $\mathrm m\cdot\mathrm m=\mathrm m^2$.

Checking the units tests the form of the expression. A numerical coefficient in front of the length product would still leave square metres, so geometry is needed to establish the value. For example, a rectangle of height $2\,\mathrm m$ and base $8\,\mathrm m$ covers $16$ squares of side one metre. Counting the squares also checks the coefficient.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A tray of constant horizontal cross-sectional area $A=90\,\mathrm{cm}^2$ holds $V=360\,\mathrm{cm}^3$ of water. One worksheet proposes $h=\dfrac{V}{A^2}$ for the depth and another proposes $h=\dfrac{2V}{A}$. Discard the dimensionally inconsistent expression. Then check the remaining one geometrically.

::grafico{assi=0 xmin=-1 xmax=15 ymin=-1 ymax=6 linee="2,0 11,0 11,4 2,4 2,0;2,4 4,5 13,5 11,4;11,0 13,1 13,5" segmenti="11.4,0,12.0,0;11.4,4,12.0,4" etichette="6.4,2.0,V = 360 cm³;6.7,-.50,A = 90 cm²;14,2.3,h" titolo="The layer has constant cross-section. Its height is perpendicular to the base"}

$$
\left[\dfrac{V}{A^2}\right]=\dfrac{\mathrm{cm}^3}{\mathrm{cm}^4}=\dfrac{1}{\mathrm{cm}},
\qquad
\left[\dfrac{2V}{A}\right]=\dfrac{\mathrm{cm}^3}{\mathrm{cm}^2}=\mathrm{cm}.
$$

$$
h=\dfrac{V}{A}=\dfrac{360\,\mathrm{cm}^3}{90\,\mathrm{cm}^2}=4\,\mathrm{cm}.
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Depth is a length. In the first worksheet, squaring the area unit gives four centimetre factors, one more than the three in the numerator. This leaves a centimetre in the denominator and rules out the formula. In the second formula the factor $2$ is dimensionless and the result comes out in centimetres, so the dimensional check alone allows the expression.

To check the factor, use the geometric volume $V=Ah$ of a layer with constant cross-section. The second worksheet would give $8\,\mathrm{cm}$. With an area of $90\,\mathrm{cm}^2$, that depth would occupy $720\,\mathrm{cm}^3$, twice the stated volume. The depth of $4\,\mathrm{cm}$ is the one that gives the required volume.

</div>
