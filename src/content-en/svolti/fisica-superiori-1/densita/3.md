## Exercise 1

A graduated cylinder goes from $37$ to $49\,\mathrm{mL}$ when a nonporous solid of mass $36\,\mathrm{g}$ is fully submerged. What is the solid's density?

::grafico{assi=0 uguale=1 xmin=-1 xmax=8 ymin=-0.5 ymax=6.4 linee="0,5.7 0,0 2,0 2,5.7;5,5.7 5,0 7,0 7,5.7" poligoni="0,0 2,0 2,3.7 0,3.7;5,0 7,0 7,4.9 5,4.9;5.4,0.8 6.6,0.8 6.6,2.8 5.4,2.8" etichette="1,4.03,37 mL;6,5.25,49 mL" titolo="The two cylinder readings differ by 12 mL"}

$$
\begin{aligned}
\rho &= \dfrac{36}{12}\\[6pt]
&= 3\,\mathrm{g/cm^3}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The final reading includes the original liquid volume and the space occupied by the solid. Subtracting the first reading removes the first contribution and gives the whole solid's volume because it is fully submerged. The displacement in millilitres equals that in cubic centimetres numerically. Divide the solid's mass by the displaced volume. The displaced volume is $V=49-37=12\,\mathrm{cm}^3$ and the density is $\rho=\dfrac{36}{12}=3\,\mathrm{g/cm^3}$.

The calculation depends on both readings. If a millilitre spilled during measurement, the final reading would drop to $48\,\mathrm{mL}$ with the same object. Assigning the $11\,\mathrm{mL}$ difference to the solid would give $\dfrac{36}{11}$, above three. This comparison shows why any loss of liquid also needs checking.

</div>

## Exercise 2

An empty container has mass $37\,\mathrm{g}$. With $17\,\mathrm{cm}^3$ of liquid inside, its total mass is $71\,\mathrm{g}$. What is the liquid's density?

$$
\begin{aligned}
\rho &= \dfrac{34}{17}\\[6pt]
&= 2\,\mathrm{g/cm^3}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The scale reads the container and liquid together. The empty container already contributes part of the total, called the tare, which must be subtracted before finding the ratio. The stated volume belongs to the liquid alone, so use the liquid mass alone. Adding the tare to the resulting mass checks the procedure. The liquid mass is $71-37=34\,\mathrm{g}$, so $\rho=\dfrac{34}{17}=2\,\mathrm{g/cm^3}$.

The check gives $37+34=71\,\mathrm{g}$, the original reading for the filled container. Dividing that reading directly by $17\,\mathrm{cm^3}$ would add the tare per unit volume to the required value. The difference would be $\dfrac{37}{17}\,\mathrm{g/cm^3}$, a contribution from the container that does not affect the liquid's own density.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A graduated cylinder reads $48\,\mathrm{mL}$ with liquid alone, $51\,\mathrm{mL}$ with an immersed hook, and $62\,\mathrm{mL}$ with the same part of the hook and a fully submerged solid. The solid is impermeable, has no attached bubbles, and has mass $22\,\mathrm{g}$. Find its density and compare it with the value obtained by using the first reading as the reference.

$$
\begin{aligned}
    V &= (62-51)\,\mathrm{cm^3}=11\,\mathrm{cm^3},\\[5pt]
    \rho &= \frac{22}{11}\,\mathrm{g/cm^3}=2\,\mathrm{g/cm^3},\\[5pt]
    \rho_{\mathrm{app}} &= \frac{22}{62-48}\,\mathrm{g/cm^3}
        =\frac{11}{7}\,\mathrm{g/cm^3}.
    \end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The last two readings include the same immersed part of the hook. Subtracting them cancels the hook's contribution, leaving the volume that the solid excludes from the liquid. This volume matches the $22\,\mathrm{g}$ mass, which belongs to the solid alone. Using the first reading as the reference would include the hook's $3\,\mathrm{mL}$ in the object's volume, giving an apparent density of about $1.57\,\mathrm{g/cm^3}$. The direction of the discrepancy agrees with the model. At fixed mass, increasing the denominator lowers the ratio. The comparison shows why the hook must be kept at the same depth for the two readings used.

</div>
