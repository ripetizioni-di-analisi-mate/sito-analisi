## Exercise 1

A graduated cylinder goes from $37$ to $49\,\mathrm{mL}$ when a nonporous solid of mass $36\,\mathrm g$ is fully submerged. What is the solid's density?

::grafico{assi=0 uguale=1 xmin=-1 xmax=8 ymin=-0.5 ymax=6.4 linee="0,5.7 0,0 2,0 2,5.7;5,5.7 5,0 7,0 7,5.7" poligoni="0,0 2,0 2,3.7 0,3.7;5,0 7,0 7,4.9 5,4.9;5.55,0.55 6.45,0.55 6.45,2.55 5.55,2.55" etichette="1,4.03,37 mL;6,5.25,49 mL" titolo="The two cylinder readings differ by 12 mL"}

$$
\begin{aligned}
\rho &= 36/12\\[6pt]
&= 3\,\mathrm{g/cm^3}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The final reading includes the original liquid volume and the space occupied by the solid. Subtracting the first reading removes the first contribution and gives the whole solid's volume because it is fully submerged. The displacement in millilitres equals that in cubic centimetres numerically. Divide the solid's mass by the displaced volume. The displaced volume is $V=49-37=12\,\mathrm{cm}^3$ and the density is $\rho=36/12=3\,\mathrm{g/cm^3}$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

An empty container has mass $37\,\mathrm g$. With $17\,\mathrm{cm}^3$ of liquid inside, its total mass is $71\,\mathrm g$. What is the liquid's density?

$$
\begin{aligned}
\rho &= \dfrac{34}{17}\\[6pt]
&= 2\,\mathrm{g/cm^3}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The scale reads the container and liquid together. The empty container already contributes part of the total, called the tare, which must be subtracted before finding the ratio. The stated volume belongs to the liquid alone, so use the liquid mass alone. Adding the tare to the resulting mass checks the procedure. The liquid mass is $71-37=34\,\mathrm g$, so $\rho=\dfrac{34}{17}=2\,\mathrm{g/cm^3}$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A sample has a scale reading of $120\,\mathrm g$ and density $8\,\mathrm{g/cm^3}$. What is its volume?

$$
\begin{aligned}
V &= \dfrac{m}{\rho}\\[6pt]
&= \dfrac{120}{8}\\[6pt]
&= 15\,\mathrm{cm}^3
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The scale gives total mass and density tells how many grams belong to a cubic centimetre. Dividing mass by that value gives the number of cubic centimetres in the sample. Multiplying the resulting volume by the mass of each unit volume checks the result. Thus $V=\dfrac{m}{\rho}=\dfrac{120}{8}=15\,\mathrm{cm}^3$.

</div>
