## Exercise 1

Mix $70\,\mathrm{cm}^3$ of liquid with density $1\,\mathrm{g/cm^3}$ and $10\,\mathrm{cm}^3$ of liquid with density $2\,\mathrm{g/cm^3}$. Assuming additive volumes, what is the mixture density?

::grafico{assi=0 xmin=-0.5 xmax=85 ymin=-0.8 ymax=1.9 linee="0,0 70,0;70,0 80,0" punti="0,0,pieno;70,0,pieno;80,0,pieno" etichette="31,0.35,70 cm³;75,0.65,10 cm³" titolo="The component at density 1 g/cm³ occupies most of the volume"}

$$
\begin{aligned}
\rho &= \dfrac{70+20}{70+10}\\[6pt]
&= 1.125\,\mathrm{g/cm^3}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Each liquid's mass is the product of its density and volume. With no loss, add the two masses. The additivity assumption allows the volumes to be added for the denominator. The first liquid occupies more space, so the ratio should be closer to its density. Therefore $\rho=\dfrac{70+20}{70+10}=1.125\,\mathrm{g/cm^3}$.

If the entire $80\,\mathrm{cm^3}$ consisted of the first liquid, its mass would be $80\,\mathrm{g}$. Replacing $10\,\mathrm{cm^3}$ with the second liquid adds one gram per cubic centimetre, or $10\,\mathrm{g}$. This recovers the total mass of $90\,\mathrm{g}$ without repeating the two products.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Prepare $150\,\mathrm{cm^3}$ of a mixture of density $2.4\,\mathrm{g/cm^3}$ using liquids of density $1.6$ and $3.6\,\mathrm{g/cm^3}$. Assume mass is conserved and volumes are additive. Find the volumes to mix.

$$
\begin{aligned}
    m_{\mathrm{mix}} &= 2.4\cdot150\,\mathrm{g}=360\,\mathrm{g},\\[5pt]
    1.6(150-V_2)+3.6V_2&=360,\\[5pt]
    2V_2&=360-240=120,\\[5pt]
    V_2&=60\,\mathrm{cm^3},\qquad V_1=90\,\mathrm{cm^3}.
    \end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Let $V_2$ be the numerical volume of the denser liquid in cubic centimetres. The total must be 150, so the first volume is $150-V_2$. Substituting these volumes into the two mass contributions gives the equation. The same relationship can be read as an exchange of equal volumes. Starting from $150\,\mathrm{cm^3}$ of the less dense liquid gives $240\,\mathrm{g}$. Each cubic centimetre replaced adds $2\,\mathrm{g}$. Adding 120 grams takes $60\,\mathrm{cm^3}$ of the denser liquid. The two volumes found have mass $144+216=360\,\mathrm{g}$, as required.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Mix $80\,\mathrm{mL}$ of a liquid of density $1.05\,\mathrm{g/mL}$ and $20\,\mathrm{mL}$ of a liquid of density $1.5\,\mathrm{g/mL}$. Mass is conserved, and the measured mixture volume is $96\,\mathrm{mL}$. Find the actual density and its percentage increase over the density predicted using additive volumes.

$$
\begin{aligned}
    m &= (1.05\cdot80+1.5\cdot20)\,\mathrm{g}
       =114\,\mathrm{g},\\[5pt]
    \rho &= \frac{114}{96}\,\mathrm{g/mL}=1.1875\,\mathrm{g/mL},\\[5pt]
    \rho_{\mathrm{add}} &= \frac{114}{100}\,\mathrm{g/mL}=1.14\,\mathrm{g/mL},\\[5pt]
    \frac{\rho-\rho_{\mathrm{add}}}{\rho_{\mathrm{add}}}
         &=\frac{100}{96}-1=\frac{1}{24}\simeq 4.17\,\%.
    \end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Conservation of mass allows the contributions of $84$ and $30\,\mathrm{g}$ to be added in both calculations. The difference is in the volume. The measurement gives $96\,\mathrm{mL}$, while the additive model gives $100\,\mathrm{mL}$. The actual density is therefore higher. The reference for the percentage change is the additive density, so divide the difference by that density. The ratio can also be found before calculating either absolute value. The same mass appears in both ratios and cancels, leaving the inverse ratio of the volumes. Volume contracts by $4\,\%$, while density rises by about $4.17\,\%$.

</div>
