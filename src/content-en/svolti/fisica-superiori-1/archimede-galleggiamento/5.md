## Exercise 1

A sealed float of mass $20\,\mathrm{kg}$ has outer volume $60\,\mathrm L$. For margin, $10\,\mathrm L$ must remain above water. In water of density $1\,\mathrm{kg/L}$, what maximum payload can be carried above water?

::grafico{f="x-20" punti="50,30,pieno,A;60,40,pieno,B" nomi="Vᵢ (L),m_c (kg)" xmin=20 xmax=65 ymin=0 ymax=49 titolo="A at 50 L leaves 10 L above water. B is the complete-immersion limit"}

$$
m_{c,\max}=(60-10)\,\mathrm L\cdot1\,\mathrm{kg/L}-20\,\mathrm{kg}=30\,\mathrm{kg}.
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The requirement leaves at most fifty litres submerged, supporting fifty kilograms total. Twenty kilograms are already used by the empty float. Thirty kilograms remain for payload. Ignoring the margin gives forty kilograms at full immersion, which fails the requirement.

For example, a $34\,\mathrm{kg}$ load gives a total mass of $54\,\mathrm{kg}$ with the float. It would displace $54\,\mathrm L$ and could still float, but only $6\,\mathrm L$ would remain above water. Having sufficient buoyancy and satisfying the design margin are two distinct checks.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A freely floating platform has vertical sides and horizontal area $0.5\,\mathrm m^2$. It receives $15\,\mathrm{kg}$ of dry load. In water of density $1000\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}$, how many centimetres does it go down?

$$
\begin{aligned}\Delta V_i&=\frac{15}{1000}\,\mathrm m^3=0.015\,\mathrm m^3\\[6pt]\Delta h&=\frac{0.015}{0.5}\,\mathrm m=3\,\mathrm{cm}.\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The added load requires fifteen more kilograms of displaced water. The mass determines the extra volume. With vertical sides the added volume is cross-section times the depth increase. The result in metres is three hundredths, equivalent to three centimetres.

Checking in reverse, a layer $0.03\,\mathrm m$ high over an area of $0.5\,\mathrm m^2$ has volume $0.015\,\mathrm m^3$, or fifteen litres. In water it supplies exactly fifteen kilograms of additional displaced mass. For a hull with sloping sides, the area at different heights would also be needed.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A float of mass $18\,\mathrm{kg}$ has maximum usable immersed volume $40\,\mathrm L$. In a liquid of density $0.9\,\mathrm{kg/L}$, how much dry payload is available?

$$
m_c=40\,\mathrm L\cdot0.9\,\mathrm{kg/L}-18\,\mathrm{kg}=18\,\mathrm{kg}.
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The usable volume displaces thirty-six kilograms of the given liquid. That mass is total capacity, including the float. Subtract the unloaded float mass to get eighteen kilograms available. The same volume of denser water would allow a larger payload.

In water of density $1\,\mathrm{kg/L}$, the same permitted volume supports $40\,\mathrm{kg}$, of which $22\,\mathrm{kg}$ is available for the load. The difference of $4\,\mathrm{kg}$ follows by multiplying the volume of $40\,\mathrm L$ by the density difference of $0.1\,\mathrm{kg/L}$.

</div>
