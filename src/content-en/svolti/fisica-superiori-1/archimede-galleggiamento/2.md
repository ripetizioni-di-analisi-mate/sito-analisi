## Exercise 1

Neglecting air buoyancy, a block weighs $90\,\mathrm N$. It hangs at rest, displacing $4\,\mathrm L$ of water of density $1\,\mathrm{kg/L}$. With $g=10\,\mathrm{N/kg}$, what does the spring scale read?

::grafico{f="90-10*x" punti="0,90,pieno,A;4,50,pieno,B" nomi="Vᵢ (L),T (N)" xmin=0 xmax=5 ymin=0 ymax=100 titolo="From the air weighing A to the immersed weighing B, increased buoyancy reduces tension by 40 N"}

$$
\begin{aligned}F_A&=(1\,\mathrm{kg/L})(4\,\mathrm L)(10\,\mathrm{N/kg})=40\,\mathrm N\\[6pt]T&=(90-40)\,\mathrm N=50\,\mathrm N.\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Four litres of water have a mass of four kilograms, so liquid supplies forty newtons of support. The upper string carries only the remaining weight. Forty newtons of buoyancy plus fifty of tension checks the initial weight. The scale reading falls while gravitational weight remains ninety newtons.

If the same body could submerge another litre while keeping the string taut, the spring scale would read $40\,\mathrm N$. On the graph this is a step to the right along a line of slope $-10\,\mathrm{N/L}$. After full submersion, lowering the body farther preserves its displaced volume and the reading stops decreasing.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A solid weighs $72\,\mathrm N$ in air with air buoyancy neglected. Fully immersed and hanging at rest in water of density $1000\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}$, its reading is $48\,\mathrm N$. What is its mean density?

$$
\rho_c=1000\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}\frac{72}{72-48}=3000\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}.
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The reading difference is the weight of displaced water. The displaced volume equals the solid's external volume, so volume and field cancel in the ratio of the weights. The solid is three times as dense as water, consistent with requiring suspension from above.

The twenty-four newtons lost are a third of the weight. Water displaced by that volume therefore weighs a third as much as the solid. If only half the volume were submerged, the drop would be twelve newtons. Applying the ratio as if the whole volume were submerged would double the estimated density.

</div>

## Exercise 3

The same rigid solid is fully immersed in oil and water in turn. Their densities are $0.8$ and $1\,\mathrm{kg/L}$. Upward tensions are $42$ and $36\,\mathrm N$ respectively. With $g=10\,\mathrm{N/kg}$, what is the solid's volume?

$$
V=\frac{42-36}{(1-0.8)10}\,\mathrm L=3\,\mathrm L.
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Weight is the same in both tests. Subtracting tensions gives the buoyancy increase in water relative to oil. Each litre contributes two more newtons of displaced-fluid weight. A difference of six newtons thus identifies three litres.

Check the volume by reconstructing the weight. For $3\,\mathrm L$ the oil gives $24\,\mathrm N$ of buoyancy, which adds to $42\,\mathrm N$ of tension to give $66\,\mathrm N$. In water the buoyancy is $30\,\mathrm N$ and the tension is $36\,\mathrm N$. The two sums give the same weight.

</div>
