## Exercise 1

A sample of mass $3.5\,\mathrm{kg}$ is in a uniform field of $8\,\mathrm{N/kg}$. What is its weight?

::grafico{f="8*x" punti="3.5,28,pieno,A" nomi="m (kg),P (N)" xmin=0 xmax=5 ymin=0 ymax=43 titolo="Point A represents the 3.5 kg sample in the field of 8 N/kg"}

$$
P=mg=(3.5\,\mathrm{kg})(8\,\mathrm{N/kg})=28\,\mathrm N.
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The field specifies how many newtons of gravitational force act on each kilogram. Since the sample has a mass of $3.5$ times one kilogram, multiply $8$ by $3.5$. The kilogram cancels and the newton remains. The result is the force magnitude, and its direction is vertically downward.

An independent check is to split the sample into portions of $3\,\mathrm{kg}$ and $0.5\,\mathrm{kg}$ in the same field. Their gravitational forces are $24\,\mathrm N$ and $4\,\mathrm N$. Both point in the same direction, so their sum gives $28\,\mathrm N$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A crate weighs $72\,\mathrm N$ where $g=9\,\mathrm{N/kg}$. What weight does an ideal force meter indicate after $2\,\mathrm{kg}$ is added at the same location?

$$
P_{\mathrm{tot}}=72\,\mathrm N+(2\,\mathrm{kg})(9\,\mathrm{N/kg})=90\,\mathrm N.
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Add the two weights because both are directed vertically downward and act on the crate-plus-contents system. The added quantity is a mass. Convert it to a weight of $18\,\mathrm N$ before adding. The initial weight corresponds to $8\,\mathrm{kg}$, and the total mass of $10\,\mathrm{kg}$ provides a second check.

The added mass is one quarter of the original mass. The weight therefore also grows by a quarter in the same field, giving a new reading of $\dfrac54\cdot72\,\mathrm N=90\,\mathrm N$. This comparison assumes the scale supports the entire box at rest.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A spring extends by $3\,\mathrm{cm}$ while holding a $0.6\,\mathrm{kg}$ sample at rest. If $k=120\,\mathrm{N/m}$, what is the local gravitational field?

$$
\begin{aligned}P&=120\cdot0.03\,\mathrm N=3.6\,\mathrm N\\[6pt]g&=\frac{3.6\,\mathrm N}{0.6\,\mathrm{kg}}=6\,\mathrm{N/kg}.\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The spring holds the sample at rest, so its restoring pull balances the weight. Convert the measurement in centimetres to metres before using $k$. The stretched spring pulls upward while weight acts downward. Dividing their common magnitude by the mass gives the local value of $g$.

The spring force is $120\cdot0.03\,\mathrm N=3.6\,\mathrm N$. Multiplying the calculated field by the mass gives back $0.6\,\mathrm{kg}\cdot6\,\mathrm{N/kg}=3.6\,\mathrm N$. Equal magnitudes and opposite directions give a zero vertical resultant.

</div>
