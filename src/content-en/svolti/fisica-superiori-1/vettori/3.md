## Exercise 1

A vector has perpendicular components $21$ and $28\,\mathrm N$. What is its magnitude?

::grafico{uguale=1 xmin=-3 xmax=27 ymin=-3 ymax=34 vettori="0,0,21,28,u" linee="0,0 21,0 21,28" punti="21,28,pieno,P" nomi="x (N),y (N)" titolo="Magnitude is the hypotenuse of the triangle with 21 N and 28 N legs"}

$$
\begin{aligned}
|u| &= \sqrt{(21)^2+(28)^2}\\[6pt]
&= 35\,\mathrm N
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The components are the legs of a right triangle. Magnitude is the hypotenuse, so add the squares of the two legs. The positive root turns squared magnitude into a length. The result exceeds either leg but is smaller than their sum because the direct path is shorter. By Pythagoras, $|u|=\sqrt{(21)^2+(28)^2}=35\,\mathrm N$.

You can also recognise the triangle with legs in the ratio three to four. Dividing both legs by seven gives lengths three and four, with hypotenuse five. Restoring the original scale gives $7\cdot5=35$. The diagram uses perpendicular axes, so this comparison preserves the triangle's proportions.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A vector has magnitude $35\,\mathrm m$ and horizontal component $21\,\mathrm m$. What is the magnitude of its vertical component?

$$
\begin{aligned}
|u_y| &= \sqrt{(35)^2-(21)^2}\\[6pt]
&= 28\,\mathrm m
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The squared magnitude is the sum of the squared components. Subtracting the squared horizontal component isolates the vertical square. Its positive square root gives the requested magnitude. The actual vertical sign would require knowing whether the arrow points up or down. Therefore $|u_y|=\sqrt{(35)^2-(21)^2}=28\,\mathrm m$.

The intermediate difference is $1225-441=784\,\mathrm{m^2}$. Substituting the result gives $21^2+28^2=1225$ again and hence the magnitude of $35\,\mathrm m$. Since squaring removes the sign, a question about the signed component would still have two possible answers, corresponding to the two symmetric tip heights.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Two vectors have zero sum and the first is $u=(7;9)\,\mathrm N$. What is the second?

- $(-7;-9)\,\mathrm N$
- $(7;9)\,\mathrm N$
- $(7;-9)\,\mathrm N$
- $(-7;9)\,\mathrm N$

$$
\begin{aligned}
v &= -u\\[6pt]
&= (-7;-9)\,\mathrm N
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

A resultant is zero when both components vanish. To satisfy the horizontal condition, take the opposite of the first vector's horizontal component. For the vertical condition do the same with its other component. The new vector is the opposite, of equal length and pointing the other way along the same line. The second vector must be $v=-u=(-7;-9)\,\mathrm N$.

Check the proposed pair by calculating $(7-7;9-9)=(0;0)$. The first and second components cannot be swapped, because each must cancel the contribution on the same axis. Even choosing a vector of equal magnitude perpendicular to the first would leave at least one sum different from zero.

</div>
