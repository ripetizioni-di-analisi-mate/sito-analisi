## Exercise 1

Masses of $1$ and $3\,\mathrm{kg}$ are attached to a light rod at $x=0$ and $x=4\,\mathrm m$ respectively. What is the centre of gravity coordinate?

::grafico{assi=0 uguale=1 linee="0,0 4,0" punti="0,0,pieno,1 kg;4,0,pieno,3 kg" etichette="2,-0.45,4 m" xmin=-1 xmax=5 ymin=-1 ymax=1.25 titolo="The masses are at the ends of the light rod"}

$$
x_G=\frac{1\cdot0+3\cdot4}{1+3}\,\mathrm m=3\,\mathrm m.
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The mass at the origin contributes to total mass but has zero arm. The right mass contributes twelve kilogram-metres. Dividing by four kilograms gives a position between the points, closer to the heavier mass.

To check the coordinate, measure the lever arms from $x=3\,\mathrm m$. The mass–arm product is $1\cdot3=3\,\mathrm{kg\,m}$ on the left and $3\cdot1=3\,\mathrm{kg\,m}$ on the right. Multiplying both by $g$ gives moments of equal magnitude and opposite directions.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A uniform rod $4\,\mathrm m$ long has mass $6\,\mathrm{kg}$. A mass of $2\,\mathrm{kg}$ is fixed to its right end. How far is the new centre of gravity from the left end?

$$
x_G=\frac{6\cdot2+2\cdot4}{6+2}\,\mathrm m=2.5\,\mathrm m.
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

For the uniform rod's moment, place its equivalent weight at the two-metre centre. The added mass acts at four metres. The weighted mean is between the two points and just half a metre from the heavier rod's centre.

The shift can also be measured from the original centre. The added mass accounts for $\dfrac{2}{8}$ of the final mass and lies $2\,\mathrm m$ to the right of the original centre. The new centre therefore moves $\dfrac{2}{8}\cdot2\,\mathrm m=0.50\,\mathrm m$, while the rod still has zero moment about its original centre.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A light bar has a $5\,\mathrm{kg}$ mass at $x=0$ and an unknown mass at $x=6\,\mathrm m$. What mass gives $x_G=2\,\mathrm m$?

$$
m(6-2)=5\cdot2\quad\Longrightarrow\quad m=2.5\,\mathrm{kg}.
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Take moments about the required centre of gravity. The known mass has a two-metre arm and the unknown mass a four-metre arm. The unknown mass is half the known one because its arm is twice as long. The common gravitational field factor cancels.

Substituting the new mass into the weighted mean gives $\dfrac{5\cdot0+2.5\cdot6}{5+2.5}\,\mathrm m=\dfrac{15}{7.5}\,\mathrm m=2\,\mathrm m$. The requested point is nearer the mass already at $x=0$, consistent with that mass being larger.

</div>
