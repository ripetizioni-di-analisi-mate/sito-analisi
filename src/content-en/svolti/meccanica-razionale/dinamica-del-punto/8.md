## Exercise 1

A particle of mass $m$ is subject to the attractive central force
$$
\vec{F} = -\frac{\kappa}{r^2}\,\hat{u}_r ,
\qquad
\kappa > 0 ,
$$
where $r$ is the distance from the center $O$ and $\hat{u}_r$ the outward radial unit vector. The particle travels along the circular orbit of center $O$ and radius $r_0$ shown in the figure. Determine the modulus of the velocity, the areal constant, the areal velocity and the period of revolution, and verify Kepler's third law.

::grafico{curve="1.5*cos(t),1.5*sin(t),0,6.283" legenda=0 uguale=1 vettori="1.06,1.06,0.42,1.7,v;1.06,1.06,0.18,0.18,F" punti="0,0,pieno,O;1.06,1.06,pieno,P" xmin=-2 xmax=2.5 ymin=-2 ymax=2.3 titolo="Circular orbit of radius $r_0$: the force is directed towards $O$ and the velocity is tangent to the orbit"}

$$
\text{circular orbit}
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
\dot{r} = 0 ,
\qquad
a_r = -r_0\,\dot{\vartheta}^2 = -\frac{v^2}{r_0}
$$
$$
m\,\frac{v^2}{r_0} = \frac{\kappa}{r_0^2}
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
v = \sqrt{\frac{\kappa}{m\,r_0}}
$$
$$
c = r_0^2\,\dot{\vartheta} = r_0\,v = \sqrt{\frac{\kappa\,r_0}{m}} ,
\qquad
\dot{A} = \frac{c}{2} = \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{\kappa\,r_0}{m}}
$$
$$
T = \frac{2\pi\,r_0}{v} = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{m\,r_0^3}{\kappa}}
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
\frac{T^2}{r_0^3} = \frac{4\pi^2\,m}{\kappa}
$$

Check with Binet's formula. For the circular orbit $u = \dfrac{1}{r_0}$ is constant, hence $\dfrac{d^2u}{d\vartheta^2} = 0$ and
$$
f = -m\,c^2\,u^2\left( \frac{d^2u}{d\vartheta^2} + u \right) = -\frac{m\,c^2}{r_0^3}
= -\frac{m}{r_0^3}\cdot\frac{\kappa\,r_0}{m} = -\frac{\kappa}{r_0^2} ,
$$
which is exactly the assigned force: the value found for $c$ is therefore correct.

<div class="spiegazione">

On the circular orbit the distance from the center is constant, hence $\dot{r} = 0$ and $\ddot{r} = 0$; the radial component of the acceleration reduces to $-r_0\,\dot{\vartheta}^2$, that is, to the centripetal acceleration $-\dfrac{v^2}{r_0}$. Projecting the fundamental equation onto the radial direction one obtains the equality between the centripetal force and the assigned force, whence the velocity.

The areal constant is $c = r^2\dot{\vartheta}$, which on the circular orbit equals $r_0\,v$; the areal velocity is half of $c$, as the theorem on the angular momentum in central motions establishes. The motion is uniform, because $v$ is constant, therefore the period is the ratio of the length of the circle to the velocity.

Squaring the period and dividing by $r_0^3$ one obtains a quantity that depends only on $m$ and on $\kappa$, that is, the same for all the circular orbits about the same center: this is **Kepler's third law**. In the gravitational case $\kappa = G M m$, and the ratio $\dfrac{T^2}{r_0^3} = \dfrac{4\pi^2}{G M}$ does not even depend on the mass of the particle.

The check uses Binet's formula, which links the orbit to the force without passing through the radial equation. Starting from the circular orbit and from the areal constant found, the initial force is recovered exactly, therefore the two results are consistent.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A particle of mass $m$ moves with central motion of center $O$ and travels along the elliptic orbit of polar equation
$$
r = \frac{p}{1 + e\cos\vartheta} ,
\qquad
p > 0 ,
\qquad
0 < e < 1 ,
$$
represented in the figure, with areal constant $c$. Determine the force that produces this orbit, the minimum and maximum distances from the center and the ratio between the velocity at the nearest point and the velocity at the farthest point.

::grafico{curve="cos(t)/(1+0.6*cos(t)),sin(t)/(1+0.6*cos(t)),0,6.283" legenda=0 uguale=1 punti="0,0,pieno,O;0.625,0,pieno,A;-2.5,0,pieno,B" xmin=-3 xmax=1.4 ymin=-1.7 ymax=1.7 titolo="Elliptic orbit with $e = 0.6$ and focus at the center of force $O$: at $A$ the distance is minimum, at $B$ maximum"}

$$
u = \frac{1}{r} = \frac{1 + e\cos\vartheta}{p}
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
\frac{d^2u}{d\vartheta^2} = -\frac{e\cos\vartheta}{p} ,
\qquad
\frac{d^2u}{d\vartheta^2} + u = \frac{1}{p}
$$
$$
f = -m\,c^2\,u^2\left( \frac{d^2u}{d\vartheta^2} + u \right) = -\frac{m\,c^2}{p}\cdot\frac{1}{r^2}
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
\vec{F} = -\frac{\kappa}{r^2}\,\hat{u}_r ,
\qquad
\kappa = \frac{m\,c^2}{p}
$$
$$
r_A = \frac{p}{1 + e} \quad (\vartheta = 0) ,
\qquad
r_B = \frac{p}{1 - e} \quad (\vartheta = \pi)
$$
$$
\text{at } A \text{ and at } B \ \ \dot{r} = 0
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
v = r\,|\dot{\vartheta}| = \frac{c}{r}
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
\frac{v_A}{v_B} = \frac{r_B}{r_A} = \frac{1 + e}{1 - e}
$$

Check of the value of $\kappa$ with the conservation of mechanical energy between $A$ and $B$. The potential energy of the force found is $V = -\dfrac{\kappa}{r}$, therefore
$$
\frac{1}{2}\,m\,\frac{c^2}{r_A^2} - \frac{\kappa}{r_A} = \frac{1}{2}\,m\,\frac{c^2}{r_B^2} - \frac{\kappa}{r_B}
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
\frac{m\,c^2}{2}\left( \frac{1}{r_A} + \frac{1}{r_B} \right) = \kappa ,
$$
and since $\dfrac{1}{r_A} + \dfrac{1}{r_B} = \dfrac{1 + e}{p} + \dfrac{1 - e}{p} = \dfrac{2}{p}$, one obtains $\kappa = \dfrac{m\,c^2}{p}$, that is, the same value given by Binet's formula.

<div class="spiegazione">

Binet's formula is applied in the inverse direction: the orbit is assigned and the force is sought. One writes $u = \dfrac{1}{r}$ as a function of $\vartheta$, differentiates twice and observes that the terms in $\cos\vartheta$ cancel in the sum $\dfrac{d^2u}{d\vartheta^2} + u$, which remains constant and equal to $\dfrac{1}{p}$.

Binet's formula then gives a force proportional to $u^2$, that is, inversely proportional to the square of the distance, and attractive because the sign is negative: the elliptic orbit with a focus at the center is produced by a Newtonian force, with $\kappa = \dfrac{m\,c^2}{p}$.

The extreme distances are read off the polar equation: the denominator is maximum for $\vartheta = 0$ and minimum for $\vartheta = \pi$, therefore $r$ is minimum at $A$ and maximum at $B$. At these two points $\dot{r} = 0$, hence the velocity is entirely transverse and equals $\dfrac{c}{r}$. The ratio of the velocities is therefore the reciprocal of the ratio of the distances: the particle is **faster** where it is closer to the center, as the constancy of the areal velocity establishes.

The check computes $\kappa$ by a completely different route, that is, by imposing that the mechanical energy take the same value at the two extreme points. The equality simplifies by dividing by $\dfrac{1}{r_A} - \dfrac{1}{r_B}$, which is not zero because $e > 0$, and gives back the same $\kappa$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A particle of mass $m$ moves with central motion of center $O$ and travels along the logarithmic spiral
$$
r = a\,e^{\beta\vartheta} ,
\qquad
a > 0 ,
\qquad
\beta > 0 ,
$$
represented in the figure, with areal constant $c > 0$ and with $\vartheta(0) = 0$. Determine the force that produces this orbit, the laws of motion $\vartheta(t)$ and $r(t)$, and the mechanical energy of the motion.

::grafico{curve="0.35*exp(0.2*t)*cos(t),0.35*exp(0.2*t)*sin(t),0,9.42" legenda=0 uguale=1 punti="0,0,pieno,O" xmin=-2.6 xmax=2.6 ymin=-2.4 ymax=2.4 titolo="Logarithmic spiral $r = a\,e^{\beta\vartheta}$ with $\beta = 0.2$: the distance from the center grows at every turn"}

$$
u = \frac{1}{r} = \frac{1}{a}\,e^{-\beta\vartheta}
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
\frac{d^2u}{d\vartheta^2} = \frac{\beta^2}{a}\,e^{-\beta\vartheta} = \beta^2\,u
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
\frac{d^2u}{d\vartheta^2} + u = ( 1 + \beta^2 )\,u
$$
$$
f = -m\,c^2\,u^2\,( 1 + \beta^2 )\,u = -\frac{m\,c^2\,( 1 + \beta^2 )}{r^3}
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
\vec{F} = -\frac{K}{r^3}\,\hat{u}_r ,
\qquad
K = m\,c^2\,( 1 + \beta^2 )
$$
$$
c = r^2\,\dot{\vartheta} = a^2\,e^{2\beta\vartheta}\,\dot{\vartheta}
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
e^{2\beta\vartheta}\,d\vartheta = \frac{c}{a^2}\,dt
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
\frac{e^{2\beta\vartheta} - 1}{2\beta} = \frac{c\,t}{a^2}
$$
$$
\vartheta(t) = \frac{1}{2\beta}\ln\left( 1 + \frac{2\beta\,c\,t}{a^2} \right) ,
\qquad
r(t) = a\,e^{\beta\vartheta(t)} = \sqrt{a^2 + 2\beta\,c\,t}
$$
$$
U = \frac{K}{2\,r^2} ,
\qquad
v^2 = \dot{r}^2 + r^2\dot{\vartheta}^2 = ( 1 + \beta^2 )\,\frac{c^2}{r^2}
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
E = \frac{1}{2}\,m\,v^2 - U = 0
$$

Check of the law of motion with the areal constant, computed directly from the expressions found:
$$
\frac{d}{dt}\,r(t)^2 = \frac{d}{dt}\,( a^2 + 2\beta\,c\,t ) = 2\beta\,c ,
\qquad
\dot{\vartheta}(t) = \frac{1}{2\beta}\cdot\frac{\dfrac{2\beta\,c}{a^2}}{1 + \dfrac{2\beta\,c\,t}{a^2}} = \frac{c}{a^2 + 2\beta\,c\,t} ,
$$
hence $r^2\,\dot{\vartheta} = ( a^2 + 2\beta\,c\,t )\cdot\dfrac{c}{a^2 + 2\beta\,c\,t} = c$, as required.

<div class="spiegazione">

One proceeds as in the previous exercise: one writes $u = \dfrac{1}{r}$ as a function of $\vartheta$ and computes $\dfrac{d^2u}{d\vartheta^2} + u$. The exponential reproduces itself at every differentiation, therefore the sum is proportional to $u$ itself, with factor $1 + \beta^2$. Binet's formula then gives a force proportional to $u^3$, that is, inversely proportional to the **cube** of the distance.

The law of motion is obtained from the areal constant alone, without using the equation of motion: one substitutes the orbit into $c = r^2\dot{\vartheta}$, separates the variables and integrates between $0$ and $t$, using $\vartheta(0) = 0$. Solving for $\vartheta$ one obtains a logarithm, and substituting into the orbit one finds that $r^2$ grows *linearly* in time. The particle therefore moves away indefinitely, but slowly: the distance grows as $\sqrt{t}$.

For the energy one computes the potential of the force, which satisfies $\dfrac{dU}{dr} = -\dfrac{K}{r^3}$ and therefore equals $\dfrac{K}{2 r^2}$. The square of the velocity in polar coordinates is $\dot{r}^2 + r^2\dot{\vartheta}^2$; using $\dot{r} = \beta\,r\,\dot{\vartheta}$, which follows from the orbit, one obtains $(1 + \beta^2)\,\dfrac{c^2}{r^2}$. The kinetic energy then equals $\dfrac{m\,c^2\,(1 + \beta^2)}{2 r^2}$, that is, exactly $U$, and the mechanical energy is zero at every instant: the logarithmic spiral is the orbit of zero energy for this force.

The check obtains the areal constant from the expressions found for $r(t)$ and $\vartheta(t)$, by differentiating and multiplying. The result is $c$, constant, therefore the law of motion is compatible with the central motion one started from.

</div>
