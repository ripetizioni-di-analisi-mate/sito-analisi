## Exercise 1

A smooth rectilinear guide is placed in a horizontal plane and rotates with constant angular velocity $\omega$ about the vertical axis through its point $O$. A particle of mass $m$, threaded on the guide, is connected to $O$ by an ideal spring of constant $k$ and zero rest length, as the top view in the figure shows. Let $s$ be the abscissa of the particle on the guide, measured from $O$. At the instant $t = 0$ one has $s(0) = a > 0$ and $\dot{s}(0) = 0$. Determine the equation of the motion relative to the guide, discuss its character as $k$ varies, write the law of motion for $k > m\,\omega^2$ and determine the reaction of the guide.

::struttura{nodi="O,0,0;P,3.2,0" linee="-1.4,0 5.6,0;0.9,0 1.15,0.3 1.6,-0.3 2.05,0.3 2.5,-0.3 2.95,0.3 3.2,0" punti="3.2,0;0,0" coppie="O,1,ω" etichette="2.3,0.62,k" quote="O,P,s,-1.15" riferimento=0 titolo="Top view of the rotating guide: the particle $P$ is pulled back towards $O$ by the spring of constant $k$"}

$$
\text{apparent forces:}
\qquad
F_\tau = m\,\omega^2\,s \ \text{(centrifugal, along the guide)} ,
\qquad
\vec{F}_c = -2m\,\vec{\omega} \wedge \vec{v}_r
$$
$$
m\,\ddot{s} = -k\,s + m\,\omega^2\,s
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
\ddot{s} + \frac{k - m\,\omega^2}{m}\,s = 0
$$
$$
\begin{cases}
k > m\,\omega^2 : & \ddot{s} + \omega_1^2\,s = 0 ,\quad \omega_1 = \sqrt{\dfrac{k - m\,\omega^2}{m}} \\[6pt]
k = m\,\omega^2 : & \ddot{s} = 0 \\[6pt]
k < m\,\omega^2 : & \ddot{s} - \mu^2\,s = 0 ,\quad \mu = \sqrt{\dfrac{m\,\omega^2 - k}{m}}
\end{cases}
$$
$$
k > m\,\omega^2 :
\qquad
s(t) = a\cos( \omega_1\,t ) ,
\qquad
T = \frac{2\pi}{\omega_1} = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{m}{k - m\,\omega^2}}
$$
$$
\Phi_\varphi = 2 m\,\omega\,\dot{s} = -2 m\,\omega\,a\,\omega_1\sin( \omega_1\,t ) ,
\qquad
\Phi_z = m g
$$

Check with the equations of motion in the fixed frame, written in polar coordinates $(s, \varphi)$ with $\varphi = \omega\,t$. The components of the acceleration are $a_r = \ddot{s} - s\,\dot{\varphi}^2$ and $a_\varphi = s\,\ddot{\varphi} + 2\,\dot{s}\,\dot{\varphi}$, therefore
$$
\begin{cases}
m\,( \ddot{s} - \omega^2\,s ) = -k\,s \\[4pt]
m\cdot 2\,\omega\,\dot{s} = \Phi_\varphi
\end{cases}
$$
that is, the same equation of motion and the same reaction obtained with the apparent forces.

<div class="spiegazione">

One studies the motion in the frame attached to the guide, which rotates with constant angular velocity $\omega$ about a fixed axis. In that frame the centrifugal force and the Coriolis force are added to the true forces. The distance of the particle from the axis of rotation is $s$, therefore the centrifugal force equals $m\,\omega^2\,s$ and is directed along the guide, in the direction of increasing $s$; the Coriolis force is orthogonal both to the axis and to the relative velocity, hence it is horizontal and orthogonal to the guide, and it is balanced by the reaction.

Along the guide there act therefore the elastic force $-k\,s$ and the centrifugal force $m\,\omega^2\,s$, both proportional to $s$ but of opposite direction. The sign of $k - m\,\omega^2$ decides the character of the motion. If the spring prevails, the motion is harmonic about $O$ with angular frequency $\omega_1$, *smaller* than the angular frequency $\sqrt{\dfrac{k}{m}}$ that one would have with the guide at rest, because the rotation weakens the restoring action. If the two forces balance each other, the relative acceleration is zero and the particle remains at rest with respect to the guide in any position. If the centrifugal force prevails, the solution contains real exponentials and the particle moves away indefinitely from $O$.

In the first case the initial data give the law of motion at once, because the initial position is $a$ and the initial relative velocity is zero.

The reaction of the guide has two components. The horizontal one, orthogonal to the guide, balances the Coriolis force and equals $2 m\,\omega\,\dot{s}$ in the direction of increasing $\varphi$; it is zero when the particle is at rest with respect to the guide and changes sign at every reversal of the relative motion. The vertical one balances the weight, because the motion takes place in a horizontal plane.

The check rewrites the problem in the fixed frame, without apparent forces, using the polar components of the acceleration with $\dot{\varphi} = \omega$ constant. The radial component gives back the same equation of motion and the transverse component the same reaction, therefore the computation with the apparent forces is correct.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A smooth circular guide of radius $R$ is placed in a vertical plane and rotates with constant angular velocity $\omega$ about its own vertical diameter, as in the figure. A bead of mass $m$ is threaded on the guide. Let $\vartheta$ be the angle between the radius that locates the bead and the downward vertical. Determine the potential of the forces that act in the rotating frame, the configurations of relative equilibrium and the condition for the existence of the inclined ones; setting $\omega^2 = \dfrac{2 g}{R}$, determine the inclined equilibrium angle and the angular frequency of the small relative oscillations about it.

::struttura{cerchi="0,0,2,foro" nodi="C,0,0;P,1.732,-1" punti="0,0;1.732,-1" tratteggi="0,2.9 0,-2.9;0,0 1.732,-1" coppie="0 2.5,1,ω" etichette="0.45,-0.95,ϑ" riferimento=0 titolo="Circular guide rotating about the vertical diameter with angular velocity $\omega$"}

$$
U = -m g\,y_P + \frac{1}{2}\,m\,\omega^2\,d^2 ,
\qquad
y_P = -R\cos\vartheta ,
\qquad
d = R\sin\vartheta
$$
$$
U(\vartheta) = m g R\cos\vartheta + \frac{1}{2}\,m\,\omega^2\,R^2\sin^2\vartheta ,
\qquad
V = -U
$$
$$
\frac{dV}{d\vartheta} = m R\sin\vartheta\,( g - \omega^2 R\cos\vartheta ) = 0
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
\vartheta = 0 ,
\qquad
\vartheta = \pi ,
\qquad
\cos\vartheta_1 = \frac{g}{\omega^2 R}
$$
$$
\text{the inclined equilibrium exists} \iff \omega^2 R \geq g
$$
$$
\omega^2 = \frac{2 g}{R}
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
\cos\vartheta_1 = \frac{1}{2} ,
\qquad
\vartheta_1 = \frac{\pi}{3}
$$
$$
\frac{d^2V}{d\vartheta^2}\bigg|_{\vartheta_1} = m\,\omega^2 R^2\sin^2\vartheta_1 = m\cdot\frac{2 g}{R}\cdot R^2\cdot\frac{3}{4} = \frac{3}{2}\,m g R > 0
$$
$$
\omega_p = \sqrt{\frac{1}{m R^2}\,\frac{d^2V}{d\vartheta^2}\bigg|_{\vartheta_1}} = \sqrt{\frac{3 g}{2 R}}
$$

Check with the linearization of the equation of the relative motion, which is $m R\,\ddot{\vartheta} = -m g\sin\vartheta + m\,\omega^2 R\sin\vartheta\cos\vartheta$. Setting $\vartheta = \vartheta_1 + \xi$ and differentiating the right-hand side with respect to $\vartheta$,
$$
R\,\ddot{\xi} = \big( -g\cos\vartheta_1 + \omega^2 R\cos(2\vartheta_1) \big)\,\xi
= \left( -\frac{g}{2} + 2 g\cdot\left( -\frac{1}{2} \right) \right)\xi = -\frac{3 g}{2}\,\xi ,
$$
hence $\ddot{\xi} + \dfrac{3 g}{2 R}\,\xi = 0$ and the angular frequency is $\sqrt{\dfrac{3 g}{2 R}}$, as obtained from the potential.

<div class="spiegazione">

In the frame attached to the guide there act the weight, the constraint reaction, the centrifugal force and the Coriolis force. The guide is smooth, hence the reaction is orthogonal to it and has zero power in the relative motion; the Coriolis force is orthogonal to the plane of the guide and has zero power as well. The weight and the centrifugal force remain, both conservative: the weight has potential $-m g\,y_P$ and the centrifugal force has potential $\dfrac{1}{2}\,m\,\omega^2 d^2$, where $d = R\sin\vartheta$ is the distance of the bead from the axis of rotation.

The configurations of relative equilibrium are the stationary points of $V = -U$. Collecting $\sin\vartheta$, the derivative vanishes for $\vartheta = 0$ and $\vartheta = \pi$, that is, at the two points on the axis, and moreover for $\cos\vartheta = \dfrac{g}{\omega^2 R}$. This last equation has a solution only if the right-hand side does not exceed $1$, that is, if $\omega^2 R \geq g$: the guide must rotate fast enough for the centrifugal force to manage to keep the bead lifted.

With $\omega^2 R = 2 g$ the condition is satisfied and the equilibrium angle is $\dfrac{\pi}{3}$. The second derivative of $V$ in that configuration is positive, hence it is a minimum of the potential energy and the equilibrium is stable; for the relative motion the same discussion as for one-dimensional conservative motions then holds, with the relative kinetic energy $\dfrac{1}{2}\,m\,R^2\,\dot{\vartheta}^2$. The angular frequency of the small oscillations is obtained from the ratio between the second derivative of the potential and the coefficient $m R^2$ of the kinetic energy.

The reaction of the guide has also a component orthogonal to the plane of the guide, which balances the Coriolis force and equals $2 m\,\omega\,R\,\dot{\vartheta}\cos\vartheta$; it is zero in the configurations of relative equilibrium, where $\dot{\vartheta} = 0$.

The check obtains the angular frequency from the equation of the relative motion, written by projecting onto the tangent to the guide, and linearizing it about $\vartheta_1$ with the derivative of the right-hand side. The coefficient found coincides with the one deduced from the potential.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A particle is dropped from rest, with respect to the Earth, from a height $h_0 = 100\,\mathrm{m}$ above a point of the equator, as in the figure. The Earth rotates with angular velocity $\omega_T = 7.29 \cdot 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{rad/s}$ and $g = 9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$ is assumed constant. Determine, to a first approximation, the horizontal deflection of the point of arrival with respect to the vertical of the starting point, saying in which direction it occurs.

::struttura{linee="-2.2,0 2.6,0" nodi="P,0,3" punti="0,3;0.9,0" tratteggi="0,3 0,0" frecce="-1.2,3.4,0.1,3.4,east" quote="0 0,0.9 0,d,-0.6;P,0 0,h_0,0.9" riferimento=0 titolo="Fall from $h_0$ at the equator: the particle touches the ground at distance $d$ from the vertical of the starting point"}

$$
\vec{a}_r = \vec{g} - 2\,\vec{\omega}_T \wedge \vec{v}_r
\qquad\text{(the centrifugal force is already included in } \vec{g} \text{)}
$$
$$
\text{first approximation:}
\qquad
\vec{v}_r \approx -g\,t\,\hat{\jmath}
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
|2\,\vec{\omega}_T \wedge \vec{v}_r| = 2\,\omega_T\,g\,t ,
\qquad\text{directed eastwards}
$$
$$
\ddot{d} = 2\,\omega_T\,g\,t ,
\qquad
d(0) = 0 ,
\quad
\dot{d}(0) = 0
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
d(t) = \frac{\omega_T\,g\,t^3}{3}
$$
$$
t_c = \sqrt{\frac{2 h_0}{g}} = \sqrt{\frac{200}{9.81}}\,\mathrm{s} = 4.52\,\mathrm{s}
$$
$$
d = \frac{\omega_T\,g\,t_c^3}{3} = \frac{2\sqrt{2}}{3}\,\omega_T\sqrt{\frac{h_0^3}{g}}
= \frac{7.29 \cdot 10^{-5} \cdot 9.81 \cdot 4.52^3}{3}\,\mathrm{m} = 2.2 \cdot 10^{-2}\,\mathrm{m} = 2.2\,\mathrm{cm}
$$

Check with an independent upper bound. The Coriolis acceleration grows from zero to the final value $2\,\omega_T\,g\,t_c$; if it were constant and equal to the final value one would obtain
$$
d_{\max} = \frac{1}{2}\,( 2\,\omega_T\,g\,t_c )\,t_c^2 = \omega_T\,g\,t_c^3 = 6.6\,\mathrm{cm} ,
$$
and the exact value is *one third* of this, as must be the case for an acceleration that grows linearly in time, since the double integration of $t$ gives $\dfrac{t^3}{6}$ instead of $\dfrac{t^3}{2}$.

<div class="spiegazione">

The terrestrial frame rotates, hence it is not inertial and in the equation of the relative motion the transport force and the Coriolis force appear. The transport force is centrifugal, because the angular velocity is constant; it is already included in the measured value of $g$, therefore only the Coriolis force remains to be added.

One proceeds by successive approximations. At the first step the deflection is neglected and the velocity of free fall is used, $\vec{v}_r \approx -g\,t\,\hat{\jmath}$. At the equator the angular velocity of the Earth is horizontal and directed from south to north, while the velocity is vertical downwards, therefore the cross product is horizontal and the Coriolis force $-2m\,\vec{\omega}_T \wedge \vec{v}_r$ is directed **eastwards**.

The modulus of the Coriolis acceleration grows proportionally to the time, hence the deflection is obtained by integrating twice starting from zero initial data. The time of fall is computed with free fall, because the correction due to the deflection is of higher order.

The result, a little more than two centimeters over one hundred meters, shows why the effect remains invisible in ordinary experiments, since the factor $\omega_T$ is extremely small compared with the other quantities involved. The deflection grows however as $h_0^{\frac{3}{2}}$, hence it becomes measurable for very high falls.

The check compares the value found with the one that would be obtained by keeping the acceleration constant at its maximum value, which is an upper bound for the true result. The ratio between the two values is exactly $3$, in agreement with the fact that the acceleration grows linearly starting from zero.

</div>
