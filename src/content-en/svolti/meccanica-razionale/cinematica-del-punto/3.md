## Exercise 1

A material point $P$ moves in the plane $Oxy$ with the polar coordinates
$$
r(t) = c\,t ,
\qquad
\vartheta(t) = \omega t ,
$$
where $c > 0$ and $\omega > 0$ are constants and $t \ge 0$. The trajectory is the spiral in the figure. Compute the radial and transverse components of the velocity and of the acceleration, the magnitude of the velocity, the equation of the trajectory in polar coordinates and the areal velocity with respect to $O$.

::struttura{nodi="O,0,0;P,1.25,1.25" punti="0,0;1.25,1.25" linee="0.0,0.0 0.04,0.01 0.07,0.02 0.1,0.05 0.13,0.09 0.14,0.14 0.14,0.19 0.12,0.24 0.1,0.3 0.06,0.35 0.0,0.39 -0.07,0.43 -0.15,0.45 -0.23,0.45 -0.32,0.44 -0.42,0.42 -0.51,0.37 -0.59,0.3 -0.67,0.22 -0.74,0.12 -0.79,0.0 -0.81,-0.13 -0.82,-0.27 -0.8,-0.41 -0.76,-0.55 -0.69,-0.69 -0.6,-0.83 -0.48,-0.94 -0.34,-1.05 -0.18,-1.12 0.0,-1.18 0.19,-1.2 0.39,-1.2 0.59,-1.15 0.79,-1.08 0.97,-0.97 1.14,-0.83 1.29,-0.66 1.42,-0.46 1.51,-0.24 1.57,0.0 1.59,0.25 1.57,0.51 1.5,0.77 1.4,1.02 1.25,1.25;0,0 1.25,1.25" frecce="1.25,1.25,1.64,1.64,v_r;1.25,1.25,0.65,1.85,v_ϑ;0,0,2.3,0,x;0,0,0,2.1,y" etichette="0.72,0.5,r" riferimento=0 titolo="Archimedean spiral: the distance from the pole grows in proportion to the angle described"}

The polar coordinates with pole $O$ are used, because the two given functions are precisely $r$ and $\vartheta$.

$$
\dot{r} = c ,
\qquad
\ddot{r} = 0 ,
\qquad
\dot{\vartheta} = \omega ,
\qquad
\ddot{\vartheta} = 0
$$
$$
\vec{v}_P = \dot{r}\,\hat{u}_r + r\dot{\vartheta}\,\hat{u}_\vartheta = c\,\hat{u}_r + c\,\omega t\,\hat{u}_\vartheta
$$
$$
\vec{a}_P = \big( \ddot{r} - r\dot{\vartheta}^2 \big)\,\hat{u}_r + \big( r\ddot{\vartheta} + 2\dot{r}\dot{\vartheta} \big)\,\hat{u}_\vartheta = -c\,\omega^2 t\,\hat{u}_r + 2\,c\,\omega\,\hat{u}_\vartheta
$$
$$
|\vec{v}_P| = \sqrt{c^2 + c^2\omega^2t^2} = c\,\sqrt{1 + \omega^2 t^2}
$$
$$
\text{trajectory:} \quad t = \frac{\vartheta}{\omega}
\quad \Longrightarrow \quad
r = \frac{c}{\omega}\,\vartheta
$$
$$
\frac{dA}{dt} = \frac{1}{2}\,r^2\,\dot{\vartheta} = \frac{1}{2}\,c^2\omega\,t^2
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The derivatives of $r$ and of $\vartheta$ are computed at once, since both functions are linear in time; the radial velocity is constant and equal to $c$, while the angular velocity is constant and equal to $\omega$.

Substituting into the formulas in polar coordinates one obtains the velocity, whose transverse component $r\dot{\vartheta} = c\omega t$ grows linearly because the point moves away from the pole while the radius keeps rotating with the same angular velocity.

In the acceleration the radial component reduces to the centripetal term $-r\dot{\vartheta}^2$, since $\ddot{r} = 0$; the transverse component reduces to the term $2\dot{r}\dot{\vartheta}$, since $\ddot{\vartheta} = 0$, and it is **constant** and positive. The transverse acceleration is therefore different from zero even though both $r$ and $\vartheta$ vary uniformly.

Eliminating time between the two coordinates one obtains $r = \dfrac{c}{\omega}\vartheta$, hence the distance from the pole is proportional to the angle described and the curve is the **Archimedean spiral**. At every complete turn the distance from the pole increases by the constant quantity $\dfrac{2\pi c}{\omega}$.

The areal velocity grows with the square of time, because the position vector lengthens while it rotates with constant angular velocity.

</div>

Check. The transverse acceleration is checked again with the identity $r\ddot{\vartheta} + 2\dot{r}\dot{\vartheta} = \dfrac{1}{r}\dfrac{d}{dt}\big( r^2\dot{\vartheta} \big)$. One has $r^2\dot{\vartheta} = c^2\omega t^2$, hence
$$
\frac{1}{r}\,\frac{d}{dt}\big( c^2\omega t^2 \big) = \frac{2c^2\omega t}{c t} = 2\,c\,\omega ,
$$
which coincides with the value found. A second check is made by passing to Cartesian coordinates: from $x = ct\cos\omega t$ and $y = ct\sin\omega t$ there follow $\dot{x} = c\cos\omega t - c\omega t\sin\omega t$ and $\dot{y} = c\sin\omega t + c\omega t\cos\omega t$, hence $\dot{x}^2 + \dot{y}^2 = c^2(1 + \omega^2t^2)$, in agreement with the magnitude of the velocity computed in polar coordinates.

## Exercise 2

A material point $P$ describes the logarithmic spiral in the figure, with
$$
r(t) = r_0\,e^{\lambda\omega t} ,
\qquad
\vartheta(t) = \omega t ,
$$
where $r_0 > 0$, $\lambda > 0$ and $\omega > 0$ are constants. Compute the velocity and the acceleration in polar coordinates and prove that the angle between the velocity and the position vector is constant.

::struttura{nodi="O,0,0;P,1.26,1.26" punti="0,0;1.26,1.26" linee="0.35,0.0 0.36,0.06 0.36,0.12 0.35,0.18 0.33,0.24 0.3,0.3 0.26,0.35 0.2,0.4 0.14,0.44 0.08,0.48 0.0,0.5 -0.08,0.51 -0.17,0.51 -0.25,0.5 -0.34,0.47 -0.43,0.43 -0.5,0.37 -0.58,0.29 -0.64,0.21 -0.69,0.11 -0.72,0.0 -0.74,-0.12 -0.74,-0.24 -0.72,-0.36 -0.67,-0.49 -0.61,-0.61 -0.53,-0.72 -0.42,-0.83 -0.3,-0.92 -0.16,-0.99 0.0,-1.03 0.17,-1.06 0.34,-1.06 0.52,-1.03 0.7,-0.97 0.88,-0.88 1.04,-0.76 1.19,-0.6 1.31,-0.43 1.41,-0.22 1.48,0.0 1.52,0.24 1.52,0.49 1.47,0.75 1.39,1.01 1.26,1.26;0,0 1.26,1.26" frecce="1.26,1.26,1.44,1.44,v_r;1.26,1.26,0.49,2.03,v_ϑ;1.26,1.26,0.67,2.21,v_P;0,0,2.3,0,x;0,0,0,2.3,y" etichette="0.72,0.5,r;1.56,1.7,β" riferimento=0 titolo="Logarithmic spiral: the velocity forms with the position vector an angle $\beta$ that does not depend on time"}

$$
\dot{r} = \lambda\,\omega\,r ,
\qquad
\ddot{r} = \lambda^2\,\omega^2\,r ,
\qquad
\dot{\vartheta} = \omega ,
\qquad
\ddot{\vartheta} = 0
$$
$$
\vec{v}_P = \lambda\,\omega\,r\,\hat{u}_r + \omega\,r\,\hat{u}_\vartheta ,
\qquad
|\vec{v}_P| = \omega\,r\,\sqrt{1 + \lambda^2}
$$
$$
\vec{a}_P = \big( \lambda^2\omega^2 r - \omega^2 r \big)\,\hat{u}_r + 2\,\lambda\,\omega^2 r\,\hat{u}_\vartheta = \omega^2 r\,\big( \lambda^2 - 1 \big)\,\hat{u}_r + 2\,\lambda\,\omega^2 r\,\hat{u}_\vartheta
$$

Angle $\beta$ between $\vec{v}_P$ and $\hat{u}_r$:
$$
\tan\beta = \frac{r\dot{\vartheta}}{\dot{r}} = \frac{\omega r}{\lambda\omega r} = \frac{1}{\lambda} ,
$$
independent of time.

<div class="spiegazione">

The derivative of $r_0e^{\lambda\omega t}$ equals $\lambda\omega\,r_0e^{\lambda\omega t} = \lambda\omega r$, hence the radial velocity is proportional to the distance from the pole; differentiating once more one obtains $\ddot{r} = \lambda^2\omega^2 r$.

Substituting into the polar formulas, both the velocity and the acceleration turn out to be proportional to $r$, therefore they grow in time with the same exponential behavior as the distance from the pole.

The angle $\beta$ between the velocity and the position vector is obtained from the ratio of the transverse component to the radial component of the velocity, which are the two orthogonal projections of $\vec{v}_P$. The ratio equals $\dfrac{1}{\lambda}$ at every instant, hence **the angle is constant**: this is the property that characterizes the logarithmic spiral, also called the equiangular spiral.

The radial component of the acceleration has the sign of $\lambda^2 - 1$, therefore it is negative for $\lambda < 1$ and positive for $\lambda > 1$. The term $\lambda^2\omega^2 r$, due to the growth of $r$, exceeds in magnitude the centripetal term $-\omega^2 r$ only when $\lambda > 1$.

</div>

Check. As $\lambda \to 0$ the spiral degenerates into the circle of radius $r_0$ traveled with angular velocity $\omega$. Setting $\lambda = 0$ in the formulas found one obtains $\vec{v}_P = \omega r_0\,\hat{u}_\vartheta$ and $\vec{a}_P = -\omega^2 r_0\,\hat{u}_r$, which are precisely the velocity and the acceleration of uniform circular motion, and the angle $\beta$ tends to $\dfrac{\pi}{2}$, that is the velocity becomes orthogonal to the radius. The transverse acceleration is also checked with the identity on the areal velocity: from $r^2\dot{\vartheta} = \omega r_0^2 e^{2\lambda\omega t}$ there follows
$$
\frac{1}{r}\,\frac{d}{dt}\big( r^2\dot{\vartheta} \big) = \frac{2\lambda\omega^2 r_0^2 e^{2\lambda\omega t}}{r_0 e^{\lambda\omega t}} = 2\,\lambda\,\omega^2 r ,
$$
in agreement with the result.

## Exercise 3

A material point $P$ moves in the plane $Oxy$ moving away from the pole with the law
$$
r(t) = r_0\,(1 + \omega t) ,
$$
and its areal velocity with respect to $O$ is constant and equal to $c > 0$; at the initial instant $\vartheta = 0$. The trajectory is drawn in the figure. Determine $\vartheta(t)$, the acceleration in polar coordinates and the limit of the angle $\vartheta$ as $t \to +\infty$.

::grafico{curve="(1+t)*cos(2*t/(1+t)),(1+t)*sin(2*t/(1+t)),0,3" punti="0,0,pieno;1,0,pieno,P₀" segmenti="0,0,-1.87,4.09" etichette="-1.7,3.4,ϑ∞" uguale=1 xmin=-2.3 xmax=1.9 ymin=-0.6 ymax=4.6 legenda=0 nomi="x,y" titolo="Trajectory with constant areal velocity: the point moves away from the pole and the direction of the position vector tends to the line of angle $\vartheta_\infty$"}

$$
\frac{dA}{dt} = \frac{1}{2}\,r^2\,\dot{\vartheta} = c
\quad \Longrightarrow \quad
\dot{\vartheta} = \frac{2c}{r^2} = \frac{2c}{r_0^2\,(1 + \omega t)^2}
$$
$$
\vartheta(t) = \int_0^t \frac{2c}{r_0^2\,(1 + \omega \tau)^2}\,d\tau = \frac{2c}{r_0^2\,\omega}\left[ 1 - \frac{1}{1 + \omega t} \right] = \frac{2c\,t}{r_0^2\,(1 + \omega t)}
$$
$$
\dot{r} = r_0\,\omega ,
\qquad
\ddot{r} = 0
$$
$$
\vec{a}_P = \big( \ddot{r} - r\dot{\vartheta}^2 \big)\,\hat{u}_r + \big( r\ddot{\vartheta} + 2\dot{r}\dot{\vartheta} \big)\,\hat{u}_\vartheta = -\,\frac{4c^2}{r^3}\,\hat{u}_r
$$
$$
\lim_{t \to +\infty} \vartheta(t) = \frac{2c}{r_0^2\,\omega} = \vartheta_\infty
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The areal velocity equals $\dfrac{1}{2}r^2\dot{\vartheta}$, therefore requiring that it be constant and equal to $c$ amounts to writing $\dot{\vartheta} = \dfrac{2c}{r^2}$. Substituting the given expression of $r$ one obtains a separable differential equation already solved for $\dot{\vartheta}$, which is integrated directly.

The integral of $\dfrac{1}{(1 + \omega\tau)^2}$ equals $-\dfrac{1}{\omega\,(1 + \omega\tau)}$, from which the expression of $\vartheta(t)$ follows after imposing $\vartheta(0) = 0$.

In the acceleration the transverse component is zero, since the areal velocity is constant and the transverse acceleration equals $\dfrac{1}{r}\dfrac{d}{dt}(r^2\dot{\vartheta})$. The radial component reduces to $-r\dot{\vartheta}^2$, since $\ddot{r} = 0$; substituting $\dot{\vartheta} = \dfrac{2c}{r^2}$ one obtains $-\dfrac{4c^2}{r^3}$, hence the acceleration is **central**, directed toward the pole, and it decreases as the cube of the distance.

The angle described stays bounded, since as $t \to +\infty$ it tends to $\vartheta_\infty = \dfrac{2c}{r_0^2\omega}$, because the angular velocity decreases as $\dfrac{1}{t^2}$. The point moves away indefinitely from the pole approaching the fixed direction identified by $\vartheta_\infty$.

</div>

Check. Differentiate the expression found for $\vartheta(t)$ and verify that it reproduces the given areal velocity:
$$
\dot{\vartheta} = \frac{d}{dt}\left[ \frac{2c}{r_0^2\omega}\left( 1 - \frac{1}{1 + \omega t} \right) \right] = \frac{2c}{r_0^2\,(1 + \omega t)^2} ,
\qquad
\frac{1}{2}\,r^2\,\dot{\vartheta} = \frac{1}{2}\,r_0^2(1 + \omega t)^2\,\frac{2c}{r_0^2(1 + \omega t)^2} = c ,
$$
as required by the problem. The sign of the radial acceleration is also checked in the limiting case $\omega \to 0$: then $r = r_0$ is constant, $\dot{\vartheta} = \dfrac{2c}{r_0^2}$ is constant and the acceleration equals $-\dfrac{4c^2}{r_0^3} = -r_0\dot{\vartheta}^2$, that is the centripetal acceleration of uniform circular motion.
