## Exercise 1

Compute, using the definition, the derivative of $f(x) = x^2 - 3x$ at a generic point $x \in \mathbb{R}$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{f(x + h) - f(x)}{h}
&= \frac{(x + h)^2 - 3(x + h) - x^2 + 3x}{h} \\[6pt]
&= \frac{2xh + h^2 - 3h}{h}
= 2x + h - 3
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} (2x + h - 3) = 2x - 3
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Write the **difference quotient** and expand the square $(x + h)^2 = x^2 + 2xh + h^2$. The terms $x^2$ and $-3x$ cancel with those subtracted, therefore *only* the terms containing $h$ remain in the numerator. After cancelling $h$ the quotient equals $2x + h - 3$, a continuous function of $h$, hence the limit as $h \to 0$ is computed by **substitution** and equals $2x - 3$. The limit exists and is finite for every $x$, therefore $f$ is **differentiable** on all of $\mathbb{R}$ with $f'(x) = 2x - 3$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Compute, using the definition, the derivative of $f(x) = \dfrac{1}{x}$ at the points $x \neq 0$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{f(x + h) - f(x)}{h}
&= \frac{1}{h} \left( \frac{1}{x + h} - \frac{1}{x} \right)
= \frac{1}{h} \cdot \frac{x - (x + h)}{x\,(x + h)} \\[6pt]
&= \frac{-h}{h\,x\,(x + h)}
= -\frac{1}{x\,(x + h)}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \left( -\frac{1}{x\,(x + h)} \right) = -\frac{1}{x^2}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The difference of the two fractions is brought to the **common denominator** $x(x + h)$, and the numerator becomes $x - (x + h) = -h$. The factor $h$ cancels with the denominator of the difference quotient, therefore for every $h \neq 0$ small enough the quotient equals $-\dfrac{1}{x(x + h)}$. As $h \to 0$ the denominator tends to $x^2$, hence the derivative is $-\dfrac{1}{x^2}$ at every point $x \neq 0$, in agreement with the **power rule** applied to $x^{-1}$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Compute, using the definition, the derivative of $f(x) = \sqrt{x}$ at the points $x > 0$ and study differentiability at $x = 0$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\sqrt{x + h} - \sqrt{x}}{h}
&= \frac{\bigl( \sqrt{x + h} - \sqrt{x} \bigr)\bigl( \sqrt{x + h} + \sqrt{x} \bigr)}{h \bigl( \sqrt{x + h} + \sqrt{x} \bigr)} \\[6pt]
&= \frac{h}{h \bigl( \sqrt{x + h} + \sqrt{x} \bigr)}
= \frac{1}{\sqrt{x + h} + \sqrt{x}}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{1}{\sqrt{x + h} + \sqrt{x}} = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{x}} \qquad (x > 0)
$$

At $x = 0$:
$$
\lim_{h \to 0^{+}} \frac{\sqrt{h} - 0}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0^{+}} \frac{1}{\sqrt{h}} = +\infty ,
$$
therefore $f$ **is not differentiable** at $0$.

<div class="spiegazione">

As $h \to 0$ the difference quotient is an **indeterminate form** $\dfrac{0}{0}$ and one **rationalizes** by multiplying numerator and denominator by $\sqrt{x + h} + \sqrt{x}$: in the numerator $(x + h) - x = h$ appears, which cancels with the factor $h$ in the denominator. The remaining denominator tends to $2\sqrt{x}$, hence $f'(x) = \dfrac{1}{2\sqrt{x}}$ at every point $x > 0$.

At $x = 0$ the domain extends *only* to the right, therefore one studies the right derivative. The difference quotient equals $\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{h}}$ and tends to $+\infty$, therefore the right derivative does not exist as a finite number and $f$ **is not differentiable** at $0$; the graph leaves the origin with a **vertical tangent**.

</div>
