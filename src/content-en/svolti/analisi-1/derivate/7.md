## Exercise 1

Compute
$$
\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sin x - x}{x^3} .
$$

With the expansion $\sin x = x - \dfrac{x^3}{6} + o(x^3)$:

$$
\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sin x - x}{x^3}
= \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{-\dfrac{x^3}{6} + o(x^3)}{x^3}
= \lim_{x \to 0} \left( -\frac{1}{6} + \frac{o(x^3)}{x^3} \right)
= -\frac{1}{6}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The numerator is a difference in the **indeterminate form** $0 - 0$, and replacing $\sin x$ with the asymptotically equivalent function $x$ is *not* legitimate, because asymptotic substitutions hold in products and quotients, and here they would cancel the numerator, suggesting the wrong value $0$. One therefore expands the sine up to the **third order**, the order of the denominator: $\sin x = x - \dfrac{x^3}{6} + o(x^3)$. The term $x$ cancels with the $-x$ of the given expression, therefore the numerator becomes $-\dfrac{x^3}{6} + o(x^3)$. Dividing by $x^3$, the first term equals $-\dfrac{1}{6}$ and the second tends to $0$ by the **definition of little-o**, hence the limit equals $-\dfrac{1}{6}$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Compute
$$
\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\ln(1 + x) - \sin x}{x^2} .
$$

With the expansions $\ln(1 + x) = x - \dfrac{x^2}{2} + o(x^2)$ and $\sin x = x + o(x^2)$:

$$
\ln(1 + x) - \sin x = x - \frac{x^2}{2} + o(x^2) - x + o(x^2) = -\frac{x^2}{2} + o(x^2)
$$

$$
\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\ln(1 + x) - \sin x}{x^2}
= \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{-\dfrac{x^2}{2} + o(x^2)}{x^2}
= -\frac{1}{2}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The denominator is of second order, therefore both functions are expanded up to $x^2$. The expansion of the logarithm is $x - \dfrac{x^2}{2} + o(x^2)$. In the expansion of the sine the term after $x$ is $-\dfrac{x^3}{6}$, which is $o(x^2)$ by the **algebra of little-o**, hence $\sin x = x + o(x^2)$ is an expansion that is **correct to second order**. The terms $x$ cancel in the difference and the sum of the two remainders is still $o(x^2)$, therefore the numerator equals $-\dfrac{x^2}{2} + o(x^2)$ and the limit is $-\dfrac{1}{2}$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Compute
$$
\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\cos x - e^{-\frac{x^2}{2}}}{x^4} .
$$

Expansion of the cosine to fourth order:
$$
\cos x = 1 - \frac{x^2}{2} + \frac{x^4}{24} + o(x^4)
$$

Expansion of the composite exponential, with $t = -\dfrac{x^2}{2}$ and $e^{t} = 1 + t + \dfrac{t^2}{2} + o(t^2)$:
$$
e^{-\frac{x^2}{2}} = 1 - \frac{x^2}{2} + \frac{x^4}{8} + o(x^4)
$$

$$
\cos x - e^{-\frac{x^2}{2}} = \left( \frac{1}{24} - \frac{1}{8} \right) x^4 + o(x^4) = -\frac{x^4}{12} + o(x^4)
$$

$$
\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\cos x - e^{-\frac{x^2}{2}}}{x^4} = -\frac{1}{12}
$$

::grafico{f="cos(x);exp(-x^2/2)" xmin=-4 xmax=4 ymin=-1.2 ymax=1.4 titolo="$y = \cos x$ and $y = e^{-\frac{x^2}{2}}$ (dashed) have the same expansion up to second order: near $0$ the graphs almost overlap"}

<div class="spiegazione">

The two functions **coincide up to second order**, since both equal $1 - \dfrac{x^2}{2}$ up to higher-order terms; an expansion stopped at $x^2$ would leave *only* $o(x^2)$ in the numerator, which is **insufficient** information relative to $x^4$. One therefore continues up to the **fourth order**. For the exponential one substitutes $t = -\dfrac{x^2}{2}$ into the expansion of $e^{t}$: the term $\dfrac{t^2}{2}$ becomes $\dfrac{x^4}{8}$ and the remainder $o(t^2)$ becomes $o(x^4)$, since $t^2$ is a multiple of $x^4$. In the difference the terms of order $0$ and $2$ cancel and the coefficients of $x^4$ remain: $\dfrac{1}{24} - \dfrac{1}{8} = -\dfrac{1}{12}$. Dividing by $x^4$, the limit equals $-\dfrac{1}{12}$.

</div>
