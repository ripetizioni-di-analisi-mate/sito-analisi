## Exercise 1

Verify that $f(x) = x^3$ satisfies the hypotheses of Lagrange's theorem on $[0, 2]$ and find the points $c$ given by the theorem.

$f$ is a polynomial, therefore it is continuous on $[0, 2]$ and differentiable on $(0, 2)$: the hypotheses are **satisfied**.

$$
\frac{f(2) - f(0)}{2 - 0} = \frac{8 - 0}{2} = 4
$$

$$
f'(c) = 3c^2 = 4 \iff c^2 = \frac{4}{3} \iff c = \pm\frac{2}{\sqrt{3}}
$$

$$
c = \frac{2}{\sqrt{3}} = \frac{2\sqrt{3}}{3} \in (0, 2)
$$

The only **Lagrange point** is $c = \dfrac{2\sqrt{3}}{3}$.

::grafico{f="x^3+0*sqrt(x*(2-x));4*x+0*sqrt(x*(2-x));4*x-3.0792" xmin=-0.5 xmax=2.5 ymin=-2 ymax=9 legenda="y = x^3;y = 4x;y = 4x - \dfrac{16\sqrt{3}}{9}" punti="0,0,pieno;2,8,pieno;1.1547,1.5396,pieno,c" titolo="The tangent to $y = x^3$ at the point with abscissa $c = \dfrac{2\sqrt{3}}{3}$ (red) is parallel to the chord between $(0, 0)$ and $(2, 8)$ (dashed), with slope $4$"}

<div class="spiegazione">

The hypotheses of the theorem are continuity on the closed interval and differentiability on the open one; a polynomial satisfies both, therefore the theorem **applies**. The **average slope** over the interval is the ratio $\dfrac{f(2) - f(0)}{2 - 0} = 4$, and the theorem guarantees *at least one* interior point at which the derivative takes this value. The equation $3c^2 = 4$ has two opposite solutions; since the Lagrange point must belong to the open interval $(0, 2)$, the negative solution is **discarded** and what remains is $c = \dfrac{2\sqrt{3}}{3}$, which lies between $0$ and $2$ since $\sqrt{3} > 1$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Find the intervals of monotonicity and the local extrema of $f(x) = x^3 - 3x^2 + 1$.

$$
f'(x) = 3x^2 - 6x = 3x\,(x - 2)
$$

$$
f'(x) > 0 \iff x < 0 \ \text{or} \ x > 2 , \qquad
f'(x) < 0 \iff 0 < x < 2
$$

$$
f(0) = 1 , \qquad f(2) = 8 - 12 + 1 = -3
$$

$f$ is strictly increasing on $(-\infty, 0]$ and on $[2, +\infty)$, strictly decreasing on $[0, 2]$; the point $x = 0$ is a **local maximum** with $f(0) = 1$, the point $x = 2$ is a **local minimum** with $f(2) = -3$.

::grafico{f="x^3-3*x^2+1" xmin=-1.5 xmax=3.5 ymin=-4 ymax=3 punti="0,1,pieno;2,-3,pieno" titolo="$y = x^3 - 3x^2 + 1$: increasing up to the local maximum $(0, 1)$, decreasing down to the local minimum $(2, -3)$, then increasing again"}

<div class="spiegazione">

The function is a polynomial, therefore it is differentiable on $\mathbb{R}$ and its monotonicity is read from the **sign of the derivative**. The derivative factors as $3x(x - 2)$ and the product is positive when the two factors have the *same* sign, that is, for $x < 0$ or $x > 2$; it is negative between the two roots. By the **monotonicity theorem** the function is strictly increasing where $f' > 0$ and strictly decreasing where $f' < 0$.

At $x = 0$ the function goes from increasing to decreasing, therefore the point is a **local maximum**; at $x = 2$ it goes from decreasing to increasing, therefore the point is a **local minimum**. Both are stationary points, in agreement with **Fermat's theorem**. The extrema are *only* local, since as $x \to \pm\infty$ the function tends to $\pm\infty$ and has neither an absolute maximum nor an absolute minimum.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Prove that for every $a, b \in \mathbb{R}$
$$
|\sin a - \sin b| \leq |a - b| .
$$

For $a = b$ the inequality is the equality $0 = 0$. For $a \neq b$, by **Lagrange's theorem** on the interval with endpoints $a$ and $b$:
$$
\sin a - \sin b = \cos(c)\,(a - b) \qquad \text{for some } c \text{ between } a \text{ and } b
$$

$$
|\sin a - \sin b| = |\cos c| \cdot |a - b| \leq 1 \cdot |a - b| = |a - b|
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The sine function is differentiable on all of $\mathbb{R}$, therefore the hypotheses of **Lagrange's theorem** are **satisfied** on every closed interval, in particular on the one with endpoints $a$ and $b$. The theorem provides a point $c$ interior to the interval at which the difference of the values can be written as derivative times length, that is, $\sin a - \sin b = \cos(c)(a - b)$. Taking absolute values and using the **boundedness of the cosine**, $|\cos c| \leq 1$, one obtains the desired inequality. The point $c$ is *not* known, and the proof does not require it: an upper bound for the derivative on the whole interval is enough. The same scheme proves every estimate of the form $|f(a) - f(b)| \leq K\,|a - b|$ with $|f'| \leq K$.

</div>
