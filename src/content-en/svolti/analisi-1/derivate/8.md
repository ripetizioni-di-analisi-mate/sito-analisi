## Exercise 1

Write the Maclaurin expansion of order $4$ of $f(x) = e^{x^2}$.

With $t = x^2$ and $e^{t} = 1 + t + \dfrac{t^2}{2} + o(t^2)$:

$$
e^{x^2} = 1 + x^2 + \frac{x^4}{2} + o(x^4)
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The function is the **composition** of the exponential with $t = x^2$. As $x \to 0$ also $t \to 0$, therefore $t = x^2$ can be substituted into the expansion of $e^{t}$. Each power of $t$ **doubles the order** in $x$: the term $t$ becomes $x^2$, the term $\dfrac{t^2}{2}$ becomes $\dfrac{x^4}{2}$ and the remainder $o(t^2)$ becomes $o(x^4)$. To reach order $4$ in $x$ the expansion of $e^{t}$ to **second order** in $t$ is therefore enough; expanding $e^{t}$ further would produce *only* terms of order higher than $x^4$, absorbed by the remainder.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Write the Maclaurin expansion of order $3$ of $f(x) = \ln(1 + \sin x)$.

Expansions needed:
$$
\sin x = x - \frac{x^3}{6} + o(x^3) , \qquad
\ln(1 + t) = t - \frac{t^2}{2} + \frac{t^3}{3} + o(t^3)
$$

Powers of $t = \sin x$ up to order $3$:
$$
t = x - \frac{x^3}{6} + o(x^3) , \qquad
t^2 = x^2 + o(x^3) , \qquad
t^3 = x^3 + o(x^3)
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\ln(1 + \sin x)
&= \left( x - \frac{x^3}{6} \right) - \frac{x^2}{2} + \frac{x^3}{3} + o(x^3) \\[6pt]
&= x - \frac{x^2}{2} + \frac{x^3}{6} + o(x^3)
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The **inner function** is $t = \sin x$, which tends to $0$ as $x \to 0$, therefore the expansion of $\ln(1 + t)$ can be composed with that of the sine. The powers of $t$ up to order $3$ in $x$ are needed. In the square $t^2 = \left( x - \dfrac{x^3}{6} + o(x^3) \right)^2$ the term $x \cdot x$ gives $x^2$, while the cross products are of order *at least* $4$, hence $t^2 = x^2 + o(x^3)$; in the cube the *only* useful term is $x^3$. Substituting the three powers into the expansion of the logarithm, the coefficient of $x^3$ comes from the sum $-\dfrac{1}{6} + \dfrac{1}{3} = \dfrac{1}{6}$, and the **required expansion** is $x - \dfrac{x^2}{2} + \dfrac{x^3}{6} + o(x^3)$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Approximate $\sin \dfrac{1}{2}$ with the Maclaurin polynomial of order $3$ of the sine and bound the error with the Lagrange remainder.

$$
T_3(x) = x - \frac{x^3}{6} , \qquad
T_3\!\left( \frac{1}{2} \right) = \frac{1}{2} - \frac{1}{48} = \frac{23}{48}
$$

Lagrange remainder with $n = 3$ and $f^{(4)}(c) = \sin c$:
$$
\left| \sin \frac{1}{2} - \frac{23}{48} \right|
= \frac{|\sin c|}{4!} \left( \frac{1}{2} \right)^{4}
\leq \frac{1}{24} \cdot \frac{1}{16}
= \frac{1}{384}
$$

$$
\sin \frac{1}{2} \approx \frac{23}{48} \approx 0{,}479 \qquad \text{with error less than } \frac{1}{384} < 0{,}003
$$

::grafico{f="sin(x);x-x^3/6" xmin=-4 xmax=4 ymin=-2 ymax=2 punti="0.5,0.4794,pieno" segmenti="0.5,0,0.5,0.4794" titolo="$y = \sin x$ and the polynomial $T_3(x) = x - \dfrac{x^3}{6}$ (dashed): at $x = \dfrac{1}{2}$ the difference between the two values is at most $\dfrac{1}{384}$"}

<div class="spiegazione">

The Maclaurin polynomial of order $3$ of the sine is $x - \dfrac{x^3}{6}$ and at $x = \dfrac{1}{2}$ it equals $\dfrac{1}{2} - \dfrac{1}{48} = \dfrac{23}{48}$. **Taylor's formula with Lagrange remainder** says that the error is $\dfrac{f^{(4)}(c)}{4!} \left( \dfrac{1}{2} \right)^{4}$ for some point $c$ between $0$ and $\dfrac{1}{2}$. The fourth derivative of the sine is the sine itself, therefore $|f^{(4)}(c)| \leq 1$ and the error is **bounded** by $\dfrac{1}{384}$, *without* knowing $c$.

The estimate can be **refined** by observing that the coefficient of $x^4$ in the expansion of the sine is **zero**, therefore $T_3 = T_4$ and the same formula with $n = 4$ bounds the error by $\dfrac{1}{5!} \left( \dfrac{1}{2} \right)^{5} = \dfrac{1}{3840}$. The value $\sin \dfrac{1}{2} = 0{,}4794\ldots$ confirms *both* bounds, since $\dfrac{23}{48} = 0{,}4792\ldots$ differs from it by less than $\dfrac{3}{10000}$.

</div>
