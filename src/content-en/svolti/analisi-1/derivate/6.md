## Exercise 1

Compute
$$
\lim_{x \to 1} \frac{\ln x}{x^2 - 1} .
$$

As $x \to 1$ the numerator and the denominator tend to $0$: form $\dfrac{0}{0}$. The functions are differentiable near $1$ and the derivative of the denominator, $2x$, does *not* vanish in a neighborhood of $1$.

$$
\lim_{x \to 1} \frac{\dfrac{1}{x}}{2x} = \lim_{x \to 1} \frac{1}{2x^2} = \frac{1}{2}
$$

$$
\lim_{x \to 1} \frac{\ln x}{x^2 - 1} = \frac{1}{2}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

As $x \to 1$ we have $\ln x \to \ln 1 = 0$ and $x^2 - 1 \to 0$, therefore the ratio is in the **indeterminate form** $\dfrac{0}{0}$ and one checks the hypotheses of **L'Hôpital's rule**: the two functions are differentiable in a neighborhood of $1$ and the derivative of the denominator equals $2x$, which is different from $0$ near $1$. The ratio of the derivatives is $\dfrac{1}{2x^2}$, a function continuous at $1$, hence its limit is computed by substitution and equals $\dfrac{1}{2}$. Since the limit of the ratio of the derivatives exists, the theorem **applies** and the original limit also equals $\dfrac{1}{2}$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Compute
$$
\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{x - \sin x}{x^3} .
$$

Form $\dfrac{0}{0}$; applying **L'Hôpital's rule** *three times*, each time to a form $\dfrac{0}{0}$ whose denominator has nonzero derivative near $0$:

$$
\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{1 - \cos x}{3x^2}
\quad\longrightarrow\quad
\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sin x}{6x}
\quad\longrightarrow\quad
\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\cos x}{6} = \frac{1}{6}
$$

$$
\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{x - \sin x}{x^3} = \frac{1}{6}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

As $x \to 0$ the numerator $x - \sin x$ and the denominator $x^3$ both tend to $0$. The ratio of the derivatives is $\dfrac{1 - \cos x}{3x^2}$, again in the form $\dfrac{0}{0}$, therefore the theorem applies *again* and one passes to $\dfrac{\sin x}{6x}$, also in the form $\dfrac{0}{0}$. A third application leads to $\dfrac{\cos x}{6}$, which is continuous at $0$ and tends to $\dfrac{1}{6}$. Since the last limit exists, the applications of the theorem are **justified backwards**: each step inherits the existence of the limit from the next one, hence the original limit equals $\dfrac{1}{6}$. Alternatively, after the second application the **fundamental limit** $\dfrac{\sin x}{x} \to 1$ directly gives $\dfrac{1}{6}$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Compute
$$
\lim_{x \to 0^{+}} x \ln x .
$$

Form $0 \cdot \infty$; the product is rewritten as a **quotient**:
$$
x \ln x = \frac{\ln x}{\dfrac{1}{x}} \qquad \text{form } \frac{-\infty}{+\infty}
$$

$$
\lim_{x \to 0^{+}} \frac{\dfrac{1}{x}}{-\dfrac{1}{x^2}} = \lim_{x \to 0^{+}} (-x) = 0
$$

$$
\lim_{x \to 0^{+}} x \ln x = 0
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

As $x \to 0^{+}$ the factor $x$ tends to $0$ and $\ln x$ tends to $-\infty$, therefore the product is in the **indeterminate form** $0 \cdot \infty$, to which L'Hôpital's rule does *not* apply directly. The product is rewritten as a quotient by moving $x$ to the denominator in the form $\dfrac{1}{x}$: the ratio of $\ln x$ to $\dfrac{1}{x}$ becomes a form $\dfrac{\infty}{\infty}$, with functions differentiable on $(0, +\infty)$ and denominator derivative $-\dfrac{1}{x^2}$ never zero. The ratio of the derivatives simplifies to $-x$, which tends to $0$, hence the theorem **applies** and the limit equals $0$. The result reflects the **hierarchy of infinities**: the logarithm diverges more slowly than every power of $x$.

</div>
