## Exercise 1

Compute the derivative of
$$
F(x) = \int_0^{x^2} e^{-t^2}\, dt .
$$

$$
F'(x) = e^{-\left( x^2 \right)^2} \cdot 2x = 2x\, e^{-x^4}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The integrand $e^{-t^2}$ is continuous on $\mathbb{R}$ and the upper limit is the differentiable function $\beta(x) = x^2$, therefore the **differentiation formula with variable limits** applies. The lower limit is constant and gives **zero contribution**, hence the derivative is the integrand evaluated at $\beta(x) = x^2$, multiplied by $\beta'(x) = 2x$. The antiderivative of $e^{-t^2}$ *cannot* be written in terms of elementary functions, therefore the derivative is obtained *only* in this way, without computing the integral.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Compute the derivative of
$$
G(x) = \int_{x}^{x^3} \frac{1}{1 + t^4}\, dt .
$$

$$
G'(x) = \frac{1}{1 + \left( x^3 \right)^4} \cdot 3x^2 - \frac{1}{1 + x^4} \cdot 1
= \frac{3x^2}{1 + x^{12}} - \frac{1}{1 + x^4}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The integrand is continuous on $\mathbb{R}$ because the denominator $1 + t^4$ never vanishes, and the two limits $\alpha(x) = x$ and $\beta(x) = x^3$ are differentiable, therefore the **formula with variable limits** applies. The derivative is the integrand evaluated at the upper limit, multiplied by the derivative $3x^2$ of the upper limit, *minus* the integrand evaluated at the lower limit, multiplied by the derivative $1$ of the lower limit. The evaluation at $x^3$ gives $\left( x^3 \right)^4 = x^{12}$ in the denominator.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Compute
$$
\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\displaystyle\int_0^{x} \sin\left( t^2 \right) dt}{x^3} .
$$

The numerator and the denominator are differentiable, both tend to $0$ as $x \to 0$, and the denominator has derivative $3x^2 \neq 0$ for $x \neq 0$. By **L'Hôpital's rule** and the **fundamental theorem of calculus**
$$
\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\displaystyle\int_0^{x} \sin\left( t^2 \right) dt}{x^3}
= \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sin\left( x^2 \right)}{3x^2}
= \frac{1}{3} ,
$$
where the last step uses the standard limit of the sine with argument $x^2 \to 0$.

<div class="spiegazione">

The integral function in the numerator is continuous and equals $0$ at $x = 0$, because the interval of integration reduces to a point, therefore the limit is an **indeterminate form** of the type $\dfrac{0}{0}$ and the hypotheses of L'Hôpital's rule are satisfied. The derivative of the numerator is computed with the **fundamental theorem**, since $\sin\left( t^2 \right)$ is continuous: it equals $\sin\left( x^2 \right)$. The derivative of the denominator is $3x^2$. The new ratio is $\dfrac{\sin\left( x^2 \right)}{3x^2}$ and, since $x^2 \to 0$, the standard limit $\dfrac{\sin u}{u} \to 1$ with $u = x^2$ gives $\dfrac{1}{3}$. The limit of the ratio of the derivatives *exists*, therefore the theorem applies and the original limit also **equals** $\dfrac{1}{3}$.

</div>
