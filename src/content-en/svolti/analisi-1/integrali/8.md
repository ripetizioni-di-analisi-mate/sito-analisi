## Exercise 1

Compute
$$
\int_{-1}^{2} |x|\, dx .
$$

By the definition of absolute value
$$
|x| =
\begin{cases}
-x & \text{if } x < 0 \\[4pt]
x & \text{if } x \geq 0
\end{cases}
$$
therefore, splitting the interval at $x = 0$,
$$
\int_{-1}^{2} |x|\, dx
= \int_{-1}^{0} (-x)\, dx + \int_0^2 x\, dx
= \left[ -\frac{x^2}{2} \right]_{-1}^{0} + \left[ \frac{x^2}{2} \right]_0^2
= \frac{1}{2} + 2
= \frac{5}{2} .
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The expression of $|x|$ changes at $x = 0$, which belongs to the interval of integration, therefore by **additivity** the integral is split at $x = 0$. On $[-1, 0]$ the absolute value equals $-x$ and on $[0, 2]$ it equals $x$; each integral is computed with the fundamental formula. The first equals $0 - \left( -\dfrac{1}{2} \right) = \dfrac{1}{2}$ and the second equals $2$, hence the sum is $\dfrac{5}{2}$. *Both* contributions are **positive**, because $|x| \geq 0$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Compute
$$
\int_0^2 \left| x^2 - 1 \right| dx .
$$

On $[0, 2]$ the sign of $x^2 - 1$ changes at $x = 1$: the expression is negative on $[0, 1)$ and positive on $(1, 2]$, therefore
$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_0^2 \left| x^2 - 1 \right| dx
&= \int_0^1 \left( 1 - x^2 \right) dx + \int_1^2 \left( x^2 - 1 \right) dx \\[6pt]
&= \left[ x - \frac{x^3}{3} \right]_0^1 + \left[ \frac{x^3}{3} - x \right]_1^2 \\[6pt]
&= \frac{2}{3} + \left( \frac{8}{3} - 2 \right) - \left( \frac{1}{3} - 1 \right)
= \frac{2}{3} + \frac{2}{3} + \frac{2}{3}
= 2 .
\end{aligned}
$$

::grafico{f="abs(x^2-1);x^2-1+0*sqrt(1-x^2)" area="abs(x^2-1);0;0;2" xmin=-0.6 xmax=2.6 ymin=-1.3 ymax=3.5 titolo="On $[0, 2]$ the graph of $y = |x^2 - 1|$ is obtained by reflecting above the $x$-axis the negative stretch of $y = x^2 - 1$ (dashed); the colored area equals $2$"}

<div class="spiegazione">

To remove the absolute value one **studies the sign** of $x^2 - 1$ on the interval of integration: the trinomial vanishes at $x = \pm 1$ and is *negative* between the roots, therefore on $[0, 1)$ we have $\left| x^2 - 1 \right| = 1 - x^2$ and on $(1, 2]$ we have $\left| x^2 - 1 \right| = x^2 - 1$. By **additivity** the integral is split at $x = 1$ and each stretch is computed with the fundamental formula. The first integral equals $\dfrac{2}{3}$; the second equals $\dfrac{2}{3} - \left( -\dfrac{2}{3} \right) = \dfrac{4}{3}$, therefore the total is $2$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Compute
$$
\int_0^{\pi} |\cos x|\, dx .
$$

On $\left[ 0, \dfrac{\pi}{2} \right]$ the cosine is nonnegative and on $\left[ \dfrac{\pi}{2}, \pi \right]$ it is nonpositive, therefore
$$
\int_0^{\pi} |\cos x|\, dx
= \int_0^{\frac{\pi}{2}} \cos x\, dx - \int_{\frac{\pi}{2}}^{\pi} \cos x\, dx
= \bigl[ \sin x \bigr]_0^{\frac{\pi}{2}} - \bigl[ \sin x \bigr]_{\frac{\pi}{2}}^{\pi}
= 1 - (0 - 1)
= 2 .
$$

::grafico{f="abs(cos(x));cos(x)+0*sqrt(-cos(x))" area="abs(cos(x));0;0;3.1416" xmin=-0.5 xmax=3.8 ymin=-1.3 ymax=1.4 titolo="Under $y = |\cos x|$ on $[0, \pi]$ there are two arches of area $1$; the dashed one is $y = \cos x$, whose integral on $[0, \pi]$ equals $0$"}

<div class="spiegazione">

The cosine vanishes at $x = \dfrac{\pi}{2}$ and **changes sign** at that point, therefore the interval is split using additivity. On the first stretch the absolute value coincides with $\cos x$; on the second stretch it equals $-\cos x$, hence a **minus sign** appears in front of the second integral. An antiderivative of $\cos x$ is $\sin x$ and the evaluation at the limits gives $1$ for the first stretch and $-(0 - 1) = 1$ for the second, therefore the integral equals $2$. *Without* the absolute value the integral of $\cos x$ on $[0, \pi]$ would equal $0$, because the two contributions would be opposite.

</div>
