## Exercise 1

Compute
$$
\int \frac{e^{\sqrt{x}}}{\sqrt{x}}\, dx .
$$

With the substitution $t = \sqrt{x}$ we have $dt = \dfrac{1}{2\sqrt{x}}\, dx$, that is $\dfrac{dx}{\sqrt{x}} = 2\, dt$, therefore
$$
\int \frac{e^{\sqrt{x}}}{\sqrt{x}}\, dx
= 2 \int e^{t}\, dt
= 2\, e^{t} + c
= 2\, e^{\sqrt{x}} + c .
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The integrand is a function of $\sqrt{x}$ accompanied by the factor $\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{x}}$, which up to the constant $2$ is the **derivative** of $\sqrt{x}$. We therefore set $t = \sqrt{x}$ and express the differential: from $dt = \dfrac{dx}{2\sqrt{x}}$ it follows that $\dfrac{dx}{\sqrt{x}}$ is replaced by $2\, dt$. The integral in the variable $t$ is immediate and at the end one **returns to the variable** $x$ with $t = \sqrt{x}$. Differentiating $2\, e^{\sqrt{x}}$ gives $2\, e^{\sqrt{x}} \cdot \dfrac{1}{2\sqrt{x}} = \dfrac{e^{\sqrt{x}}}{\sqrt{x}}$, therefore the result is **correct**.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Compute
$$
\int \frac{x}{\sqrt{x+1}}\, dx .
$$

With the substitution $t = \sqrt{x+1}$ we have $x = t^2 - 1$ and $dx = 2t\, dt$, therefore
$$
\begin{aligned}
\int \frac{x}{\sqrt{x+1}}\, dx
&= \int \frac{t^2 - 1}{t} \cdot 2t\, dt
= 2 \int (t^2 - 1)\, dt \\[6pt]
&= \frac{2}{3}\, t^3 - 2t + c \\[6pt]
&= \frac{2}{3}\, (x+1)\sqrt{x+1} - 2\sqrt{x+1} + c
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The root is **eliminated** with the substitution $t = \sqrt{x+1}$, also written in the *inverse form* $x = t^2 - 1$, from which $dx = 2t\, dt$. After the substitution the factor $t$ in the denominator cancels with the factor $2t$ of the differential, therefore what remains is the **integral of the polynomial** $2(t^2 - 1)$, which is computed with the table. To return to the variable $x$ one substitutes $t = \sqrt{x+1}$, and $t^3$ becomes $(x+1)\sqrt{x+1}$. Differentiating the result gives $\sqrt{x+1} - \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{x+1}} = \dfrac{(x+1) - 1}{\sqrt{x+1}} = \dfrac{x}{\sqrt{x+1}}$, which is the integrand.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Compute
$$
\int \sqrt{1 - x^2}\, dx .
$$

With the inverse substitution $x = \sin t$, $t \in \left[ -\dfrac{\pi}{2},\ \dfrac{\pi}{2} \right]$, we have $dx = \cos t\, dt$ and $\sqrt{1 - \sin^2 t} = \cos t$, therefore
$$
\int \sqrt{1 - x^2}\, dx
= \int \cos^2 t\, dt
= \int \frac{1 + \cos 2t}{2}\, dt
= \frac{t}{2} + \frac{\sin 2t}{4} + c
= \frac{t + \sin t \cos t}{2} + c .
$$

Returning to the variable $x$ with $t = \arcsin x$, $\sin t = x$ and $\cos t = \sqrt{1 - x^2}$:
$$
\int \sqrt{1 - x^2}\, dx = \frac{\arcsin x + x \sqrt{1 - x^2}}{2} + c .
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The root $\sqrt{1 - x^2}$ suggests the **inverse substitution** $x = \sin t$, because $1 - \sin^2 t = \cos^2 t$. On the interval chosen for $t$ the cosine is nonnegative, therefore $\sqrt{\cos^2 t} = \cos t$ *without* absolute value. The integral of $\cos^2 t$ is computed with the **half-angle formula** $\cos^2 t = \dfrac{1 + \cos 2t}{2}$, and the integral of $\cos 2t$ is $\dfrac{\sin 2t}{2}$ because the derivative of $\sin 2t$ is $2 \cos 2t$. With the **double-angle formula** $\sin 2t = 2 \sin t \cos t$ the result is rewritten in terms of $\sin t$ and $\cos t$. The substitution is invertible on the chosen interval, therefore $t = \arcsin x$; since $\sin t = x$, we have $\cos t = \sqrt{1 - x^2}$ and the result returns to the variable $x$.

</div>
