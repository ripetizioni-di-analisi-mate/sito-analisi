## Exercise 1

Compute
$$
\int_0^1 x \left( x^2 + 1 \right)^3 dx .
$$

With the substitution $t = x^2 + 1$ we have $dt = 2x\, dx$; for $x = 0$ we get $t = 1$ and for $x = 1$ we get $t = 2$, therefore
$$
\int_0^1 x \left( x^2 + 1 \right)^3 dx
= \frac{1}{2} \int_1^2 t^3\, dt
= \frac{1}{2} \left[ \frac{t^4}{4} \right]_1^2
= \frac{16 - 1}{8}
= \frac{15}{8} .
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The factor $x$ appears in the integrand, and up to the constant $2$ it is the derivative of $x^2 + 1$, therefore the substitution $t = x^2 + 1$ transforms the integral into an **immediate** one. Since the integral is definite, together with the variable **the limits are transformed**: one substitutes the values $x = 0$ and $x = 1$ into $t = x^2 + 1$ and obtains the new limits $1$ and $2$. The computation is completed in the variable $t$ with the fundamental formula, *without* returning to the variable $x$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Compute
$$
\int_0^{\frac{\pi}{2}} e^{\sin x} \cos x\, dx .
$$

With the substitution $t = \sin x$ we have $dt = \cos x\, dx$; for $x = 0$ we get $t = 0$ and for $x = \dfrac{\pi}{2}$ we get $t = 1$, therefore
$$
\int_0^{\frac{\pi}{2}} e^{\sin x} \cos x\, dx
= \int_0^1 e^t\, dt
= \bigl[ e^t \bigr]_0^1
= e - 1 .
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The factor $\cos x$ is the **derivative** of $\sin x$, therefore the substitution $t = \sin x$ absorbs the differential *exactly*. The new limits are computed by evaluating $\sin x$ at the original limits: $\sin 0 = 0$ and $\sin \dfrac{\pi}{2} = 1$. What remains is the integral of $e^t$ between $0$ and $1$, which by the **fundamental formula** equals $e^1 - e^0 = e - 1$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Compute
$$
\int_0^4 \frac{1}{1 + \sqrt{x}}\, dx .
$$

With the substitution $t = \sqrt{x}$, that is $x = t^2$, we have $dx = 2t\, dt$; for $x = 0$ we get $t = 0$ and for $x = 4$ we get $t = 2$, therefore
$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_0^4 \frac{1}{1 + \sqrt{x}}\, dx
&= \int_0^2 \frac{2t}{1 + t}\, dt
= \int_0^2 \left( 2 - \frac{2}{1 + t} \right) dt \\[6pt]
&= \bigl[ 2t - 2 \ln(1 + t) \bigr]_0^2
= 4 - 2 \ln 3 .
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The root is eliminated with the substitution $t = \sqrt{x}$, which on the interval $[0, 4]$ is **invertible** with inverse $x = t^2$, from which $dx = 2t\, dt$ and new limits $0$ and $2$. The integrand in the variable $t$ is the rational function $\dfrac{2t}{1 + t}$, with numerator and denominator of the *same degree*: one writes $2t = 2(1 + t) - 2$ and the ratio splits into $2 - \dfrac{2}{1 + t}$. The first term gives $2t$ and the second gives $2 \ln(1 + t)$, **without absolute value** because $1 + t > 0$ on the interval of integration. The evaluation at the limits gives $(4 - 2 \ln 3) - (0 - 2 \ln 1) = 4 - 2 \ln 3$.

</div>
