## Exercise 1

Compute
$$
\int x\, e^x\, dx .
$$

$$
\int x\, e^x\, dx
= x\, e^x - \int 1 \cdot e^x\, dx
= x\, e^x - e^x + c
= (x - 1)\, e^x + c
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The integrand is the product of a polynomial and an exponential, therefore one **integrates by parts** differentiating the polynomial: we set $f(x) = x$ and $g'(x) = e^x$, from which $f'(x) = 1$ and $g(x) = e^x$. The new integral is that of $e^x$, which is immediate. Differentiating $(x - 1)\, e^x$ gives $e^x + (x - 1)\, e^x = x\, e^x$, therefore the result is **correct**.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Compute
$$
\int x \ln x\, dx .
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\int x \ln x\, dx
&= \frac{x^2}{2} \ln x - \int \frac{x^2}{2} \cdot \frac{1}{x}\, dx \\[6pt]
&= \frac{x^2}{2} \ln x - \frac{1}{2} \int x\, dx \\[6pt]
&= \frac{x^2}{2} \ln x - \frac{x^2}{4} + c
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

When the logarithm appears it is convenient to **differentiate it**, because its derivative $\dfrac{1}{x}$ is a *rational* function. We therefore set $f(x) = \ln x$ and $g'(x) = x$, from which $f'(x) = \dfrac{1}{x}$ and $g(x) = \dfrac{x^2}{2}$. In the new integral the product $\dfrac{x^2}{2} \cdot \dfrac{1}{x}$ simplifies to $\dfrac{x}{2}$, hence what remains is the immediate integral of $x$ multiplied by $\dfrac{1}{2}$, which equals $\dfrac{x^2}{4}$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Compute
$$
\int e^x \sin x\, dx .
$$

Setting $I = \displaystyle\int e^x \sin x\, dx$ and integrating by parts twice, we obtain
$$
\begin{aligned}
I
&= e^x \sin x - \int e^x \cos x\, dx \\[6pt]
&= e^x \sin x - \left( e^x \cos x + \int e^x \sin x\, dx \right) \\[6pt]
&= e^x (\sin x - \cos x) - I .
\end{aligned}
$$

From the equality $I = e^x (\sin x - \cos x) - I$ it follows that $2I = e^x (\sin x - \cos x)$, therefore
$$
\int e^x \sin x\, dx = \frac{e^x (\sin x - \cos x)}{2} + c .
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

In the first integration by parts we set $f(x) = \sin x$ and $g'(x) = e^x$, therefore the integral of $e^x \cos x$ appears. In this one we set $f(x) = \cos x$ and $g'(x) = e^x$; since the derivative of $\cos x$ is $-\sin x$, in the integral of $e^x \cos x$ **the original integral** $I$ **reappears**, added to $e^x \cos x$. The equality obtained is an **equation in the unknown** $I$: moving $I$ to the left-hand side gives $2I = e^x(\sin x - \cos x)$ and one divides by $2$. The constant $c$ is added *at the end*, because the result represents the set of all antiderivatives.

</div>
