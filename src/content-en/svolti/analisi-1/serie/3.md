## Exercise 1

Study the behavior of the series
$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \sin \frac{1}{n} .
$$

$$
\frac{1}{n} \to 0, \qquad
\lim_{n \to +\infty} \frac{\sin \dfrac{1}{n}}{\dfrac{1}{n}} = 1
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\sin \frac{1}{n} \sim \frac{1}{n}
$$
$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{n} \ \text{diverges}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \sin \frac{1}{n} \ \text{diverges to } +\infty
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

For $n \geq 1$ one has $0 < \dfrac{1}{n} \leq 1 < \pi$, therefore $\sin \dfrac{1}{n} > 0$ and **limit comparison** applies. Since $\dfrac{1}{n} \to 0$, the **standard limit of the sine** with the substitution $y = \dfrac{1}{n}$ gives $\sin \dfrac{1}{n} \sim \dfrac{1}{n}$. The two series then have the same behavior; the harmonic series diverges, therefore the given series **diverges** to $+\infty$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Study the behavior of the series
$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \left( 1 - \cos \frac{1}{n} \right) .
$$

$$
\lim_{n \to +\infty} \frac{1 - \cos \dfrac{1}{n}}{\dfrac{1}{n^2}} = \frac{1}{2}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
1 - \cos \frac{1}{n} \sim \frac{1}{2n^2}
$$
$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{n^2} \ \text{converges}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \left( 1 - \cos \frac{1}{n} \right) \ \text{converges}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The general term is *nonnegative*, because the cosine does not exceed $1$. Since $\dfrac{1}{n} \to 0$, the standard limit $\dfrac{1 - \cos y}{y^2} \to \dfrac{1}{2}$ with $y = \dfrac{1}{n}$ gives $1 - \cos \dfrac{1}{n} \sim \dfrac{1}{2n^2}$. **Limit comparison** with the generalized harmonic series of exponent $\alpha = 2$ applies, and since $\alpha = 2 > 1$ that series converges; hence the given series also **converges**.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Study the behavior of the series
$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \left( \sqrt[n]{n} - 1 \right) .
$$

$$
\sqrt[n]{n} - 1 = e^{\frac{\ln n}{n}} - 1, \qquad \frac{\ln n}{n} \to 0
$$
$$
\lim_{n \to +\infty} \frac{e^{\frac{\ln n}{n}} - 1}{\dfrac{\ln n}{n}} = 1
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\sqrt[n]{n} - 1 \sim \frac{\ln n}{n}
$$
$$
\frac{\ln n}{n} \geq \frac{1}{n} \ \text{for } n \geq 3,
\qquad
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{n} \ \text{diverges}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \left( \sqrt[n]{n} - 1 \right) \ \text{diverges to } +\infty
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The root is rewritten in **exponential form**, $\sqrt[n]{n} = e^{\frac{\ln n}{n}}$, and one observes that the exponent $\dfrac{\ln n}{n}$ tends to $0$ by the **hierarchy of infinities**. The standard limit $\dfrac{e^y - 1}{y} \to 1$ with $y = \dfrac{\ln n}{n}$ therefore gives $\sqrt[n]{n} - 1 \sim \dfrac{\ln n}{n}$, and for $n \geq 2$ both quantities are positive.

It remains to establish the behavior of $\displaystyle\sum \frac{\ln n}{n}$. For $n \geq 3$ one has $\ln n \geq 1$, therefore $\dfrac{\ln n}{n} \geq \dfrac{1}{n}$; the harmonic series diverges, hence by the **comparison test** $\displaystyle\sum \frac{\ln n}{n}$ also diverges. By limit comparison the given series has the same behavior and **diverges** to $+\infty$.

</div>
