## Exercise 1

Study the absolute convergence and the convergence of the series
$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^n}{\sqrt{n}} .
$$

$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \left| \frac{(-1)^n}{\sqrt{n}} \right|
= \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{n^{\frac{1}{2}}}
\quad \text{diverges} \ \left( \alpha = \frac{1}{2} \leq 1 \right)
$$
$$
b_n = \frac{1}{\sqrt{n}} :
\qquad
\begin{cases}
b_n \geq 0 \\[4pt]
b_{n+1} \leq b_n \\[4pt]
b_n \to 0
\end{cases}
$$
The series **converges conditionally**. By the error estimate, $|s - s_N| \leq \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{N+1}}$.

<div class="spiegazione">

The series of absolute values is the generalized harmonic series with exponent $\alpha = \dfrac{1}{2}$, which diverges because $\alpha \leq 1$; therefore there is *no* absolute convergence. The series is alternating with $b_n = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{n}}$: the sequence is positive, decreasing because the square root is increasing and lies in the denominator, and infinitesimal. The three hypotheses of the **Leibniz test** are satisfied, hence the series converges; the convergence is **conditional**. The error estimate says that, stopping the sum at the $N$-th term, the error does not exceed the *first neglected term* $b_{N+1}$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Study the absolute convergence and the convergence of the series
$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} (-1)^n\, \frac{n^2}{2^n} .
$$

$$
\left| a_n \right| = \frac{n^2}{2^n},
\qquad
\frac{|a_{n+1}|}{|a_n|} = \frac{(n+1)^2}{2\, n^2}
\xrightarrow[n \to +\infty]{} \frac{1}{2} < 1
$$
The series **converges absolutely**, therefore it converges.

<div class="spiegazione">

One studies the series of absolute values *first*, which has positive terms with an $n$-th power: the **ratio test** is the suitable one. The ratio equals $\dfrac{(n+1)^2}{2n^2}$ and tends to $\dfrac{1}{2}$, because $\dfrac{(n+1)^2}{n^2} \to 1$. Since the limit is less than $1$, the series of absolute values converges, that is, the given series **converges absolutely**. Absolute convergence implies convergence, therefore the Leibniz test is *not* needed.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Study the absolute convergence and the convergence of the series
$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} (-1)^n\, \frac{n}{n^2 + 1} .
$$

$$
|a_n| = \frac{n}{n^2 + 1} \sim \frac{1}{n}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} |a_n| \ \text{diverges}
$$
$$
f(x) = \frac{x}{x^2 + 1}, \qquad
f'(x) = \frac{1 - x^2}{(x^2 + 1)^2} \leq 0 \ \text{for } x \geq 1
$$
$$
b_n = \frac{n}{n^2 + 1} :
\qquad
\begin{cases}
b_n \geq 0 \\[4pt]
b_{n+1} \leq b_n \ \text{for } n \geq 1 \\[4pt]
b_n \to 0
\end{cases}
$$
The series **converges conditionally**.

<div class="spiegazione">

The absolute value of the general term is asymptotic to $\dfrac{1}{n}$, because $\dfrac{n}{n^2+1} \cdot n = \dfrac{n^2}{n^2+1} \to 1$; the harmonic series diverges, therefore by **limit comparison** the series of absolute values diverges and there is *no* absolute convergence.

One then checks the hypotheses of the **Leibniz test** for $b_n = \dfrac{n}{n^2+1}$. Positivity and the zero limit are immediate, because the numerator has lower degree than the denominator. For monotonicity one studies the function $f(x) = \dfrac{x}{x^2+1}$: the derivative has numerator $1 - x^2$, which is less than or equal to $0$ for $x \geq 1$, therefore $f$ is decreasing on $[1, +\infty)$ and the sequence $b_n = f(n)$ is decreasing from $n = 1$. The three hypotheses are satisfied, hence the series **converges**, and the convergence is **conditional**.

</div>
