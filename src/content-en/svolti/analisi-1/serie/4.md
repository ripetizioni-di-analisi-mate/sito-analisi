## Exercise 1

Study the behavior of the series
$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{5^n}{n!} .
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n}
&= \frac{5^{n+1}}{(n+1)!} \cdot \frac{n!}{5^n} \\[6pt]
&= \frac{5}{n+1} \xrightarrow[n \to +\infty]{} 0 < 1
\end{aligned}
$$
The series **converges**.

<div class="spiegazione">

The general term contains an $n$-th power and a factorial, therefore the **ratio test** is applied. In the ratio $\dfrac{a_{n+1}}{a_n}$ the power simplifies, leaving the factor $5$, and from the ratio of the factorials, since $(n+1)! = (n+1) \cdot n!$, what remains is $\dfrac{1}{n+1}$. The limit of the ratio equals $0$, which is less than $1$, therefore the hypothesis of the test is satisfied and the series **converges**.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Study the behavior of the series
$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{n!}{n^n} .
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n}
&= \frac{(n+1)!}{(n+1)^{n+1}} \cdot \frac{n^n}{n!} \\[6pt]
&= \frac{(n+1)\, n^n}{(n+1)^{n+1}}
= \left( \frac{n}{n+1} \right)^{\! n} \\[6pt]
&= \frac{1}{\left( 1 + \dfrac{1}{n} \right)^{\! n}} \xrightarrow[n \to +\infty]{} \frac{1}{e} < 1
\end{aligned}
$$
The series **converges**.

<div class="spiegazione">

The ratio simplifies in two steps. From the quotient of the factorials the factor $n + 1$ remains, which cancels with one of the factors of $(n+1)^{n+1}$ in the denominator; what remains is the ratio $\left( \dfrac{n}{n+1} \right)^{\! n}$, which is the reciprocal of $\left( 1 + \dfrac{1}{n} \right)^{\! n}$. The latter sequence tends to $e$ by the **standard limit**, therefore the ratio tends to $\dfrac{1}{e}$. Since $\dfrac{1}{e} < 1$, the series **converges**.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Study the behavior of the series
$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{(n!)^2}{(2n)!} .
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n}
&= \frac{\bigl( (n+1)! \bigr)^2}{(2n+2)!} \cdot \frac{(2n)!}{(n!)^2} \\[6pt]
&= \frac{(n+1)^2}{(2n+1)(2n+2)} \\[6pt]
&= \frac{n+1}{2(2n+1)} \xrightarrow[n \to +\infty]{} \frac{1}{4} < 1
\end{aligned}
$$
The series **converges**.

<div class="spiegazione">

From the ratio of the factorials in the numerator $(n+1)^2$ remains, because $(n+1)! = (n+1) \cdot n!$ appears squared; in the denominator, since $(2n+2)! = (2n+2)(2n+1) \cdot (2n)!$, the two factors $(2n+1)$ and $(2n+2)$ remain. Canceling $n + 1$ with $2n + 2 = 2(n+1)$ gives $\dfrac{n+1}{2(2n+1)}$, a ratio of first-degree polynomials whose limit is the ratio of the **leading coefficients**, that is, $\dfrac{1}{4}$. Since the limit is *less* than $1$, the series **converges**.

</div>
