## Exercise 1

Study the behavior of the series
$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \left( \frac{n}{2n+1} \right)^{\! n} .
$$

$$
\sqrt[n]{a_n} = \frac{n}{2n+1} \xrightarrow[n \to +\infty]{} \frac{1}{2} < 1
$$
The series **converges**.

<div class="spiegazione">

The general term is an $n$-th power, therefore the **root test** is applied: the $n$-th root removes the exponent and leaves the base $\dfrac{n}{2n+1}$. The limit of the base is the ratio of the leading coefficients of the two first-degree polynomials, that is, $\dfrac{1}{2}$. Since the limit is *less* than $1$, the series **converges**.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Study the behavior of the series
$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{n^3}{3^n} .
$$

$$
\sqrt[n]{a_n} = \frac{\left( \sqrt[n]{n} \right)^3}{3}, \qquad
\sqrt[n]{n} = e^{\frac{\ln n}{n}} \xrightarrow[n \to +\infty]{} e^0 = 1
$$
$$
\sqrt[n]{a_n} \xrightarrow[n \to +\infty]{} \frac{1}{3} < 1
$$
The series **converges**.

<div class="spiegazione">

The $n$-th root of the denominator $3^n$ equals $3$; in the numerator $\left( \sqrt[n]{n} \right)^3$ remains. The sequence $\sqrt[n]{n}$ tends to $1$, because $\sqrt[n]{n} = e^{\frac{\ln n}{n}}$ and the exponent $\dfrac{\ln n}{n}$ tends to $0$ by the **hierarchy of infinities**. Therefore the $n$-th root of the general term tends to $\dfrac{1}{3}$, which is less than $1$, and the series **converges**. The polynomial factor $n^3$ does *not* change the limit of the root: what decides is the base $3$ of the power.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Study the behavior of the series
$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{2^n} \left( 1 + \frac{1}{n} \right)^{\! n^2} .
$$

$$
\sqrt[n]{a_n} = \frac{1}{2} \left( 1 + \frac{1}{n} \right)^{\! n}
\xrightarrow[n \to +\infty]{} \frac{e}{2} > 1
$$
The series **diverges** to $+\infty$.

<div class="spiegazione">

The $n$-th root of $2^n$ equals $2$; the power with exponent $n^2$ has an $n$-th root with exponent $\dfrac{n^2}{n} = n$, therefore $\left( 1 + \dfrac{1}{n} \right)^{\! n}$ remains, which tends to $e$ by the **standard limit**. The limit of the root is therefore $\dfrac{e}{2}$. Since $e > 2$, the limit is *greater* than $1$ and the root test gives **divergence** to $+\infty$. The **necessary condition** leads to the same result, given that with a limit of the root greater than $1$ the general term tends to $+\infty$ and is not infinitesimal.

</div>
