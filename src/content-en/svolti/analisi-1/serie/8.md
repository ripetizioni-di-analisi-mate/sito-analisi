## Exercise 1

Determine the set of convergence of the power series
$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{x^n}{n^2} .
$$

$$
\frac{|c_{n+1}|}{|c_n|} = \frac{n^2}{(n+1)^2}
\xrightarrow[n \to +\infty]{} 1
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad R = 1
$$
$$
x = 1 : \ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{n^2} \ \text{converges};
\qquad
x = -1 : \ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^n}{n^2} \ \text{converges absolutely}
$$
Set of convergence: $[-1, 1]$.

<div class="spiegazione">

The coefficients are $c_n = \dfrac{1}{n^2}$ and the ratio of the absolute values tends to $1$, therefore the **radius of convergence** is $R = 1$ and the series converges absolutely on $(-1, 1)$. At the endpoints one *substitutes the value*: at $x = 1$ one obtains the generalized harmonic series with exponent $2$, which converges; at $x = -1$ the series of absolute values is the same, therefore the convergence is absolute. Both endpoints belong to the set of convergence, which is the **closed interval** $[-1, 1]$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Determine the set of convergence of the power series
$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{n\, x^n}{3^n} .
$$

$$
\frac{|c_{n+1}|}{|c_n|} = \frac{n+1}{3^{n+1}} \cdot \frac{3^n}{n}
= \frac{n+1}{3n}
\xrightarrow[n \to +\infty]{} \frac{1}{3}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad R = 3
$$
$$
x = 3 : \ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} n \ \text{diverges};
\qquad
x = -3 : \ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} (-1)^n\, n \ \text{does not converge}
$$
Set of convergence: $(-3, 3)$.

<div class="spiegazione">

The coefficients are $c_n = \dfrac{n}{3^n}$; in the ratio the power leaves the factor $\dfrac{1}{3}$ and the ratio $\dfrac{n+1}{n}$ tends to $1$, therefore the limit is $\dfrac{1}{3}$ and the radius is its **reciprocal**, $R = 3$. At $x = 3$ the general term becomes $n$, which tends to $+\infty$, and at $x = -3$ it becomes $(-1)^n n$, whose absolute value tends to $+\infty$: at *both* endpoints the necessary condition is not satisfied and the series **does not converge**. The set of convergence is the **open interval** $(-3, 3)$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Determine the set of convergence of the power series
$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{(x-2)^n}{n\, 4^n} .
$$

$$
\frac{|c_{n+1}|}{|c_n|} = \frac{n\, 4^n}{(n+1)\, 4^{n+1}}
= \frac{n}{4(n+1)}
\xrightarrow[n \to +\infty]{} \frac{1}{4}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad R = 4
$$
$$
|x - 2| < 4 \iff -2 < x < 6
$$
$$
x = 6 : \ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{n} \ \text{diverges};
\qquad
x = -2 : \ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^n}{n} \ \text{converges (Leibniz)}
$$
Set of convergence: $[-2, 6)$.

<div class="spiegazione">

The series is **centered** at $x_0 = 2$, with coefficients $c_n = \dfrac{1}{n\, 4^n}$. The ratio of the absolute values tends to $\dfrac{1}{4}$, therefore $R = 4$ and the series converges absolutely for $|x - 2| < 4$, that is, on the interval $(-2, 6)$ with center $2$.

At the endpoints one substitutes. At $x = 6$ one has $(x-2)^n = 4^n$, which cancels with the denominator, and the harmonic series remains, which **diverges**. At $x = -2$ one has $(x-2)^n = (-4)^n = (-1)^n 4^n$ and $\displaystyle\sum \frac{(-1)^n}{n}$ remains: the sequence $\dfrac{1}{n}$ is positive, decreasing and infinitesimal, therefore by the **Leibniz test** the series **converges**. The left endpoint is *included* and the right one is *excluded*, hence the set of convergence is $[-2, 6)$.

</div>
