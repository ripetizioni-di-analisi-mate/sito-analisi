## Exercise 1

Determine the behavior of
$$
\int_0^{+\infty} \frac{dx}{\sqrt{x}\,(1 + x)} .
$$

$$
\int_0^{+\infty} \frac{dx}{\sqrt{x}\,(1 + x)}
= \int_0^{1} \frac{dx}{\sqrt{x}\,(1 + x)} + \int_1^{+\infty} \frac{dx}{\sqrt{x}\,(1 + x)}
$$

$$
\frac{1}{\sqrt{x}\,(1 + x)} \sim \frac{1}{\sqrt{x}} \quad (x \to 0^{+}),
\qquad
\frac{1}{\sqrt{x}\,(1 + x)} \sim \frac{1}{x^{\frac{3}{2}}} \quad (x \to +\infty)
$$

$$
\text{both pieces converge, therefore the integral converges}
$$

::grafico{f="1/(sqrt(x)*(1+x))" area="1/(sqrt(x)*(1+x));0;0.0001;10" segmenti="1,0,1,0.5" xmin=-0.5 xmax=10 ymin=-0.3 ymax=4 titolo="The region under $y = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{x}(1 + x)}$ is unbounded near $x = 0$ and as $x \to +\infty$; the integral is split at $x = 1$ (dashed segment)"}

<div class="spiegazione">

There are *two* critical points, $0$, where the integrand is unbounded, and $+\infty$, therefore the integral **is split** at $1$ and each piece is studied. Near $0$ the factor $1 + x$ tends to $1$, hence the integrand is asymptotic to $\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{x}}$; the order of infinity is $\dfrac{1}{2} < 1$ and the piece converges. At infinity the denominator $\sqrt{x}\,(1 + x)$ is asymptotic to $x \cdot \sqrt{x} = x^{\frac{3}{2}}$, therefore the order of infinitesimal of the integrand is $\dfrac{3}{2} > 1$ and the second piece also converges. *All* the pieces converge, hence the integral **converges**.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Determine the behavior of
$$
\int_0^{+\infty} \frac{dx}{x\,(1 + x)} .
$$

$$
\int_0^{+\infty} \frac{dx}{x\,(1 + x)}
= \int_0^{1} \frac{dx}{x\,(1 + x)} + \int_1^{+\infty} \frac{dx}{x\,(1 + x)}
$$

$$
\frac{1}{x\,(1 + x)} \sim \frac{1}{x} \quad (x \to 0^{+}),
\qquad
\int_0^{1} \frac{dx}{x} \ \text{diverges}
$$

$$
\text{the piece on } (0, 1] \ \text{diverges, therefore the integral diverges}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Here too the critical points are $0$ and $+\infty$ and the integral is split at $1$. Near $0$ the factor $1 + x$ tends to $1$, therefore the integrand is asymptotic to $\dfrac{1}{x}$; the benchmark with $\alpha = 1$ at $0$ diverges, hence the first piece diverges by **limit comparison**. Since one piece does not converge, the integral over $(0, +\infty)$ does not converge; the integrand is positive, so the integral **diverges** to $+\infty$. The second piece converges, because at infinity the integrand is asymptotic to $\dfrac{1}{x^{2}}$, and yet the convergence of *a single* piece is not enough.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Compute
$$
\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} \frac{dx}{1 + x^{2}} .
$$

$$
\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} \frac{dx}{1 + x^{2}}
= \int_{-\infty}^{0} \frac{dx}{1 + x^{2}} + \int_0^{+\infty} \frac{dx}{1 + x^{2}}
$$

$$
\int_s^{0} \frac{dx}{1 + x^{2}} = -\arctan s \to \frac{\pi}{2} \quad (s \to -\infty),
\qquad
\int_0^{t} \frac{dx}{1 + x^{2}} = \arctan t \to \frac{\pi}{2} \quad (t \to +\infty)
$$

$$
\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} \frac{dx}{1 + x^{2}}
= \frac{\pi}{2} + \frac{\pi}{2}
= \pi
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The critical points are the two infinite endpoints, therefore the integral is split at $0$ and each piece is treated with its own definition, with **two independent limits**. An antiderivative of the integrand is $\arctan x$, which equals $0$ at $0$. As $s \to -\infty$ one has $\arctan s \to -\dfrac{\pi}{2}$, therefore the piece on $(-\infty, 0]$ equals $\dfrac{\pi}{2}$; as $t \to +\infty$ one has $\arctan t \to \dfrac{\pi}{2}$, therefore the piece on $[0, +\infty)$ also equals $\dfrac{\pi}{2}$. Both pieces converge, hence the integral **converges** and its value is the sum, $\pi$.

</div>
