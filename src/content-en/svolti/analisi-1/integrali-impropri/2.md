## Exercise 1

Compute
$$
\int_0^{1} \frac{dx}{\sqrt{x}} .
$$

$$
\int_t^{1} \frac{dx}{\sqrt{x}}
= \bigl[ 2\sqrt{x} \bigr]_t^{1}
= 2 - 2\sqrt{t}
$$

$$
\int_0^{1} \frac{dx}{\sqrt{x}}
= \lim_{t \to 0^{+}} \left( 2 - 2\sqrt{t} \right)
= 2
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The integrand tends to $+\infty$ as $x \to 0^{+}$, therefore the **critical point** is $0$: by the definition one integrates over $[t, 1]$ with $t > 0$ and passes to the limit as $t \to 0^{+}$. An antiderivative of $x^{-1/2}$ is $2x^{1/2} = 2\sqrt{x}$. As $t \to 0^{+}$ the term $2\sqrt{t}$ tends to $0$, hence the integral **converges** and equals $2$. The result is consistent with the benchmark of powers at $0$, since the exponent $\alpha = \dfrac{1}{2}$ is *less* than $1$ and the predicted value is $\dfrac{1}{1 - \alpha} = 2$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Compute
$$
\int_0^{4} \frac{dx}{\sqrt{4 - x}} .
$$

$$
\int_0^{t} \frac{dx}{\sqrt{4 - x}}
= \bigl[ -2\sqrt{4 - x} \bigr]_0^{t}
= 4 - 2\sqrt{4 - t}
$$

$$
\int_0^{4} \frac{dx}{\sqrt{4 - x}}
= \lim_{t \to 4^{-}} \left( 4 - 2\sqrt{4 - t} \right)
= 4
$$

::grafico{f="1/sqrt(4-x)" area="1/sqrt(4-x);0;0;3.9999" asintoti="x=4" xmin=-0.5 xmax=5 ymin=-0.3 ymax=5 titolo="Near $x = 4$ the function $\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{4 - x}}$ is unbounded, but the shaded area on $[0, 4)$ is finite and equals $4$"}

<div class="spiegazione">

The **critical point** is the upper endpoint $4$, where the denominator vanishes and the integrand tends to $+\infty$, therefore one integrates over $[0, t]$ with $t < 4$ and passes to the limit as $t \to 4^{-}$. An antiderivative of $(4 - x)^{-1/2}$ is $-2\sqrt{4 - x}$, because differentiating with the **chain rule** gives $-2 \cdot \dfrac{-1}{2\sqrt{4 - x}} = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{4 - x}}$. At $x = 0$ the antiderivative equals $-4$, therefore the integral over $[0, t]$ equals $4 - 2\sqrt{4 - t}$. As $t \to 4^{-}$ the root $\sqrt{4 - t}$ tends to $0$, hence the integral **converges** and equals $4$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Compute
$$
\int_0^{1} \ln x\,dx .
$$

$$
\int_t^{1} \ln x\,dx
= \bigl[ x \ln x - x \bigr]_t^{1}
= -1 - t \ln t + t
$$

$$
\lim_{t \to 0^{+}} t \ln t
= \lim_{s \to +\infty} \left( -\frac{\ln s}{s} \right)
= 0
\qquad \left( s = \frac{1}{t} \right)
$$

$$
\int_0^{1} \ln x\,dx
= \lim_{t \to 0^{+}} \left( -1 - t \ln t + t \right)
= -1
$$

::grafico{f="log(x)" area="0;log(x);0.0001;1" xmin=-0.3 xmax=2.6 ymin=-4 ymax=1.2 titolo="On $(0, 1]$ the graph of $y = \ln x$ lies below the $x$-axis and is unbounded near $0$: the shaded area is finite and equals $1$, therefore the integral equals $-1$"}

<div class="spiegazione">

The logarithm tends to $-\infty$ as $x \to 0^{+}$, therefore the integrand is unbounded near $0$ and the integral is **improper** with critical point $0$. An antiderivative of $\ln x$ is $x \ln x - x$, as one checks by differentiating, since the derivative is $\ln x + 1 - 1 = \ln x$. At the endpoint $1$ the antiderivative equals $-1$, therefore it remains to compute the limit of $t \ln t$ as $t \to 0^{+}$. With the substitution $s = \dfrac{1}{t}$ the product becomes $-\dfrac{\ln s}{s}$, which tends to $0$ by the **hierarchy of infinities** (chapter on limits). The integral therefore **converges** to the value $-1$; the negative sign corresponds to the sign of the logarithm, which is *negative* on $(0, 1)$.

</div>
