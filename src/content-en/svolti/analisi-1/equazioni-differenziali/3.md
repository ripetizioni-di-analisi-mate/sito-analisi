## Exercise 1

Find the general solution of
$$
y' + y = e^{x} .
$$

Here $a(x) = 1$, so $A(x) = x$ and the integrating factor is $e^{x}$:
$$
\left( e^{x}\, y \right)' = e^{x} \left( y' + y \right) = e^{2x}
$$

$$
e^{x}\, y = \int e^{2x}\, dx = \frac{e^{2x}}{2} + C
$$

$$
y = \frac{e^{x}}{2} + C e^{-x}, \qquad C \in \mathbb{R}.
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The equation is **linear** with $a(x) = 1$ and $b(x) = e^{x}$, so one multiplies by the **integrating factor** $e^{A(x)}$ with $A$ an antiderivative of $a$, that is, by $e^{x}$. After the multiplication, the left-hand side is the derivative of the product $e^{x} y$, therefore the equation can be integrated directly. An antiderivative of $e^{2x}$ is $\dfrac{e^{2x}}{2}$, and dividing by $e^{x}$ gives the general solution. The term $Ce^{-x}$ is the general solution of the associated homogeneous equation $y' + y = 0$, while $\dfrac{e^{x}}{2}$ is a **particular solution** of the complete equation, in accordance with the **structure theorem**.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Find the general solution of
$$
y' + 2x\, y = x .
$$

Here $a(x) = 2x$, so $A(x) = x^2$ and the integrating factor is $e^{x^2}$:
$$
\left( e^{x^2}\, y \right)' = x\, e^{x^2}
$$

$$
e^{x^2}\, y = \int x\, e^{x^2}\, dx = \frac{e^{x^2}}{2} + C
$$

$$
y = \frac{1}{2} + C e^{-x^2}, \qquad C \in \mathbb{R}.
$$

::grafico{f="0.5;0.5+exp(-x^2);0.5-exp(-x^2)" xmin=-3 xmax=3 ymin=-1 ymax=2 titolo="The constant solution $y = \dfrac{1}{2}$ (in color) and the solutions with $C = 1$ (dashed) and $C = -1$ (in red): far from the origin the term $Ce^{-x^2}$ becomes negligible"}

<div class="spiegazione">

The coefficient $a(x) = 2x$ is *not* constant, so the integrating factor contains its **antiderivative** $x^2$. Multiplying by $e^{x^2}$, the left-hand side becomes the derivative of $e^{x^2} y$ and the right-hand side becomes $x e^{x^2}$. This function is integrated by recognizing that the derivative of $e^{x^2}$ is $2x\, e^{x^2}$, therefore an antiderivative of $x e^{x^2}$ is $\dfrac{e^{x^2}}{2}$. Dividing by $e^{x^2}$ gives the **general solution**, the sum of the constant particular solution $\dfrac{1}{2}$ and the general solution of the homogeneous equation $Ce^{-x^2}$. The constant $y \equiv \dfrac{1}{2}$ can also be verified directly, since it has zero derivative and $2x \cdot \dfrac{1}{2} = x$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Find the general solution, for $x > 0$, of
$$
y' - \frac{y}{x} = x^2 .
$$

Here $a(x) = -\dfrac{1}{x}$, so $A(x) = -\ln x$ and the integrating factor is $e^{-\ln x} = \dfrac{1}{x}$:
$$
\left( \frac{y}{x} \right)' = \frac{1}{x} \left( y' - \frac{y}{x} \right) = x
$$

$$
\frac{y}{x} = \int x\, dx = \frac{x^2}{2} + C
$$

$$
y = \frac{x^3}{2} + C x, \qquad C \in \mathbb{R}.
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

For $x > 0$ the coefficients $a(x) = -\dfrac{1}{x}$ and $b(x) = x^2$ are continuous, so the **solution formula** applies on *this half-line*. An antiderivative of $-\dfrac{1}{x}$ is $-\ln x$, therefore the integrating factor is $e^{-\ln x}$, that is, $\dfrac{1}{x}$. After the multiplication, the left-hand side is the derivative of the quotient $\dfrac{y}{x}$, as one checks with the **product rule** applied to $y \cdot \dfrac{1}{x}$. Integration gives $\dfrac{y}{x} = \dfrac{x^2}{2} + C$, and multiplying by $x$ gives the general solution: the term $Cx$ solves the associated homogeneous equation, the term $\dfrac{x^3}{2}$ is a **particular solution** of the complete equation.

</div>
