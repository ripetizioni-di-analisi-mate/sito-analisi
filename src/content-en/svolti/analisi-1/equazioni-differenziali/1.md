## Exercise 1

Find the general solution of
$$
y' = 3x^2\, y .
$$

The constant function $y \equiv 0$ is a solution. For $y \neq 0$:
$$
\int \frac{dy}{y} = \int 3x^2\, dx
$$

$$
\ln |y| = x^3 + c
$$

$$
|y| = e^{c}\, e^{x^3}
\quad\implies\quad
y = C e^{x^3}, \qquad C \neq 0 .
$$

For $C = 0$ one recovers the zero solution, so the **general solution** is $y = Ce^{x^3}$ with $C \in \mathbb{R}$.

::grafico{f="exp(x^3);0.5*exp(x^3);-exp(x^3)" xmin=-2 xmax=1.4 ymin=-4 ymax=5 titolo="Three solutions $y = Ce^{x^3}$: $C = 1$ in color, $C = \dfrac{1}{2}$ dashed, $C = -1$ in red; as $x \to -\infty$ they all tend to $0$"}

<div class="spiegazione">

The equation has the form $y' = g(x)\,h(y)$ with $g(x) = 3x^2$ and $h(y) = y$, so it is **separable**. The factor $h(y) = y$ vanishes for $y = 0$, therefore the zero function is a **constant solution** and must be recorded *before* dividing. In the regions where $y \neq 0$ one divides both sides by $y$ and integrates: the left-hand side with respect to $y$, the right-hand side with respect to $x$. From the equality $\ln|y| = x^3 + c$ one obtains $|y| = e^{c} e^{x^3}$, and removing the absolute value turns the constant $\pm e^{c}$ into a nonzero constant $C$. Since the choice $C = 0$ gives back the zero solution, a single formula collects *all* the solutions.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Find the general solution of
$$
y' = e^{x - y} .
$$

$$
y' = e^{x}\, e^{-y}
\quad\implies\quad
\int e^{y}\, dy = \int e^{x}\, dx
$$

$$
e^{y} = e^{x} + C
$$

$$
y = \ln\!\left( e^{x} + C \right), \qquad C \in \mathbb{R},
$$
on every interval where $e^{x} + C > 0$.

<div class="spiegazione">

By the properties of powers, $e^{x-y}$ is the product $e^{x} e^{-y}$, so the equation is **separable** with $g(x) = e^{x}$ and $h(y) = e^{-y}$. The factor $e^{-y}$ never vanishes, therefore **there are no constant solutions**. Dividing by $e^{-y}$, that is, multiplying by $e^{y}$, separates the variables, and integrating both sides gives $e^{y} = e^{x} + C$. The left-hand side is always positive, so the equality defines a solution *only* where $e^{x} + C$ is positive: for $C \geq 0$ on all of $\mathbb{R}$, for $C < 0$ on the half-line $x > \ln(-C)$. Applying the logarithm gives the explicit form of the solution.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Find the general solution of
$$
y' = x \left( 1 + y^2 \right) .
$$

$$
\int \frac{dy}{1 + y^2} = \int x\, dx
$$

$$
\arctan y = \frac{x^2}{2} + C
$$

$$
y = \tan\!\left( \frac{x^2}{2} + C \right),
$$
on every interval where $\dfrac{x^2}{2} + C$ stays within an interval of the form $\left( -\dfrac{\pi}{2} + k\pi,\; \dfrac{\pi}{2} + k\pi \right)$.

<div class="spiegazione">

The equation is separable with $g(x) = x$ and $h(y) = 1 + y^2$. The factor $1 + y^2$ is always greater than or equal to $1$, so **there are no constant solutions** and the division is legitimate everywhere. An antiderivative of $\dfrac{1}{1 + y^2}$ is $\arctan y$, therefore integrating both sides gives $\arctan y = \dfrac{x^2}{2} + C$. Applying the tangent gives the explicit form $y = \tan\!\left( \dfrac{x^2}{2} + C \right)$, which by direct verification satisfies the equation on every interval where the tangent is defined. Since the tangent has vertical asymptotes at the points $\dfrac{\pi}{2} + k\pi$, each solution is defined *only* on the intervals where the argument avoids these values.

</div>
