## Exercise 1

Find the general solution of
$$
y' + y = y^2 .
$$

The zero function $y \equiv 0$ is a solution. For $y \neq 0$, with $\alpha = 2$ we set $z = y^{1 - 2} = y^{-1}$, from which $z' = -y^{-2}\, y'$. Dividing the equation by $y^{2}$:
$$
y^{-2}\, y' + y^{-1} = 1
\quad\implies\quad
-z' + z = 1
\quad\implies\quad
z' - z = -1 .
$$

Integrating factor $e^{-x}$:
$$
\left( e^{-x}\, z \right)' = -e^{-x}
\quad\implies\quad
e^{-x}\, z = e^{-x} + C
\quad\implies\quad
z = 1 + C e^{x} .
$$

Returning to the variable $y$:
$$
y = \frac{1}{1 + C e^{x}}, \qquad C \in \mathbb{R},
$$
together with the solution $y \equiv 0$.

<div class="spiegazione">

The equation is a **Bernoulli** equation with $a(x) = 1$, $b(x) = 1$ and $\alpha = 2$. Since $\alpha > 0$, the zero function is a solution and must be recorded *before* the substitution, which requires $y \neq 0$. The prescribed substitution is $z = y^{1 - \alpha}$, that is, the reciprocal of $y$; dividing the equation by $y^2$ and recognizing $-y^{-2} y'$ as the derivative of $z$, one obtains the **linear equation** $z' - z = -1$. This is solved with the **integrating factor** $e^{-x}$ and gives $z = 1 + Ce^{x}$. Finally the substitution is inverted: $y$ is the reciprocal of $z$, so each solution is defined on the intervals where $1 + Ce^{x}$ does not vanish. For $C = 0$ one obtains the constant solution $y \equiv 1$, which indeed satisfies the equation because $0 + 1 = 1^2$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Find the general solution, for $x > 0$, of
$$
y' + \frac{y}{x} = x\, y^2 .
$$

The zero function $y \equiv 0$ is a solution. For $y \neq 0$ we set $z = y^{-1}$, from which $z' = -y^{-2} y'$. Dividing by $y^2$:
$$
y^{-2}\, y' + \frac{1}{x}\, y^{-1} = x
\quad\implies\quad
z' - \frac{z}{x} = -x .
$$

Integrating factor $e^{-\ln x} = \dfrac{1}{x}$:
$$
\left( \frac{z}{x} \right)' = -1
\quad\implies\quad
\frac{z}{x} = -x + C
\quad\implies\quad
z = Cx - x^2 .
$$

Returning to the variable $y$:
$$
y = \frac{1}{Cx - x^2}, \qquad C \in \mathbb{R},
$$
together with the solution $y \equiv 0$.

<div class="spiegazione">

The equation is a **Bernoulli** equation with $a(x) = \dfrac{1}{x}$, $b(x) = x$ and $\alpha = 2$, posed on the half-line $x > 0$ where the coefficients are continuous. The substitution $z = y^{-1}$ transforms the equation into the linear equation $z' - \dfrac{z}{x} = -x$, in which the sign of the coefficient of $z$ has *changed* because the division by $y^2$ and the relation $z' = -y^{-2} y'$ introduce the factor $-1$. An antiderivative of $-\dfrac{1}{x}$ is $-\ln x$, so the **integrating factor** is $\dfrac{1}{x}$ and the multiplied left-hand side becomes the derivative of $\dfrac{z}{x}$. Integration yields $z = Cx - x^2$, and the reciprocal gives the solution in $y$, defined on the intervals where $Cx - x^2 = x(C - x)$ does not vanish.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Find the positive solutions of
$$
y' + 2y = 2\sqrt{y} .
$$

For $y > 0$, with $\alpha = \dfrac{1}{2}$ we set $z = \sqrt{y}$, from which $z' = \dfrac{y'}{2\sqrt{y}}$. Dividing the equation by $\sqrt{y}$:
$$
\frac{y'}{\sqrt{y}} + 2\sqrt{y} = 2
\quad\implies\quad
2z' + 2z = 2
\quad\implies\quad
z' + z = 1 .
$$

Integrating factor $e^{x}$:
$$
\left( e^{x}\, z \right)' = e^{x}
\quad\implies\quad
e^{x}\, z = e^{x} + C
\quad\implies\quad
z = 1 + C e^{-x} .
$$

Returning to the variable $y$, where $1 + Ce^{-x} > 0$:
$$
y = \left( 1 + C e^{-x} \right)^{2}, \qquad C \in \mathbb{R}.
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The equation is a **Bernoulli** equation with $\alpha = \dfrac{1}{2}$; since the exponent is *not* an integer, the square root requires $y \geq 0$ and we look for positive solutions. The substitution is $z = y^{1 - \alpha} = \sqrt{y}$. Dividing the equation by $\sqrt{y}$ and observing that $\dfrac{y'}{\sqrt{y}} = 2z'$, one obtains the **linear equation** $z' + z = 1$, which is solved with the integrating factor $e^{x}$ and gives $z = 1 + Ce^{-x}$. Since $z$ is a square root, the formula is acceptable *only* where $z > 0$; on these intervals one squares and obtains the solution in $y$. For $C = 0$ one finds the constant solution $y \equiv 1$, which satisfies the equation because $0 + 2 = 2\sqrt{1}$.

</div>
