## Exercise 1

Solve the Cauchy problem
$$
\begin{cases}
y' + y = 1 \\[4pt]
y(0) = 2
\end{cases}
$$

**General solution.** The integrating factor is $e^{x}$:
$$
\left( e^{x}\, y \right)' = e^{x}
\quad\implies\quad
e^{x}\, y = e^{x} + C
\quad\implies\quad
y = 1 + C e^{-x} .
$$

**Initial condition.**
$$
y(0) = 1 + C = 2
\quad\implies\quad
C = 1
\quad\implies\quad
y = 1 + e^{-x} .
$$

The solution is **defined on all of** $\mathbb{R}$.

::grafico{f="1+exp(-x);1;1-exp(-x)" punti="0,2,pieno" xmin=-1.5 xmax=5 ymin=-2 ymax=5 titolo="The solution of the problem, $y = 1 + e^{-x}$ (in color), passes through $(0, 2)$; dashed, the constant solution $y = 1$, that is, $C = 0$; in red, the one with $C = -1$"}

<div class="spiegazione">

The equation is linear with constant coefficients, with $a(x) = 1$ and $b(x) = 1$ continuous on all of $\mathbb{R}$, so the solution of the Cauchy problem **exists, is unique**, and is defined on the whole line. Multiplying by the integrating factor $e^{x}$ and integrating gives the general solution $y = 1 + Ce^{-x}$, the sum of the constant solution $y \equiv 1$ and the general solution of the homogeneous equation. The initial condition imposes $1 + C = 2$, so $C = 1$. As $x \to +\infty$ the term $e^{-x}$ tends to $0$, therefore the solution tends to the **equilibrium value** $1$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Solve, for $x > 0$, the Cauchy problem
$$
\begin{cases}
x\, y' + y = 2x \\[4pt]
y(1) = 3
\end{cases}
$$

**Normal form.** Dividing by $x > 0$:
$$
y' + \frac{y}{x} = 2 .
$$

**General solution.** The integrating factor is $e^{\ln x} = x$ and the left-hand side multiplied by $x$ is the derivative of $x y$:
$$
\left( x\, y \right)' = 2x
\quad\implies\quad
x\, y = x^2 + C
\quad\implies\quad
y = x + \frac{C}{x} .
$$

**Initial condition.**
$$
y(1) = 1 + C = 3
\quad\implies\quad
C = 2
\quad\implies\quad
y = x + \frac{2}{x} ,
$$
defined on $(0, +\infty)$.

<div class="spiegazione">

To reduce to the **normal form** $y' + a(x) y = b(x)$ one divides by $x$, an operation that is legitimate because the problem is posed on $x > 0$. The coefficient $a(x) = \dfrac{1}{x}$ has antiderivative $\ln x$ on this half-line, so the integrating factor is $e^{\ln x} = x$ and one returns to the original equation, whose left-hand side $x y' + y$ is the derivative of the product $xy$. Integration gives $xy = x^2 + C$, and the initial condition at the point $x_0 = 1$ determines $C = 2$. Since the coefficients are continuous on $(0, +\infty)$ and the initial point belongs to this half-line, the **interval of existence** of the solution is $(0, +\infty)$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Solve the Cauchy problem
$$
\begin{cases}
y' - 2y = e^{2x} \\[4pt]
y(0) = 1
\end{cases}
$$

**General solution.** The integrating factor is $e^{-2x}$:
$$
\left( e^{-2x}\, y \right)' = e^{-2x}\, e^{2x} = 1
\quad\implies\quad
e^{-2x}\, y = x + C
\quad\implies\quad
y = (x + C)\, e^{2x} .
$$

**Initial condition.**
$$
y(0) = C = 1
\quad\implies\quad
y = (x + 1)\, e^{2x} ,
$$
defined on all of $\mathbb{R}$.

<div class="spiegazione">

The coefficient of $y$ is $a(x) = -2$, so $A(x) = -2x$ and the integrating factor is $e^{-2x}$. After the multiplication, the right-hand side becomes $e^{-2x} e^{2x} = 1$, therefore the integration is immediate and gives $e^{-2x} y = x + C$. The forcing term $e^{2x}$ *solves the associated homogeneous equation*, and for this reason the factor $x$ appears in the particular solution: it is the same **resonance** phenomenon encountered in the method of undetermined coefficients for the second order. The initial condition gives $C = 1$, and since the coefficients are continuous on all of $\mathbb{R}$ the solution is **defined on the whole line**.

</div>
