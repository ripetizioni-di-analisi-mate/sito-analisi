## Exercise 1

Solve the Cauchy problem and state the interval of existence of the solution:
$$
\begin{cases}
y' = y^2 \\[4pt]
y(0) = 1
\end{cases}
$$

**General solution.** The constant $y \equiv 0$ is a solution, but it does not satisfy the initial condition. For $y \neq 0$:
$$
\int \frac{dy}{y^2} = \int dx
\quad\implies\quad
-\frac{1}{y} = x + C
\quad\implies\quad
y = -\frac{1}{x + C} .
$$

**Initial condition.**
$$
y(0) = -\frac{1}{C} = 1
\quad\implies\quad
C = -1
\quad\implies\quad
y = \frac{1}{1 - x} .
$$

**Interval of existence.** The solution is defined on $(-\infty, 1)$, the interval containing $x_0 = 0$ on which the denominator does not vanish.

::grafico{f="1/(1-x)+0*sqrt(1-x);1/(1-x)+0*sqrt(x-1)" asintoti="x=1" punti="0,1,pieno" xmin=-4 xmax=3.5 ymin=-4 ymax=6 titolo="The formula $y = \dfrac{1}{1 - x}$ has two branches: the solution of the problem is the branch in color, defined on $(-\infty, 1)$ and passing through $(0, 1)$; the dashed branch does not contain the initial point"}

<div class="spiegazione">

The function $f(x, y) = y^2$ and its partial derivative with respect to $y$ are continuous on the whole plane, so the **local existence and uniqueness theorem** applies and the solution is **unique**. The zero solution is discarded immediately, since at $x = 0$ it takes the value $0$ and the initial condition requires the value $1$. Separating the variables and integrating gives the family $y = -\dfrac{1}{x + C}$; the initial condition determines $C = -1$. The formula $\dfrac{1}{1 - x}$ defines a function on two intervals separated by the point $x = 1$, but the solution of the Cauchy problem is a function on an interval containing the initial point, therefore *only* $(-\infty, 1)$ is kept. As $x \to 1^{-}$ the solution tends to $+\infty$: the interval of existence is **bounded on the right** even though $f$ is defined everywhere.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Solve the Cauchy problem and state the interval of existence of the solution:
$$
\begin{cases}
y' = -\dfrac{x}{y} \\[8pt]
y(0) = 2
\end{cases}
$$

$$
\int y\, dy = \int (-x)\, dx
\quad\implies\quad
\frac{y^2}{2} = -\frac{x^2}{2} + C
\quad\implies\quad
y^2 = 2C - x^2 .
$$

**Initial condition.** $y(0)^2 = 2C = 4$, so $y^2 = 4 - x^2$; since $y(0) = 2 > 0$ the positive root is chosen:
$$
y = \sqrt{4 - x^2} .
$$

**Interval of existence.** The solution is defined on $(-2, 2)$.

::grafico{f="sqrt(4-x^2);-sqrt(4-x^2)" punti="0,2,pieno;-2,0,vuoto;2,0,vuoto" uguale=1 xmin=-3 xmax=3 ymin=-2.5 ymax=2.8 titolo="$y^2 = 4 - x^2$ describes the circle with center at the origin and radius $2$: the solution is the upper semicircle (in color), passing through $(0, 2)$, with the endpoints $(-2, 0)$ and $(2, 0)$ excluded"}

<div class="spiegazione">

The equation is separable with $h(y) = \dfrac{1}{y}$, defined for $y \neq 0$; the initial condition assigns the value $2$, so we work in the **region** $y > 0$. Multiplying by $y$ and integrating gives the relation $y^2 = 2C - x^2$, which describes a family of circles centered at the origin. The initial condition fixes $2C = 4$. Taking the square root produces two functions, one positive and one negative; since the initial value is *positive*, the solution is the **positive root**. At the endpoints $x = \pm 2$ the solution equals $0$ and the equation loses meaning, since the denominator $y$ vanishes and the derivative tends to infinity; therefore the interval of existence is the **open interval** $(-2, 2)$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Solve the Cauchy problem and state the interval of existence of the solution:
$$
\begin{cases}
y' = (2x + 1)\, e^{-y} \\[4pt]
y(0) = 0
\end{cases}
$$

$$
\int e^{y}\, dy = \int (2x + 1)\, dx
\quad\implies\quad
e^{y} = x^2 + x + C .
$$

**Initial condition.** $e^{0} = C = 1$, so
$$
e^{y} = x^2 + x + 1
\quad\implies\quad
y = \ln\!\left( x^2 + x + 1 \right).
$$

**Interval of existence.** The discriminant of $x^2 + x + 1$ is $1 - 4 = -3 < 0$, so the polynomial is always positive and the solution is defined on all of $\mathbb{R}$.

<div class="spiegazione">

The equation is separable with $g(x) = 2x + 1$ and $h(y) = e^{-y}$; the factor $e^{-y}$ never vanishes, so **there are no constant solutions**. Multiplying by $e^{y}$ and integrating gives $e^{y} = x^2 + x + C$, and the initial condition gives $C = 1$. The logarithm can be applied *only* where the right-hand side is positive, therefore the interval of existence coincides with the set where $x^2 + x + 1 > 0$ around the initial point. The discriminant of the trinomial is negative and the coefficient of $x^2$ is positive, so the trinomial is positive for every $x$ and the solution is **defined on all of** $\mathbb{R}$.

</div>
