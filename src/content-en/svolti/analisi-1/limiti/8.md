## Exercise 1

Find the asymptotes of
$$
f(x) = \frac{x^2 - 3x}{x - 2} .
$$

**Domain.** $x - 2 \neq 0$, therefore $D = \mathbb{R} \setminus \{2\}$.

**Vertical asymptote.**
$$
\lim_{x \to 2^{+}} \frac{x^2 - 3x}{x - 2} = \frac{-2}{0^{+}} = -\infty, \qquad
\lim_{x \to 2^{-}} \frac{x^2 - 3x}{x - 2} = \frac{-2}{0^{-}} = +\infty
$$

**Horizontal asymptote.**
$$
\lim_{x \to \pm\infty} \frac{x^2 - 3x}{x - 2} = \lim_{x \to \pm\infty} \frac{x^2 \left( 1 - \dfrac{3}{x} \right)}{x \left( 1 - \dfrac{2}{x} \right)} = \lim_{x \to \pm\infty} x \cdot \frac{1 - \dfrac{3}{x}}{1 - \dfrac{2}{x}} = \pm\infty
$$

**Oblique asymptote.**
$$
m = \lim_{x \to \pm\infty} \frac{f(x)}{x} = \lim_{x \to \pm\infty} \frac{x^2 - 3x}{x^2 - 2x} = 1
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
q = \lim_{x \to \pm\infty} \bigl( f(x) - x \bigr)
&= \lim_{x \to \pm\infty} \frac{x^2 - 3x - x^2 + 2x}{x - 2} \\[6pt]
&= \lim_{x \to \pm\infty} \frac{-x}{x - 2} = -1
\end{aligned}
$$

Asymptotes: the line $x = 2$ (**vertical**) and the line $y = x - 1$ (**oblique**, as $x \to +\infty$ and as $x \to -\infty$).

::grafico{f="(x*x-3*x)/(x-2);x-1" xmin=-6 xmax=10 ymin=-10 ymax=12 asintoti="x=2" titolo="$y = \dfrac{x^2 - 3x}{x - 2}$ with the vertical asymptote $x = 2$ and the oblique asymptote $y = x - 1$ (dashed)"}

<div class="spiegazione">

The denominator vanishes at $x = 2$, therefore the line $x = 2$ is the candidate **vertical asymptote** and one computes the one-sided limits. The numerator at $x = 2$ equals $4 - 6 = -2$, which is *different* from zero, hence the limits are **infinite**. As $x \to 2^{+}$ the denominator $x - 2$ is positive and tends to $0$, therefore the ratio tends to $-\infty$; as $x \to 2^{-}$ the denominator is negative and the ratio tends to $+\infty$.

As $x \to \pm\infty$ the degree of the numerator exceeds that of the denominator by one, therefore the function tends to infinity and **there is no horizontal asymptote**. One then looks for the oblique asymptote with the two limits of the definition: $m$ is the limit of $\dfrac{f(x)}{x}$, a ratio of polynomials of the same degree whose highest-degree coefficients are both equal to $1$; $q$ is the limit of $f(x) - mx$, which after the subtraction becomes a ratio with numerator $-x$ and denominator $x - 2$. Both limits are finite, with $m \neq 0$, therefore the line $y = x - 1$ is an **oblique asymptote**. The computations are the same as $x \to +\infty$ and as $x \to -\infty$, hence the asymptote is the same on both sides.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Find the asymptotes of
$$
f(x) = \frac{x^2 + 1}{x^2 - 4} .
$$

**Domain.** $x^2 - 4 \neq 0$, therefore $D = \mathbb{R} \setminus \{-2, 2\}$.

**Vertical asymptotes.**
$$
\lim_{x \to 2^{+}} \frac{x^2 + 1}{x^2 - 4} = \frac{5}{0^{+}} = +\infty, \qquad
\lim_{x \to 2^{-}} \frac{x^2 + 1}{x^2 - 4} = \frac{5}{0^{-}} = -\infty
$$
$$
\lim_{x \to -2^{-}} \frac{x^2 + 1}{x^2 - 4} = \frac{5}{0^{+}} = +\infty, \qquad
\lim_{x \to -2^{+}} \frac{x^2 + 1}{x^2 - 4} = \frac{5}{0^{-}} = -\infty
$$

**Horizontal asymptote.**
$$
\lim_{x \to \pm\infty} \frac{x^2 + 1}{x^2 - 4}
= \lim_{x \to \pm\infty} \frac{1 + \dfrac{1}{x^2}}{1 - \dfrac{4}{x^2}}
= \frac{1 + 0}{1 - 0} = 1
$$

Asymptotes: the lines $x = -2$ and $x = 2$ (**vertical**) and the line $y = 1$ (**horizontal**, as $x \to +\infty$ and as $x \to -\infty$).

::grafico{f="(x^2+1)/(x^2-4)" xmin=-6 xmax=6 ymin=-6 ymax=8 asintoti="x=-2;x=2;y=1" titolo="$y = \dfrac{x^2 + 1}{x^2 - 4}$: vertical asymptotes $x = -2$ and $x = 2$, horizontal asymptote $y = 1$"}

<div class="spiegazione">

The denominator vanishes at $x = 2$ and at $x = -2$, while the numerator $x^2 + 1$ is *always* greater than or equal to $1$, hence at both points the one-sided limits are infinite and the two lines are **vertical asymptotes**. To determine the signs, observe that $x^2 - 4$ is positive for $|x| > 2$ and negative for $|x| < 2$. As $x \to 2^{+}$ we have $x > 2$, therefore the denominator tends to $0^{+}$ and the ratio tends to $+\infty$; as $x \to 2^{-}$ we have $|x| < 2$, therefore the denominator tends to $0^{-}$ and the ratio tends to $-\infty$. As $x \to -2^{-}$ we have $x < -2$, therefore $|x| > 2$, the denominator tends to $0^{+}$ and the ratio tends to $+\infty$; as $x \to -2^{+}$ the denominator tends to $0^{-}$ and the ratio tends to $-\infty$.

At infinity the numerator and the denominator have the same degree: one factors out $x^2$ in both and, since the terms $\dfrac{1}{x^2}$ and $\dfrac{4}{x^2}$ tend to $0$, the ratio tends to $1$ both as $x \to +\infty$ and as $x \to -\infty$. Since the limit at infinity is finite, the line $y = 1$ is a **horizontal asymptote** on both sides and the function **has no oblique asymptotes**.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Find the asymptotes of
$$
f(x) = x + \sqrt{x^2 + 1} .
$$

**Domain.** $x^2 + 1 > 0$ for every $x$, therefore $D = \mathbb{R}$. The function is continuous on all of $\mathbb{R}$, hence **there are no vertical asymptotes**.

**As $x \to -\infty$.**
$$
x + \sqrt{x^2 + 1}
= \frac{\left( x + \sqrt{x^2 + 1} \right)\left( \sqrt{x^2 + 1} - x \right)}{\sqrt{x^2 + 1} - x}
= \frac{1}{\sqrt{x^2 + 1} - x}
$$
$$
\lim_{x \to -\infty} \left( x + \sqrt{x^2 + 1} \right)
= \lim_{x \to -\infty} \frac{1}{\sqrt{x^2 + 1} - x} = 0
$$

**As $x \to +\infty$.**
$$
\lim_{x \to +\infty} \left( x + \sqrt{x^2 + 1} \right) = +\infty
$$
$$
m = \lim_{x \to +\infty} \frac{x + \sqrt{x^2 + 1}}{x}
= \lim_{x \to +\infty} \left( 1 + \sqrt{1 + \frac{1}{x^2}} \right) = 2
$$
$$
q = \lim_{x \to +\infty} \left( x + \sqrt{x^2 + 1} - 2x \right)
= \lim_{x \to +\infty} \left( \sqrt{x^2 + 1} - x \right)
= \lim_{x \to +\infty} \frac{1}{\sqrt{x^2 + 1} + x} = 0
$$

Asymptotes: the line $y = 0$ (**horizontal**, as $x \to -\infty$) and the line $y = 2x$ (**oblique**, as $x \to +\infty$).

::grafico{f="x+sqrt(x^2+1);2*x" xmin=-6 xmax=4 ymin=-1 ymax=8 titolo="$y = x + \sqrt{x^2 + 1}$: it tends to $0$ as $x \to -\infty$ and approaches the line $y = 2x$ (dashed) as $x \to +\infty$"}

<div class="spiegazione">

The domain is all of $\mathbb{R}$ and the function is continuous at every point, hence there are no candidate vertical asymptotes. The two directions of infinity must be studied *separately*, because the root $\sqrt{x^2 + 1}$ tends to $+\infty$ on both sides while the term $x$ changes sign.

As $x \to -\infty$ the sum is in the **indeterminate form** $+\infty - \infty$. Multiply and divide by $\sqrt{x^2 + 1} - x$: in the numerator the product $(b + x)(b - x) = b^2 - x^2$ appears with $b = \sqrt{x^2 + 1}$, which eliminates the root and leaves $x^2 + 1 - x^2 = 1$. The denominator $\sqrt{x^2 + 1} - x$ is the sum of two terms that both tend to $+\infty$, therefore the ratio tends to $0$ and the line $y = 0$ is a **horizontal asymptote** as $x \to -\infty$.

As $x \to +\infty$ both terms tend to $+\infty$, therefore the function tends to $+\infty$ and there is no horizontal asymptote on this side. One looks for the oblique asymptote using the definition. In the limit for $m$ one divides by $x$: since $x > 0$, we have $\dfrac{\sqrt{x^2 + 1}}{x} = \sqrt{1 + \dfrac{1}{x^2}}$, which tends to $1$, therefore $m = 2$. In the limit for $q$ the difference $\sqrt{x^2 + 1} - x$ remains, again of the form $+\infty - \infty$, which after **rationalization** becomes $\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{x^2 + 1} + x}$ and tends to $0$, therefore $q = 0$. Both limits are finite, with $m \neq 0$, hence the line $y = 2x$ is an **oblique asymptote** as $x \to +\infty$.

</div>
