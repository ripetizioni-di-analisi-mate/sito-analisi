## Exercise 1

Determine for which value of $k \in \mathbb{R}$ the function
$$
f(x) =
\begin{cases}
\dfrac{e^{2x} - 1}{x} & \text{if } x \neq 0 \\[10pt]
k & \text{if } x = 0
\end{cases}
$$
is continuous at $x = 0$, and classify the point $x = 0$ for the other values of $k$.

$$
\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{e^{2x} - 1}{x}
= \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{e^{2x} - 1}{2x} \cdot 2
= 1 \cdot 2 = 2
$$

$$
f \text{ continuous at } 0 \iff \lim_{x \to 0} f(x) = f(0) \iff k = 2
$$

For $k \neq 2$ the point $x = 0$ is a **removable discontinuity**.

<div class="spiegazione">

For $x \neq 0$ the function is a quotient of continuous functions with a nonzero denominator, therefore it is **continuous** at every point other than $0$ for *any* $k$. At $x = 0$ continuity requires that the limit of $f$ exist, be finite and coincide with $f(0) = k$. The limit is computed with the **fundamental limit** $\dfrac{e^{y} - 1}{y} \to 1$, after the change of variable $y = 2x$: one multiplies and divides by $2$ and obtains $2$. Therefore $f$ is continuous at $0$ *if and only if* $k = 2$.

For $k \neq 2$ the limit at $0$ exists, is finite and equals $2$, but it is *different* from the value $f(0) = k$: by definition the point $0$ is a **removable discontinuity**, which is removed by setting $k = 2$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Determine for which value of $k \in \mathbb{R}$ the function
$$
f(x) =
\begin{cases}
\dfrac{\ln(1 + 3x)}{x} & \text{if } x > 0 \\[10pt]
x^{2} + k & \text{if } x \leq 0
\end{cases}
$$
is continuous at $x = 0$, and classify the point $x = 0$ for the other values of $k$.

$$
\lim_{x \to 0^{+}} \frac{\ln(1 + 3x)}{x}
= \lim_{x \to 0^{+}} \frac{\ln(1 + 3x)}{3x} \cdot 3
= 1 \cdot 3 = 3
$$

$$
\lim_{x \to 0^{-}} \left( x^{2} + k \right) = k = f(0)
$$

$$
f \text{ continuous at } 0 \iff \lim_{x \to 0^{+}} f(x) = \lim_{x \to 0^{-}} f(x) = f(0) \iff k = 3
$$

For $k \neq 3$ the point $x = 0$ is a **jump discontinuity**, with jump $3 - k$.

<div class="spiegazione">

For $x > 0$ the argument of the logarithm satisfies $1 + 3x > 0$ and the denominator does not vanish, therefore the function is **continuous** at every positive point; for $x < 0$ it is a polynomial, continuous everywhere. It remains to study the **junction point** $x = 0$, where continuity requires that the two one-sided limits exist, be finite and coincide with $f(0)$.

The right-hand limit is computed with the **fundamental limit** $\dfrac{\ln(1 + y)}{y} \to 1$, after the change of variable $y = 3x$: one multiplies and divides by $3$ and obtains $3$. The left-hand limit is the limit of a polynomial, therefore it equals $0 + k = k$, which coincides with $f(0)$ since the point $0$ belongs to the second piece. Hence $f$ is continuous at $0$ *if and only if* $k = 3$.

For $k \neq 3$ the one-sided limits both exist and are finite but are different, since the right-hand one equals $3$ and the left-hand one equals $k$: by definition the point $0$ is a **discontinuity of the first kind**, that is, a **jump**, and the size of the jump is the difference $3 - k$ between the two one-sided limits.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Determine for which value of $k \in \mathbb{R}$ the function
$$
f(x) =
\begin{cases}
\left( 1 + kx \right)^{\frac{1}{x}} & \text{if } x > 0 \\[8pt]
e^{2} + x & \text{if } x \leq 0
\end{cases}
$$
is continuous at $x = 0$, and classify the point $x = 0$ for the other values of $k$.

For $k \neq 0$:
$$
\left( 1 + kx \right)^{\frac{1}{x}}
= \left[ \left( 1 + kx \right)^{\frac{1}{kx}} \right]^{k}
$$

$$
\lim_{x \to 0^{+}} \left( 1 + kx \right)^{\frac{1}{kx}} = e, \qquad
\lim_{x \to 0^{+}} \left( 1 + kx \right)^{\frac{1}{x}} = e^{k}
$$

For $k = 0$ we have $f(x) = 1$ for every $x > 0$, therefore the right-hand limit equals $1 = e^{0}$: the formula $e^{k}$ covers all cases.

$$
\lim_{x \to 0^{-}} \left( e^{2} + x \right) = e^{2} = f(0)
$$

$$
f \text{ continuous at } 0 \iff e^{k} = e^{2} \iff k = 2
$$

For $k \neq 2$ the point $x = 0$ is a **jump discontinuity**, with jump $e^{k} - e^{2}$.

<div class="spiegazione">

For $x \leq 0$ the function is a shifted first-degree polynomial, continuous everywhere, with $f(0) = e^{2}$. For $x > 0$ the base $1 + kx$ is positive in a right neighborhood of $0$ for any $k$, since for $x$ small enough the term $kx$ has absolute value less than $1$, therefore the power is **well defined** near the junction point.

The right-hand limit is in the **indeterminate form** $1^{\infty}$, since the base tends to $1$ and the exponent $\dfrac{1}{x}$ tends to $+\infty$. For $k \neq 0$ the exponent is rewritten as $\dfrac{1}{x} = \dfrac{1}{kx} \cdot k$: setting $u = kx$, we have $u \to 0$ and the power $(1 + u)^{\frac{1}{u}}$ tends to $e$ by the **fundamental exponential limit**, hence the right-hand limit equals $e^{k}$ by the continuity of the function $u^{v}$ with $u > 0$. For $k = 0$ the first piece is the constant $1$, and in this case too the right-hand limit coincides with $e^{k} = e^{0} = 1$.

The left-hand limit equals $e^{2}$ and coincides with $f(0)$, therefore continuity at $0$ is equivalent to the equality $e^{k} = e^{2}$. The exponential function is **injective**, hence the equality holds *if and only if* $k = 2$.

For $k \neq 2$ the one-sided limits both exist and are finite but are different, since the right-hand one equals $e^{k}$ and the left-hand one equals $e^{2}$: by definition the point $0$ is a **discontinuity of the first kind**, that is, a **jump**, with size $e^{k} - e^{2}$.

</div>
