## Exercise 1

Prove with the characterization that
$$
\sup \left\{ 1 - \frac{1}{n} : n \in \mathbb{N},\ n \geq 1 \right\} = 1
$$
and determine whether $1$ is the maximum of the set.

$$
1 - \frac{1}{n} < 1 \quad \text{for every } n \geq 1
$$

$$
1 - \frac{1}{n} > 1 - \varepsilon
\iff \frac{1}{n} < \varepsilon
\iff n > \frac{1}{\varepsilon}
$$

$$
\sup A = 1, \qquad 1 \notin A: \ \text{it is not the maximum}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The characterization requires *two* conditions. The first is that $1$ is an **upper bound**: since $\dfrac{1}{n} > 0$ for every $n \geq 1$, every element $1 - \dfrac{1}{n}$ is less than $1$, so the condition is **satisfied**.

The second condition is that, for every $\varepsilon > 0$, the set contains an element greater than $1 - \varepsilon$. The inequality $1 - \dfrac{1}{n} > 1 - \varepsilon$ is equivalent, after cancelling $1$ and changing sign, to $\dfrac{1}{n} < \varepsilon$, that is to $n > \dfrac{1}{\varepsilon}$. A natural number greater than $\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon}$ exists by the **Archimedean property**, so the second condition is **satisfied** and $\sup A = 1$.

The maximum **does not exist**: indeed the equality $1 - \dfrac{1}{n} = 1$ would require $\dfrac{1}{n} = 0$, which is impossible, so $1 \notin A$, and the supremum is a maximum *only* when it belongs to the set.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Prove with the characterization that
$$
\inf \left\{ \frac{3n + 2}{n} : n \in \mathbb{N},\ n \geq 1 \right\} = 3
$$
and determine whether $3$ is the minimum of the set.

$$
\frac{3n + 2}{n} = 3 + \frac{2}{n} > 3 \quad \text{for every } n \geq 1
$$

$$
3 + \frac{2}{n} < 3 + \varepsilon
\iff \frac{2}{n} < \varepsilon
\iff n > \frac{2}{\varepsilon}
$$

$$
\inf A = 3, \qquad 3 \notin A: \ \text{it is not the minimum}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Term-by-term division rewrites the elements as $3 + \dfrac{2}{n}$, and in this form the two conditions of the characterization of the infimum can be checked directly. First condition: since $\dfrac{2}{n} > 0$, every element exceeds $3$, so $3$ is a **lower bound**.

Second condition: for every $\varepsilon > 0$ an element less than $3 + \varepsilon$ is needed. The inequality $3 + \dfrac{2}{n} < 3 + \varepsilon$ is equivalent to $\dfrac{2}{n} < \varepsilon$, that is to $n > \dfrac{2}{\varepsilon}$, and such a large natural number exists by the **Archimedean property**. Both conditions are **satisfied**, so $\inf A = 3$.

The minimum **does not exist**, because $3$ does *not* belong to the set; indeed the equality $3 + \dfrac{2}{n} = 3$ would require $\dfrac{2}{n} = 0$, which no natural number achieves.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Determine the supremum and infimum of
$$
A = \left\{ \frac{2n - 1}{n + 1} : n \in \mathbb{N},\ n \geq 1 \right\} ,
$$
proving the value of the supremum with the characterization.

$$
\frac{2n - 1}{n + 1} = \frac{2(n + 1) - 3}{n + 1} = 2 - \frac{3}{n + 1}
$$

$$
2 - \frac{3}{n + 1} < 2 \quad \text{for every } n \geq 1
$$

$$
2 - \frac{3}{n + 1} > 2 - \varepsilon
\iff \frac{3}{n + 1} < \varepsilon
\iff n > \frac{3}{\varepsilon} - 1
$$

$$
\sup A = 2, \qquad \inf A = \min A = \frac{1}{2}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The numerator can be rewritten as $2(n+1) - 3$, so each element equals $2 - \dfrac{3}{n+1}$. In this form the **candidate** supremum is $2$, because the subtracted quantity $\dfrac{3}{n+1}$ becomes small as $n$ increases.

Verification with the characterization. First condition: $\dfrac{3}{n+1} > 0$, so every element is less than $2$ and $2$ is an **upper bound**. Second condition: given $\varepsilon > 0$, the inequality $2 - \dfrac{3}{n+1} > 2 - \varepsilon$ is equivalent to $\dfrac{3}{n+1} < \varepsilon$, that is to $n + 1 > \dfrac{3}{\varepsilon}$, and a natural number $n > \dfrac{3}{\varepsilon} - 1$ exists by the **Archimedean property**. Hence $\sup A = 2$; the maximum **does not exist**, because no element reaches $2$.

For the infimum, observe that as $n$ increases the term $\dfrac{3}{n+1}$ decreases, so the elements increase: the smallest is obtained with $n = 1$ and equals $2 - \dfrac{3}{2} = \dfrac{1}{2}$. Since $\dfrac{1}{2}$ is a lower bound and belongs to the set, it is the **minimum**, and it coincides with the **infimum**.

</div>
