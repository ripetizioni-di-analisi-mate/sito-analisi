## Exercise 1

Prove by induction that $4^n - 1$ is divisible by $3$ for every $n \in \mathbb{N}$.

$$
n = 0: \qquad 4^0 - 1 = 0 = 3 \cdot 0
$$

$$
4^{n+1} - 1 = 4 \cdot 4^n - 1 = 4\,(4^n - 1) + 3
$$

$$
4^n - 1 = 3m \implies 4^{n+1} - 1 = 3\,(4m + 1)
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Base case: for $n = 0$ one obtains $0$, which is **divisible** by $3$ because $0 = 3 \cdot 0$.

Step: assume that $4^n - 1$ is divisible by $3$, that is $4^n - 1 = 3m$ with $m$ an integer. To make the quantity of the **inductive hypothesis** appear, write $4^{n+1} - 1 = 4 \cdot 4^n - 1$ and **add and subtract** $4$: the result is $4(4^n - 1) + 3$. The first term equals $4 \cdot 3m = 3 \cdot 4m$ by the inductive hypothesis, so the whole expression equals $3(4m + 1)$, which is a **multiple** of $3$. By the principle of induction the divisibility holds for every $n$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Prove by induction that $7^n - 2^n$ is divisible by $5$ for every $n \in \mathbb{N}$.

$$
n = 0: \qquad 7^0 - 2^0 = 0 = 5 \cdot 0
$$

$$
7^{n+1} - 2^{n+1} = 7 \cdot 7^n - 2 \cdot 2^n = 7\,(7^n - 2^n) + 5 \cdot 2^n
$$

$$
7^n - 2^n = 5m \implies 7^{n+1} - 2^{n+1} = 5\,(7m + 2^n)
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Base case: for $n = 0$ the difference equals $1 - 1 = 0$, which is **divisible** by $5$.

Step: assume $7^n - 2^n = 5m$ with $m$ an integer. In the next difference, **add and subtract** the term $7 \cdot 2^n$:
since $7 \cdot 7^n - 7 \cdot 2^n = 7(7^n - 2^n)$ and $7 \cdot 2^n - 2 \cdot 2^n = 5 \cdot 2^n$, one obtains $7^{n+1} - 2^{n+1} = 7(7^n - 2^n) + 5 \cdot 2^n$. The first term is $7 \cdot 5m$ by the **inductive hypothesis** and the second is *already* a multiple of $5$, so the sum equals $5(7m + 2^n)$. By the principle of induction the divisibility holds for every $n$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Prove by induction that $n^3 - n$ is divisible by $6$ for every $n \in \mathbb{N}$.

$$
n = 0: \qquad 0^3 - 0 = 0 = 6 \cdot 0
$$

$$
(n+1)^3 - (n+1) = n^3 + 3n^2 + 2n = (n^3 - n) + 3n^2 + 3n = (n^3 - n) + 3n(n+1)
$$

$$
n^3 - n = 6m, \quad n(n+1) = 2h \implies (n+1)^3 - (n+1) = 6\,(m + h)
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Base case: for $n = 0$ the expression equals $0$, which is **divisible** by $6$.

Step: assume $n^3 - n = 6m$ with $m$ an integer. Expanding the cube, $(n+1)^3 - (n+1) = n^3 + 3n^2 + 3n + 1 - n - 1 = n^3 + 3n^2 + 2n$. To make the inductive hypothesis appear, **add and subtract** $n$: the expression becomes $(n^3 - n) + 3n^2 + 3n$, and the last two terms are grouped as $3n(n+1)$. The product $n(n+1)$ is the product of two consecutive natural numbers, *one* of which is even, so $n(n+1) = 2h$ with $h$ an integer, and $3n(n+1) = 6h$. Adding the two multiples of $6$ gives $6(m + h)$, therefore the step is **verified**. By the principle of induction the divisibility holds for every $n$.

</div>
