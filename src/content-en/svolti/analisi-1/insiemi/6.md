## Exercise 1

Prove by induction that $2^n > n$ for every $n \geq 1$.

$$
n = 1: \qquad 2^1 = 2 > 1
$$

$$
2^{n+1} = 2 \cdot 2^n > 2n = n + n \geq n + 1
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Base case: for $n = 1$ the inequality becomes $2 > 1$, which is **true**.

Step: assume $2^n > n$ and estimate $2^{n+1}$. The next power is written as $2 \cdot 2^n$ and, multiplying both sides of the **inductive hypothesis** by $2$, one obtains $2 \cdot 2^n > 2n$. Since $n \geq 1$, we have $2n = n + n \geq n + 1$, so $2^{n+1} > n + 1$, which is the inequality with $n + 1$ in place of $n$. By the **principle of induction** the inequality holds for every $n \geq 1$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Prove by induction that $2^n \geq n^2$ for every $n \geq 4$.

$$
n = 4: \qquad 2^4 = 16 \geq 16 = 4^2
$$

$$
2^{n+1} = 2 \cdot 2^n \geq 2n^2
$$

$$
2n^2 \geq (n+1)^2
\iff n^2 - 2n - 1 \geq 0
\iff (n - 1)^2 \geq 2
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Base case: for $n = 4$ both sides equal $16$, so the inequality is **satisfied** with equality. The base case starts from $4$ because for $n = 3$ the inequality is **false**: $8 < 9$.

Step: from the **inductive hypothesis** $2^n \geq n^2$, multiplying by $2$, it follows that $2^{n+1} \geq 2n^2$. To conclude we need $2n^2 \geq (n+1)^2$. Expanding the square, this inequality is equivalent to $2n^2 \geq n^2 + 2n + 1$, that is to $n^2 - 2n - 1 \geq 0$, that is to $(n-1)^2 \geq 2$. Since in the step we consider $n \geq 4$, we have $(n-1)^2 \geq 9 \geq 2$, so the condition is **verified**. Chaining the two estimates, $2^{n+1} \geq 2n^2 \geq (n+1)^2$, and the step is **proved**. By the principle of induction the inequality holds for every $n \geq 4$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Prove by induction that $n! > 2^n$ for every $n \geq 4$.

$$
n = 4: \qquad 4! = 24 > 16 = 2^4
$$

$$
(n+1)! = (n+1) \cdot n! > (n+1) \cdot 2^n \geq 2 \cdot 2^n = 2^{n+1}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Base case: for $n = 4$ we have $4! = 24$ and $2^4 = 16$, so the inequality is true. For $n = 1, 2, 3$ it is **false**, since $1! < 2$, $2! < 4$ and $3! < 8$, therefore the base case starts from $4$.

Step: by the **recursive formula** for the factorial, $(n+1)! = (n+1) \cdot n!$. By the **inductive hypothesis** $n! > 2^n$ and, multiplying by the *positive* number $n + 1$, one obtains $(n+1)! > (n+1) \cdot 2^n$. Since in the step $n \geq 4$, the factor $n + 1$ is at least $5$, hence greater than $2$, and therefore $(n+1) \cdot 2^n \geq 2 \cdot 2^n = 2^{n+1}$. Chaining the estimates gives $(n+1)! > 2^{n+1}$, that is the inequality with $n + 1$ in place of $n$. By the **principle of induction** the inequality holds for every $n \geq 4$.

</div>
