## Exercise 1

Prove that $\sqrt{3}$ is irrational.

$$
\sqrt{3} = \frac{m}{n} \ \text{in lowest terms}
\implies m^2 = 3n^2
$$

$$
3 \ \text{divides} \ m \implies m = 3k \implies 9k^2 = 3n^2 \implies n^2 = 3k^2 \implies 3 \ \text{divides} \ n
$$

$$
\text{contradiction: } \sqrt{3} \notin \mathbb{Q}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Suppose, **by contradiction**, that $\sqrt{3}$ is rational and write it as a fraction $\dfrac{m}{n}$ in lowest terms, that is with $m$ and $n$ having no common divisors. Squaring gives $m^2 = 3n^2$, so $m^2$ is divisible by $3$. Since $3$ is **prime**, if $3$ divides $m^2$ then $3$ divides $m$: indeed, if $m$ were not divisible by $3$, no factor $3$ would appear in the factorization of $m^2$, which has the same prime factors as $m$. We then write $m = 3k$ with $k$ an integer.

Substituting, $9k^2 = 3n^2$, from which $n^2 = 3k^2$: $n^2$ is also divisible by $3$ and, by the same reasoning, $3$ divides $n$. Hence $m$ and $n$ have the common divisor $3$, which **contradicts** the assumption that the fraction is in lowest terms. The contradiction shows that $\sqrt{3}$ **is not rational**.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Prove that $\log_2 3$ is irrational.

$$
\log_2 3 = \frac{m}{n} \ \text{with} \ m, n \ \text{positive integers}
\implies 2^{\frac{m}{n}} = 3
\implies 2^m = 3^n
$$

$$
2^m \ \text{even}, \quad 3^n \ \text{odd} \implies \text{contradiction: } \log_2 3 \notin \mathbb{Q}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Suppose, **by contradiction**, that $\log_2 3$ is rational. Since $3 > 1$, the logarithm is positive, so we can write $\log_2 3 = \dfrac{m}{n}$ with $m$ and $n$ positive integers. By the **definition of logarithm** the equality means $2^{\frac{m}{n}} = 3$ and, raising both sides to the power $n$, one obtains $2^m = 3^n$.

This equality between integers is **impossible**: the number $2^m$ is even, because $m \geq 1$ and therefore the factor $2$ appears at least once, while $3^n$ is odd, because it is a product of odd numbers. An integer cannot be *both* even and odd, therefore the assumption of rationality is absurd and $\log_2 3$ is **irrational**.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Prove that $\sqrt{2} + \sqrt{3}$ is irrational.

$$
\sqrt{2} + \sqrt{3} = q \in \mathbb{Q}
\implies q^2 = 5 + 2\sqrt{6}
\implies \sqrt{6} = \frac{q^2 - 5}{2} \in \mathbb{Q}
$$

$$
\sqrt{6} = \frac{m}{n} \ \text{in lowest terms}
\implies m^2 = 6n^2
\implies m = 2k
\implies 2k^2 = 3n^2
\implies n \ \text{even}
$$

$$
\text{contradiction: } \sqrt{6} \notin \mathbb{Q} \implies \sqrt{2} + \sqrt{3} \notin \mathbb{Q}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Suppose, **by contradiction**, that $\sqrt{2} + \sqrt{3} = q$ with $q$ rational. Squaring gives $q^2 = 2 + 2\sqrt{6} + 3 = 5 + 2\sqrt{6}$, so $\sqrt{6} = \dfrac{q^2 - 5}{2}$. The right-hand side is rational, because sums, products and quotients of rationals, with nonzero divisor, are rational; hence $\sqrt{6}$ *would* also be rational.

It remains to show that $\sqrt{6}$ is **irrational**, with the same argument used for $\sqrt{2}$. Let $\sqrt{6} = \dfrac{m}{n}$ with the fraction in lowest terms; squaring, $m^2 = 6n^2$. The right-hand side is even, so $m^2$ is even and therefore $m$ is even: $m = 2k$. Substituting, $4k^2 = 6n^2$, that is $2k^2 = 3n^2$: the left-hand side is even, so $3n^2$ is even and, since $3$ is odd, $n^2$ is even, from which $n$ is even. Then $m$ and $n$ have the common divisor $2$, contradicting the assumption that the fraction is in lowest terms. Hence $\sqrt{6}$ is irrational, the equality $\sqrt{6} = \dfrac{q^2 - 5}{2}$ is absurd and $\sqrt{2} + \sqrt{3}$ **is not rational**.

</div>
