## Exercise 1

Prove by induction that, for every $n \geq 1$,
$$
\sum_{k=1}^{n} (3k - 2) = \frac{n(3n - 1)}{2} .
$$

$$
n = 1: \qquad 3 \cdot 1 - 2 = 1, \qquad \frac{1 \cdot (3 - 1)}{2} = 1
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\sum_{k=1}^{n+1} (3k - 2)
&= \frac{n(3n - 1)}{2} + (3n + 1) \\[6pt]
&= \frac{3n^2 - n + 6n + 2}{2} \\[6pt]
&= \frac{(n+1)(3n + 2)}{2}
= \frac{(n+1)\bigl(3(n+1) - 1\bigr)}{2}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Base case: for $n = 1$ the sum reduces to the single term $3 \cdot 1 - 2 = 1$ and the formula gives $\dfrac{1 \cdot 2}{2} = 1$, so the two sides **coincide**.

Step: assume the formula is true for $n$ and compute the sum up to $n + 1$. The sum up to $n + 1$ is the sum up to $n$ plus the additional term, which equals $3(n+1) - 2 = 3n + 1$; by the **inductive hypothesis** the sum up to $n$ equals $\dfrac{n(3n-1)}{2}$. Bringing everything to the same denominator gives $\dfrac{3n^2 + 5n + 2}{2}$, and the trinomial factors as $(n+1)(3n+2)$, since its roots are $-1$ and $-\dfrac{2}{3}$. The result is the formula with $n + 1$ in place of $n$, so the step is **verified** and, by the **principle of induction**, the identity holds for every $n \geq 1$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Prove by induction that, for every $n \geq 1$,
$$
\sum_{k=1}^{n} k^2 = \frac{n(n+1)(2n+1)}{6} .
$$

$$
n = 1: \qquad 1^2 = 1, \qquad \frac{1 \cdot 2 \cdot 3}{6} = 1
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\sum_{k=1}^{n+1} k^2
&= \frac{n(n+1)(2n+1)}{6} + (n+1)^2 \\[6pt]
&= \frac{(n+1)\bigl(n(2n+1) + 6(n+1)\bigr)}{6} \\[6pt]
&= \frac{(n+1)(2n^2 + 7n + 6)}{6}
= \frac{(n+1)(n+2)(2n+3)}{6}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Base case: for $n = 1$ both sides equal $1$, so the formula is **true**.

Step: the sum of the squares up to $n + 1$ is the sum up to $n$, which by the **inductive hypothesis** equals $\dfrac{n(n+1)(2n+1)}{6}$, plus the square $(n+1)^2$. It is convenient to factor out the **common factor** $n + 1$ right away, so that the computation stays on a second-degree polynomial: in the remaining factor one obtains $2n^2 + n + 6n + 6 = 2n^2 + 7n + 6$. This trinomial has roots $-2$ and $-\dfrac{3}{2}$, therefore it factors as $(n+2)(2n+3)$. The final expression is $\dfrac{(n+1)(n+2)(2n+3)}{6}$, which coincides with the formula evaluated at $n + 1$, since $n + 2 = (n+1) + 1$ and $2n + 3 = 2(n+1) + 1$. By the **principle of induction** the formula holds for every $n \geq 1$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Prove by induction that, for every $n \geq 1$,
$$
\sum_{k=1}^{n} \frac{1}{k(k+1)} = \frac{n}{n+1} .
$$

$$
n = 1: \qquad \frac{1}{1 \cdot 2} = \frac{1}{2}, \qquad \frac{1}{1 + 1} = \frac{1}{2}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\sum_{k=1}^{n+1} \frac{1}{k(k+1)}
&= \frac{n}{n+1} + \frac{1}{(n+1)(n+2)} \\[6pt]
&= \frac{n(n+2) + 1}{(n+1)(n+2)} \\[6pt]
&= \frac{(n+1)^2}{(n+1)(n+2)}
= \frac{n+1}{n+2}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Base case: for $n = 1$ the sum contains the single term $\dfrac{1}{1 \cdot 2} = \dfrac{1}{2}$ and the formula gives the *same* value.

Step: to the sum up to $n$, which by the **inductive hypothesis** equals $\dfrac{n}{n+1}$, we add the term with index $n + 1$, that is $\dfrac{1}{(n+1)(n+2)}$. The common denominator is $(n+1)(n+2)$ and the numerator becomes $n(n+2) + 1 = n^2 + 2n + 1$, which is the square $(n+1)^2$. Cancelling the factor $n + 1$, common to numerator and denominator, leaves $\dfrac{n+1}{n+2}$, that is the formula with $n + 1$ in place of $n$. By the **principle of induction** the identity holds for every $n \geq 1$.

</div>
