## Exercise 1

Determine whether the following limit exists:
$$
\lim_{n \to +\infty} (-1)^n\, \frac{n}{n + 1} .
$$

$$
a_{2k} = \frac{2k}{2k + 1} \to 1, \qquad
a_{2k+1} = -\frac{2k + 1}{2k + 2} \to -1
$$

$$
1 \neq -1 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \text{the limit does not exist}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The factor $(-1)^n$ equals $1$ for even indices and $-1$ for odd indices, therefore we consider the two corresponding **subsequences**. The subsequence of even index tends to $1$ and the one of odd index to $-1$. If the sequence had limit $L$, every subsequence of it would tend to $L$; since the two subsequences have different limits, the limit **does not exist** and the sequence is indeterminate. The sequence is nevertheless bounded, because $|a_n| < 1$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Determine the behavior of the sequence
$$
a_n = n^{(-1)^n}, \qquad n \geq 1 .
$$

$$
a_{2k} = (2k)^{1} = 2k \to +\infty, \qquad
a_{2k+1} = (2k + 1)^{-1} = \frac{1}{2k + 1} \to 0
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The exponent equals $1$ for even indices and $-1$ for odd indices. The subsequence of even index is $2k$, which diverges to $+\infty$; the one of odd index is $\dfrac{1}{2k + 1}$, which tends to $0$. The two subsequences have different limits, therefore the sequence **has no limit**. Moreover it is not bounded above, because the subsequence of even index diverges; it is bounded below, since all its terms are positive.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Compute, if it exists,
$$
\lim_{n \to +\infty} \frac{2 + (-1)^n}{n} .
$$

$$
a_{2k} = \frac{3}{2k} \to 0, \qquad
a_{2k+1} = \frac{1}{2k + 1} \to 0
$$

$$
\frac{1}{n} \leq \frac{2 + (-1)^n}{n} \leq \frac{3}{n}
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
\lim_{n \to +\infty} \frac{2 + (-1)^n}{n} = 0
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The subsequences of even index and of odd index both tend to $0$. In general, two subsequences with the same limit are not enough to conclude that the sequence has that limit. In this case, however, the two subsequences together contain *all* the terms of the sequence: having fixed $\varepsilon > 0$, the terms of even index are at distance less than $\varepsilon$ from $0$ from a certain index on, and the same holds for those of odd index, therefore it holds for all the terms beyond the larger of the two indices. Hence $a_n \to 0$. The same conclusion is obtained with the **squeeze theorem**: the numerator $2 + (-1)^n$ lies between $1$ and $3$, therefore $a_n$ lies between $\dfrac{1}{n}$ and $\dfrac{3}{n}$, which both tend to $0$.

</div>
