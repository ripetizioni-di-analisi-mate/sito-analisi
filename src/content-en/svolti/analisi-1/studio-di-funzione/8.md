## Exercise 1

Study the function
$$
f(x) = \sqrt[3]{x^2}\,(x - 5)
$$
and sketch its qualitative graph.

**Domain.** The cube root is defined for every real number, therefore $D = \mathbb{R}$.

**Symmetries and periodicity.** $f(-x) = \sqrt[3]{x^2}\,(-x - 5)$ is different from both $f(x)$ and $-f(x)$, therefore **neither evenness nor oddness**; it is not periodic.

**Sign and intersections.** The factor $\sqrt[3]{x^2}$ is positive for $x \neq 0$, therefore the sign of $f$ is the sign of $x - 5$: $f(x) < 0$ for $x < 5$ with $x \neq 0$, $f(x) > 0$ for $x > 5$. The zeros are $0$ and $5$.

**Limits and asymptotes.** The expression with fractional exponents is convenient, $f(x) = x^{\frac{5}{3}} - 5x^{\frac{2}{3}}$, where $x^{\frac{p}{3}}$ denotes $\sqrt[3]{x^p}$ also for $x < 0$, because the cube root is defined on all of $\mathbb{R}$. The term $x^{\frac{5}{3}}$ *dominates*, therefore
$$
\lim_{x \to \pm\infty} f(x) = \pm\infty .
$$
Since $\dfrac{f(x)}{x} = x^{\frac{2}{3}} - 5x^{-\frac{1}{3}} \to +\infty$ as $x \to \pm\infty$, **there are no oblique asymptotes**; there are no vertical or horizontal asymptotes.

**Continuity.** $f$ is a product of continuous functions, therefore it is continuous on $\mathbb{R}$.

**Differentiability.** For $x \neq 0$,
$$
f'(x) = \frac{5}{3}\,x^{\frac{2}{3}} - \frac{10}{3}\,x^{-\frac{1}{3}} = \frac{5(x - 2)}{3\sqrt[3]{x}} .
$$
As $x \to 0^+$ the numerator tends to $-10$ and the denominator to $0^+$, therefore $f'(x) \to -\infty$; as $x \to 0^-$ the denominator tends to $0^-$, therefore $f'(x) \to +\infty$. The two limits are infinite with *opposite* signs, therefore $0$ is a **cusp** and $f$ **is not differentiable** at $0$.

**Monotonicity, maxima and minima.** The sign of $f'$ is the sign of $\dfrac{x - 2}{\sqrt[3]{x}}$: positive for $x < 0$, negative for $0 < x < 2$, positive for $x > 2$. By the monotonicity test $f$ is increasing on $(-\infty, 0]$, decreasing on $[0, 2]$, increasing on $[2, +\infty)$. By the **first derivative test**, which does *not* require differentiability at the point, the cusp $0$ is a **relative maximum point** with $f(0) = 0$; the stationary point $2$ is a **relative minimum** point with
$$
f(2) = \sqrt[3]{4}\,(2 - 5) = -3\sqrt[3]{4} .
$$
The absolute maximum and minimum **do not exist**, because the function is unbounded in both directions.

**Concavity and inflection points.** For $x \neq 0$,
$$
f''(x) = \frac{10}{9}\,x^{-\frac{1}{3}} + \frac{10}{9}\,x^{-\frac{4}{3}} = \frac{10\,(x + 1)}{9\sqrt[3]{x^4}} .
$$
The denominator is positive for $x \neq 0$, therefore the sign of $f''$ is the sign of $x + 1$: the function is concave on $(-\infty, -1]$ and convex on $[-1, 0)$ and on $(0, +\infty)$. At $-1$ the second derivative changes sign, therefore it is an **inflection point**, with $f(-1) = \sqrt[3]{1}\,(-6) = -6$; at $0$ the concavity does *not* change and the point is the cusp.

**Graph.**

<div class="spiegazione">

The **cusp** is recognized from the one-sided limits of the derivative: both infinite but with *opposite* signs, therefore the graph reaches $(0, 0)$ with a vertical tangent from both sides and forms a spike there. Fermat's theorem does not classify this point, because the derivative at $0$ does not exist; the **first derivative test** instead applies, since it requires only continuity at $0$ and the sign of $f'$ on the sides, positive before and negative after, and it gives the **relative maximum**.

The switch to **fractional exponents**, $f(x) = x^{\frac{5}{3}} - 5x^{\frac{2}{3}}$, makes the computation of the derivatives mechanical; in the factoring of $f''$ one collects $\dfrac{10}{9}x^{-\frac{4}{3}}$ and $x + 1$ remains. The factor $x^{-\frac{4}{3}} = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt[3]{x^4}}$ is positive for every $x \neq 0$, because $x^4 > 0$, therefore it does *not* affect the sign.

The relative maximum at $0$ equals $0$ and the function is negative in a whole punctured neighborhood of $0$, in agreement with the sign found at the beginning: the graph touches the $x$-axis at the origin from below, **without crossing it**.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Study the function
$$
f(x) = |x|\,e^{x}
$$
and sketch its qualitative graph.

**Domain.** $D = \mathbb{R}$.

**Symmetries and periodicity.** $f(-x) = |x|\,e^{-x}$ is different from both $f(x)$ and $-f(x)$, therefore **neither evenness nor oddness**; it is not periodic.

**Sign and intersections.** The factor $e^x$ is positive and $|x| \geq 0$, therefore $f(x) \geq 0$ for every $x$, with $f(x) = 0$ only at $x = 0$. The graph touches the axes *only* at the origin.

**Limits and asymptotes.** As $x \to +\infty$ both factors tend to $+\infty$, therefore $f(x) \to +\infty$. As $x \to -\infty$ the product $|x|\,e^{x} = \dfrac{-x}{e^{-x}}$ is a ratio between a power and an exponential, therefore by the **hierarchy of infinities**
$$
\lim_{x \to -\infty} |x|\,e^{x} = 0^+
$$
and $y = 0$ is a **horizontal asymptote** as $x \to -\infty$. As $x \to +\infty$ we have $\dfrac{f(x)}{x} = e^x \to +\infty$, therefore **no oblique asymptote**.

**Continuity.** Product of continuous functions, therefore continuous on $\mathbb{R}$.

**Differentiability.** Removing the absolute value,
$$
f(x) =
\begin{cases}
x\,e^{x} & \text{if } x \geq 0 \\[4pt]
-x\,e^{x} & \text{if } x < 0
\end{cases}
\qquad
f'(x) =
\begin{cases}
(x + 1)\,e^{x} & \text{if } x > 0 \\[4pt]
-(x + 1)\,e^{x} & \text{if } x < 0
\end{cases}
$$
The one-sided limits of $f'$ at $0$ equal $1$ from the right and $-1$ from the left, finite and different, therefore $0$ is a **corner point** and $f$ is differentiable on $\mathbb{R} \setminus \{0\}$.

**Monotonicity, maxima and minima.** For $x > 0$ we have $f'(x) = (x + 1)e^x > 0$. For $x < 0$ the sign of $f'$ is the sign of $-(x + 1)$: positive for $x < -1$, negative for $-1 < x < 0$. By the monotonicity test $f$ is increasing on $(-\infty, -1]$, decreasing on $[-1, 0]$, increasing on $[0, +\infty)$. By the **first derivative test** $-1$ is a relative maximum point with $f(-1) = \dfrac{1}{e}$, while the corner point $0$ is a relative minimum point with $f(0) = 0$; since $f \geq 0$ everywhere, $0$ is also the **absolute minimum**. The absolute maximum does not exist, because $f \to +\infty$.

**Concavity and inflection points.** For $x > 0$ we have $f''(x) = (x + 2)e^x > 0$, therefore convexity. For $x < 0$ we have $f''(x) = -(x + 2)e^x$, positive for $x < -2$ and negative for $-2 < x < 0$: the function is convex on $(-\infty, -2]$, concave on $[-2, 0]$, convex on $[0, +\infty)$. At $-2$ the second derivative changes sign, therefore it is an **inflection point** with $f(-2) = \dfrac{2}{e^2}$; at $0$ the concavity changes again, but the point is a corner point and has *no* tangent line, therefore it **is not an inflection point**.

**Graph.**

::grafico{f="abs(x)*exp(x)" xmin=-6 xmax=1.5 ymin=-0.5 ymax=3 asintoti="y=0" titolo="$y = |x| e^x$: maximum at $\left(-1, \dfrac{1}{e}\right)$, corner point and minimum at $(0, 0)$, inflection point at $\left(-2, \dfrac{2}{e^2}\right)$"}

<div class="spiegazione">

At the corner point the same logic as for the cusp holds: the derivative at $0$ does not exist, therefore Fermat's theorem says nothing, but the continuity of $f$ at $0$ and the sign of $f'$ on the two sides, negative before and positive after, make it possible to apply the **first derivative test** and to conclude that $0$ is a **minimum point**. The difference from the cusp lies in the one-sided limits of the derivative, here *finite* ($-1$ and $1$): the graph forms an edge with two oblique half-tangents, not a spike with vertical tangent.

The limit as $x \to -\infty$ is reduced to the **hierarchy of infinities** by rewriting the product as a ratio, $\dfrac{-x}{e^{-x}}$, with the mental substitution $t = -x \to +\infty$ that transforms it into $\dfrac{t}{e^{t}} \to 0$. The graph therefore flattens onto the $x$-axis **from above**, consistently with the inflection point at $-2$ and with the concavity on $[-2, 0]$.

</div>
