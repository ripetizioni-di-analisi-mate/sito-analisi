## Exercise 1

Study the function
$$
f(x) = x \ln x
$$
and sketch its qualitative graph.

**Domain.** The logarithm requires a positive argument, therefore $D = (0, +\infty)$.

**Symmetries and periodicity.** The domain is not symmetric with respect to the origin, therefore the function **can be neither even nor odd**; it is not periodic.

**Sign and intersections.** On $D$ the factor $x$ is positive, therefore the sign of $f$ is the sign of $\ln x$: $f(x) < 0$ for $0 < x < 1$, $f(x) > 0$ for $x > 1$, $f(1) = 0$. The only intersection with the axes is the point $(1, 0)$.

**Limits and asymptotes.** As $x \to 0^+$ the product is a form $0 \cdot \infty$; with the substitution $x = \dfrac{1}{t}$ the product becomes $-\dfrac{\ln t}{t}$ with $t \to +\infty$, and by the **hierarchy of infinities** the logarithm is negligible with respect to the power, therefore
$$
\lim_{x \to 0^+} x \ln x = 0^-, \qquad
\lim_{x \to +\infty} x \ln x = +\infty .
$$
**There is no vertical asymptote** at $0$, because the limit is finite; **there is no oblique asymptote**, because $\dfrac{f(x)}{x} = \ln x \to +\infty$.

**Continuity.** $f$ is a product of continuous functions on $D$, therefore it is continuous on $D$.

**Differentiability and monotonicity.** $f$ is differentiable on $D$ and
$$
f'(x) = \ln x + 1 .
$$
We have $f'(x) > 0$ for $\ln x > -1$, that is for $x > \dfrac{1}{e}$. By the **monotonicity test** $f$ is decreasing on $\left(0, \dfrac{1}{e}\right]$ and increasing on $\left[\dfrac{1}{e}, +\infty\right)$.

**Maxima and minima.** The only stationary point is $\dfrac{1}{e}$, where the derivative changes from negative to positive, therefore by the **first derivative test** it is a **relative minimum point**, with
$$
f\!\left(\frac{1}{e}\right) = \frac{1}{e} \ln \frac{1}{e} = -\frac{1}{e} .
$$
Because of the pattern of monotonicity the value $-\dfrac{1}{e}$ is also the **absolute minimum**; the absolute maximum does *not* exist, because $f \to +\infty$.

**Concavity and inflection points.** The second derivative is
$$
f''(x) = \frac{1}{x} > 0 \qquad \text{for every } x \in D ,
$$
therefore $f$ is **strictly convex** on the whole domain and **has no inflection points**.

**Graph.**

<div class="spiegazione">

The limit at $0^+$ is the delicate step. The factor $x$ tends to $0$ and $\ln x$ tends to $-\infty$, therefore the product is an **indeterminate form**; the **hierarchy of infinities** establishes that the power prevails over the logarithm, hence the product tends to $0$, with negative sign because $\ln x < 0$ near $0$. The graph approaches the origin from below, but the point $(0, 0)$ does *not* belong to the graph, because $0$ is not in the domain.

Since the limit at $0^+$ is finite, the line $x = 0$ **is not a vertical asymptote**, although it is an endpoint of the domain: the definition of vertical asymptote requires an *infinite* one-sided limit. The study of asymptotes at a boundary point of the domain always starts from computing the limit, not from the mere exclusion of the point.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Study the function
$$
f(x) = \frac{\ln x}{x}
$$
and sketch its qualitative graph.

**Domain.** We need a positive argument of the logarithm and a nonzero denominator; the first condition implies the second, therefore $D = (0, +\infty)$.

**Symmetries and periodicity.** The domain is not symmetric with respect to the origin, therefore **no evenness or oddness**; it is not periodic.

**Sign and intersections.** On $D$ the denominator is positive, therefore the sign of $f$ is the sign of $\ln x$: $f(x) < 0$ for $0 < x < 1$, $f(x) > 0$ for $x > 1$, $f(1) = 0$. The only intersection with the axes is $(1, 0)$.

**Limits and asymptotes.** At the endpoints of the domain:
$$
\lim_{x \to 0^+} \frac{\ln x}{x} = -\infty, \qquad
\lim_{x \to +\infty} \frac{\ln x}{x} = 0^+ ,
$$
the first because the numerator tends to $-\infty$ and the denominator to $0^+$, the second by the hierarchy of infinities. Therefore $x = 0$ is a **vertical asymptote** and $y = 0$ is a **horizontal asymptote** as $x \to +\infty$.

**Continuity.** $f$ is a quotient of continuous functions with nonzero denominator on $D$, therefore it is continuous on $D$.

**Differentiability and monotonicity.** $f$ is differentiable on $D$ and
$$
f'(x) = \frac{\dfrac{1}{x} \cdot x - \ln x}{x^2} = \frac{1 - \ln x}{x^2} .
$$
We have $f'(x) > 0$ for $\ln x < 1$, that is for $0 < x < e$. By the **monotonicity test** $f$ is increasing on $(0, e]$ and decreasing on $[e, +\infty)$.

**Maxima and minima.** The only stationary point is $e$, where the derivative changes from positive to negative, therefore $e$ is a **relative maximum point** and, because of the pattern of monotonicity, also an **absolute** one, with $f(e) = \dfrac{1}{e}$. The absolute minimum does *not* exist, because $f \to -\infty$ as $x \to 0^+$.

**Concavity and inflection points.** Differentiating $f'(x) = (1 - \ln x)\,x^{-2}$,
$$
f''(x) = -\frac{1}{x} \cdot x^{-2} + (1 - \ln x)\,(-2x^{-3}) = \frac{2\ln x - 3}{x^3} ,
$$
therefore $f''(x) > 0$ for $\ln x > \dfrac{3}{2}$, that is for $x > e^{3/2}$. The function is **concave** on $\left(0, e^{3/2}\right]$ and **convex** on $\left[e^{3/2}, +\infty\right)$; at $e^{3/2}$ the second derivative changes sign, therefore it is an **inflection point**, with $f\!\left(e^{3/2}\right) = \dfrac{3}{2}\,e^{-3/2}$.

**Graph.**

<div class="spiegazione">

The two limits at the endpoints of the domain are of a different nature. At $0^+$ there is *no* indeterminate form, since the numerator tends to $-\infty$ and the denominator to $0$ while staying positive, therefore the quotient tends to $-\infty$. At infinity the ratio is a form $\dfrac{\infty}{\infty}$ between a logarithm and a power, and by the **hierarchy of infinities** the power in the denominator wins, hence the limit is $0$; the function is positive for $x > 1$, therefore the asymptote $y = 0$ is approached **from above**.

The absolute maximum at $x = e$ has a useful reading beyond the study: from the inequality $\dfrac{\ln x}{x} \leq \dfrac{1}{e}$, valid for *every* $x > 0$, it follows that $e \ln x \leq x$, that is $x^e \leq e^x$. The comparison between $e^{\pi}$ and $\pi^e$ is decided precisely in this way.

</div>
