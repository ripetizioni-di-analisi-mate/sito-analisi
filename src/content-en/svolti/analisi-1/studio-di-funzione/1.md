## Exercise 1

Study the function
$$
f(x) = \frac{x^2}{x - 1}
$$
and sketch its qualitative graph.

**Domain.** The denominator must be nonzero, therefore $D = (-\infty, 1) \cup (1, +\infty)$.

**Symmetries and periodicity.** $f(-x) = \dfrac{x^2}{-x - 1}$ is different from both $f(x)$ and $-f(x)$, therefore the function is **neither even nor odd**; it is not periodic.

**Sign and intersections.** The numerator $x^2$ is positive for $x \neq 0$ and zero at $0$, therefore the sign of $f$ coincides with the sign of $x - 1$. Hence $f(x) > 0$ for $x > 1$, $f(x) < 0$ for $x < 1$ with $x \neq 0$, and the graph meets the axes *only* at the origin.

**Limits and asymptotes.** Polynomial division gives
$$
f(x) = x + 1 + \frac{1}{x - 1} .
$$
At the endpoints of the domain:
$$
\lim_{x \to \pm\infty} f(x) = \pm\infty, \qquad
\lim_{x \to 1^-} f(x) = -\infty, \qquad
\lim_{x \to 1^+} f(x) = +\infty ,
$$
therefore $x = 1$ is a **vertical asymptote**. Since $f(x) - (x + 1) = \dfrac{1}{x - 1} \to 0$ as $x \to \pm\infty$, the line $y = x + 1$ is an **oblique asymptote** on both sides.

**Continuity.** $f$ is a quotient of polynomials with nonzero denominator on $D$, therefore it is continuous on $D$.

**Differentiability and monotonicity.** $f$ is differentiable on all of $D$ and
$$
f'(x) = 1 - \frac{1}{(x - 1)^2} = \frac{(x - 1)^2 - 1}{(x - 1)^2} = \frac{x(x - 2)}{(x - 1)^2} .
$$
The denominator is positive on $D$, therefore $f'(x) > 0$ for $x < 0$ or $x > 2$. By the **monotonicity test** $f$ is increasing on $(-\infty, 0]$, decreasing on $[0, 1)$, decreasing on $(1, 2]$, increasing on $[2, +\infty)$.

**Maxima and minima.** The stationary points are $0$ and $2$. At $0$ the derivative changes from positive to negative, therefore by the **first derivative test** $0$ is a **relative maximum point** with $f(0) = 0$; at $2$ the derivative changes from negative to positive, therefore $2$ is a **relative minimum point** with $f(2) = 4$. Since $f$ tends to $+\infty$ and to $-\infty$, the absolute maximum and minimum do *not* exist.

**Concavity and inflection points.** Differentiating $f'(x) = 1 - (x - 1)^{-2}$ gives
$$
f''(x) = \frac{2}{(x - 1)^3} ,
$$
negative for $x < 1$ and positive for $x > 1$. The function is **concave** on $(-\infty, 1)$ and **convex** on $(1, +\infty)$; the change of concavity occurs at $x = 1$, which does *not* belong to the domain, therefore **there are no inflection points**.

**Graph.**

<div class="spiegazione">

The **division with remainder** of the numerator by the denominator, $x^2 = (x - 1)(x + 1) + 1$, is the step that simplifies the whole study. Since the difference $f(x) - (x + 1)$ is $\dfrac{1}{x - 1}$ and tends to $0$, the line $y = x + 1$ is an oblique asymptote *without* the need to compute separately the limits of $\dfrac{f(x)}{x}$ and of $f(x) - mx$. The same expression makes the second derivative immediate.

For the one-sided limits at $1$ the numerator tends to $1 > 0$, while the denominator tends to $0$ changing sign: from the left $x - 1 < 0$, therefore the quotient tends to $-\infty$; from the right $x - 1 > 0$, therefore it tends to $+\infty$. The result is **consistent with the sign** already found, since $f < 0$ to the left of $1$ and $f > 0$ to the right.

The value of the relative maximum, $f(0) = 0$, is *less* than the value of the relative minimum, $f(2) = 4$. No rule requires the opposite: the two points lie on **branches separated** by the vertical asymptote, and on each branch the function reaches arbitrarily large and arbitrarily small values.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Study the function
$$
f(x) = \frac{x^2 + 4}{x}
$$
and sketch its qualitative graph.

**Domain.** The denominator vanishes at $0$, therefore $D = (-\infty, 0) \cup (0, +\infty)$.

**Symmetries and periodicity.** $f(-x) = \dfrac{x^2 + 4}{-x} = -f(x)$, therefore the function is **odd** and the graph is symmetric with respect to the origin; it is not periodic.

**Sign and intersections.** The numerator $x^2 + 4$ is always positive, therefore the sign of $f$ is the sign of $x$. We have $f(x) > 0$ for $x > 0$ and $f(x) < 0$ for $x < 0$; the function never vanishes and the graph **does not meet the axes**.

**Limits and asymptotes.** It is convenient to write
$$
f(x) = x + \frac{4}{x} .
$$
At the endpoints of the domain:
$$
\lim_{x \to \pm\infty} f(x) = \pm\infty, \qquad
\lim_{x \to 0^-} f(x) = -\infty, \qquad
\lim_{x \to 0^+} f(x) = +\infty ,
$$
therefore $x = 0$ is a **vertical asymptote**. Since $f(x) - x = \dfrac{4}{x} \to 0$ as $x \to \pm\infty$, the line $y = x$ is an **oblique asymptote** on both sides.

**Continuity.** $f$ is a quotient of polynomials with nonzero denominator on $D$, therefore it is continuous on $D$.

**Differentiability and monotonicity.** $f$ is differentiable on $D$ and
$$
f'(x) = 1 - \frac{4}{x^2} = \frac{x^2 - 4}{x^2} .
$$
Therefore $f'(x) > 0$ for $|x| > 2$. By the monotonicity test $f$ is increasing on $(-\infty, -2]$, decreasing on $[-2, 0)$, decreasing on $(0, 2]$, increasing on $[2, +\infty)$.

**Maxima and minima.** The stationary points are $-2$ and $2$. By the **first derivative test** $-2$ is a **relative maximum point** with $f(-2) = -4$, while $2$ is a **relative minimum point** with $f(2) = 4$, in agreement with the odd symmetry. The absolute maximum and minimum do *not* exist, because the function is unbounded in both directions.

**Concavity and inflection points.** Differentiating $f'(x) = 1 - 4x^{-2}$ gives
$$
f''(x) = \frac{8}{x^3} ,
$$
negative for $x < 0$ and positive for $x > 0$. The function is **concave** on $(-\infty, 0)$ and **convex** on $(0, +\infty)$; the point $0$ does not belong to the domain, therefore **there are no inflection points**.

**Graph.**

::grafico{f="x+4/x;x" xmin=-8 xmax=8 ymin=-10 ymax=10 asintoti="x=0" titolo="$y = \dfrac{x^2 + 4}{x}$: maximum at $(-2, -4)$, minimum at $(2, 4)$, vertical asymptote $x = 0$ and oblique asymptote $y = x$ (dashed)"}

<div class="spiegazione">

The **odd symmetry**, verified in the second step, halves the work: every result found for $x > 0$ carries over to $x < 0$ by changing the sign of both coordinates. The relative minimum $(2, 4)$ gives the relative maximum $(-2, -4)$, and convexity on $(0, +\infty)$ gives concavity on $(-\infty, 0)$.

On the right branch the function is positive, decreases down to the value $4$ and then increases toward $+\infty$ along the asymptote $y = x$: the value $4$ is the **minimum of that branch**, that is $f(x) \geq 4$ for every $x > 0$. It is *not*, however, an absolute minimum, because on the left branch the function takes negative values of arbitrarily large absolute value.

</div>
