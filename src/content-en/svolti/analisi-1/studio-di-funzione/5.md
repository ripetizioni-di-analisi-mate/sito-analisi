## Exercise 1

Study the function
$$
f(x) = \sqrt{x^2 - 4}
$$
and sketch its qualitative graph.

**Domain.** The radicand must be nonnegative, therefore $x^2 - 4 \geq 0$, that is $D = (-\infty, -2] \cup [2, +\infty)$.

**Symmetries and periodicity.** $f(-x) = \sqrt{x^2 - 4} = f(x)$, therefore the function is **even**; it is not periodic.

**Sign and intersections.** A square root is nonnegative, therefore $f(x) \geq 0$ on all of $D$ and $f(x) = 0$ only for $x = \pm 2$. The point $0$ does not belong to the domain, therefore **there is no intersection** with the $y$-axis.

**Limits and asymptotes.** $f(x) \to +\infty$ as $x \to \pm\infty$. For the oblique asymptote at $+\infty$:
$$
m = \lim_{x \to +\infty} \frac{\sqrt{x^2 - 4}}{x} = \lim_{x \to +\infty} \sqrt{1 - \frac{4}{x^2}} = 1 ,
$$
$$
q = \lim_{x \to +\infty} \left( \sqrt{x^2 - 4} - x \right)
= \lim_{x \to +\infty} \frac{-4}{\sqrt{x^2 - 4} + x} = 0 ,
$$
therefore $y = x$ is an **oblique asymptote** as $x \to +\infty$; by even symmetry, $y = -x$ is an **oblique asymptote** as $x \to -\infty$. There are *no* vertical asymptotes, because at the boundary points $\pm 2$ the function equals $0$.

**Continuity.** $f$ is the composition of the polynomial $x^2 - 4$ with the square root, both continuous, therefore it is continuous on $D$.

**Differentiability.** At the interior points of the domain
$$
f'(x) = \frac{2x}{2\sqrt{x^2 - 4}} = \frac{x}{\sqrt{x^2 - 4}} .
$$
As $x \to 2^+$ we have $f'(x) \to +\infty$ and as $x \to -2^-$ we have $f'(x) \to -\infty$, therefore at $\pm 2$ the graph has a **vertical tangent** and $f$ **is not differentiable** at the two boundary points.

**Monotonicity, maxima and minima.** The sign of $f'$ is the sign of $x$: the function is decreasing on $(-\infty, -2]$ and increasing on $[2, +\infty)$. There are no stationary points. The points $\pm 2$ are **relative minimum** points, because $f(\pm 2) = 0$ and $f \geq 0$; since $0$ is the smallest possible value for a root, it is also the **absolute minimum**, attained at both points. The absolute maximum does not exist, because $f \to +\infty$.

**Concavity and inflection points.** Differentiating $f'(x) = x\,(x^2 - 4)^{-1/2}$,
$$
f''(x) = (x^2 - 4)^{-1/2} - x^2\,(x^2 - 4)^{-3/2}
= \frac{(x^2 - 4) - x^2}{(x^2 - 4)^{3/2}}
= \frac{-4}{(x^2 - 4)^{3/2}} < 0 ,
$$
therefore $f$ is **concave** on each of the two intervals of the domain and **has no inflection points**.

**Graph.**

<div class="spiegazione">

The limit that gives $q$ is computed by **rationalization**: multiplying and dividing $\sqrt{x^2 - 4} - x$ by $\sqrt{x^2 - 4} + x$ one obtains in the numerator the difference of squares $-4$, while the denominator tends to $+\infty$, therefore $q = 0$. Without rationalizing, the difference is a form $\infty - \infty$ and *nothing* can be concluded.

The points $\pm 2$ show a minimum that **Fermat's theorem** does not see: they are **endpoints of the domain**, therefore the theorem does *not* apply, and indeed the derivative does not vanish there, in fact it does not exist, because the difference quotient tends to infinity. The minimum is recognized by **direct comparison**, since $f(\pm 2) = 0$ and the root does not take negative values. The vertical tangent at those points says that the graph starts from the point $(\pm 2, 0)$ with infinite slope, a detail to be shown in the qualitative graph.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Study the function
$$
f(x) = x \sqrt{1 - x^2}
$$
and sketch its qualitative graph.

**Domain.** The radicand must be nonnegative, therefore $1 - x^2 \geq 0$, that is $D = [-1, 1]$.

**Symmetries and periodicity.** $f(-x) = -x\sqrt{1 - x^2} = -f(x)$, therefore the function is **odd** and the graph is symmetric with respect to the origin; it is not periodic.

**Sign and intersections.** The root is nonnegative, therefore the sign of $f$ is the sign of $x$ at the interior points: $f(x) > 0$ for $0 < x < 1$, $f(x) < 0$ for $-1 < x < 0$. The zeros are $x = 0$ and $x = \pm 1$.

**Limits and asymptotes.** The domain is a closed and bounded interval and the function is defined also at the endpoints, with $f(\pm 1) = 0$; there are no limits at infinity to compute and no **asymptotes** of any kind.

**Continuity.** $f$ is a product of continuous functions, therefore it is continuous on $[-1, 1]$.

**Differentiability.** At the interior points
$$
f'(x) = \sqrt{1 - x^2} + x \cdot \frac{-x}{\sqrt{1 - x^2}} = \frac{1 - 2x^2}{\sqrt{1 - x^2}} .
$$
As $x \to \pm 1$ (from the inside) the numerator tends to $-1$ and the denominator to $0^+$, therefore $f'(x) \to -\infty$: at $\pm 1$ the graph has a **vertical tangent** and $f$ **is not differentiable**.

**Monotonicity, maxima and minima.** We have $f'(x) > 0$ for $x^2 < \dfrac{1}{2}$, that is for $|x| < \dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$. The function is decreasing on $\left[-1, -\dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\right]$, increasing on $\left[-\dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}, \dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\right]$, decreasing on $\left[\dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}, 1\right]$. By the **first derivative test** $-\dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$ is a relative minimum point and $\dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$ a relative maximum point, with
$$
f\!\left(\pm\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\right) = \pm\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} \cdot \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} = \pm\frac{1}{2} .
$$
The function is continuous on a closed and bounded interval, therefore by **Weierstrass's theorem** the absolute maximum and minimum exist; the comparison of the values at the candidates, $f(\pm 1) = 0$ and $f\!\left(\pm\dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\right) = \pm\dfrac{1}{2}$, gives **absolute maximum** $\dfrac{1}{2}$ and **absolute minimum** $-\dfrac{1}{2}$.

**Concavity and inflection points.** Differentiating $f'(x) = (1 - 2x^2)(1 - x^2)^{-1/2}$,
$$
f''(x) = -4x\,(1 - x^2)^{-1/2} + (1 - 2x^2)\,x\,(1 - x^2)^{-3/2}
= \frac{x\,(2x^2 - 3)}{(1 - x^2)^{3/2}} .
$$
On $(-1, 1)$ the factor $2x^2 - 3$ is negative and the denominator is positive, therefore the sign of $f''$ is opposite to the sign of $x$: the function is **convex** on $[-1, 0]$ and **concave** on $[0, 1]$. At $0$ the second derivative changes sign, therefore the origin is an **inflection point**, with tangent $y = x$ because $f'(0) = 1$.

**Graph.**

::grafico{f="x*sqrt(1-x^2)" xmin=-1.3 xmax=1.3 ymin=-0.8 ymax=0.8 titolo="$y = x\sqrt{1 - x^2}$: absolute extrema $\pm\dfrac{1}{2}$ at $x = \pm\dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$, inflection point at the origin"}

<div class="spiegazione">

The computation of $f''$ is best set up on the expression $f'(x) = (1 - 2x^2)(1 - x^2)^{-1/2}$ with the **product rule**; in the final factoring one collects $x\,(1 - x^2)^{-3/2}$ and the factor $-4(1 - x^2) + (1 - 2x^2) = 2x^2 - 3$ remains. A frequent error in this factoring changes the sign of the concavity, therefore it is worth checking the result at a point, for example $f''$ must be *negative* near the maximum $\dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$.

Here **Weierstrass's theorem** genuinely applies, unlike what happens on unbounded domains: the function is continuous on $[-1, 1]$, closed and bounded, therefore the absolute maximum and minimum certainly **exist** and *only* need to be identified by comparing the values at the stationary points and at the endpoints of the domain. Oddness guarantees that the two extreme values are opposite.

</div>
