## Exercise 1

Study the function
$$
f(x) = \sin x + \cos x
$$
on the period $[0, 2\pi]$ and sketch its qualitative graph.

**Domain and periodicity.** The function is defined on $\mathbb{R}$ and is **periodic** with period $2\pi$, because sine and cosine are; the study is restricted to $[0, 2\pi]$. On the chosen period questions of evenness do not arise.

**Sign and intersections.** With the **addition formula** for the sine,
$$
f(x) = \sqrt{2}\,\sin\!\left(x + \frac{\pi}{4}\right) .
$$
Therefore $f(x) = 0$ when $x + \dfrac{\pi}{4} = k\pi$, that is at $x = \dfrac{3\pi}{4}$ and $x = \dfrac{7\pi}{4}$ within $[0, 2\pi]$. The sign follows from the sine: $f(x) > 0$ on $\left[0, \dfrac{3\pi}{4}\right)$ and on $\left(\dfrac{7\pi}{4}, 2\pi\right]$, $f(x) < 0$ on $\left(\dfrac{3\pi}{4}, \dfrac{7\pi}{4}\right)$. The intersection with the $y$-axis is $(0, 1)$.

**Limits and asymptotes.** The function is defined and bounded on all of $\mathbb{R}$, therefore there are no limits at the endpoints to compute and no **asymptotes**.

**Continuity.** Sum of continuous functions, therefore continuous.

**Differentiability and monotonicity.** $f$ is differentiable everywhere and
$$
f'(x) = \cos x - \sin x = \sqrt{2}\,\cos\!\left(x + \frac{\pi}{4}\right) .
$$
On $[0, 2\pi]$ the derivative vanishes at $x = \dfrac{\pi}{4}$ and $x = \dfrac{5\pi}{4}$ and is positive when the cosine of $x + \dfrac{\pi}{4}$ is positive. By the **monotonicity test** $f$ is increasing on $\left[0, \dfrac{\pi}{4}\right]$, decreasing on $\left[\dfrac{\pi}{4}, \dfrac{5\pi}{4}\right]$, increasing on $\left[\dfrac{5\pi}{4}, 2\pi\right]$.

**Maxima and minima.** By the first derivative test $\dfrac{\pi}{4}$ is a relative maximum point with $f\!\left(\dfrac{\pi}{4}\right) = \sqrt{2}$, and $\dfrac{5\pi}{4}$ is a relative minimum point with $f\!\left(\dfrac{5\pi}{4}\right) = -\sqrt{2}$. The function is continuous on the closed and bounded interval $[0, 2\pi]$, therefore by **Weierstrass's theorem** it has an absolute maximum and minimum; the comparison with the values at the endpoints, $f(0) = f(2\pi) = 1$, confirms that $\sqrt{2}$ is the **absolute maximum** and $-\sqrt{2}$ the **absolute minimum**, and by periodicity they are so on all of $\mathbb{R}$.

**Concavity and inflection points.** The second derivative is
$$
f''(x) = -\sin x - \cos x = -f(x) ,
$$
therefore $f''$ is positive where $f$ is negative. The function is concave on $\left[0, \dfrac{3\pi}{4}\right]$, convex on $\left[\dfrac{3\pi}{4}, \dfrac{7\pi}{4}\right]$, concave on $\left[\dfrac{7\pi}{4}, 2\pi\right]$; the points $\dfrac{3\pi}{4}$ and $\dfrac{7\pi}{4}$ are **inflection points**, and they coincide with the **zeros** of the function.

**Graph.**

<div class="spiegazione">

The rewriting $f(x) = \sqrt{2}\,\sin\!\left(x + \dfrac{\pi}{4}\right)$ comes from factoring out $\sqrt{2}$ and from the **addition formula**, since $\sin x \cdot \dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2} + \cos x \cdot \dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2} = \sin\!\left(x + \dfrac{\pi}{4}\right)$. From this form zeros, sign, maxima and minima are read directly on the shifted sinusoid, and the computations with the derivative serve as a *check*.

The identity $f'' = -f$ shortens the last step: the inflection points fall exactly **at the zeros** of the function and the concavity is **opposite to the sign**. The property holds for *every* combination of sine and cosine with the same argument and is the reason why the sinusoid always crosses the $x$-axis with an inflection point.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Study the function
$$
f(x) = \cos^2 x
$$
on the period $[0, \pi]$ and sketch its qualitative graph.

**Domain and periodicity.** The function is defined on $\mathbb{R}$. With the **double-angle formula**,
$$
\cos^2 x = \frac{1 + \cos 2x}{2} ,
$$
therefore the **period is** $\pi$ and the study is restricted to $[0, \pi]$. Moreover $f(\pi - x) = \cos^2(\pi - x) = \cos^2 x = f(x)$, therefore on the period the graph is **symmetric** with respect to the line $x = \dfrac{\pi}{2}$.

**Sign and intersections.** A square is nonnegative, therefore $f(x) \geq 0$, with $f(x) = 0$ *only* where $\cos x = 0$, that is at $x = \dfrac{\pi}{2}$. The intersection with the $y$-axis is $(0, 1)$.

**Limits and asymptotes.** The function is defined and bounded on $\mathbb{R}$: no limit to compute, **no asymptote**.

**Continuity.** Product of continuous functions, therefore continuous.

**Differentiability and monotonicity.** $f$ is differentiable everywhere and
$$
f'(x) = -2\cos x \sin x = -\sin 2x .
$$
On $(0, \pi)$ we have $f'(x) > 0$ when $\sin 2x < 0$, that is for $\dfrac{\pi}{2} < x < \pi$. By the **monotonicity test** $f$ is decreasing on $\left[0, \dfrac{\pi}{2}\right]$ and increasing on $\left[\dfrac{\pi}{2}, \pi\right]$; the stationary points in $[0, \pi]$ are $0$, $\dfrac{\pi}{2}$ and $\pi$.

**Maxima and minima.** By the first derivative test $\dfrac{\pi}{2}$ is a relative minimum point with $f\!\left(\dfrac{\pi}{2}\right) = 0$; since $f \geq 0$, the value $0$ is also the **absolute minimum**. At the endpoints $f(0) = f(\pi) = 1$, and since $\cos^2 x \leq 1$ everywhere, $1$ is the **absolute maximum**, attained at $0$ and $\pi$; by periodicity, at all points $k\pi$ with $k \in \mathbb{Z}$.

**Concavity and inflection points.** The second derivative is
$$
f''(x) = -2\cos 2x ,
$$
positive when $\cos 2x < 0$, that is for $\dfrac{\pi}{4} < x < \dfrac{3\pi}{4}$. The function is concave on $\left[0, \dfrac{\pi}{4}\right]$, convex on $\left[\dfrac{\pi}{4}, \dfrac{3\pi}{4}\right]$, concave on $\left[\dfrac{3\pi}{4}, \pi\right]$; the points $\dfrac{\pi}{4}$ and $\dfrac{3\pi}{4}$ are **inflection points**, both with $y$-coordinate $\dfrac{1}{2}$.

**Graph.**

<div class="spiegazione">

The **double-angle formula** reveals the true period. At first sight one would say $2\pi$, inherited from the cosine, but the expression $\dfrac{1 + \cos 2x}{2}$ shows that the function is a cosine of argument $2x$ shifted and rescaled, therefore the **period** is $\pi$. Studying the function on $[0, 2\pi]$ would not be an error, but it would double the work by repeating the same drawing twice.

The absolute maxima fall at the endpoints $0$ and $\pi$ of the interval of study, which are **stationary points**, since $f'(0) = f'(\pi) = 0$. On the full domain $\mathbb{R}$ they are relative and absolute maxima in every respect, with horizontal tangent: the restriction to one period makes them *appear* as boundary points, but the function continues beyond by periodicity.

</div>
