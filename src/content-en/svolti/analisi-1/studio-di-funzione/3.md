## Exercise 1

Study the function
$$
f(x) = x\,e^{-x}
$$
and sketch its qualitative graph.

**Domain.** The exponential is defined everywhere, therefore $D = \mathbb{R}$.

**Symmetries and periodicity.** $f(-x) = -x\,e^{x}$ is different from both $f(x)$ and $-f(x)$, therefore the function is **neither even nor odd**; it is not periodic.

**Sign and intersections.** The factor $e^{-x}$ is always positive, therefore the sign of $f$ is the sign of $x$: $f(x) > 0$ for $x > 0$, $f(x) < 0$ for $x < 0$, $f(0) = 0$. The graph meets the axes *only* at the origin.

**Limits and asymptotes.** As $x \to +\infty$ the ratio $\dfrac{x}{e^{x}}$ tends to $0$ by the **hierarchy of infinities**, therefore
$$
\lim_{x \to +\infty} x\,e^{-x} = 0^+, \qquad
\lim_{x \to -\infty} x\,e^{-x} = -\infty .
$$
The line $y = 0$ is a **horizontal asymptote** as $x \to +\infty$. As $x \to -\infty$ we have $\dfrac{f(x)}{x} = e^{-x} \to +\infty$, therefore **there is no oblique asymptote**; there are no vertical asymptotes.

**Continuity.** $f$ is a product of continuous functions, therefore it is continuous on $\mathbb{R}$.

**Differentiability and monotonicity.** $f$ is differentiable on $\mathbb{R}$ and
$$
f'(x) = e^{-x} - x\,e^{-x} = (1 - x)\,e^{-x} .
$$
Since $e^{-x} > 0$, we have $f'(x) > 0$ for $x < 1$. By the **monotonicity test** $f$ is increasing on $(-\infty, 1]$ and decreasing on $[1, +\infty)$.

**Maxima and minima.** The only stationary point is $1$, where the derivative changes from positive to negative, therefore by the **first derivative test** $1$ is a relative maximum point with $f(1) = \dfrac{1}{e}$. Since $f$ increases before $1$ and decreases after, the value $\dfrac{1}{e}$ is also the **absolute maximum**. The absolute minimum **does not exist**, because $f \to -\infty$ as $x \to -\infty$.

**Concavity and inflection points.** Differentiating again,
$$
f''(x) = -e^{-x} - (1 - x)\,e^{-x} = (x - 2)\,e^{-x} ,
$$
therefore $f''(x) > 0$ for $x > 2$. The function is **concave** on $(-\infty, 2]$ and **convex** on $[2, +\infty)$; since $f''$ changes sign at $2$, the point $2$ is an **inflection point**, with $f(2) = \dfrac{2}{e^2}$.

**Graph.**

<div class="spiegazione">

The limit as $x \to +\infty$ is a form of the type $\infty \cdot 0$, which is resolved by rewriting the product as the ratio $\dfrac{x}{e^{x}}$. By the **hierarchy of infinities** the exponential grows faster than *every* power, therefore the ratio tends to $0$; the function stays positive for $x > 0$, hence the graph approaches the asymptote $y = 0$ **from above**.

The relative maximum at $1$ is also **absolute** because of the pattern of monotonicity, since the function increases on all of $(-\infty, 1]$ and decreases on all of $[1, +\infty)$: every other value of $f$ is less than $f(1)$. The inflection point at $2$ is consistent with the horizontal asymptote, since a concave function *cannot* flatten onto a horizontal line while staying above it, and the change of concavity occurs just after the maximum.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Study the function
$$
f(x) = e^{-x^2}
$$
and sketch its qualitative graph.

**Domain.** $D = \mathbb{R}$.

**Symmetries and periodicity.** $f(-x) = e^{-(-x)^2} = e^{-x^2} = f(x)$, therefore the function is **even**; it is not periodic.

**Sign and intersections.** An exponential is always positive, therefore $f(x) > 0$ for every $x$ and the graph **does not meet the $x$-axis**; it meets the $y$-axis at the point $(0, 1)$.

**Limits and asymptotes.** As $x \to \pm\infty$ the exponent $-x^2$ tends to $-\infty$, therefore
$$
\lim_{x \to \pm\infty} e^{-x^2} = 0^+
$$
and the line $y = 0$ is a **horizontal asymptote** on both sides; there are no vertical asymptotes.

**Continuity.** $f$ is a composition of continuous functions, therefore it is continuous on $\mathbb{R}$.

**Differentiability and monotonicity.** $f$ is differentiable on $\mathbb{R}$ and
$$
f'(x) = -2x\,e^{-x^2} .
$$
Since $e^{-x^2} > 0$, we have $f'(x) > 0$ for $x < 0$. By the **monotonicity test** $f$ is increasing on $(-\infty, 0]$ and decreasing on $[0, +\infty)$.

**Maxima and minima.** The only stationary point is $0$, where the derivative changes from positive to negative, therefore $0$ is a **relative maximum point** and, because of the pattern of monotonicity, also an **absolute** one, with $f(0) = 1$. The absolute minimum does not exist: $f$ is positive and tends to $0$, therefore $0$ is the infimum but it is *not* an attained value.

**Concavity and inflection points.** Differentiating the product,
$$
f''(x) = -2\,e^{-x^2} + 4x^2 e^{-x^2} = (4x^2 - 2)\,e^{-x^2} ,
$$
therefore $f''(x) > 0$ for $x^2 > \dfrac{1}{2}$, that is for $|x| > \dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$. The function is convex on $\left(-\infty, -\dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\right]$, concave on $\left[-\dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}, \dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\right]$, convex on $\left[\dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}, +\infty\right)$. At the points $\pm\dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$ the second derivative changes sign, therefore both are **inflection points**, with $f\!\left(\pm\dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\right) = e^{-\frac{1}{2}} = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{e}}$.

**Graph.**

::grafico{f="exp(-x^2)" xmin=-3 xmax=3 ymin=-0.3 ymax=1.3 asintoti="y=0" titolo="$y = e^{-x^2}$: maximum at $(0, 1)$, inflection points at $x = \pm\dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$, horizontal asymptote $y = 0$"}

<div class="spiegazione">

The **evenness**, verified at the beginning, organizes the whole study: it suffices to derive monotonicity, concavity and notable points for $x \geq 0$ and reflect the result across the $y$-axis. In particular the two inflection points are symmetric and have the same ordinate.

The distinction between **infimum** and **minimum** is the step to justify carefully. The function is always positive and tends to $0$ at infinity, therefore no positive value is a better lower bound than $0$; on the other hand $f(x) = 0$ has no solutions, hence the infimum $0$ is *not* attained and the absolute minimum **does not exist**. Weierstrass's theorem does not contradict this fact, because the domain $\mathbb{R}$ is not a bounded interval and the hypothesis of the theorem is not satisfied.

</div>
