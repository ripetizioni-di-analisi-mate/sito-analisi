## Exercise 1

Solve in $\mathbb{C}$ the equation
$$
z^2 + 4z + 13 = 0 .
$$

$$
\Delta = 16 - 52 = -36 < 0
$$
$$
z = \frac{-4 \pm i\sqrt{36}}{2} = \frac{-4 \pm 6i}{2} = -2 \pm 3i
$$

::grafico{xmin=-5.5 xmax=2.5 ymin=-4 ymax=4 uguale=1 nomi="Re,Im" punti="-2,3,pieno,−2 + 3i;-2,-3,pieno,−2 − 3i" segmenti="-2,3,-2,-3" titolo="The solutions $-2 + 3i$ and $-2 - 3i$ are conjugate: the two points are symmetric with respect to the real axis"}

<div class="spiegazione">

The discriminant equals $\Delta = 4^2 - 4 \cdot 13 = -36$, which is negative, so the equation **has no real solutions** and the **quadratic formula** is applied in its complex version, with $i\sqrt{-\Delta}$ in place of $\sqrt{\Delta}$. Since $\sqrt{-\Delta} = \sqrt{36} = 6$, the solutions are $z = \dfrac{-4 \pm 6i}{2} = -2 \pm 3i$, **complex conjugates** as happens for *every* polynomial with real coefficients.

As a check, for $z = -2 + 3i$ we have $z^2 = 4 - 12i + 9i^2 = -5 - 12i$, so $z^2 + 4z + 13 = -5 - 12i - 8 + 12i + 13 = 0$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Solve in $\mathbb{C}$ the equation
$$
2z^2 + 2z + 5 = 0 .
$$

$$
\Delta = 4 - 40 = -36 < 0
$$
$$
z = \frac{-2 \pm i\sqrt{36}}{4} = \frac{-2 \pm 6i}{4} = -\frac{1}{2} \pm \frac{3}{2}\, i
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The discriminant equals $\Delta = 2^2 - 4 \cdot 2 \cdot 5 = -36$, which is negative. In the **quadratic formula** the denominator is $2a = 4$, because the leading coefficient is $2$ and *not* $1$; with $\sqrt{-\Delta} = 6$ the solutions are $\dfrac{-2 \pm 6i}{4}$, which simplify to $-\dfrac{1}{2} \pm \dfrac{3}{2}\, i$.

As a check, for $z = -\dfrac{1}{2} + \dfrac{3}{2} i$ we have $z^2 = \dfrac{1}{4} - \dfrac{3}{2} i + \dfrac{9}{4} i^2 = -2 - \dfrac{3}{2} i$, so $2z^2 + 2z + 5 = -4 - 3i - 1 + 3i + 5 = 0$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Solve in $\mathbb{C}$ the equation
$$
z^4 + 3z^2 - 4 = 0 .
$$

$$
t = z^2 : \qquad t^2 + 3t - 4 = 0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
t = \frac{-3 \pm \sqrt{9 + 16}}{2} = \frac{-3 \pm 5}{2}
$$
$$
t_1 = 1, \qquad t_2 = -4
$$
$$
z^2 = 1 \ \Longrightarrow\ z = \pm 1, \qquad
z^2 = -4 \ \Longrightarrow\ z = \pm 2i
$$

The solutions are $z = 1$, $z = -1$, $z = 2i$, $z = -2i$.

<div class="spiegazione">

The equation is **biquadratic**, so with the substitution $t = z^2$ it reduces to a quadratic equation in $t$, which has discriminant $9 + 16 = 25$ and real solutions $t_1 = 1$ and $t_2 = -4$.

We then return to the unknown $z$ by solving the two pure equations. From $z^2 = 1$ the real solutions $\pm 1$ follow. The equation $z^2 = -4$ has no real solutions, because a real square is *never* negative; in $\mathbb{C}$ the numbers whose square is $-4$ are $\pm 2i$, since $(\pm 2i)^2 = 4 i^2 = -4$. In total there are **four** solutions, in agreement with the **fundamental theorem of algebra** applied to a polynomial of degree four.

</div>
