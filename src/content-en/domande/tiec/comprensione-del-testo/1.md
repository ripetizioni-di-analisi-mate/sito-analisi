> A printer found an envelope in the case of an old press. It contained three proofs of the same poster. On the first the title filled the entire margin, on the second just the last letter stuck out, and on the third the words fitted comfortably. None of the proofs was dated. In her report the printer called them A, B and C, in the order in which she had found them in the envelope. A colleague proposed displaying them as three stages of a correction. She agreed to exhibit them but asked the label to say three versions. The order of a stack someone might have rearranged was insufficient to establish the history of the work.

Why does the printer prefer the word “versions”?

- It means that A, B and C were already printed on the proofs
- It describes a visible difference without assuming an uncertain sequence
- It establishes which proof was approved
- It shows that the posters came from three print shops

## Explanation

The three layouts visibly differ. There are no dates, and the letters were assigned only after discovery. Calling them stages would introduce a sequence the envelope cannot document.
