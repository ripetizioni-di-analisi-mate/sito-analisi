Read the following passage.

> Each spring, high school seniors visit universities during open houses. They attend presentations about degree programs, receive brochures, and meet a few faculty members. These events are useful, but they are not enough on their own to help students make a choice. An hour-long presentation shows a program as the people who run it describe it. It reveals little about the demands of first-year exams or the opportunities graduates actually find.
>
> It would be more useful to supplement the presentations by letting visitors attend regular classes for a few days alongside enrolled students and talk with recent graduates. Some object that classrooms are already crowded and visitors would disrupt classes. Yet setting aside a few seats in selected classes and taking reservations would be enough to give prospective students a concrete sense of the program.

Which position does the author of the passage support?

- University open houses are useless and should be abolished
- University open houses should be supplemented by opportunities to attend regular classes and talk with recent graduates
- University classrooms are too crowded to accommodate high school students
- Students should choose a program solely on the basis of job prospects

## Explanation

The **thesis** appears in the conditional statement “It would be more useful to supplement the presentations by letting visitors attend regular classes for a few days,” which follows the discussion of the presentations’ limitations. The author says that open houses are useful and are insufficient on their own, so the author is not calling for their abolition. Crowded classrooms are someone else’s objection. The author responds by proposing a reservation system for seats. The passage mentions career opportunities as one piece of information that presentations leave out, without making them the sole criterion for choosing a program.
