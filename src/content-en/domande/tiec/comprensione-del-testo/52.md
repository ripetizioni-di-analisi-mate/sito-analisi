> Labels at a small exhibition give the true dimensions of objects beside their photographs. Visitors see a photograph of a buckle followed by a door, both printed to fill a whole page. “They look the same height,” a boy remarks. The guide asks him to cover the measurements with a finger and look again a minute later. He recognises the door from its handle but cannot tell how large the buckle is. It might belong to a shoe or to a broad belt. The guide explains that dimensions are especially useful when everyday experience offers more than one plausible scale.

Which example would be closest to that of the buckle?

- A ruler with readable graduations and units
- An isolated photograph of a screw that might belong to a watch or furniture
- A door beside an adult of known height
- A box with three dimensions clearly printed on its label

## Explanation

Without a reference, a screw could belong to objects at very different scales. This is the uncertainty illustrated by the buckle. In the other cases the reader already has a unit or a visible comparison.
