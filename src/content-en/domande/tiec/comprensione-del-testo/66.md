Read the following passage.

> Each spring, high school seniors visit universities during open houses. They attend presentations about degree programs, receive brochures, and meet a few faculty members. These events are useful, but they are not enough on their own to help students make a choice. An hour-long presentation shows a program as the people who run it describe it. It reveals little about the demands of first-year exams or the opportunities graduates actually find.
>
> It would be more useful to supplement the presentations by letting visitors attend regular classes for a few days alongside enrolled students and talk with recent graduates. Some object that classrooms are already crowded and visitors would disrupt classes. Yet setting aside a few seats in selected classes and taking reservations would be enough to give prospective students a concrete sense of the program.

In the passage, the word “opportunities” (Italian “sbocchi”) refers to

- the places where a river flows into the sea
- the emergency exits from classrooms
- the expenses incurred during a degree program
- the employment prospects open to graduates

## Explanation

The sentence says that a presentation “reveals little about the demands of first-year exams or the opportunities graduates actually find.” In this **context**, opportunities are the career paths open to people who complete the program. The geographic sense, where a river flows into the sea, is a dictionary meaning of the original Italian word “sbocco,” but does not fit the sentence. Neither classroom exits nor the costs of studying are intended meanings of the word.
