Read the following passage.

> Every summer, many small towns hold literary festivals, inviting writers to speak, organizing events in the town square, and setting up bookstalls. A festival’s success is often judged by attendance, and towns proudly announce that they drew more than ten thousand visitors in a weekend. This is a convenient measure, but what it mostly measures is the ability to attract passing visitors.
>
> A festival leaves its mark when its effects continue beyond the event itself. The town library may see more books checked out in the following months, schools may prepare for author visits by reading the authors’ books, and book clubs may form. For this reason, towns would do well to count these slower effects alongside attendance figures. They reveal whether the festival changed the habits of the people who live there.

In the sentence “This is a convenient measure, but what it mostly measures is the ability to attract passing visitors,” the author

- recognizes one strength of counting attendance and identifies a limitation
- denies that counting attendance has any value
- proposes counting attendance as the sole measure of success
- explains why festivals attract passing visitors

## Explanation

The first part of the sentence acknowledges a strength of counting attendance, namely its convenience. The part introduced by *but* identifies what the count actually measures, the ability to attract passing visitors, and thus shows its limitation. The author therefore recognizes a strength and identifies a limitation. Since the author later proposes counting lasting effects *alongside* attendance, the author neither denies the value of counting attendance nor proposes it as the sole criterion. The sentence does not say what causes people to attend.
