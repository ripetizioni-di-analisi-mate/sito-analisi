The function $f$ is twice differentiable on $\mathbb{R}$ and vanishes at the points $x = 0$, $x = 1$ and $x = 2$. Which statement is certainly true?

- $f'$ vanishes at exactly two points of $(0, 2)$
- $f'$ vanishes at at least three points of $(0, 2)$
- $f'(1) = 0$
- $f'$ vanishes at at least two points of $(0, 2)$ and $f''$ at at least one point of $(0, 2)$

## Explanation

The function $f$ is continuous and differentiable on $[0, 1]$ and $f(0) = f(1) = 0$, so by **Rolle's theorem** there exists $c_1 \in (0, 1)$ with $f'(c_1) = 0$. In the same way, since $f(1) = f(2) = 0$, there exists $c_2 \in (1, 2)$ with $f'(c_2) = 0$. The function $f'$ is differentiable and $f'(c_1) = f'(c_2)$, therefore Rolle's theorem applied to $f'$ on $[c_1, c_2]$ gives a point of $(c_1, c_2)$ at which $f''$ vanishes. The other statements are not guaranteed. The polynomial $x(x - 1)(x - 2)$ has derivative $3x^2 - 6x + 2$, which vanishes at only two points and equals $-1$ at $x = 1$. The function $\sin(2\pi x)$ has zero derivative at four points of $(0, 2)$.
