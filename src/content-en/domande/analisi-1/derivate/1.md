- $f'(x) = 3x^2$
- $f'(x) = 3x$
- $f'(x) = x^2$
- $f'(x) = \dfrac{x^4}{4}$

## Explanation

The difference quotient of $f$ at the point $x$ is $\dfrac{(x + h)^3 - x^3}{h}$. Since $(x + h)^3 = x^3 + 3x^2 h + 3x h^2 + h^3$, for $h \neq 0$ we have
$$
\frac{(x + h)^3 - x^3}{h} = \frac{3x^2 h + 3x h^2 + h^3}{h} = 3x^2 + 3xh + h^2 .
$$
As $h \to 0$ the terms $3xh$ and $h^2$ tend to $0$, so the limit exists, is finite and equals $3x^2$. Therefore $f$ is **differentiable** on $\mathbb{R}$ and $f'(x) = 3x^2$.
