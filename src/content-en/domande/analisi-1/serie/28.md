The series $\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{n}$ and $\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \sin \frac{1}{n}$ both diverge. Which statement about the series of the differences $\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \left( \frac{1}{n} - \sin \frac{1}{n} \right)$ is true?

- It converges, because the general term is asymptotic to $\dfrac{1}{6n^3}$
- It diverges to $+\infty$, because the general term is asymptotic to $\dfrac{1}{n}$
- It is oscillatory, because the general term changes sign
- It diverges to $+\infty$, because $\displaystyle\sum \frac{1}{n}$ and $\displaystyle\sum \sin \frac{1}{n}$ both diverge

## Explanation

As $t \to 0$ the Taylor expansion $\sin t = t - \dfrac{t^3}{6} + o(t^3)$ holds. Since $\dfrac{1}{n} \to 0$, with $t = \dfrac{1}{n}$ one obtains
$$
\frac{1}{n} - \sin \frac{1}{n} = \frac{1}{6n^3} + o\!\left( \frac{1}{n^3} \right) ,
$$
so the general term is eventually positive and asymptotic to $\dfrac{1}{6n^3}$.

The series $\displaystyle\sum \frac{1}{n^3}$ converges, hence by the **limit comparison test** the given series **converges**.

The fact that $\sum \dfrac{1}{n}$ and $\sum \sin \dfrac{1}{n}$ both diverge allows no conclusion, because in general nothing can be said about the behavior of the difference of two non-convergent series.
