Let $g(x) = x \sin \dfrac{1}{x}$ for $x \neq 0$ and $f(y) = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } y \neq 0 \\ 0 & \text{if } y = 0 \end{cases}$. We have $g(x) \to 0$ as $x \to 0$ and $f(y) \to 1$ as $y \to 0$. Which statement about $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 0} f(g(x))$ is true?

- The limit does not exist
- The limit equals $1$, by the theorem on the limit of a composite function
- The limit equals $0$, because $f(0) = 0$
- The limit equals $1$, because $f$ is continuous at $0$

## Explanation

The theorem on the limit of a composite function requires $g(x) \neq 0$ in a neighborhood of $0$, or $f$ continuous at $0$. Neither hypothesis is satisfied, because $g\left(\dfrac{1}{k\pi}\right) = \dfrac{1}{k\pi} \sin(k\pi) = 0$ for every integer $k \neq 0$, and $f$ is not continuous at $0$ because $f(0) = 0 \neq 1$.

At the points $x_k = \dfrac{1}{k\pi}$, which tend to $0$, we have $f(g(x_k)) = f(0) = 0$. At the points $t_k = \dfrac{2}{(4k + 1)\pi}$, which tend to $0$, we have $g(t_k) = t_k \neq 0$ and therefore $f(g(t_k)) = 1$. In every neighborhood of $0$ the composite function therefore takes both the value $0$ and the value $1$, so the limit **does not exist**.
