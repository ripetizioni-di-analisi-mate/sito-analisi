What is $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\ln(\cos x)}{x^2}$?

- $\dfrac{1}{2}$
- $0$
- $-\dfrac{1}{2}$
- $-1$

## Explanation

As $x \to 0$ we have $\cos x \to 1$, so the numerator tends to $0$ and the limit is in the indeterminate form $\dfrac{0}{0}$. We write $\ln(\cos x) = \ln\bigl(1 + (\cos x - 1)\bigr)$, and since $\cos x - 1 \to 0$, the fundamental limit of the logarithm gives $\ln(\cos x) \sim \cos x - 1$. From the **fundamental sine limit** we have $\cos x - 1 \sim -\dfrac{x^2}{2}$. Since in a quotient a function can be replaced by an asymptotically equivalent one, the limit equals
$$
\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{-\dfrac{x^2}{2}}{x^2} = -\frac{1}{2} .
$$
