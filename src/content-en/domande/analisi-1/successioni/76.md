The sequence equals $a_n = 1$ when $n$ is a perfect square and $a_n = \dfrac{1}{n}$ otherwise. Which statement about its limit is true?

- It does not exist, because the subsequences $\left( a_{k^2} \right)$ and $\left( a_{k^2 + 1} \right)$ have different limits
- It equals $0$, because perfect squares become rarer and rarer
- It equals $0$, because finitely many terms do not change the limit
- It equals $1$

## Explanation

The indices $k^2$ are strictly increasing, and $a_{k^2} = 1$ for every $k$, so the subsequence $\left( a_{k^2} \right)$ tends to $1$. The indices $k^2 + 1$ are strictly increasing too and, for $k \geq 1$, the number $k^2 + 1$ is not a perfect square, because $k^2 < k^2 + 1 < (k + 1)^2$. Hence $a_{k^2 + 1} = \dfrac{1}{k^2 + 1} \to 0$.

The two subsequences have different limits, so by the theorem on the **limit of subsequences** the limit of $(a_n)$ does not exist. There are infinitely many perfect squares, so the terms equal to $1$ are not finitely many.
