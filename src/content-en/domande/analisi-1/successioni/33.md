The theorem on the product of a bounded sequence and an infinitesimal one applies to only one of these products, and gives limit $0$. Which one?

- $n \cdot \sin \dfrac{1}{n}$
- $\sin n \cdot \dfrac{1}{\ln(n + 1)}$
- $(-1)^n \cdot \dfrac{n}{n + 1}$
- $\cos n \cdot \left( 1 + \dfrac{1}{n} \right)$

## Explanation

The theorem requires a bounded factor and an infinitesimal factor. In $\sin n \cdot \dfrac{1}{\ln(n + 1)}$ we have $|\sin n| \leq 1$ and, since $\ln(n + 1) \to +\infty$, the second factor tends to $0$. Hence the theorem applies and the product **tends to** $0$.

In the product $n \cdot \sin \dfrac{1}{n}$ the factor $n$ is not bounded, and the limit equals $1$ by the standard limit of the sine. In $(-1)^n \cdot \dfrac{n}{n + 1}$ and in $\cos n \cdot \left( 1 + \dfrac{1}{n} \right)$ neither factor is infinitesimal, so the theorem does not apply.
