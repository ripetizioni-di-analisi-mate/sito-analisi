For which values of $k \in \mathbb{R}$ does the equation $\ln x = kx$ have exactly one solution?

- $k \leq 0$ or $k = \dfrac{1}{e}$
- Only $k = \dfrac{1}{e}$
- $k < 0$ or $k = \dfrac{1}{e}$
- $0 < k < \dfrac{1}{e}$

## Explanation

For $x > 0$ the equation is equivalent to $\dfrac{\ln x}{x} = k$, so the solutions are the abscissas of the points shared by the graph of $g(x) = \dfrac{\ln x}{x}$ and the line $y = k$. The derivative $g'(x) = \dfrac{1 - \ln x}{x^2}$ is positive for $0 < x < e$ and negative for $x > e$, so $g$ has maximum $g(e) = \dfrac{1}{e}$. Moreover, $g(x) \to -\infty$ as $x \to 0^+$ and, by the hierarchy of infinities, $g(x) \to 0^+$ as $x \to +\infty$. For $k \leq 0$ the line meets the graph only once, at a point of $(0, 1]$, because $g(x) > 0$ for every $x > 1$. For $k = \dfrac{1}{e}$ the only solution is $x = e$, while for $0 < k < \dfrac{1}{e}$ there are two solutions. Therefore the equation has **exactly one solution** for $k \leq 0$ or $k = \dfrac{1}{e}$.
