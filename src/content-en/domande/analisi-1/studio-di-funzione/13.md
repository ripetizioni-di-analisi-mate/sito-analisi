For which values of $k \in \mathbb{R}$ does the equation $x\,e^{-x} = k$ have exactly two solutions?

- $0 \leq k < \dfrac{1}{e}$
- $0 < k < \dfrac{1}{e}$
- $k < \dfrac{1}{e}$
- $0 < k \leq \dfrac{1}{e}$

## Explanation

Let $f(x) = x\,e^{-x}$. The derivative $f'(x) = (1 - x)\,e^{-x}$ is positive for $x < 1$ and negative for $x > 1$, so $f$ increases on $(-\infty, 1]$, decreases on $[1, +\infty)$ and has maximum $f(1) = \dfrac{1}{e}$. Moreover, $f(x) \to -\infty$ as $x \to -\infty$ and, by the hierarchy of infinities, $f(x) \to 0^+$ as $x \to +\infty$. The solutions of the equation are the abscissas of the points shared by the graph and the line $y = k$. For $0 < k < \dfrac{1}{e}$ the line meets the graph once on $(0, 1)$ and once on $(1, +\infty)$, so there are **two** solutions. For $k = \dfrac{1}{e}$ the only solution is $x = 1$. For $k \leq 0$ there is only one solution, because $f(x) > 0$ for every $x > 0$ and on $(-\infty, 0]$ the function $f$ increases strictly from $-\infty$ to $0$.
