- $\dfrac{1}{2}$

- $2$

## Explanation

As $x \to 0$ the numerator tends to $\displaystyle\int_0^0 \ln(1 + t)\, dt = 0$ and the denominator tends to $0$, so the limit takes the indeterminate form $\dfrac{0}{0}$. Since $\ln(1 + t)$ is continuous, by the **fundamental theorem of calculus** the derivative of the numerator is $\ln(1 + x)$, while the derivative of the denominator is $2x$, which is different from $0$ for $x \neq 0$.

The limit of the ratio of the derivatives exists by the standard limit $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\ln(1 + x)}{x} = 1$, so **l'Hôpital's rule** applies and
$$
\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\displaystyle\int_0^{x} \ln(1 + t)\, dt}{x^2} = \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\ln(1 + x)}{2x} = \frac{1}{2} .
$$
