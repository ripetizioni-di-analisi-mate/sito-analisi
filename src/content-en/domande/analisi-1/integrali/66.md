The numerator of $\dfrac{x^3}{x^2 + 1}$ has greater degree than the denominator. Which of the following functions is an antiderivative of it?

- $\dfrac{x^2}{2} + \dfrac{1}{2} \ln\left( x^2 + 1 \right)$
- $\dfrac{x^2}{2} - \arctan x$
- $\dfrac{x^2}{2} - \dfrac{1}{2} \ln\left( x^2 + 1 \right)$
- $\dfrac{x^2}{2} - \ln\left( x^2 + 1 \right)$

## Explanation

The degree of the numerator is greater than the degree of the denominator, so one performs **polynomial division**. Since $x^3 = x \left( x^2 + 1 \right) - x$, one has
$$
\frac{x^3}{x^2 + 1} = x - \frac{x}{x^2 + 1} .
$$
The first term has antiderivative $\dfrac{x^2}{2}$. The second is reducible to the standard integral $\displaystyle\int \frac{f'(x)}{f(x)}\, dx = \ln|f(x)| + c$ with $f(x) = x^2 + 1$, so it has antiderivative $\dfrac{1}{2} \ln\left( x^2 + 1 \right)$. Therefore an antiderivative is $\dfrac{x^2}{2} - \dfrac{1}{2} \ln\left( x^2 + 1 \right)$.
