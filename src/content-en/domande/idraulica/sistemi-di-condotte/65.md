A pipe receives $Q_m$ upstream and supplies a uniform withdrawal along its length, leaving a downstream throughflow of $Q_v < Q_m$. What shape does the hydraulic grade line take in the long-pipeline model?

- A straight line because resistance is uniformly distributed
- A curve that becomes less steep downstream
- A curve that becomes steeper downstream
- Two straight segments meeting at the midpoint of the pipe

## Explanation

An element of length $dx$ at distance $x$ from the upstream end has resistance $\dfrac{r}{L}\,dx$ and carries discharge $Q(x) = Q_m - p\,x$, giving a head loss of $dH = \dfrac{r}{L}\,Q(x)^2\,dx$. The slope of the hydraulic grade line at section $x$ is therefore
$$
J(x) = \frac{r}{L}\left(Q_m - p\,x\right)^2 .
$$
Discharge decreases continuously downstream, so the magnitude of the slope also **decreases continuously**. The hydraulic grade line is steepest at the upstream end and becomes progressively flatter downstream.

A straight hydraulic grade line occurs only for $p = 0$, when there is no withdrawal along the pipe. A withdrawal concentrated at one point would instead produce two straight segments.
