In the branched network shown, a reservoir with free surface at $100.00\,\mathrm{m}$ feeds pipe $S\!-\!A$ ($r = 520\,\mathrm{s^2/m^5}$), which carries $100\,\mathrm{L/s}$ to node $A$. From $A$, branches $A\!-\!B$ ($r = 1240\,\mathrm{s^2/m^5}$) and $A\!-\!C$ ($r = 2560\,\mathrm{s^2/m^5}$) each deliver $50\,\mathrm{L/s}$ to terminal nodes $B$ and $C$ at elevations $62.00\,\mathrm{m}$ and $55.00\,\mathrm{m}$. The pressure at each terminal node must remain at least $20.00\,\mathrm{m}$ of water. By how much can the reservoir surface be lowered?

::schema{nodi="S,3,12,-;A,13,12;B,24,17;C,24,6,-" condotte="S-A,0.5,;A-B,0.36,;A-C,0.36," acqua="0,9 3,9 3,15 0,15" solidi="0,8 3,8 3,9 0,9" pelo="0,3,15,100.00 m" frecce="24.8,17,26.6,17,;24.8,6,26.6,6," etichette="8,13.4,r = 520;17.4,16.4,r = 1240;17.4,7.6,r = 2560;28.6,17,50 L/s;28.6,6,50 L/s;24,18.8,z_B = 62.00 m;24,4.2,z_C = 55.00 m;24.74,5.26,C" titolo="Branched network with two terminal nodes"}

- $13.40\,\mathrm{m}$
- $9.70\,\mathrm{m}$
- $3.30\,\mathrm{m}$
- $18.00\,\mathrm{m}$

## Explanation

The head losses in the three branches are
$$
\Delta H_{SA} = 520 \cdot 0.100^2 = 5.20\,\mathrm{m} ,
\quad
\Delta H_{AB} = 1240 \cdot 0.050^2 = 3.10\,\mathrm{m} ,
\quad
\Delta H_{AC} = 2560 \cdot 0.050^2 = 6.40\,\mathrm{m} .
$$
For each terminal junction, the reservoir water-surface elevation must cover the junction elevation, minimum pressure head, and head losses along the path from the reservoir. Junction $B$ requires $62.00 + 20.00 + 3.10 + 5.20 = 90.30\,\mathrm{m}$ and junction $C$ requires $55.00 + 20.00 + 6.40 + 5.20 = 86.60\,\mathrm{m}$.

Both requirements must hold, so the larger governs. Junction $B$ is critical because its elevation is higher despite the smaller loss along its supply path. The reservoir level may fall to $90.30\,\mathrm{m}$, a permissible drop of $100.00 - 90.30 = 9.70\,\mathrm{m}$.

The value $13.40\,\mathrm{m}$ accounts only for junction $C$. The value $3.30\,\mathrm{m}$ adds the losses in all three branches as though they lay on one path, and $18.00\,\mathrm{m}$ neglects all losses.
