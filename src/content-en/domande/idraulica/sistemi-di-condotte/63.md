The pipe shown has length $1600\,\mathrm{m}$ and total resistance $r = 1200\,\mathrm{s^2/m^5}$ and supplies a total discharge of $120\,\mathrm{L/s}$ uniformly along its length. Its ends connect to reservoirs at the same free-surface elevation, with no other inflows or withdrawals. In the long-pipeline model, how far below the reservoir surfaces does the hydraulic grade line fall at the minimum-head section?

- $0.720\,\mathrm{m}$
- $2.16\,\mathrm{m}$
- $5.76\,\mathrm{m}$
- $1.44\,\mathrm{m}$

## Explanation

The two reservoir levels are equal and the pipe is symmetric, so each reservoir supplies half the withdrawal, or $60\,\mathrm{L/s}$. The opposing flows meet at the midpoint, where the discharge is zero and the hydraulic grade line reaches its minimum.

Each half has resistance $\dfrac{r}{2} = 600\,\mathrm{s^2/m^5}$ and behaves as a pipe with uniformly distributed withdrawal, an inflow of $Q_m = 60\,\mathrm{L/s}$, and zero discharge at its downstream end. The head loss for uniform withdrawal is therefore
$$
\Delta H = \frac{1}{3}\cdot\frac{r}{2}\,Q_m^2 = \frac{600 \cdot 0.060^2}{3} = 0.720\,\mathrm{m} .
$$
The hydraulic grade line thus falls by $0.720\,\mathrm{m}$ from one reservoir to the midpoint and rises symmetrically to the other reservoir.

The value $2.16\,\mathrm{m}$ assumes that $60\,\mathrm{L/s}$ flows through the entire half-pipe. The value $5.76\,\mathrm{m}$ assumes supply from one end only, and $1.44\,\mathrm{m}$ assigns the resistance of the entire pipe to each half.
