A network branch carries turbulent flow below the fully rough regime, so its Darcy friction factor $\lambda$ decreases as the Reynolds number rises. The branch discharge is doubled. How does the head loss change?

- It quadruples because the branch resistance stays constant
- It increases by a factor greater than four
- It doubles because head loss is proportional to discharge
- It increases by a factor smaller than four

## Explanation

The head loss in the branch is $\Delta H = r\,Q^2$ with $r = \dfrac{8\,\lambda\,L}{g\,\pi^2 D^5}$, so its resistance is proportional to $\lambda$. The Reynolds number $Re = \dfrac{v D}{\nu}$ is proportional to velocity and doubles when the discharge doubles. The new friction factor $\lambda'$ is therefore smaller than $\lambda$.

The new head loss is
$$
\Delta H' = \frac{\lambda'}{\lambda}\,r\,(2Q)^2 = 4\,\frac{\lambda'}{\lambda}\,\Delta H ,
$$
Since $\dfrac{\lambda'}{\lambda} < 1$, the head loss **increases by a factor of less than four**. In this flow regime the branch resistance varies with discharge. A calculation that initially treats $r$ as constant must therefore be repeated with an updated $\lambda$.

Head loss is proportional to discharge in laminar flow, where $\lambda = \dfrac{64}{Re}$. In turbulent flow, $\lambda$ decreases more slowly than $\dfrac{1}{Re}$, so the head loss more than doubles.
