Two small circular sharp-edged orifices with the same discharge coefficient $\mu$ are cut in the vertical wall of a constant-level tank. The first has diameter $100\,\mathrm{mm}$ and its centroid $1.00\,\mathrm{m}$ below the free surface, the second has its centroid $4.00\,\mathrm{m}$ below the free surface. What diameter must the second orifice have for the two orifices to discharge the same flow into the atmosphere?

- $70.7\,\mathrm{mm}$
- $50.0\,\mathrm{mm}$
- $35.4\,\mathrm{mm}$
- $25.0\,\mathrm{mm}$

## Explanation

The discharge of a small orifice is $Q = \mu A \sqrt{2 g h}$, so for the same discharge and coefficient the area is inversely proportional to the square root of the head. Imposing the same discharge on the two orifices gives
$$
\mu A_1 \sqrt{2 g \cdot 1.00} = \mu A_2 \sqrt{2 g \cdot 4.00} ,
$$
hence $A_2 = \dfrac{A_1}{2}$, since the outflow velocity of the second orifice is twice as large. The area is proportional to the square of the diameter, therefore
$$
D_2 = \frac{D_1}{\sqrt{2}} = \frac{100}{1.414} = 70.7\,\mathrm{mm} .
$$
The value $50.0\,\mathrm{mm}$ halves the diameter instead of the area, the value $25.0\,\mathrm{mm}$ divides the diameter by the ratio of the heads, and the value $35.4\,\mathrm{mm}$ uses the exponent $\dfrac{3}{2}$ of the head, which belongs to the rectangular weir.
