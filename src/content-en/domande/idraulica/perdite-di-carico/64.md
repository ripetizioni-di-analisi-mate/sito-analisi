Two head losses are measured along a pipe carrying oil: $1.33\,\mathrm{m}$ at discharge $1.0\,\mathrm{L/s}$ and $2.66\,\mathrm{m}$ at discharge $2.0\,\mathrm{L/s}$. Which flow regime is consistent with both measurements?

- Laminar, because head loss is proportional to discharge
- Fully rough turbulent, because head loss is proportional to the square of discharge
- Turbulent, because head loss increases as the discharge increases
- The regime cannot be determined without the oil viscosity

## Explanation

In laminar flow, the Hagen–Poiseuille law gives $J = \dfrac{32\,\nu\,v}{g\,D^2}$. For a given pipe and fluid, the head loss is therefore proportional to the mean velocity and hence to the discharge. In turbulent flow, discharge varies roughly with the square root of the energy gradient. In the fully rough regime, where $\lambda$ is constant, the Darcy–Weisbach equation gives a head loss proportional to $v^2$. In either turbulent case, doubling the discharge would approximately quadruple the head loss. The measurements give $\dfrac{2.66}{1.33} = 2$. The head loss therefore doubles when the discharge doubles, which indicates **laminar** flow. The two measurements alone establish this result without a value for viscosity.
