Water flows steadily through a horizontal constant-diameter pipe with a uniform friction head loss per unit length. How does pressure vary downstream?

- It stays constant because velocity is the same at every section
- It rises linearly because the flow loses velocity head
- It decreases only along upward-sloping pipe sections
- It falls linearly, with a pressure-head gradient equal to the energy gradient

## Explanation

The pipe diameter is constant, so the **continuity equation** gives the same velocity and velocity head at every section. The centerline elevation is also constant because the pipe is horizontal. By **Bernoulli’s equation with head loss**, total head decreases by $J L$ over a length $L$. Thus
$$
\frac{p_1}{\gamma} - \frac{p_2}{\gamma} = H_1 - H_2 = J L .
$$
Pressure head therefore decreases **linearly** downstream, at a rate equal to the hydraulic gradient. Two sections separated by $L$ have a pressure difference of $\gamma J L$. This is the basis for measuring pipe friction losses in the laboratory using two pressure taps along a straight, horizontal pipe.
