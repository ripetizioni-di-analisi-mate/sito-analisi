A pipe of diameter $500\,\mathrm{mm}$ carries water at mean speed $1.20\,\mathrm{m/s}$. A model pipe of diameter $100\,\mathrm{mm}$ carries the same water. What mean speed in the model gives the same Reynolds number as in the full-size pipe?

- $0.240\,\mathrm{m/s}$
- $6.00\,\mathrm{m/s}$
- $1.20\,\mathrm{m/s}$
- $30.0\,\mathrm{m/s}$

## Explanation

Under comparable conditions, transition from laminar to turbulent pipe flow is governed by the Reynolds number, regardless of the liquid or pipe size. To reproduce the full-scale flow regime, match the Reynolds numbers. Use subscript $p$ for the full-scale pipe and $m$ for the model. Since both use the same fluid, kinematic viscosity cancels and the condition becomes $v_m D_m = v_p D_p$, giving
$$
v_m = v_p\,\frac{D_p}{D_m} = 1.20 \cdot \frac{500}{100}\,\mathrm{m/s} = 6.00\,\mathrm{m/s} .
$$
The smaller model requires a higher velocity. At the full-scale velocity $1.20\,\mathrm{m/s}$ the model Reynolds number would be one-fifth of the full-scale value. Squaring the diameter ratio would give $30.0\,\mathrm{m/s}$, which preserves discharge but fails to match the Reynolds number.
