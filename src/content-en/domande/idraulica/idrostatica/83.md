An open tank contains oil of specific gravity $0.80$ floating on water. Gauge pressure is plotted against depth on the vertical wall. What happens to the pressure diagram at the interface?

- Pressure jumps because the two liquids have different specific weights
- The slope stays the same because pressure is proportional to depth below the free surface
- Pressure is continuous and increases more slowly with depth below the interface than in the oil
- Pressure is continuous and increases more rapidly with depth below the interface than in the oil

## Explanation

Pressure is continuous across the interface, so the diagram has no jump. Let $y$ be depth. Applying the hydrostatic pressure law in each liquid gives $\dfrac{dp}{dy} = \gamma_i$, where $\gamma_i$ is the specific weight of the liquid at that point. The diagram therefore has two linear segments with different slopes. In oil the pressure increases by $0.80 \cdot 9.81 = 7.85\,\mathrm{kPa}$ per meter of depth, and in water by $9.81\,\mathrm{kPa}$, so pressure increases more rapidly below the interface. The pressure diagram is triangular in the oil and trapezoidal in the water, with a change of slope at the interface. Pressure is directly proportional to depth below the free surface only in a liquid of constant specific weight.
