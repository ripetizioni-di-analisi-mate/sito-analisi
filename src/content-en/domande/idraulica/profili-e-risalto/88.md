Can a jump take a subcritical flow at depth $1.60\,\mathrm{m}$ abruptly to supercritical flow at depth $0.40\,\mathrm{m}$ if the two depths are conjugate, giving the same momentum function?

- Yes, because the momentum function is conserved in both directions
- No, because the energy loss would be negative and the flow would gain energy
- Yes, provided that the bed is horizontal
- No, because two depths with the same momentum function cannot lie on opposite sides of critical depth

## Explanation

The energy loss between conjugate depths is $\Delta E = E_1 - E_2 = \dfrac{(h_2 - h_1)^3}{4\,h_1 h_2}$, where $h_1$ is the upstream depth and $h_2$ is the downstream depth. With $h_1 = 1.60\,\mathrm{m}$ and $h_2 = 0.40\,\mathrm{m}$, the result is
$$
\Delta E = \frac{(0.40 - 1.60)^3}{4 \cdot 1.60 \cdot 0.40} = \frac{-1.728}{2.56} = -0.675\,\mathrm{m} ,
$$
so the downstream specific energy would exceed the upstream specific energy. Without an external energy source the water cannot gain total head. A jump from subcritical to supercritical flow **is impossible**. A hydraulic jump always goes from supercritical to subcritical flow. Conjugate depths always lie on opposite sides of $h_c$. Conservation of the momentum function is necessary but does not by itself establish that the transition is possible.
