After many years, a cast-iron pipeline connecting two constant-level reservoirs has become encrusted. Its effective diameter is smaller and its absolute roughness greater. Which statement about the discharge is correct?

- It increases because the flow accelerates in the narrower section
- It stays the same because the energy gradient depends only on elevation difference and length
- It decreases only because the diameter is smaller, while roughness has no effect
- It decreases because both the smaller diameter and the greater roughness increase the pipe resistance

## Explanation

The reservoir elevation difference and pipe length are unchanged, so the energy gradient remains $J = \dfrac{z_1 - z_2}{L}$ and the total head loss still equals the elevation difference. Since $z_1 - z_2 = r Q^2$, the discharge is $Q = \sqrt{\dfrac{z_1 - z_2}{r}}$, where $r = \dfrac{8\,\lambda\,L}{g\,\pi^2 D^5}$. The smaller effective diameter increases $r$ through the factor $D^{-5}$. An increase in $\varepsilon$ combined with a decrease in $D$ also increases the relative roughness, $\dfrac{\varepsilon}{D}$, and hence the Darcy friction factor $\lambda$ given by the Colebrook–White equation. Both changes increase $r$, so the discharge decreases.
