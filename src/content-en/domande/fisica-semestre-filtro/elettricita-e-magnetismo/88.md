In a loop of resistance $R$ the magnetic flux changes by the same amount $\Delta\Phi_B$ in two trials. In the second trial the change takes twice as long. Neglecting self-inductance, how does the charge that crosses a cross-section of the wire during the change compare?

- It is the same in both trials.
- It is twice as large in the second trial.
- It is half as large in the second trial.
- It depends only on the final value of the flux.

## Explanation

By the Faraday-Neumann-Lenz law the average electromotive force has magnitude $|\mathcal E_{\mathrm m}|=\dfrac{|\Delta\Phi_B|}{\Delta t}$, so the average current in the loop is $I_{\mathrm m}=\dfrac{|\Delta\Phi_B|}{R\,\Delta t}$. The charge that crosses the wire is the average current multiplied by the duration, therefore
$$
q=I_{\mathrm m}\,\Delta t=\frac{|\Delta\Phi_B|}{R\,\Delta t}\,\Delta t=\frac{|\Delta\Phi_B|}{R}.
$$
The duration cancels, so the charge depends only on the change in flux and on the resistance. In the second trial the average current is halved and flows for twice the time, therefore the charge **is the same in both trials**. The answer "half as large" comes from considering only the electromotive force, which is halved in the second trial.
