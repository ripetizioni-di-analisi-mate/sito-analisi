Two adjacent infinite plane layers have opposite uniform charge densities: $\rho = -\rho_0$ for $-a < x < 0$ and $\rho = +\rho_0$ for $0 < x < a$, with $\rho_0 = 2.0\,\mathrm{\mu C/m^3}$ and $a = 3.0\,\mathrm{cm}$; outside the two layers there are no charges. What is the magnitude of the electric field on the plane $x = 0$?

- $3.4 \cdot 10^3\,\mathrm{N/C}$
- $0$
- $6.8 \cdot 10^3\,\mathrm{N/C}$
- $1.4 \cdot 10^4\,\mathrm{N/C}$

## Explanation

Each thin sheet parallel to the plane $x = 0$ produces, like an infinite plane, a field of constant magnitude directed in opposite senses in the two half-spaces. The total charge per unit area of the two layers is zero, so in the region $x < -a$ the contributions add up to zero, and the field vanishes.

The Gaussian surface is a cylinder with its axis parallel to the $x$ axis, one base of area $A$ in the region $x < -a$ and the other on the plane $x = 0$. The field is parallel to the $x$ axis and vanishes on the first base, so the flux through that base and the flux through the lateral surface are zero. Therefore the outward flux from the cylinder is $E_x(0)\,A$.

The cylinder encloses the charge $-\rho_0 a A$, therefore **Gauss's law** gives
$$
E_x(0) = -\frac{\rho_0 a}{\varepsilon_0} = -\frac{2.0 \cdot 10^{-6} \cdot 0.030}{8.85 \cdot 10^{-12}}\,\mathrm{N/C} \approx -6.8 \cdot 10^3\,\mathrm{N/C} .
$$
The field therefore has magnitude $6.8 \cdot 10^3\,\mathrm{N/C}$ and points from the positive layer toward the negative one. The value $3.4 \cdot 10^3\,\mathrm{N/C}$ is the external field of only one of the two layers, $\dfrac{\rho_0 a}{2\varepsilon_0}$.
