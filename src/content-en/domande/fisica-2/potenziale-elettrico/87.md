A very long straight wire carries a uniformly distributed charge with density $\lambda = +2.0\,\mathrm{nC/m}$. An electron starts from rest at $4.0\,\mathrm{cm}$ from the wire and moves toward it. What is its kinetic energy, in electronvolts, when it is $1.0\,\mathrm{cm}$ from the wire? Gravity is neglected.

- $50\,\mathrm{eV}$
- $25\,\mathrm{eV}$
- $36\,\mathrm{eV}$
- $1.0 \cdot 10^2\,\mathrm{eV}$

## Explanation

For the wire, the potential difference between two points at distances $r_1$ and $r_2$ is $V(r_1) - V(r_2) = \dfrac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0} \ln \dfrac{r_2}{r_1} = 2k\lambda \ln \dfrac{r_2}{r_1}$.

With $r_1 = 1.0\,\mathrm{cm}$ and $r_2 = 4.0\,\mathrm{cm}$ one gets $2 \cdot 8.99 \cdot 10^9 \cdot 2.0 \cdot 10^{-9} \cdot \ln 4\,\mathrm{V} \approx 50\,\mathrm{V}$, so the potential at the final point exceeds that at the initial point by about $50\,\mathrm{V}$.

The electron, with charge $-e$, moves toward higher potentials, therefore its potential energy decreases by $e \cdot 50\,\mathrm{V} = 50\,\mathrm{eV}$. By conservation of energy, starting from rest, it then has a kinetic energy of about $50\,\mathrm{eV}$.
