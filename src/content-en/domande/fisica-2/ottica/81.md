A slit is exactly as wide as the wavelength of the light that illuminates it perpendicularly, that is, $a = \lambda$, and the diffraction pattern is observed on a very wide screen. Which statement is true?

- The central maximum has an angular width of $1^\circ$
- Many secondary maxima are observed, separated by minima at intervals of $1\,\mathrm{rad}$
- The light goes on in a straight line, as geometrical optics predicts
- The first minima are at $90^\circ$ and the central maximum fills the whole half-space beyond the slit

## Explanation

The diffraction minima lie in the directions with $a\sin\theta = m\lambda$, with $m = \pm 1, \pm 2, \dots$, so for $a = \lambda$ in the directions with $\sin\theta = m$.

Since $|\sin\theta| \leq 1$, only $m = \pm 1$ gives a direction, namely $\theta = \pm 90^\circ$, grazing the screen that contains the slit. Therefore between $-90^\circ$ and $90^\circ$ there is no minimum, and the central maximum fills the whole half-space beyond the slit. An aperture as wide as the wavelength thus spreads the light in all directions, like a source of secondary waves in the Huygens-Fresnel principle.
