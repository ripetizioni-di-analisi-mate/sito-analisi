An ideal inductor is fed by an ideal source of $12\,\mathrm{V}$ through a resistor of $6.0\,\Omega$, and a resistor of $500\,\Omega$ is connected in parallel across the inductor. The circuit is in steady state. At the instant $t = 0$ a switch in series with the source is opened, and the current of the inductor closes through the $500\,\Omega$ resistor. What is the magnitude of the potential difference across the inductor just after opening?

- $12\,\mathrm{V}$
- $0\,\mathrm{V}$
- $1.0 \cdot 10^{3}\,\mathrm{V}$
- $5.0 \cdot 10^{2}\,\mathrm{V}$

## Explanation

In steady state the voltage across the ideal inductor is zero, so no current flows in the $500\,\Omega$ resistor and the inductor carries $I_0 = \dfrac{12}{6.0}\,\mathrm{A} = 2.0\,\mathrm{A}$. The current in the inductor cannot change with a jump, therefore just after opening the inductor still drives $2.0\,\mathrm{A}$, now through the $500\,\Omega$ resistor. The potential difference across the inductor equals that across the resistor:
$$
|v_L| = 2.0 \cdot 500\,\mathrm{V} = 1.0 \cdot 10^{3}\,\mathrm{V} .
$$
The voltage is much higher than that of the source; this is the origin of overvoltages when inductive circuits are opened.
