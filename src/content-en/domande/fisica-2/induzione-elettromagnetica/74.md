A coaxial cable has an inner conductor of radius $a$ and an outer conductor of radius $b = 4a$, both of negligible thickness; the two conductors carry equal and opposite currents of $10\,\mathrm{A}$. Between the conductors the field is $B = \dfrac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi r}$, elsewhere it is zero. How much magnetic energy is stored in one meter of cable?

- $1.4 \cdot 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{J}$
- $2.8 \cdot 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{J}$
- $6.9 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\mathrm{J}$
- $1.0 \cdot 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{J}$

## Explanation

The **energy density of the magnetic field** is $u_B = \dfrac{B^2}{2\mu_0} = \dfrac{\mu_0 I^2}{8\pi^2 r^2}$. The cylindrical shell of radius $r$, thickness $dr$ and length $\ell$ has volume $2\pi r \ell\,dr$, so the energy per unit length is
$$
\frac{U}{\ell} = \int_a^b \frac{\mu_0 I^2}{8\pi^2 r^2}\,2\pi r\,dr = \frac{\mu_0 I^2}{4\pi} \ln\frac{b}{a} .
$$
With $\dfrac{\mu_0}{4\pi} = 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{T \cdot m/A}$ one obtains $\dfrac{U}{\ell} = 10^{-7} \cdot 10^2 \cdot \ln 4\,\mathrm{J/m} \approx 1.4 \cdot 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{J/m}$. The value $1.0 \cdot 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{J}$ is obtained by omitting the logarithm that comes from the integration.
