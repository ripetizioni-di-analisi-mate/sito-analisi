The graph shows the characteristic curve of a tungsten filament lamp. How does the ratio $\dfrac{\Delta V}{I}$ change when the applied voltage increases, and why?

::grafico{f="(x>=0?1:NaN)*sqrt(x)" xmin=-0.6 xmax=16.8 ymin=-0.4 ymax=4.8 nomi="ΔV,I" punti="4,2,pieno;16,4,pieno" segmenti="4,0,4,2;16,0,16,4" titolo="Characteristic curve of a filament lamp: current $I$ as a function of the voltage $\Delta V$, in arbitrary units"}

- It stays constant, because the filament is metallic and therefore ohmic
- It increases, because the filament heats up and its resistivity increases
- It decreases, because the current increases with the voltage
- It increases, because the cross section of the filament decreases with the voltage

## Explanation

At the two marked points the voltage goes from $4$ to $16$ units, so it becomes four times larger, while the current goes from $2$ to $4$ units and only doubles. Hence the ratio $\dfrac{\Delta V}{I}$ goes from $2$ to $4$ units and increases with the voltage. The curve is not a straight line through the origin, therefore the lamp does not obey Ohm's law. The cause is temperature: as the current grows the filament heats up, and the resistivity of tungsten increases with temperature.
