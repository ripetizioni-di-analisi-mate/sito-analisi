The electric field of a plane wave in vacuum propagating along the $x$-axis is
$$
\vec{E}(x,t) = E_0 \left[ \cos(kx - \omega t)\,\hat{\jmath} + \sin(kx - \omega t)\,\hat{k} \right] ,
$$
with $E_0$, $k$ and $\omega$ positive constants. How does the electric field vary in time at a fixed point?

- It oscillates along the fixed direction $\dfrac{\hat{\jmath} + \hat{k}}{\sqrt{2}}$ with amplitude $\sqrt{2}\,E_0$
- It has constant magnitude $E_0$ and its direction rotates in the $yz$-plane
- It has constant magnitude $\sqrt{2}\,E_0$ and its direction rotates in the $yz$-plane
- It has constant magnitude $E_0$ and its direction rotates in the $xy$-plane

## Explanation

The field is the superposition of two harmonic waves linearly polarized along $y$ and along $z$, out of phase by a quarter of a period.
The square of the magnitude is $E_0^2 \left[ \cos^2(kx - \omega t) + \sin^2(kx - \omega t) \right] = E_0^2$, so the magnitude equals $E_0$ at every instant.
The field has components only along $\hat{\jmath}$ and $\hat{k}$, therefore it stays in the $yz$-plane, perpendicular to the direction of propagation.
At a fixed point the angle $kx - \omega t$ between the field and $\hat{\jmath}$ varies linearly in time, so the direction of the field **rotates in the $yz$-plane** with angular velocity $\omega$. A wave of this kind is said to be circularly polarized.
