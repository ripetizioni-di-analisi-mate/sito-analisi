A simply supported beam carries only a force $F$, down, which instead of being concentrated is evenly distributed over the section $CD$ of length $2\varepsilon$, i.e. with the load $q = \dfrac{F}{2\varepsilon}$. How do the shear force and bending moment vary between $C$ and $D$?

- The shear jumps by $-\dfrac{F}{2}$ at $C$ and jumps again at $D$, and the moment diagram has two corners
- The shear is constant and the moment diagram has a corner at the midpoint of $CD$
- The shear decreases linearly by $F$, and the moment diagram is a parabolic arc tangent to the adjacent straight segments
- The shear decreases linearly by $\dfrac{F}{2\varepsilon}$ and the moment is linear

## Explanation

At section $CD$ the load is uniform, so $\dfrac{dT}{dx} = -\dfrac{F}{2\varepsilon}$ and the shear is a straight line. Its variation between $C$ and $D$ is the opposite of the resultant load, i.e. $-\dfrac{F}{2\varepsilon} \cdot 2\varepsilon = -F$. Since $\dfrac{d^2M}{dx^2} = -q$ is constant, the moment in the segment is a parabola arch. In $C$ and $D$ there are no concentrated forces or couples, so the shear is continuous and the moment diagram has continuous tangent, that is, the parabola connects to the segments of the unloaded segments. For $\varepsilon \to 0$ the decrease of $F$ becomes the $-F$ jump of the shear and the arch of a parabola becomes the corner of the moment under the concentrated force.
