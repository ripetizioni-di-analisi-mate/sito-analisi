We look for a polynomial of degree $3$ that vanishes at $1$, $2$, $3$ and $4$. Which statement is true?

- It exists, and it is $(x - 1)(x - 2)(x - 3)(x - 4)$
- It exists only if one of the four roots is double
- It does not exist, because a polynomial of degree $3$ has at most $3$ roots
- It exists, and it is unique up to the leading coefficient

## Explanation

Let $P$ be a nonzero polynomial that vanishes at $1$, $2$, $3$ and $4$. By the **factor theorem** we have $P(x) = (x - 1)\,Q_1(x)$, and since $P(2) = 1 \cdot Q_1(2) = 0$, the quotient $Q_1$ also vanishes at $2$. Repeating the argument for $2$, $3$ and $4$ gives $P(x) = (x - 1)(x - 2)(x - 3)(x - 4)\,Q(x)$, with $Q$ nonzero. In a product the degrees add up, so $P$ has degree at least $4$. Therefore no polynomial of degree $3$ vanishes at four distinct numbers, and the product $(x - 1)(x - 2)(x - 3)(x - 4)$ has degree $4$.
