How many linear maps $f : \mathbb{R}^3 \to \mathbb{R}^2$ satisfy $f(1, 0, 1) = (1, 1)$ and $f(0, 1, 1) = (2, 0)$?

- None, because two vectors do not form a basis of $\mathbb{R}^3$
- Exactly one, because $(1, 0, 1)$ and $(0, 1, 1)$ are linearly independent
- Exactly one, because $(1, 1)$ and $(2, 0)$ are linearly independent
- Infinitely many

## Explanation

The vectors $(1, 0, 1)$ and $(0, 1, 1)$ are not proportional, so they are linearly independent, and together with $(0, 0, 1)$ they form a basis of $\mathbb{R}^3$, since the determinant of the matrix having the three vectors as columns equals $1$. By the **existence and uniqueness theorem**, for every choice of $w \in \mathbb{R}^2$ there is exactly one linear map with the two given values and $f(0, 0, 1) = w$. Different choices of $w$ give different maps, therefore there are **infinitely many** such maps.
