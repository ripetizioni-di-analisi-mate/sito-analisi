A matrix $A \in M_3(\mathbb{R})$ has the three distinct eigenvalues $1$, $2$ and $3$. How many lines through the origin of $\mathbb{R}^3$ does $A$ map to themselves, that is, lines $r$ with $Av \in r$ for every $v \in r$?

- Infinitely many
- $3$
- $6$
- $1$

## Explanation

A line $\langle v \rangle$ is mapped to itself if and only if $Av$ is a multiple of $v$, that is, if and only if $v$ is an eigenvector. Every eigenvalue is simple, so every eigenspace has dimension $1$ and is a line. By the theorem on eigenvectors associated with distinct eigenvalues, a vector with nonzero components in two different eigenspaces is not an eigenvector, hence every eigenvector lies on one of the three lines $V_1$, $V_2$, $V_3$. The lines mapped to themselves are therefore exactly **three**. There would be infinitely many if some eigenspace had dimension at least $2$, as happens for $I_3$.
