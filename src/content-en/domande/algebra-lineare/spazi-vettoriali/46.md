In $\mathbb{R}^2$ consider the usual sum and the scalar multiplication defined by $\lambda \odot (x, y) = (\lambda x, 0)$. Which property of the definition of a vector space does not hold?

- $1 \odot v = v$ for every $v$
- $u + v = v + u$ for every $u, v$
- $\lambda \odot (u + v) = \lambda \odot u + \lambda \odot v$ for every $\lambda, u, v$
- $(\lambda + \mu) \odot v = \lambda \odot v + \mu \odot v$ for every $\lambda, \mu, v$

## Explanation

For $v = (0, 1)$ we have $1 \odot (0, 1) = (0, 0)$, which is different from $(0, 1)$; therefore the property $1 \odot v = v$ **does not hold**.

The sum is the usual one, so it is commutative. The product acts on the first component as the usual product and sends the second one to $0$, hence
$$
\lambda \odot (u + v) = (\lambda (u_1 + v_1), 0) = (\lambda u_1, 0) + (\lambda v_1, 0) ,
$$
and in the same way $(\lambda + \mu) \odot v = (\lambda v_1 + \mu v_1, 0) = \lambda \odot v + \mu \odot v$. Therefore the only property that fails is $1 \odot v = v$, and $\mathbb{R}^2$ with these operations is not a vector space.
