Two samples of water are mixed, and the formula for the equilibrium temperature, which neglects the container, gives $45\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$. In fact, the mixing takes place in a heavy earthenware jug that starts at room temperature, $20\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$, and is wrapped in an insulating cloth. How does the actual final temperature compare?

- Higher than $45\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$, because the jug releases heat to the water
- Lower than $45\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$, because the jug also absorbs heat from the water
- Equal to $45\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$, because the jug is insulated from the surroundings
- Equal to $20\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$, because the jug brings the water to its own temperature

## Explanation

The formula used neglects the heat capacity of the container. The jug starts at $20\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$, a temperature lower than $45\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$, so during the process it absorbs energy from the water. The heat absorbed by the jug then appears as an additional term in the sum, and the energy left for the water decreases. Therefore, the common final temperature is lower than $45\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$. The insulating cloth reduces heat transfer to the surroundings, while heat transfer between the jug and the water still takes place.
