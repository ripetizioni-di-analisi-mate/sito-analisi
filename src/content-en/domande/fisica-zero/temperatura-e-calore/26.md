On a summer day at noon, the sand on the beach is scorching while the seawater stays cool, even though the Sun delivers about the same energy per kilogram to both. The specific heat capacity of sand is about $800\,\mathrm{J/(kg\cdot K)}$, and that of water is $4180\,\mathrm{J/(kg\cdot K)}$. Which statement explains the difference?

- Sand has a lower specific heat capacity, so it absorbs less energy from the Sun
- Sand has a higher melting point than water
- Water has a higher specific heat capacity, so it transfers its energy to the sand
- Sand has a lower specific heat capacity, so for the same energy per kilogram its temperature rises more

## Explanation

For one kilogram of a substance, the specific heat equation gives $\Delta T=\dfrac{Q}{mc}$ with $m=1\,\mathrm{kg}$. For the same energy received per kilogram, the temperature change is therefore inversely proportional to the specific heat capacity. The specific heat capacity of sand is about five times smaller than that of water, so the sand warms about five times as much. The energy absorbed is the same by assumption; only the temperature increase it produces is different.
