A thin jar of negligible heat capacity contains $1.0\,\mathrm{kg}$ of oil at $80\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ and is placed in $1.0\,\mathrm{kg}$ of water at $20\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$, inside an insulated container. The specific heat capacity of the oil is about half that of water. What can be said about the common final temperature?

- It is equal to $50\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$, because the masses are equal
- It is lower than $50\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$, because the water has the larger heat capacity
- It is higher than $50\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$, because the oil has the lower specific heat capacity
- It is equal to $20\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$, because the water absorbs the energy of the oil without warming

## Explanation

For equal masses, the heat capacity of the water is about twice that of the oil. In the energy balance, the energy released by the oil equals the energy absorbed by the water, so the temperature of the water changes by about half as much as that of the oil. Therefore, the final temperature is closer to $20\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ and lower than $50\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$. With a ratio of exactly $2$ between the heat capacities, the result would be $\dfrac{80+2\cdot20}{3}=40\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$.
