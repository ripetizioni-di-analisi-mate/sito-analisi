In an insulated container, $1.0\,\mathrm{kg}$ of water at $70\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ and $1.0\,\mathrm{kg}$ of water at $30\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ reach a common temperature of $50\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$. What temperature would be obtained by repeating the experiment with oil instead of water, with the same masses and the same initial temperatures?

- Again $50\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$, because the specific heat capacity, equal for both samples, cancels
- A temperature lower than $50\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$, because oil has a lower specific heat capacity
- A temperature higher than $50\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$, because oil has a lower specific heat capacity
- It cannot be determined without knowing the specific heat capacity of the oil

## Explanation

When the two objects are made of the same substance, $c_1=c_2=c$, and in the formula for the equilibrium temperature the factor $c$ appears in every term of the numerator and the denominator. It therefore cancels, leaving $t_e=\dfrac{m_1t_1+m_2t_2}{m_1+m_2}$. With equal masses this gives the average $\dfrac{70+30}{2}=50\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$, whatever the liquid, provided it does not change state and the system is isolated.
