A water rocket is launched straight up with a velocity of $15\,\mathrm{m/s}$; air resistance is negligible, $g=10\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$, and the axis points upward with its origin at the launch point. At $t=1\,\mathrm{s}$ and at $t=2\,\mathrm{s}$ the rocket is at the same height, $10\,\mathrm{m}$. What distinguishes the two instants?

- At both instants the velocity is zero
- At $1\,\mathrm{s}$ the rocket rises with $v=5\,\mathrm{m/s}$; at $2\,\mathrm{s}$ it falls with $v=-5\,\mathrm{m/s}$
- At $1\,\mathrm{s}$ the rocket falls; at $2\,\mathrm{s}$ it rises
- At both instants the rocket rises with $v=5\,\mathrm{m/s}$

## Explanation

The velocity is $v=15-10t$, with $v$ in $\mathrm{m/s}$ and $t$ in seconds. At $t=1\,\mathrm{s}$ it is $5\,\mathrm{m/s}$, positive, so the rocket is rising. At $t=2\,\mathrm{s}$ it is $-5\,\mathrm{m/s}$, negative, so the rocket is falling. The highest point is reached at $t_s=1.5\,\mathrm{s}$, between the two instants, which explains why the height is the same with opposite velocities.
