An accumulation law is generated by a deterministic integrable force of interest $\delta(t)$. Why does moving a value directly from $s$ to $u$ give the same result as moving it in two stages through $t$?

- The integral from $s$ to $u$ is the sum of the integrals over consecutive intervals, and exponentiating their sum gives their product of exponentials.
- The force of interest must have the same value at all three times.
- Exponentiating the sum of the integrals gives the sum of the factors.
- The integral of the force over each interval must equal zero.

## Explanation

The force of interest generates the factor $r(s,u)=\exp\!\left(\int_s^u\delta(x)\,\mathrm dx\right)$. Additivity of the integral gives $\int_s^u\delta=\int_s^t\delta+\int_t^u\delta$. Exponentiating a sum multiplies the exponentials, so
$$
r(s,u)=\exp\!\left(\int_s^t\delta\right)\exp\!\left(\int_t^u\delta\right)=r(s,t)\,r(t,u).
$$
The identity holds for any integrable force of interest, regardless of its values.
