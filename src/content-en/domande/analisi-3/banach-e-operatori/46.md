Let $(X,d)$ be a nonempty complete metric space and let $T:X\to X$ be a map such that $T^2=T\circ T$ is a contraction. If $p$ is the unique fixed point of $T^2$, which step proves that $p$ is a fixed point of $T$?

- From $T^2p=p$ it follows that $T$ is the identity on $X$.
- $T^2(Tp)=T(T^2p)=Tp$, so the uniqueness of the fixed point of $T^2$ gives $Tp=p$.
- The Lipschitz constant of $T$ is the square root of that of $T^2$, so $T$ is a contraction.
- Every orbit of $T$ is constant from the second term on.

## Explanation

The Banach fixed point theorem applies to $T^2$, so $T^2$ has exactly one fixed point $p$.
Since $T$ commutes with $T^2$, we have $T^2(Tp)=T(T^2p)=Tp$, hence $Tp$ is also a fixed point of $T^2$.
By uniqueness it follows that $Tp=p$, that is, $p$ is a fixed point of $T$.
Every fixed point of $T$ is also a fixed point of $T^2$, therefore $p$ is the only fixed point of $T$.
The Lipschitz constant of $T$ can be larger than one even when $T^2$ is a contraction, as the map $T(x,y)=(2y,0)$ on $\mathbb R^2$ shows, since $T^2=0$.
