What is $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 0} \left( 1 + \sin x \right)^{\frac{1}{x}}$?

- $1$
- $e$
- $+\infty$
- The limit does not exist

## Explanation

As $x \to 0$ the base tends to $1$ and the exponent $\dfrac{1}{x}$ tends to $\pm\infty$, so the limit has the indeterminate form $1^{\infty}$. The function is written as $e^{\frac{\ln(1 + \sin x)}{x}}$ and the limit of the exponent is computed. Since $\sin x \to 0$, with the substitution $t = \sin x$ in the first factor one obtains
$$
\frac{\ln(1 + \sin x)}{x} = \frac{\ln(1 + \sin x)}{\sin x} \cdot \frac{\sin x}{x} \to 1 \cdot 1 = 1
$$
by the **standard limits** of the logarithm and of the sine. The exponential function is continuous, therefore the limit equals $e^{1} = e$, both from the right and from the left. The value $1$ comes from considering only the base, and the answer "the limit does not exist" comes from the different sign of the exponent to the right and to the left of $0$, which here does not change the result.
