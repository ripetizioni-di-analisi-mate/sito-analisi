Which of the following limits as $x \to 0$ exists and is finite?

- $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sin x}{x^2}$
- $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{e^{x} - 1}{x^2}$
- $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\ln\left( 1 + x^2 \right)}{x}$
- $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{1 - e^{x}}{x^3}$

## Explanation

Each quotient is reduced to a **standard limit** multiplied by a power of $x$. We have $\dfrac{\sin x}{x^2} = \dfrac{\sin x}{x} \cdot \dfrac{1}{x}$ and $\dfrac{e^{x} - 1}{x^2} = \dfrac{e^{x} - 1}{x} \cdot \dfrac{1}{x}$; in both the first factor tends to $1$ and the second is infinite, so these limits are not finite. In the same way $\dfrac{1 - e^{x}}{x^3} = -\dfrac{e^{x} - 1}{x} \cdot \dfrac{1}{x^2}$ tends to $-\infty$. With $t = x^2$, on the other hand,
$$
\frac{\ln\left( 1 + x^2 \right)}{x} = \frac{\ln\left( 1 + x^2 \right)}{x^2} \cdot x \to 1 \cdot 0 = 0 ,
$$
so the only finite limit is that of $\dfrac{\ln\left( 1 + x^2 \right)}{x}$, which equals $0$.
