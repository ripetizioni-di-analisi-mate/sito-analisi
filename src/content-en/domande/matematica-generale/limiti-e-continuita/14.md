The equation $x^3 - 3x + 1 = 0$ has exactly one solution in the interval $(0, 1)$. In which of the following intervals does it lie?

- $\left( 0, \dfrac{1}{4} \right)$
- $\left( \dfrac{1}{4}, \dfrac{1}{2} \right)$
- $\left( \dfrac{1}{2}, \dfrac{3}{4} \right)$
- $\left( \dfrac{3}{4}, 1 \right)$

## Explanation

Let $f(x) = x^3 - 3x + 1$, which is a polynomial and is therefore continuous on every closed interval. We have
$$
f\left( \frac{1}{4} \right) = \frac{1}{64} - \frac{3}{4} + 1 = \frac{17}{64},
\qquad
f\left( \frac{1}{2} \right) = \frac{1}{8} - \frac{3}{2} + 1 = -\frac{3}{8} .
$$
The function $f$ is continuous on the closed interval $\left[ \dfrac{1}{4}, \dfrac{1}{2} \right]$ and has opposite signs at the endpoints, positive at $\dfrac{1}{4}$ and negative at $\dfrac{1}{2}$. The hypotheses of **Bolzano's theorem** are therefore satisfied, so $f$ has at least one zero in $\left( \dfrac{1}{4}, \dfrac{1}{2} \right)$. Since the solution in $(0, 1)$ is unique, this is the only one. It is approximately $0{,}347$.
