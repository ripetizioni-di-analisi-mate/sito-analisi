The function $f(x, y) = y$ has minimum $0$ on the curve $x^2 = y^3$, at the origin. With $g(x, y) = x^2 - y^3$, which statement is true?

- The Lagrange multiplier equations have no solutions, and at the origin $\nabla g = (0, 0)$
- The Lagrange multiplier equations have the origin as their only solution, with $\lambda = 0$
- The origin is not a constrained minimum point, because it does not satisfy the Lagrange multiplier equations
- The Lagrange multiplier equations have the origin as their only solution, with $\lambda = -\dfrac{1}{3}$

## Explanation

On the curve we have $y^3 = x^2 \geq 0$, so $y \geq 0$. Therefore $f(x, y) = y \geq 0 = f(0, 0)$ at every point of the curve, and the origin is the minimum point. The Lagrange multiplier equations are
$$
\begin{cases}
0 = 2\lambda x \\[4pt]
1 = -3\lambda y^2 \\[4pt]
x^2 = y^3
\end{cases}
$$
The second equation requires $\lambda \neq 0$ and $y \neq 0$, so the first gives $x = 0$ and the constraint gives $y = 0$, which contradicts the second. The system therefore **has no solutions**. At the origin we have $\nabla g = (2x, -3y^2) = (0, 0)$, so the hypothesis $\nabla g(P_0) \neq (0, 0)$ of the **Lagrange multiplier theorem** is not satisfied, and the theorem does not apply at that point.
