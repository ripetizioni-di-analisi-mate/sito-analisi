Which statement about the absolute extrema of $f(x, y) = e^{-(x^2 + y^2)}$ is true?

- $f$ has absolute maximum $1$ and no absolute minimum
- $f$ has absolute maximum $1$ and absolute minimum $0$
- $f$ has a saddle point at $(0, 0)$
- $f$ has at $(0, 0)$ a local maximum that is not absolute

## Explanation

Since $x^2 + y^2 \geq 0$ and the exponential function is increasing, we have $f(x, y) \leq e^{0} = 1$ for every $(x, y)$, with equality only at the origin. Hence $(0, 0)$ is an **absolute maximum** point and the maximum equals $1$. The exponential is always positive, so $f(x, y) > 0$ at every point of the plane and the value $0$ is not attained. Along the $x$-axis the function equals $f(x, 0) = e^{-x^2}$, which tends to $0$ as $x \to +\infty$. If $P$ were an absolute minimum point, we would have $f(P) > 0$, and a point of the $x$-axis far enough from the origin would give a value smaller than $f(P)$. Therefore $f$ **has no absolute minimum**.
