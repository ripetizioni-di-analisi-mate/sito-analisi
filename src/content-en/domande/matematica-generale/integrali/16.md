Which of the following functions is an antiderivative of $f(x) = \sin(2x)$ on $\mathbb{R}$?

- $\sin^2 x$
- $\cos^2 x$
- $-2\cos(2x)$
- $\dfrac{\cos(2x)}{2}$

## Explanation

The integral can be reduced to the standard integral $\displaystyle\int \sin t\,dt = -\cos t + c$ with $t = 2x$ and $dt = 2\,dx$, so the antiderivatives of $\sin(2x)$ are $-\dfrac{\cos(2x)}{2} + c$. Since $\cos(2x) = 1 - 2\sin^2 x$, we have
$$
\sin^2 x = -\frac{\cos(2x)}{2} + \frac{1}{2} .
$$
The function $\sin^2 x$ therefore differs from $-\dfrac{\cos(2x)}{2}$ by a constant, so the theorem on antiderivatives of the same function makes it an **antiderivative** of $\sin(2x)$. The functions $\dfrac{\cos(2x)}{2}$ and $\cos^2 x = \dfrac{\cos(2x)}{2} + \dfrac{1}{2}$ are antiderivatives of $-\sin(2x)$. The expression $-2\cos(2x)$ multiplies by $2$ where division is needed.
