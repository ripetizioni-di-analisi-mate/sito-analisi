Which of the following functions is an antiderivative of $f(x) = \dfrac{1}{x}$ on the interval $(-\infty, 0)$?

- $\ln x$
- $\ln(-x) + 3$
- $-\ln(-x)$
- $-\dfrac{1}{x^2}$

## Explanation

For $x < 0$ we have $-x > 0$, so $\ln(-x)$ is defined on the whole interval $(-\infty, 0)$. By the chain rule
$$
\big( \ln(-x) + 3 \big)' = \frac{-1}{-x} = \frac{1}{x} ,
$$
therefore $\ln(-x) + 3$ is an **antiderivative** of $\dfrac{1}{x}$ on $(-\infty, 0)$; the constant $3$ does not change the derivative. The function $\ln x$ is not defined for $x < 0$. The derivative of $-\ln(-x)$ is $-\dfrac{1}{x}$, which has the opposite sign. The function $-\dfrac{1}{x^2}$ is the derivative of $\dfrac{1}{x}$, not an antiderivative of it.
