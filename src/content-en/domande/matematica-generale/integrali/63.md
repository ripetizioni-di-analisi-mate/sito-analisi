Let $G$ be the function defined on $\mathbb{R} \setminus \{0\}$ by
$$
G(x) =
\begin{cases}
-\dfrac{1}{x} + 1 & \text{if } x > 0 \\[8pt]
-\dfrac{1}{x} - 2 & \text{if } x < 0
\end{cases}
$$
Which statement is true?

- $G$ is not an antiderivative of $\dfrac{1}{x^2}$, because $G(x) + \dfrac{1}{x}$ is not constant
- $G$ is an antiderivative of $\dfrac{1}{x^2}$ only on $(0, +\infty)$
- $G$ is an antiderivative of $\dfrac{1}{x^2}$ on $\mathbb{R} \setminus \{0\}$
- $G$ is an antiderivative of $-\dfrac{1}{x^2}$ on $\mathbb{R} \setminus \{0\}$

## Explanation

For $x > 0$ we have $G'(x) = \dfrac{1}{x^2}$, because the constant $1$ has zero derivative. For $x < 0$ we again have $G'(x) = \dfrac{1}{x^2}$, because the constant $-2$ also has zero derivative. Hence $G'(x) = \dfrac{1}{x^2}$ at every point of $\mathbb{R} \setminus \{0\}$, and $G$ is an **antiderivative** of $\dfrac{1}{x^2}$ on its whole domain. The difference $G(x) + \dfrac{1}{x}$ equals $1$ for $x > 0$ and $-2$ for $x < 0$. The theorem on antiderivatives of the same function guarantees a constant difference only on an interval, and $\mathbb{R} \setminus \{0\}$ is the union of two disjoint intervals, therefore the constant can be different on the two half-lines.
