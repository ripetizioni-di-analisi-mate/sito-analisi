Let $F(x) = \displaystyle\int_x^{3} \sqrt{1 + t^3}\,dt$ for $x > -1$. What is $F'(x)$?

- $\sqrt{1 + x^3}$
- $\dfrac{3x^2}{2\sqrt{1 + x^3}}$
- $\sqrt{28} - \sqrt{1 + x^3}$
- $-\sqrt{1 + x^3}$

## Explanation

The variable $x$ appears in the lower limit, so we swap the limits and change the sign:
$$
F(x) = -\int_3^{x} \sqrt{1 + t^3}\,dt .
$$
The function $\sqrt{1 + t^3}$ is continuous for $t > -1$, therefore the **fundamental theorem of calculus** applies to the accumulation function $\displaystyle\int_3^{x} \sqrt{1 + t^3}\,dt$, whose derivative is $\sqrt{1 + x^3}$. Hence $F'(x) = -\sqrt{1 + x^3}$. The function $\dfrac{3x^2}{2\sqrt{1 + x^3}}$ is the derivative of the integrand, not that of $F$.
