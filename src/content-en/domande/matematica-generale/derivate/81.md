The figure shows the graph of the derivative $f'$ of a function $f$ differentiable on $\mathbb{R}$, with $f'(x) = x^{2} - x - 2$. At which point does $f$ have a local maximum?

::grafico{f="x^2-x-2" punti="-1,0,pieno;2,0,pieno" xmin=-3 xmax=4 ymin=-3 ymax=5 legenda="y = f'(x)" titolo="The graph of the derivative $f'(x) = x^2 - x - 2$, which vanishes at $-1$ and at $2$"}

- $x = 2$
- $x = \dfrac{1}{2}$
- $x = 0$
- $x = -1$

## Explanation

One has $f'(x) = (x + 1)(x - 2)$, positive for $x < -1$ and for $x > 2$, negative on $(-1, 2)$, as the graph lying above and below the $x$-axis shows. Hence $f$ is increasing on $(-\infty, -1]$ and decreasing on $[-1, 2]$. Therefore $f$ has a **local maximum** at $-1$. At $2$ the derivative changes from negative to positive, so $2$ is a local minimum point. At $\dfrac{1}{2}$ it is the derivative $f'$ that is smallest, not the function $f$.
