For the logistic function $f(x) = \dfrac{1}{1 + e^{-x}}$, which identity holds for every real $x$?

- $f'(x) = f(x)^2$
- $f'(x) = 1 - f(x)$
- $f'(x) = f(x)\,\big( 1 + f(x) \big)$
- $f'(x) = f(x)\,\big( 1 - f(x) \big)$

## Explanation

By the chain rule the derivative of $e^{-x}$ is $-e^{-x}$; therefore, differentiating the reciprocal,
$$
f'(x) = -\frac{-e^{-x}}{\left( 1 + e^{-x} \right)^2} = \frac{e^{-x}}{\left( 1 + e^{-x} \right)^2} .
$$
Moreover $1 - f(x) = \dfrac{1 + e^{-x} - 1}{1 + e^{-x}} = \dfrac{e^{-x}}{1 + e^{-x}}$.

Hence $f(x)\,\big( 1 - f(x) \big) = \dfrac{1}{1 + e^{-x}} \cdot \dfrac{e^{-x}}{1 + e^{-x}} = f'(x)$ for every $x$. The other identities are false. At $x = 0$ one has $f(0) = \dfrac{1}{2}$ and $f'(0) = \dfrac{1}{4}$, while $1 - f(0) = \dfrac{1}{2}$ and $f(0)\,\big( 1 + f(0) \big) = \dfrac{3}{4}$. The identity $f'(x) = f(x)^2$ holds at $x = 0$, but for $x = \ln 3$ one has $f(\ln 3) = \dfrac{3}{4}$, hence $f'(\ln 3) = \dfrac{3}{4} \cdot \dfrac{1}{4} = \dfrac{3}{16}$, while $f(\ln 3)^2 = \dfrac{9}{16}$.
