The function $f(x) = \ln x$ on the interval $[1, e]$ has its absolute minimum at $x = 1$, where $f'(1) = 1 \neq 0$. Why is this fact consistent with Fermat's theorem?

- Because $f$ is not differentiable at $x = 1$
- Because $x = 1$ is not an interior point of the interval
- Because Fermat's theorem concerns only maximum points
- Because Fermat's theorem requires $f$ to be defined on all of $\mathbb{R}$

## Explanation

Fermat's theorem assumes that $x_0$ is an *interior* point of the interval, a point of local maximum or minimum, and that $f$ is differentiable at $x_0$; the conclusion is $f'(x_0) = 0$. The function $\ln x$ is differentiable at $1$, with $f'(1) = 1$, and the theorem applies equally to maxima and minima. The point $x = 1$, however, is the left endpoint of $[1, e]$, so the hypothesis of an interior point is not satisfied and the theorem does not apply. Indeed $\ln x$ is increasing, therefore on $[1, e]$ the minimum is at the left endpoint and the maximum at the right endpoint, and the derivative vanishes at neither of them.
