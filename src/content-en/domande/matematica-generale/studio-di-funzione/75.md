A function $f$ differentiable on $\mathbb{R}$ has $f'(x) > 0$ for $x < 2$, $f'(x) < 0$ for $x > 2$, and $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to -\infty} f(x) = \lim_{x \to +\infty} f(x) = 0$. How many solutions does the equation $f(x) = 0$ have?

- One
- Two
- None
- The data do not determine it

## Explanation

Let $x < 2$. Since $f$ is increasing on $(-\infty, 2]$, for every $t < x$ we have $f(t) < f(x)$. Letting $t$ tend to $-\infty$ gives $0 \leq f(x)$, and equality is excluded because, taking $t < x$, we also have $f(x) > f(t) \geq 0$. So $f(x) > 0$ for $x < 2$. The same reasoning with the decrease on $[2, +\infty)$ and the limit as $x \to +\infty$ gives $f(x) > 0$ for $x > 2$, and $f(2)$ is the maximum, which is positive too. Therefore $f$ is positive on all of $\mathbb{R}$, and the equation $f(x) = 0$ **has no solutions**; the $x$-axis is a horizontal asymptote on the right and on the left.
