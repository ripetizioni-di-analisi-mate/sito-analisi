For the function $f(x) = \dfrac{\ln x}{x^2}$, defined for $x > 0$, what is the absolute maximum?

- $\dfrac{1}{2\sqrt{e}}$
- $\dfrac{1}{2e}$
- $\dfrac{1}{e}$
- $\dfrac{1}{e^2}$

## Explanation

By the quotient rule
$$
f'(x) = \frac{\frac{1}{x} \cdot x^2 - 2x \ln x}{x^4} = \frac{1 - 2\ln x}{x^3} .
$$
Since $x^3 > 0$ in the domain, the sign of $f'$ is that of $1 - 2\ln x$, which is positive for $\ln x < \dfrac{1}{2}$, that is, for $x < \sqrt{e}$, and negative for $x > \sqrt{e}$. Hence $f$ increases on $(0, \sqrt{e}]$ and decreases on $[\sqrt{e}, +\infty)$. By the theorem on functions with a single stationary point, $x = \sqrt{e}$ is therefore the **absolute maximum** point. The maximum equals
$$
f(\sqrt{e}) = \frac{\ln \sqrt{e}}{e} = \frac{1}{2e} .
$$
