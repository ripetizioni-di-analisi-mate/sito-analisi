The function $f(x) = \dfrac{x^2 + x}{x^2 + 1}$ has the horizontal asymptote $y = 1$ as $x \to \pm\infty$. At which point does the graph of $f$ cross its horizontal asymptote?

- At the point $(1, 1)$
- At the point $(0, 1)$
- At the point $(-1, 1)$
- The graph does not cross its horizontal asymptote

## Explanation

The numerator and the denominator have the same degree and leading coefficients equal to $1$, so $f(x) \to 1$ as $x \to \pm\infty$. The intersections with the line $y = 1$ are the solutions of the equation
$$
\frac{x^2 + x}{x^2 + 1} = 1 \iff x^2 + x = x^2 + 1 \iff x = 1 ,
$$
where we used $x^2 + 1 > 0$. Therefore the graph **crosses the asymptote** at the point $(1, 1)$. A horizontal asymptote describes the function only as $x \to \pm\infty$, so the graph can cross it at finite points. The points $(0, 1)$ and $(-1, 1)$ do not lie on the graph, because $f(0) = f(-1) = 0$.
