Two astronauts, of masses $80\,\mathrm{kg}$ and $60\,\mathrm{kg}$, move together at $0.50\,\mathrm{m/s}$ relative to a space station, far from any celestial body. At some instant they push each other along the direction of motion, and right after the $80\,\mathrm{kg}$ astronaut moves at $0.20\,\mathrm{m/s}$ in the same direction as before. Neglecting external forces, what is the velocity of the center of mass of the two astronauts after the push?

- $0$
- $0.20\,\mathrm{m/s}$
- $0.50\,\mathrm{m/s}$
- $0.90\,\mathrm{m/s}$

## Explanation

The forces of the push are internal to the system of the two astronauts and the external forces are neglected, so the system is isolated. By **conservation of momentum** the total momentum $\vec{P}$ stays constant. Since $\vec{P} = M\,\vec{v}_{cm}$ with constant total mass $M$, the velocity of the center of mass also stays constant and is still $0.50\,\mathrm{m/s}$. The $60\,\mathrm{kg}$ astronaut instead moves at
$$
v = \frac{140 \cdot 0.50 - 80 \cdot 0.20}{60}\,\mathrm{m/s} = 0.90\,\mathrm{m/s} ,
$$
and indeed $\dfrac{80 \cdot 0.20 + 60 \cdot 0.90}{140}\,\mathrm{m/s} = 0.50\,\mathrm{m/s}$.
