A forced oscillator with natural angular frequency $\omega_0 = 50\,\mathrm{rad/s}$ and damping constant $\gamma = 0{,}50\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$ has, at resonance, amplitude $4{,}0\,\mathrm{cm}$. With the same driving force the damping is reduced to $\gamma = 0{,}25\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$. What is, to a good approximation, the new resonance amplitude?

- $2{,}0\,\mathrm{cm}$
- $16\,\mathrm{cm}$
- $8{,}0\,\mathrm{cm}$
- $4{,}0\,\mathrm{cm}$

## Explanation

With small damping the resonance amplitude is $A_{\max} \approx \dfrac{F_0}{2m\gamma\omega_0}$, so for fixed $F_0$, $m$ and $\omega_0$ it is inversely proportional to $\gamma$. Halving $\gamma$ doubles the amplitude, which becomes $8{,}0\,\mathrm{cm}$. The exact formula $A_{\max} = \dfrac{F_0}{2m\gamma\sqrt{\omega_0^2 - \gamma^2}}$ gives the same value within $0{,}01\%$, because $\gamma$ is much smaller than $\omega_0$. The value $2{,}0\,\mathrm{cm}$ is obtained by assuming the amplitude proportional to $\gamma$, and the value $16\,\mathrm{cm}$ by assuming it inversely proportional to $\gamma^2$.
