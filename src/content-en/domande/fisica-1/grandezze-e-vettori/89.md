A particle moves so that at every instant its acceleration $\vec{a}$ is parallel to the position vector $\vec{r}$, as happens under the action of a central force. Which statement about the vector $\vec{h} = \vec{r} \times \vec{v}$ is true?

- $\vec{h}$ is constant
- $\vec{h}$ is zero at every instant
- $\vec{h}$ has constant direction and varying magnitude
- $\dfrac{d\vec{h}}{dt} = \vec{v} \times \vec{a}$, in general nonzero

## Explanation

By the differentiation rule for the cross product
$$
\frac{d\vec{h}}{dt} = \frac{d\vec{r}}{dt} \times \vec{v} + \vec{r} \times \frac{d\vec{v}}{dt} = \vec{v} \times \vec{v} + \vec{r} \times \vec{a} .
$$
The first term is zero because the cross product of a vector with itself is the zero vector, and the second is zero because $\vec{a}$ is parallel to $\vec{r}$. Hence $\dfrac{d\vec{h}}{dt} = \vec{0}$ at every instant, and $\vec{h}$ is **constant** in magnitude and direction. The vector $\vec{h}$ is zero only if $\vec{v}$ is parallel to $\vec{r}$; in uniform circular motion, for example, the acceleration points toward the center and is therefore parallel to $\vec{r}$, while $\vec{v}$ is perpendicular to $\vec{r}$ and $\vec{h}$ is nonzero. The expression $\vec{v} \times \vec{a}$ is obtained by differentiating both factors in the same term instead of applying the product rule.
