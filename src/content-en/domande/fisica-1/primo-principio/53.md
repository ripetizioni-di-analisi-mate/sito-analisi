A solid of mass $0.50\,\mathrm{kg}$, at low temperature, has specific heat $c(T) = k\,T$ with $k = 2.0\,\mathrm{J/(kg\cdot K^2)}$. How much heat is needed to warm it from $100\,\mathrm{K}$ to $300\,\mathrm{K}$?

- $40\,\mathrm{kJ}$
- $20\,\mathrm{kJ}$
- $60\,\mathrm{kJ}$
- $80\,\mathrm{kJ}$

## Explanation

The specific heat varies with temperature, so the law of calorimetry holds for an infinitesimal interval, $\delta Q = m\,c(T)\,dT$, and the total heat is an integral:
$$
Q = m \int_{100}^{300} k\,T\,dT = m\,k\,\frac{300^2 - 100^2}{2}\,\mathrm{J} = 0.50 \cdot 2.0 \cdot \frac{8.0 \cdot 10^4}{2}\,\mathrm{J} = 4.0 \cdot 10^4\,\mathrm{J} .
$$
Therefore $40\,\mathrm{kJ}$ are needed. Treating the initial or the final specific heat as constant gives $20\,\mathrm{kJ}$ and $60\,\mathrm{kJ}$, and forgetting the factor $\dfrac{1}{2}$ of the integral gives $80\,\mathrm{kJ}$.
