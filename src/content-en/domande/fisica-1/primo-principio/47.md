A bimetallic strip is made of a brass strip ($\alpha = 1.9 \cdot 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{K^{-1}}$) and a steel strip ($\alpha = 1.2 \cdot 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{K^{-1}}$) welded together along their whole length, and it is straight at $20\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$. What happens when it is heated to $100\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$?

- It bends, with the steel on the outer side of the curve
- It stays straight and lengthens as much as the brass alone would
- It bends, with the brass on the outer side of the curve
- It stays straight, because the two strips are welded together

## Explanation

By the law of linear expansion $\Delta L = \alpha\,L_0\,\Delta T$, for the same initial length and the same heating the strip with the larger coefficient, that is the brass, lengthens more. The two strips are welded along their whole length, so they cannot slide over each other and the strip bends. In an arc formed by two welded strips the longer one lies on the outer side, hence the brass is on the **outer** side of the curve. Bimetallic-strip thermostats are based on this fact.
