A constant tangential force of magnitude $F$ is applied to the rim of a uniform disk of mass $M$ and radius $R$, free to rotate without friction about its own fixed axis. The disk is replaced by a uniform disk of the same mass and radius $2R$, and the same tangential force is applied to its rim. How does the angular acceleration change?

- It doubles
- It halves
- It stays the same
- It becomes one quarter

## Explanation

The torque of the force is $F R$ and the moment of inertia is $\dfrac{1}{2} M R^2$, so by the equation of rotational motion
$$
\alpha = \frac{F R}{\frac{1}{2} M R^2} = \frac{2F}{M R} .
$$
With radius $2R$ the torque of the force doubles and the moment of inertia becomes four times as large. Therefore the angular acceleration **halves**. The value reduced to one quarter is obtained by taking the moment of inertia into account and leaving the torque of the force unchanged.
