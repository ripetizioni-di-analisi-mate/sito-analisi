A block slides on a rough horizontal surface, with a constant coefficient of kinetic friction, until it stops. How does its mechanical energy vary as a function of the distance traveled?

- It decreases linearly with the distance traveled
- It decreases in proportion to the square of the distance traveled
- It stays constant, because the surface is horizontal
- It increases, because friction heats the block

## Explanation

On the horizontal surface the normal force is $mg$, so friction has constant magnitude $\mu_d\, mg$ and always opposes the displacement. After a distance $s$ its work is $-\mu_d\, mg\, s$.

By the theorem on the **work of nonconservative forces** the change in mechanical energy equals this work, so $E(s) = E_0 - \mu_d\, mg\, s$, which **decreases linearly** with the distance traveled. The mechanical energy lost becomes internal energy of the block and the surface, which warm up.
