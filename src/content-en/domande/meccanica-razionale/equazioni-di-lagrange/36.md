On a system with one degree of freedom, with Lagrangian coordinate $\vartheta$, the Rayleigh dissipation function is $\mathcal{D} = \dfrac{1}{2}\,h\,\ell^2\,\dot{\vartheta}^2$. What is the Lagrangian component $Q_\vartheta^{*}$ of the viscous resistance?

- $h\,\ell^2\,\dot{\vartheta}$
- $-\dfrac{1}{2}\,h\,\ell^2\,\dot{\vartheta}$
- $-h\,\ell^2\,\dot{\vartheta}$
- $-h\,\ell^2\,\ddot{\vartheta}$

## Explanation

The viscous resistances $-h_i\,\vec{v}_i$ have Lagrangian components that are obtained by differentiating the dissipation function with respect to the generalized velocities, with the negative sign:
$$
Q_h^{*} = -\frac{\partial \mathcal{D}}{\partial \dot{q}_h} .
$$
Here $\mathcal{D}$ is a quadratic form in $\dot{\vartheta}$, therefore the derivative is $h\,\ell^2\,\dot{\vartheta}$ and the Lagrangian component is $Q_\vartheta^{*} = -h\,\ell^2\,\dot{\vartheta}$. The factor $\dfrac{1}{2}$ disappears in the differentiation of the square, as happens in the passage from the kinetic energy to the kinetic momentum. The negative sign says that the load opposes the sense of the generalized velocity, hence it dissipates energy.
