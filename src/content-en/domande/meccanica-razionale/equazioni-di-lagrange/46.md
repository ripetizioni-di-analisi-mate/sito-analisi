The material points $P_1$ and $P_2$ are connected by a rigid rod of negligible mass and length $\ell$ and move freely in the plane. The rod exerts the reaction $\vec{\Phi}_1$ on $P_1$ and the reaction $\vec{\Phi}_2$ on $P_2$. Why is the sum $\vec{\Phi}_1 \cdot \delta P_1 + \vec{\Phi}_2 \cdot \delta P_2$ zero in every virtual displacement?

- Each reaction is orthogonal to the virtual displacement of its own point of application
- The two reactions are zero, because the rod has negligible mass
- The two reactions are opposite and directed along the rod, and $\delta P_1 - \delta P_2$ is orthogonal to the rod
- The virtual displacements of the two points are equal

## Explanation

The rod has negligible mass, therefore the forces it receives from the two points have zero resultant and zero moment; it follows that the reactions it exerts are opposite, $\vec{\Phi}_2 = -\vec{\Phi}_1$, and directed along the line $P_1 P_2$. The sum of the virtual works can then be written as $\vec{\Phi}_1 \cdot (\delta P_1 - \delta P_2)$. Differentiating the rigidity constraint $|P_1 - P_2|^2 = \ell^2$ gives
$$
(P_1 - P_2) \cdot (\delta P_1 - \delta P_2) = 0 ,
$$
hence $\delta P_1 - \delta P_2$ is orthogonal to the rod and its scalar product with $\vec{\Phi}_1$ is zero. Each reaction taken alone, instead, does nonzero virtual work in a translation along the rod, in which $\delta P_1 = \delta P_2$ is parallel to $\vec{\Phi}_1$. The constraint is therefore **ideal** even though neither reaction is orthogonal to the displacement of its own point.
