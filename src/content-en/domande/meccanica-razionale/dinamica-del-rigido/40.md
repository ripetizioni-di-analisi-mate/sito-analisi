A rigid body has the point $O$ fixed, held by an ideal constraint. Why do the Euler equations not contain the constraint reaction at $O$?

- Because the reaction is applied at $O$, hence its moment with respect to $O$ is zero
- Because the constraint is ideal, hence the reaction is zero
- Because the sum of the moments of the constraint reactions is always zero
- Because the reaction at $O$ is parallel to $\vec{\omega}$

## Explanation

The Euler equations are the three components, on the principal body-fixed frame, of the second cardinal equation with pole at the fixed point $O$. The moment of a force $\vec{\Phi}$ applied at $O$ with respect to the pole $O$ equals $(O - O) \wedge \vec{\Phi} = \vec{0}$, therefore the constraint reaction does not appear in $\vec{M}_O^{(e)}$, where only the active forces remain. The reaction at $O$ is not zero, and it is obtained, once the motion has been integrated, from the first cardinal equation $\vec{\Phi}_O + \vec{R}^{(a)} = M\,\vec{a}_G$. An ideal constraint is a constraint whose reactions do zero virtual work, a condition that does not force the reaction to vanish.
