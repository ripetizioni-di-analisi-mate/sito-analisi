A cat is dropped from rest with its back facing downward, and at the beginning its angular momentum about its center of mass $G$ is zero. Air resistance is negligible and the cat, which is not a rigid body, moves its legs, tail and torso during the fall. Can it land on its feet, that is, rotated by half a turn with respect to the initial orientation?

- No, because $\vec{K}_G$ stays zero and therefore the cat cannot change its orientation
- Yes, because $\vec{K}_G$ stays zero, but a body that changes its shape can change its orientation
- Yes, because the muscular forces have a nonzero resultant moment about $G$
- Yes, because the weight has a nonzero resultant moment about $G$

## Explanation

The only external force is the weight, and the center of the weights of the single elements coincides with the center of mass, hence the moment of the external forces about $G$ vanishes. The muscular forces are internal forces and have zero resultant moment. The second cardinal equation with pole $G$ therefore gives $\dot{\vec{K}}_G = \vec{0}$, and $\vec{K}_G$ stays zero during the whole fall. For a rigid body $\vec{K}_G = \mathbb{I}_G\,\vec{\omega}$, hence $\vec{K}_G = \vec{0}$ would imply $\vec{\omega} = \vec{0}$ and a constant orientation. The cat instead changes its shape, for instance by rotating the front and the rear part of its body in opposite directions with the legs now tucked in and now stretched out, and in this way it rotates by half a turn while $\vec{K}_G$ stays zero.
