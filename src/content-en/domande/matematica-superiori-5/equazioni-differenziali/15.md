The coefficient and the right-hand side of the linear equation $y' + \dfrac{y}{x - 3} = \dfrac{1}{x + 1}$ are not defined on all of $\mathbb{R}$. On which interval is the maximal solution satisfying $y(0) = 1$ defined?

- $(-1, 3) \cup (3, +\infty)$
- $(3, +\infty)$
- $(-1, 3)$
- $[-1, 3)$

## Explanation

The coefficients $a(x) = \dfrac{1}{x - 3}$ and $b(x) = \dfrac{1}{x + 1}$ are continuous on the intervals $(-\infty, -1)$, $(-1, 3)$ and $(3, +\infty)$. The solutions of a linear equation are defined on the whole interval of continuity of the coefficients that contains the initial point; since $0 \in (-1, 3)$, the solution is defined on $(-1, 3)$.

On this interval an antiderivative of $a$ is $\ln(3 - x)$, so the integrating factor is $3 - x$ and we get
$$
y = \frac{4\ln(x + 1) - x + 3}{3 - x} .
$$
As $x \to 3^-$ the numerator tends to $4\ln 4 \neq 0$ and as $x \to -1^+$ it tends to $-\infty$, so the solution cannot be extended beyond the endpoints and the maximal interval is $(-1, 3)$.
