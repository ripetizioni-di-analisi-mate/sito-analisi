Let $f(x)=x+\dfrac{1}{x}$, with $x\ne0$. Then $f(-0.1)=-10.1$ and $f(0.1)=10.1$. Why does the segment joining the points of the graph with abscissas $-0.1$ and $0.1$ not represent the behaviour of $f$ between these two abscissas?

- The two points lie on two branches of the graph separated by a vertical asymptote.
- The function is defined at $0$ from the right only.
- The origin is a local minimum point of $f$.
- Between the two abscissas the graph of $f$ coincides with the $x$-axis.

## Explanation

The function is not defined at $x=0$, and the limits as $x\to0^-$ and as $x\to0^+$ are $-\infty$ and $+\infty$. Hence the line $x=0$ is a vertical asymptote and splits the graph into two branches. The segment passes through the origin, which is the midpoint of its endpoints, while the graph of $f$ has no points on the line $x=0$. Therefore the segment joins two separate branches and suggests a zero that the function does not have.
