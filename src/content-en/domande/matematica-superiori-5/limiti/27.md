A function $f$ is defined in a punctured neighbourhood of $0$, and all that is known is that $\displaystyle\lim_{n\to+\infty} f\left(\dfrac{1}{n}\right)=1$ and $\displaystyle\lim_{n\to+\infty} f\left(-\dfrac{1}{n}\right)=1$. Which conclusion about the limit of $f(x)$ as $x\to0$ is justified by this information?

- The limit may fail to exist, because the information concerns only two sequences.
- The two one-sided limits are different.
- The limit exists and equals $0$.
- The limit exists and equals $1$.

## Explanation

Consider the function equal to $1$ at the points $\dfrac{1}{n}$ and $-\dfrac{1}{n}$ and equal to $0$ at every other point. This function satisfies both conditions, but in every neighbourhood of $0$ it takes both the value $1$ and the value $0$. Therefore for this function the limit as $x\to0$ does not exist. The two sequences are thus not enough to decide whether the limit exists. If instead the limit exists, the two conditions imply that its value is $1$.
