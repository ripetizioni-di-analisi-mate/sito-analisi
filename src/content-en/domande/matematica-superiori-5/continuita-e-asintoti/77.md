For the function $f(x)=\dfrac{x^2}{x+1}$ we have computed $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to+\infty}\dfrac{f(x)}{x}=1$. What further computation is needed to find the right oblique asymptote, and with what result?

- The limit of $f(x)$ as $x\to-1$, which gives the vertical asymptote $x=-1$
- The value $f(0)=0$, which gives the intercept $q=0$
- The limit of $\dfrac{f(x)}{x^2}$ as $x\to+\infty$, which equals $0$
- The limit of $f(x)-x$ as $x\to+\infty$, which equals $-1$

## Explanation

To compute an oblique asymptote, the line $y=mx+q$ is obtained from two limits, $m=\displaystyle\lim_{x\to+\infty}\dfrac{f(x)}{x}$ and $q=\displaystyle\lim_{x\to+\infty}\big(f(x)-mx\big)$. The first limit gives $m=1$, so the limit of $f(x)-x$ remains to be computed. Since $f(x)-x=\dfrac{x^2-x^2-x}{x+1}=-\dfrac{x}{x+1}$, this limit equals $-1$. Therefore $q=-1$ and the right oblique asymptote is the line $y=x-1$. The value $f(0)$ is the height of the graph at $x=0$, which in general differs from the intercept of the asymptote.
