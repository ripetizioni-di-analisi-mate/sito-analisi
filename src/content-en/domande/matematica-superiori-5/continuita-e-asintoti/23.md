The function $f$ equals $\sin \dfrac{1}{x}$ for $x \neq 0$ and $0$ for $x = 0$. Which statement about the behavior of $f$ at $x = 0$ is true?

- The limit as $x \to 0$ equals $1$, so the discontinuity is removable
- The right-hand and left-hand limits equal $1$ and $-1$, so at $x = 0$ there is a jump of size $2$
- The limit as $x \to 0$ equals $0$, so $f$ is continuous at $x = 0$
- The right-hand limit does not exist, so at $x = 0$ there is a discontinuity of the second kind

## Explanation

At the points $x_k = \dfrac{1}{\dfrac{\pi}{2} + 2k\pi}$, with $k$ a positive integer, we have $f(x_k) = 1$, and at the points $y_k = \dfrac{1}{\dfrac{3\pi}{2} + 2k\pi}$ we have $f(y_k) = -1$. Both sequences of points tend to $0$ from the right, so in every right neighborhood of $0$ the function takes both the value $1$ and the value $-1$. Therefore the right-hand limit does not exist, and in the same way the left-hand limit does not exist, because $f$ is odd. One of the right-hand and left-hand limits does not exist, so the discontinuity is **of the second kind**.
