Let $f(x) = x^2 \sin \dfrac{1}{x}$ for $x \neq 0$ and $f(0) = 0$. Which statement about the point $x = 0$ is true?

- $f$ is not differentiable at $0$, because $f'(x)$ has no limit as $x \to 0$
- $f$ is not continuous at $0$
- $f$ is differentiable at $0$ and $f'(0) = 1$
- $f$ is differentiable at $0$ and $f'(0) = 0$

## Explanation

The difference quotient at $0$ is $\dfrac{f(h)}{h} = h \sin \dfrac{1}{h}$. Since $\left| h \sin \dfrac{1}{h} \right| \leq |h|$, by the squeeze theorem the quotient tends to $0$, so $f$ is differentiable at $0$ and $f'(0) = 0$. For $x \neq 0$ we have $f'(x) = 2x \sin \dfrac{1}{x} - \cos \dfrac{1}{x}$. This function has no limit as $x \to 0$, because $\cos \dfrac{1}{x}$ takes the values $1$ and $-1$ at points arbitrarily close to $0$. The **limit of the derivative** criterion therefore gives no information, and differentiability is established with the difference quotient.
