For $f(x)=|x|$ the quotient $\dfrac{f(h)-f(-h)}{2h}$ equals $0$ for every $h>0$. Why can we not conclude from this that $f'(0)=0$?

- The right derivative at $0$ equals $1$ and the left one equals $-1$, so $f'(0)$ does not exist
- The quotient $\dfrac{f(h)-f(-h)}{2h}$ is undefined for $h>0$
- The function $|x|$ is discontinuous at $0$
- The quotient $\dfrac{f(h)-f(-h)}{2h}$ depends on the value of $h$

## Explanation

Since $|h|=|-h|=h$, the numerator $f(h)-f(-h)$ is zero and the quotient equals $0$ for every $h>0$. The derivative at $0$ is instead the limit of the difference quotient $\dfrac{f(h)-f(0)}{h}=\dfrac{|h|}{h}$. This quotient equals $1$ for $h>0$ and $-1$ for $h<0$, so the right derivative is $1$ and the left derivative is $-1$. Since the two derivatives are different, $f$ is not differentiable at $0$, even though the symmetric quotient tends to $0$.
