A copper wire is heated and its resistance rises from $8.0\,\Omega$ to $10\,\Omega$. Changes in length and cross-section due to thermal expansion are neglected. How does the resistivity of the copper change?

- It increases by $25\%$.
- It increases by $20\%$.
- It decreases by $20\%$.
- It stays constant.

## Explanation

The resistance of a uniform wire is $R=\rho\dfrac{L}{A}$. Since length and cross-section do not change, the ratio $\dfrac{L}{A}$ is the same before and after heating, so the resistivity changes in the same proportion as the resistance. The ratio of the final to the initial value is $\dfrac{\rho_f}{\rho_i}=\dfrac{10}{8.0}=1.25$. Therefore the resistivity **increases by $25\%$**. The value $20\%$ comes from comparing the $2.0\,\Omega$ change with the final value instead of the initial one, while a $20\%$ decrease is that of the current at fixed voltage.
