Two plane pulses cross in vacuum with $\vec E_1=f(x-ct)\hat y$ and $\vec E_2=-f(x+ct)\hat y$. At full overlap their total electric field vanishes everywhere. Where is the energy they carry at that instant?

- The energy is all in the magnetic field, which equals $\dfrac{f(x)}{c}\,\hat{k}$
- The energy is split equally between the electric field and the magnetic field
- The energy is all in the magnetic field, which equals $\dfrac{2f(x)}{c}\,\hat{k}$
- The energy is zero, and it reappears when the pulses separate

## Explanation

For a wave propagating toward increasing $x$ the magnetic field is $\dfrac{E}{c}\,\hat{k}$, and for a wave propagating toward decreasing $x$ it is $-\dfrac{E}{c}\,\hat{k}$.
Hence $\vec{B}_1 = \dfrac{f(x - ct)}{c}\,\hat{k}$ and $\vec{B}_2 = -\dfrac{-f(x + ct)}{c}\,\hat{k} = \dfrac{f(x + ct)}{c}\,\hat{k}$, and at $t = 0$ the two magnetic fields add up to $\dfrac{2f(x)}{c}\,\hat{k}$.
Therefore at that instant the energy is **all in the magnetic field**, with density $\dfrac{B^2}{2\mu_0} = \dfrac{2 f^2}{\mu_0 c^2} = 2\varepsilon_0 f^2$.
This is the sum of the energy densities $\varepsilon_0 f^2$ of the two separate pulses, so energy is conserved.
