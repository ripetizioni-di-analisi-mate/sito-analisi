An electric heater warms $0.25\,\mathrm{kg}$ of a material, which stays in the same phase, with negligible heat losses. When the material is at $25\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$ the heater has supplied a total of $1200\,\mathrm J$ since it was switched on; when it is at $37\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$ it has supplied a total of $3600\,\mathrm J$. What is the mean specific heat capacity of the material over this temperature interval?

- $200\,\mathrm{J/(kg\,K)}$
- $50\,\mathrm{J/(kg\,K)}$
- $1200\,\mathrm{J/(kg\,K)}$
- $800\,\mathrm{J/(kg\,K)}$

## Explanation

Between $25\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$ and $37\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$ the material absorbs the heat $Q=(3600-1200)\,\mathrm J=2400\,\mathrm J$, while its temperature rises by $\Delta T=12\,\mathrm K$. The specific heat capacity is the heat absorbed divided by the mass and by the temperature change, so
$$
c=\frac{Q}{m\,\Delta T}=\frac{2400}{0.25\cdot12}\,\mathrm{J/(kg\,K)}=800\,\mathrm{J/(kg\,K)}.
$$
The energy of $3600\,\mathrm J$ also includes the heat supplied before the material reached $25\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$, therefore it does not correspond to the $12\,\mathrm K$ rise.
