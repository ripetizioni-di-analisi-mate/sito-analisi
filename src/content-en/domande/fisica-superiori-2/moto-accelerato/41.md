Two stopping tests with the same car, on the same road surface and with the same driver, give total distances of $15\,\mathrm m$ starting from $10\,\mathrm{m/s}$ and $50\,\mathrm m$ starting from $20\,\mathrm{m/s}$. In both tests the reaction time $t_r$ and the deceleration $b$ are the same. What is $t_r$?

- $0.50\,\mathrm s$
- $0.10\,\mathrm s$
- $1.50\,\mathrm s$
- $2.50\,\mathrm s$

## Explanation

The total stopping distance is $d=v_0t_r+\dfrac{v_0^2}{2b}$. Dividing by $v_0$ gives $\dfrac{d}{v_0}=t_r+\dfrac{v_0}{2b}$, which grows linearly with $v_0$. For the two tests the ratio is $\dfrac{15}{10}\,\mathrm s=1.5\,\mathrm s$ and $\dfrac{50}{20}\,\mathrm s=2.5\,\mathrm s$. Since the speed increases by $10\,\mathrm{m/s}$ and the ratio by $1\,\mathrm s$, we have $\dfrac{1}{2b}=0.10\,\mathrm{s^2/m}$, that is $b=5\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$. From the first test it then follows that $t_r=1.5\,\mathrm s-0.10\cdot10\,\mathrm s=0.50\,\mathrm s$. The values $1.50\,\mathrm s$ and $2.50\,\mathrm s$ are obtained by attributing the whole distance to the reaction phase.
