The function $f(x, y) = x + 2y$ on the square $K = [0, 1] \times [0, 1]$ has an absolute maximum at the point $(1, 1)$, where $\nabla f(1, 1) = (1, 2) \neq (0, 0)$. Why is this fact compatible with Fermat's theorem?

- Fermat's theorem concerns only minimum points
- The function has no partial derivatives at $(1, 1)$
- The point $(1, 1)$ is not interior to $K$
- The point $(1, 1)$ is an absolute maximum point but not a local maximum point

## Explanation

Since $x \leq 1$ and $y \leq 1$ on $K$, we have $f(x, y) \leq 3 = f(1, 1)$, so $(1, 1)$ is an absolute maximum point. **Fermat's theorem** requires the extremum point to be interior to the domain. The point $(1, 1)$ is a vertex of the square, therefore every disk centered at $(1, 1)$ contains points that do not belong to $K$, and this hypothesis is not satisfied. The partial derivatives of $f$ exist at every point, an absolute maximum is also a local maximum, and the theorem holds in the same way for maxima and minima, so the other three answers are false. For this reason the boundary points are among the candidates together with the critical points.
