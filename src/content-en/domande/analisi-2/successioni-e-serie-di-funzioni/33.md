For the sequence of functions $f_n(x) = \dfrac{\sin(n x)}{n^2}$ on $[0, \pi]$, which statement about the differentiation theorem for sequences is correct?

- The theorem does not apply, because $f_n'(0) = n \to +\infty$
- The theorem does not apply, because $f_n'$ converges only pointwise on $[0, \pi]$
- The theorem does not apply, because $f_n(x_0)$ does not converge at any point $x_0 \in [0, \pi]$
- The hypotheses are satisfied and the limit $f = 0$ satisfies $f' = \displaystyle\lim_{n \to +\infty} f_n' = 0$

## Explanation

The $f_n$ are of class $C^1$ and $f_n(0) = 0$ for every $n$, so the sequence $f_n(x_0)$ converges at the point $x_0 = 0$. By the chain rule we have $f_n'(x) = \dfrac{n \cos(n x)}{n^2} = \dfrac{\cos(n x)}{n}$, therefore $\displaystyle\sup_{[0, \pi]} |f_n'| = \dfrac{1}{n} \to 0$ and the derivatives converge uniformly to $0$. Hence *both* hypotheses of the **differentiation theorem for sequences** are satisfied. It follows that $f_n$ converges uniformly to a function $f$ of class $C^1$ with $f' = 0$; since $|f_n| \leq \dfrac{1}{n^2}$, the limit function is $f = 0$.
