Let $f_n(x) = \dfrac{\ln\left(1 + n^2 x^2\right)}{n}$ on $[-1, 1]$. Which statement is true?

- $f_n \to 0$ uniformly and $f_n' \to 0$ uniformly on $[-1, 1]$
- $f_n \to 0$ uniformly, $f_n' \to 0$ only pointwise, and the differentiation theorem for sequences does not apply
- $f_n$ does not converge uniformly to $0$, because $\ln\left(1 + n^2\right) \to +\infty$
- $f_n'$ does not converge pointwise at $x = 0$, because $f_n'\left(\dfrac{1}{n}\right) = 1$ for every $n$

## Explanation

The function $f_n$ is even and increasing in $|x|$, so $\sup_{[-1, 1]} |f_n| = f_n(1) = \dfrac{\ln\left(1 + n^2\right)}{n} \to 0$ and the convergence to $0$ is uniform. The derivative is $f_n'(x) = \dfrac{2 n x}{1 + n^2 x^2}$, which tends to $0$ for every fixed $x$, and in particular $f_n'(0) = 0$. At the point $x = \dfrac{1}{n}$ the derivative equals $1$, so $\sup_{[-1, 1]} |f_n'| \geq 1$ and the convergence of the derivatives is not uniform. The hypothesis on the derivatives in the **differentiation theorem for sequences** is not satisfied, even though in this case the derivative of the limit and the limit of the derivatives are both zero.
