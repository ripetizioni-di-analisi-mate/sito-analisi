As $\alpha \in \mathbb{R}$ varies, for which values does the series $\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{n^{\alpha}} \sin\left(\frac{x}{n}\right)$ converge normally on $[-1, 1]$?

- $\alpha > 1$
- $\alpha \geq 0$
- $\alpha > -1$
- $\alpha > 0$

## Explanation

On $[-1, 1]$ the argument $\dfrac{x}{n}$ lies in $\left[-\dfrac{1}{n}, \dfrac{1}{n}\right] \subset \left[-\dfrac{\pi}{2}, \dfrac{\pi}{2}\right]$, where the sine is increasing, so $\sup_{[-1, 1]} \left| \sin\left(\dfrac{x}{n}\right) \right| = \sin\dfrac{1}{n}$. Therefore $M_n = \dfrac{1}{n^{\alpha}} \sin\dfrac{1}{n}$, and since $\sin\dfrac{1}{n} \sim \dfrac{1}{n}$ we have $M_n \sim \dfrac{1}{n^{\alpha + 1}}$. By the **limit comparison test** the series $\sum M_n$ converges if and only if $\alpha + 1 > 1$, that is, $\alpha > 0$. Hence the series converges normally on $[-1, 1]$ exactly for $\alpha > 0$.
