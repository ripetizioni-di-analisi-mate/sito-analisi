On $[0, 1]$ consider $f_n(x) = x^{1 + \frac{1}{n}}$, which converges uniformly to $f(x) = x$. Which statement about the derivatives is true?

- $f_n'$ converges uniformly to $1$ on $[0, 1]$, so $f' = 1$
- $f_n'$ converges uniformly on $[0, 1]$ to a function that is discontinuous at $x = 0$
- $f_n'(0) \to 1$, in agreement with $f'(0) = 1$
- $f_n'$ does not converge uniformly on $[0, 1]$, and $f_n'(0) = 0$ for every $n$ while $f'(0) = 1$

## Explanation

The derivative is $f_n'(x) = \left(1 + \dfrac{1}{n}\right) x^{\frac{1}{n}}$. For $0 < x \leq 1$ we have $x^{\frac{1}{n}} \to 1$, so $f_n'(x) \to 1$, while $f_n'(0) = 0$ for every $n$. The limit of the derivatives is therefore discontinuous at $x = 0$. Since the $f_n'$ are continuous, by the theorem on the **continuity of the uniform limit** the convergence of the derivatives is not uniform on $[0, 1]$. Indeed, at $x = 0$ the limit of the derivatives is $0$, while the derivative of the limit $f(x) = x$ is $1$.
