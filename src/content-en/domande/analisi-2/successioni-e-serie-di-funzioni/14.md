For the function $f(x) = \displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{\sin(n x)}{n^4}$, which is of class $C^1$ on $\mathbb{R}$, what is $f'(x)$?

- $\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{\cos(n x)}{n^4}$
- $-\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{\cos(n x)}{n^3}$
- $\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{\cos(n x)}{n^5}$
- $\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{\cos(n x)}{n^3}$

## Explanation

Since $\left| \dfrac{\sin(n x)}{n^4} \right| \leq \dfrac{1}{n^4}$ and $\sum \dfrac{1}{n^4}$ converges, the series converges totally on $\mathbb{R}$, so in particular it converges at $x_0 = 0$. The terms $\dfrac{\sin(n x)}{n^4}$ are of class $C^1$ and their derivative is $\dfrac{n \cos(n x)}{n^4} = \dfrac{\cos(n x)}{n^3}$, by the chain rule. Since $\left| \dfrac{\cos(n x)}{n^3} \right| \leq \dfrac{1}{n^3}$ and $\sum \dfrac{1}{n^3}$ converges, the series of derivatives converges totally, hence uniformly, on every interval $[-a, a]$. Therefore the theorem on **term-by-term differentiation** applies on every interval $[-a, a]$. Since every $x \in \mathbb{R}$ belongs to an interval $[-a, a]$, for every $x \in \mathbb{R}$ we have
$$
f'(x) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{\cos(n x)}{n^3} .
$$
