For the sequence of functions $f_n(x) = \dfrac{\sin(n x)}{\sqrt{n}}$ on $\mathbb{R}$, which statement is correct?

- $f_n \to 0$ uniformly on $\mathbb{R}$ and $f_n'(0) = \sqrt{n} \to +\infty$
- $f_n \to 0$ uniformly on $\mathbb{R}$ and $f_n'(0) = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{n}} \to 0$
- $f_n \to 0$ only pointwise on $\mathbb{R}$ and $f_n'(0) = \sqrt{n} \to +\infty$
- $f_n \to 0$ uniformly on $\mathbb{R}$ and $f_n' \to 0$ uniformly on $\mathbb{R}$

## Explanation

Since $|\sin(n x)| \leq 1$, with equality at $x = \dfrac{\pi}{2n}$, we have $\sup_{\mathbb{R}} |f_n| = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{n}}$. This sequence tends to $0$, so by the **supremum criterion** $f_n \to 0$ uniformly on $\mathbb{R}$. By the chain rule we have $f_n'(x) = \dfrac{n \cos(n x)}{\sqrt{n}} = \sqrt{n} \cos(n x)$, therefore $f_n'(0) = \sqrt{n} \to +\infty$. The derivatives do not converge at $0$, so they do not converge uniformly on $\mathbb{R}$, and the **differentiation theorem for sequences** does not apply.
