For the sequence of functions $f_n(x) = \sqrt{x^2 + \dfrac{1}{n^2}}$ on $[-1, 1]$, which statement is correct?

- $f_n \to |x|$ uniformly on $[-1, 1]$, but $f_n'$ does not converge uniformly on $[-1, 1]$
- $f_n \to |x|$ uniformly on $[-1, 1]$ and $f_n'$ converges uniformly on $[-1, 1]$
- $f_n \to |x|$ only pointwise on $[-1, 1]$, because $|x|$ is not differentiable at $0$
- $f_n \to x$ uniformly on $[-1, 1]$ and $f_n' \to 1$ uniformly on $[-1, 1]$

## Explanation

For every $x \in [-1, 1]$ we have
$$
0 \leq f_n(x) - |x| = \frac{\frac{1}{n^2}}{\sqrt{x^2 + \frac{1}{n^2}} + |x|} \leq \frac{\frac{1}{n^2}}{\frac{1}{n}} = \frac{1}{n} ,
$$
so by the **sup criterion** $f_n \to |x|$ uniformly on $[-1, 1]$. The derivatives are $f_n'(x) = \dfrac{x}{\sqrt{x^2 + \frac{1}{n^2}}}$, which tend to $-1$ for $x < 0$, to $0$ at $x = 0$ and to $1$ for $x > 0$. This limit is discontinuous, while the $f_n'$ are continuous, therefore by the theorem on the continuity of the uniform limit the $f_n'$ **do not converge uniformly**. If the derivatives converged uniformly, the **differentiation theorem for sequences** would give a limit of class $C^1$, whereas $|x|$ is not differentiable at $0$.
