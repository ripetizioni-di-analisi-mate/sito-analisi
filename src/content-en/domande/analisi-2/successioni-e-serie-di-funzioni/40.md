For the sequence of functions $f_n(x) = \dfrac{\arctan(n x)}{n}$ on $\mathbb{R}$, which statement is correct?

- $f_n \to 0$ uniformly on $\mathbb{R}$, but $f_n'(0) = 1$ for every $n$, while the derivative of the limit at $0$ equals $0$
- $f_n \to 0$ uniformly on $\mathbb{R}$ and $f_n' \to 0$ uniformly on $\mathbb{R}$
- $f_n \to 0$ only pointwise on $\mathbb{R}$, because $f_n'(0) = 1$ for every $n$
- $f_n \to 0$ uniformly on $\mathbb{R}$ and $f_n'(0) = \dfrac{1}{n} \to 0$

## Explanation

Since $|\arctan t| < \dfrac{\pi}{2}$ for every $t \in \mathbb{R}$, we have $\displaystyle\sup_{\mathbb{R}} |f_n| \leq \dfrac{\pi}{2n} \to 0$, so by the **supremum criterion** $f_n \to 0$ uniformly on $\mathbb{R}$. By the chain rule we have
$$
f_n'(x) = \frac{1}{n} \cdot \frac{n}{1 + n^2 x^2} = \frac{1}{1 + n^2 x^2} ,
$$
therefore $f_n'(0) = 1$ for every $n$, while for $x \neq 0$ we have $f_n'(x) \to 0$. The limit of the derivatives therefore equals $1$ at $x = 0$ and $0$ for $x \neq 0$; since it is discontinuous and the $f_n'$ are continuous, by the theorem on the continuity of the uniform limit the $f_n'$ do not converge uniformly on $\mathbb{R}$. Therefore the hypothesis on the derivatives in the **differentiation theorem for sequences** is not satisfied, and indeed $\displaystyle\lim_{n \to +\infty} f_n'(0) = 1$ differs from the derivative at $0$ of the limit $f = 0$.
