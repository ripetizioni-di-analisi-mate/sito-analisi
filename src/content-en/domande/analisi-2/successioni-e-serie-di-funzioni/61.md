The figure shows the graphs of $f_n(x) = \dfrac{1}{n}\, e^{-\frac{x}{n}}$ on $[0, +\infty)$ for $n = 1, 2, 4$. We have $\displaystyle\sup_{x \geq 0} |f_n(x)| = \dfrac{1}{n}$, so $f_n \to 0$ uniformly on $[0, +\infty)$. What is $\displaystyle\lim_{n \to +\infty} \int_0^{+\infty} f_n(x)\, dx$?

::grafico{f="exp(-x);exp(-x/2)/2;exp(-x/4)/4" xmin=0 xmax=12 ymin=-0.05 ymax=1.1 legenda="n = 1;n = 2;n = 4" titolo="$y = \dfrac{1}{n}\,e^{-\frac{x}{n}}$ for $n = 1, 2, 4$"}

- $0$
- $1$
- $1 - \dfrac{1}{e}$
- $+\infty$

## Explanation

For every $n$ the improper integral equals
$$
\int_0^{+\infty} \frac{1}{n}\, e^{-\frac{x}{n}}\, dx = \lim_{b \to +\infty} \Bigl[ -e^{-\frac{x}{n}} \Bigr]_0^b = 1 ,
$$
so the limit of the integrals is $1$, while the integral of the limit $f = 0$ is $0$. The theorem on **passing to the limit under the integral sign** requires a closed and bounded interval $[a, b]$, and the half-line $[0, +\infty)$ does not satisfy this hypothesis. Therefore the theorem does not apply, and in this case the exchange of limit and integral fails despite the uniform convergence. As $n$ grows the graph of $f_n$ becomes lower and wider, and the area under the graph stays equal to $1$.
