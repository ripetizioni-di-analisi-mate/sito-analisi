Which argument proves that $f(x, y) = e^{x} \sin y + x^2 y$ is differentiable at every point of $\mathbb{R}^2$?

- $f$ is continuous on $\mathbb{R}^2$, so it is differentiable
- The partial derivatives exist at every point, so $f$ is differentiable
- The partial derivatives exist and are continuous on $\mathbb{R}^2$, so the total differential theorem applies
- At every point the directional derivatives along every unit vector exist, so $f$ is differentiable

## Explanation

The partial derivatives are $f_x = e^{x} \sin y + 2xy$ and $f_y = e^{x} \cos y + x^2$, continuous on $\mathbb{R}^2$ because they are compositions of continuous functions. Therefore the **total differential theorem** applies and $f$ is differentiable at every point. Continuity and the existence of partial or directional derivatives are *necessary* but not sufficient conditions. For example, $\sqrt{x^2 + y^2}$ is continuous without being differentiable at the origin, and $\dfrac{xy}{x^2 + y^2}$ extended by $0$ has partial derivatives at the origin without being differentiable there.
