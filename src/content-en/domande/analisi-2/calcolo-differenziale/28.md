For which of the following functions does the total differential theorem guarantee differentiability at $(0, 0)$?

- $f(x, y) = x\,|y|$
- $f(x, y) = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}$
- $f(x, y) = \sqrt{1 + x^2 + y^2}$
- $f(x, y) = |x| + |y|$

## Explanation

For $f(x, y) = \sqrt{1 + x^2 + y^2}$ we have $f_x = \dfrac{x}{\sqrt{1 + x^2 + y^2}}$ and $f_y = \dfrac{y}{\sqrt{1 + x^2 + y^2}}$, which are continuous on $\mathbb{R}^2$ because the radicand is always at least $1$; therefore the **total differential theorem** applies. For $\sqrt{x^2 + y^2}$ and for $|x| + |y|$ the partial derivatives at the origin do not exist, because the restrictions to the axes equal $|t|$. The function $x\,|y|$ is differentiable at the origin, but its derivative $f_y$ does not exist at the points $(x, 0)$ with $x \neq 0$. So for $x\,|y|$ the hypothesis of the theorem on the existence of the partial derivatives in a neighborhood of the origin is not satisfied.
