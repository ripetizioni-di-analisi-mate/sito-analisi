For the function
$$
f(x, y) =
\begin{cases}
\dfrac{\sin\left( x^2 + y^2 \right)}{x^2 + y^2} & (x, y) \neq (0, 0) \\[10pt]
0 & (x, y) = (0, 0)
\end{cases}
$$
which statement at the point $(0, 0)$ is true?

- It is differentiable at $(0, 0)$, with zero differential
- It is continuous at $(0, 0)$, but it is not differentiable
- It is not differentiable at $(0, 0)$, because it is not continuous
- It is differentiable at $(0, 0)$, because it is a composition of differentiable functions

## Explanation

Setting $u = x^2 + y^2$, we have $u \to 0$ as $(x, y) \to (0, 0)$, so by the standard limit $\dfrac{\sin u}{u} \to 1$ the limit of $f$ at the origin equals $1$. This limit is different from $f(0, 0) = 0$, therefore $f$ is **not continuous** at $(0, 0)$. Since differentiability implies continuity, $f$ is **not differentiable** at $(0, 0)$. The argument about composite functions holds only at points other than the origin, where $f$ coincides with the formula.
