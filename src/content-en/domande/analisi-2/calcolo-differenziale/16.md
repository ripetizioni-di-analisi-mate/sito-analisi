A function $f$ is differentiable at the point $(1, 2)$ and $\nabla f(1, 2) = (3, -1)$. Which statement is certainly true?

- The partial derivatives of $f$ are continuous at $(1, 2)$
- $f$ is continuous at $(1, 2)$
- $f$ has all second derivatives at $(1, 2)$
- The directional derivative of $f$ at $(1, 2)$ is zero in every direction

## Explanation

By the theorem **differentiability implies continuity**, $f$ is continuous at $(1, 2)$. Continuity of the partial derivatives does not follow from differentiability, because the total differential theorem gives only the opposite implication; for example, $(x^2 + y^2) \sin \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{x^2 + y^2}}$ is differentiable at the origin with discontinuous partial derivatives. The existence of the second derivatives is not guaranteed either. Finally, by the gradient formula the derivative along $\mathbf{v} = (1, 0)$ equals $3$, so it is not zero in every direction.
