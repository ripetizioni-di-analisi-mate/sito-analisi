Which integral in the variable $t$ is equal to the line integral $\displaystyle\int_\gamma y \, ds$ along the arc of astroid
$$
\gamma(t) = (\cos^3 t,\; \sin^3 t), \qquad t \in \left[ 0, \frac{\pi}{2} \right] ?
$$

- $\displaystyle\int_0^{\frac{\pi}{2}} \sin^3 t \, dt$
- $\displaystyle\int_0^{\frac{\pi}{2}} 3\sin^4 t \cos t \, dt$
- $\displaystyle\int_0^{\frac{\pi}{2}} 9\sin^5 t \cos^2 t \, dt$
- $\displaystyle\int_0^{\frac{\pi}{2}} 3\sin^3 t \cos t \, dt$

## Explanation

By the definition of line integral of the first kind we substitute $y = \sin^3 t$ and multiply by $\lVert \gamma'(t) \rVert$.

We have $\gamma'(t) = \left( -3\cos^2 t \sin t,\; 3\sin^2 t \cos t \right)$, so
$$
\lVert \gamma'(t) \rVert = 3\sin t \cos t \sqrt{\cos^2 t + \sin^2 t} = 3\sin t \cos t ,
$$
since $\sin t \cos t \geq 0$ on $\left[ 0, \dfrac{\pi}{2} \right]$.

Therefore $\displaystyle\int_\gamma y \, ds = \int_0^{\frac{\pi}{2}} 3\sin^4 t \cos t \, dt$, which equals $\dfrac{3}{5}$. The integrand $9\sin^5 t \cos^2 t$ is obtained by multiplying by $\lVert \gamma'(t) \rVert^2$ instead of the norm.
