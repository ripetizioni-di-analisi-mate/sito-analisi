What is the opposite curve of $\gamma(t) = (\cos t,\; \sin t)$, $t \in [0, \pi]$?

- $(\cos t,\; -\sin t)$, $t \in [0, \pi]$
- $(-\cos t,\; -\sin t)$, $t \in [0, \pi]$
- $(-\cos t,\; \sin t)$, $t \in [0, \pi]$
- $(\sin t,\; \cos t)$, $t \in [0, \pi]$

## Explanation

The opposite curve of $\gamma : [a, b] \to \mathbb{R}^2$ is $-\gamma(t) = \gamma(a + b - t)$. Here $a + b = \pi$, and since $\cos(\pi - t) = -\cos t$ and $\sin(\pi - t) = \sin t$, we get
$$
-\gamma(t) = (-\cos t,\; \sin t), \qquad t \in [0, \pi].
$$
This curve traverses the upper semicircle from the point $(-1, 0)$ to the point $(1, 0)$, that is, the same trace as $\gamma$ in the opposite direction. The curves $(\cos t,\; -\sin t)$ and $(-\cos t,\; -\sin t)$ traverse the lower semicircle, and $(\sin t,\; \cos t)$ traverses the semicircle with $x \geq 0$, so they have a different trace.
