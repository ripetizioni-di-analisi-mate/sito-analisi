Let $\delta(u) = (1 - u^2,\; 1 - u^2)$, $u \in [0, 1]$, be the curve that traverses the segment from the point $(1, 1)$ to the origin. What is $\displaystyle\int_\delta x \, ds$?

- $-\dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$
- $\dfrac{1}{2}$
- $\dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$
- $\dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{3}$

## Explanation

We have $\delta'(u) = (-2u,\; -2u)$, so $\lVert \delta'(u) \rVert = 2\sqrt{2}\, u$ for $u \geq 0$.

By the definition of line integral of the first kind
$$
\int_\delta x \, ds = \int_0^1 \left( 1 - u^2 \right) 2\sqrt{2}\, u \, du = 2\sqrt{2} \left( \frac{1}{2} - \frac{1}{4} \right) = \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} .
$$

The result coincides with the integral along $\gamma(t) = (t, t)$, $t \in [0, 1]$, that is $\displaystyle\int_0^1 \sqrt{2}\, t \, dt = \dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$. Indeed the integral of the first kind **does not depend** on the parametrization nor on the orientation, so the sign stays positive even though $\delta$ traverses the segment backwards.
