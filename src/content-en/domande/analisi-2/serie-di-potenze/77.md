The function $f(x) = \dfrac{e^x - 1}{x}$, extended with $f(0) = 1$, has a Maclaurin expansion valid on all of $\mathbb{R}$. Which one?

- $\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{x^n}{n!}$
- $\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{x^n}{n!}$
- $\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{x^n}{n+1}$
- $\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{x^n}{(n+1)!}$

## Explanation

From the exponential expansion, removing the term with $n = 0$, we have for every $x$
$$
e^x - 1 = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{x^n}{n!} .
$$
For $x \neq 0$ we divide by $x$ and obtain $\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{x^{n-1}}{n!}$, which with the index $k = n - 1$ becomes $\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \frac{x^k}{(k+1)!}$.

At $x = 0$ the series equals $\dfrac{1}{1!} = 1 = f(0)$, so the equality holds on all of $\mathbb{R}$ and, by the **uniqueness of the power series expansion**, it is the Maclaurin series of $f$.
