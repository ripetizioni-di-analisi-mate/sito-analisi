Knowing that $\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{n^2} = \frac{\pi^2}{6}$, what is $\displaystyle\int_0^1 \frac{\ln(1 + x)}{x} \, dx$?

- $\dfrac{\pi^2}{6}$
- $\ln 2$
- $\dfrac{\pi^2}{24}$
- $\dfrac{\pi^2}{12}$

## Explanation

Dividing the expansion of $\ln(1 + x)$ by $x$ we have, for $0 < |x| < 1$,
$$
\frac{\ln(1 + x)}{x} = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^{n+1} \, x^{n-1}}{n} .
$$
By **term-by-term integration**, for $0 < y < 1$ we obtain $\displaystyle\int_0^y \frac{\ln(1 + x)}{x} \, dx = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^{n+1} \, y^n}{n^2}$. This series also converges at $y = 1$, so by **Abel's theorem** the equality holds for $y = 1$.

The series $\displaystyle\sum \frac{(-1)^{n+1}}{n^2}$ is obtained by subtracting twice the terms of even index from the series $\displaystyle\sum \frac{1}{n^2}$. Since $\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{(2k)^2} = \frac{1}{4} \cdot \frac{\pi^2}{6}$, the integral equals $\dfrac{\pi^2}{6} - 2 \cdot \dfrac{\pi^2}{24} = \dfrac{\pi^2}{12}$.
