For the function $f(x) = \ln(1 + x^3) - x^3$, what is the lowest-order derivative that does not vanish at $x = 0$, and what value does it take?

- $f^{(6)}(0) = -\dfrac{1}{2}$
- $f^{(3)}(0) = 6$
- $f^{(6)}(0) = -360$
- $f^{(6)}(0) = 360$

## Explanation

The standard expansion $\ln(1 + y) = \displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^{n+1} \, y^n}{n}$ holds for $-1 < y \leq 1$. Substituting $y = x^3$ gives, for $|x| < 1$,
$$
\ln(1 + x^3) = x^3 - \frac{x^6}{2} + \frac{x^9}{3} - \cdots
$$
Subtracting $x^3$ cancels the term of degree $3$, so $f(x) = -\dfrac{x^6}{2} + \dfrac{x^9}{3} - \cdots$ for $|x| < 1$.

By the **uniqueness of the expansion** the coefficient of $x^k$ is $\dfrac{f^{(k)}(0)}{k!}$. The coefficients of $x, x^2, \dots, x^5$ are zero, therefore $f^{(k)}(0) = 0$ for $1 \leq k \leq 5$. The coefficient of $x^6$ is $-\dfrac{1}{2}$, so $f^{(6)}(0) = -\dfrac{6!}{2} = -360$.
