- $\sin 1$
- $\dfrac{\sin 1}{2}$
- $2 \sin 1$
- $\dfrac{1 - \cos 1}{2}$

## Explanation

The function $\cos(y^2)$ has no elementary antiderivative with respect to $y$, so we **change the order of integration**. The limits describe the region $0 \leq x \leq 1$, $x \leq y \leq 1$, which is the triangle with vertices $(0, 0)$, $(0, 1)$ and $(1, 1)$; with respect to the $y$-axis the same triangle is described by $0 \leq y \leq 1$ and $0 \leq x \leq y$. Therefore
$$
\int_0^1 \left( \int_0^y \cos(y^2)\, dx \right) dy = \int_0^1 y \cos(y^2)\, dy .
$$
The last integral is reducible to the immediate integral $\displaystyle\int \cos t \, dt = \sin t + c$ with $t = y^2$, so it equals $\left[ \dfrac{\sin(y^2)}{2} \right]_0^1 = \dfrac{\sin 1}{2}$.
