Let $f(x, y) = \dfrac{x^2 - y^2}{\left( x^2 + y^2 \right)^2}$ for $(x, y) \in [0, 1] \times [0, 1]$, $(x, y) \neq (0, 0)$. Integrating first in $y$ and then in $x$ gives $\dfrac{\pi}{4}$; integrating first in $x$ and then in $y$ gives $-\dfrac{\pi}{4}$. Which statement is true?

- Fubini's theorem guarantees that the double integral of $f$ equals $0$
- One of the two computations is wrong, because the two orders of integration always give the same value
- Fubini's theorem does not apply, because $f$ is neither continuous nor bounded on the square
- Fubini's theorem guarantees that the double integral of $f$ equals $\dfrac{\pi}{4}$

## Explanation

**Fubini's theorem** requires $f$ to be continuous on the closed rectangle. Along the line $y = 0$ we have $f(x, 0) = \dfrac{1}{x^2}$, which tends to $+\infty$ as $x \to 0^+$, so $f$ is not bounded near the origin and does not extend to a continuous function on the square. Therefore the hypothesis of the theorem is not satisfied, and the theorem says nothing about the equality of the two iterated integrals. The two values $\dfrac{\pi}{4}$ and $-\dfrac{\pi}{4}$ are both correct, and they show that without continuity the order of integration can change the result.
