The equation $x y^3 + y = 2$ and the point $(1, 1)$, which lies on the curve, satisfy the hypotheses of the implicit function theorem with respect to $y$. Which statement is guaranteed by the theorem?

- There exist a neighborhood $I$ of $1$ and a unique function $\varphi$ of class $C^1$, with values close to $1$, such that $\varphi(1) = 1$ and $x\,\varphi(x)^3 + \varphi(x) = 2$ for every $x \in I$
- There exists a unique function $\varphi : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ such that $\varphi(1) = 1$ and $x\,\varphi(x)^3 + \varphi(x) = 2$ for every $x \in \mathbb{R}$
- There exist a neighborhood $I$ of $1$ and a function $\varphi$ such that $\varphi(1) = 1$ and $x\,\varphi(x)^3 + \varphi(x) = 2$ on $I$, which however may fail to be differentiable at $1$
- There exist a neighborhood $I$ of $1$ and a unique function $\varphi$ such that $\varphi(1) = 1$, $x\,\varphi(x)^3 + \varphi(x) = 2$ on $I$ and $\varphi'(1) = 0$

## Explanation

Let $F(x, y) = x y^3 + y - 2$, of class $C^1$ on $\mathbb{R}^2$; we have $F(1, 1) = 0$ and $F_y(1, 1) = 3xy^2 + 1 \,\big|_{(1,1)} = 4 \neq 0$. The implicit function theorem then guarantees a neighborhood $I$ of $1$, a neighborhood $J$ of $1$ and a **unique** function $\varphi : I \to J$ of class $C^1$ with $\varphi(1) = 1$ and $F(x, \varphi(x)) = 0$ on $I$. The conclusion is **local**: for $x = -\dfrac{1}{100}$ the equation $-\dfrac{y^3}{100} + y - 2 = 0$ has three real solutions, so on all of $\mathbb{R}$ the function is not unique. The derivative exists and equals $\varphi'(1) = -\dfrac{F_x(1,1)}{F_y(1,1)} = -\dfrac{1}{4}$, therefore it is different from $0$.
