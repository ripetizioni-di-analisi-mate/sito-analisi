The denominator of $f(x, y) = \dfrac{x - y}{x + y - 2}$ vanishes on the line $x + y = 2$, which passes through the point $(1, 1)$. What can be concluded about the limit of $f$ as $(x, y) \to (1, 1)$ from the restrictions to the lines $y = 1$ and $x = 1$?

- The limit equals $1$
- The limit does not exist
- The limit equals $-1$
- The limit equals $0$

## Explanation

On the line $y = 1$, for $x \neq 1$, we have $f(x, 1) = \dfrac{x - 1}{x - 1} = 1$, so the restriction tends to $1$. On the line $x = 1$, for $y \neq 1$, we have $f(1, y) = \dfrac{1 - y}{y - 1} = -1$, so the restriction tends to $-1$. Since two restrictions produce different limits, by the **restriction theorem** the limit **does not exist**.
