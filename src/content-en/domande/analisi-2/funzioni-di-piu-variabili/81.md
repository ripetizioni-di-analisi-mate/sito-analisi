We study the limit as $(x, y) \to (0, 0)$ of $f(x, y) = \dfrac{\ln(1 + x^2 + 2y^2)}{x^2 + y^2}$. Which statement is true?

- The limit equals $1$, by the standard limit of the logarithm
- The limit does not exist
- The limit equals $2$
- The limit equals $\dfrac{3}{2}$

## Explanation

On the $x$-axis the restriction is $\dfrac{\ln(1 + x^2)}{x^2}$, which tends to $1$ by the **standard limit of the logarithm**. On the $y$-axis the restriction is
$$
\frac{\ln(1 + 2y^2)}{y^2} = 2 \cdot \frac{\ln(1 + 2y^2)}{2y^2} ,
$$
which tends to $2$. Two restrictions produce different limits, so, by the **restriction theorem**, the limit does not exist. The standard limit does not apply to the whole function, because the argument of the logarithm is $x^2 + 2y^2$, while the denominator is $x^2 + y^2$.
