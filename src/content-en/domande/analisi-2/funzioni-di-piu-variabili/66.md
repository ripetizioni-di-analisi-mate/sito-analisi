For the function $f(x, y) = \dfrac{xy}{x^2 + y^2}$ the restrictions to the $x$-axis and to the $y$-axis are constantly equal to $0$. Which statement about the limit as $(x, y) \to (0, 0)$ is true?

- The limit equals $0$, because the restrictions to the two axes tend to $0$
- The limit equals $\dfrac{1}{2}$
- The limit does not exist, because the restriction to the line $y = x$ equals $\dfrac{1}{2}$
- The limit equals $0$, because $|xy| \leq \dfrac{x^2 + y^2}{2}$

## Explanation

On the line $y = x$, for $x \neq 0$, we have $f(x, x) = \dfrac{x^2}{2x^2} = \dfrac{1}{2}$. The restrictions to the axes tend to $0$ and the restriction to the bisector tends to $\dfrac{1}{2}$, so two restrictions produce different limits. Therefore, by the **restriction theorem**, the limit **does not exist**. The inequality $|xy| \leq \dfrac{x^2 + y^2}{2}$ is true, but it only gives $|f(x, y)| \leq \dfrac{1}{2}$, which does not tend to $0$.
