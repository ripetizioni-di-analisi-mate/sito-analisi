For the function $f(x, y) = \dfrac{x^2 - 2y}{x^2 + y^2}$, which statement about the limit as $(x, y) \to (0, 0)$ is true?

- The limit does not exist, because the restriction to the $y$-axis has no limit
- The limit equals $1$, because the restriction to the $x$-axis is constantly equal to $1$
- The limit equals $0$, because the numerator tends to $0$
- The limit equals $-\infty$

## Explanation

On the $y$-axis, for $y \neq 0$, we have $f(0, y) = \dfrac{-2y}{y^2} = -\dfrac{2}{y}$. This restriction tends to $-\infty$ as $y \to 0^+$ and to $+\infty$ as $y \to 0^-$, so it has no limit. If the limit of $f$ existed, by the **restriction theorem** the limit of the restriction would also exist and have the same value. Therefore the limit of $f$ **does not exist**, even though the restriction to the $x$-axis is constantly equal to $1$.
