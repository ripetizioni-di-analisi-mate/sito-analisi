The function $f(x, y) = x^{y}$ is defined for $x > 0$, and at the origin it takes the form $0^0$. How does $f$ behave as $(x, y) \to (0, 0)$?

- The limit equals $1$, because the restriction to every line $y = mx$ tends to $1$
- The limit equals $0$, because the base tends to $0$
- The limit does not exist
- The limit equals $\dfrac{1}{e}$

## Explanation

On the $x$-axis, for $x > 0$, we have $f(x, 0) = x^{0} = 1$, so the restriction tends to $1$. Consider the curve $x = e^{-\frac{1}{y}}$ with $y > 0$, which tends to the origin as $y \to 0^+$ and lies in the domain. Along this curve we have
$$
f\!\left( e^{-\frac{1}{y}}, y \right) = \left( e^{-\frac{1}{y}} \right)^{y} = e^{-1} .
$$
Since two restrictions produce different limits, by the **restriction theorem** the limit **does not exist**. The restrictions to the lines $y = mx$ all tend to $1$, because $mx \ln x \to 0$ as $x \to 0^+$, so they are not enough to establish the existence of the limit.
