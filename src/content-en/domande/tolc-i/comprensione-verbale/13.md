The same street appears on two neighborhood maps. The pedestrian map marks sidewalks, crossings and sections with steps. The maintenance map instead shows pipes, technical access points and paving materials. Neither reproduces every feature of the place: each selects what matters for its intended use.

A resident observes that the pedestrian map omits valve locations. The curator replies that this omission does not make it inaccurate for planning a walk, provided it does not hide obstacles relevant to pedestrians. She adds that a more detailed map can become less readable if necessary details fail to stand out. She therefore distinguishes the accuracy of the information shown from the total amount of information: good selection reduces content without distorting what must guide route choice.

What limit accompanies the defense of the pedestrian map?

- All actual steps must be removed
- Every valve must be shown
- Obstacles relevant to walkers must not be hidden
- The map must have more symbols than the technical one
- Every resident must approve it

## Explanation

The phrase “provided” introduces the requirement making the omission acceptable. The map may leave out technical information unrelated to walking but must retain obstacles relevant to the route. Neither a physical alteration of the street nor unanimous resident approval is required.
