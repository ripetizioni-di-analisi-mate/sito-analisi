To renovate a museum entrance, an initial design places a ramp along a secondary route. A group of visitors observes that the solution overcomes the steps but forces some people to separate from their companions and use a less recognizable entrance. The designers therefore revise the plan, incorporating the step-free route into the main entrance.

The final report distinguishes the physical possibility of entering from the quality of the access experience. It does not argue that every difference in route is always unacceptable: different buildings may have different constraints. In this case, however, consultation with users revealed a problem that checking gradients alone had not identified. The ramp remains necessary, but its position changes. The example illustrates that a measurable requirement can be indispensable without covering every aspect of a good solution.

Which relation between the ramp and a good solution does the passage support?

- Any ramp alone guarantees an optimal experience
- The ramp is necessary, but its presence alone does not exhaust access quality
- The ramp becomes useless after consulting users
- A good solution always requires no ramps
- Access quality depends only on gradient

## Explanation

The report preserves the ramp’s necessity while adding criteria such as a shared route and recognizability. The central distinction is between an indispensable requirement and a complete assessment. The design changes the ramp’s position rather than removing it.
