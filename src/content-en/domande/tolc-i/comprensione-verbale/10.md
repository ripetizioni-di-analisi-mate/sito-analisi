A survey of adult commuters finds that habitual bicycle users report greater well-being on average. The questionnaire was completed voluntarily, and each participant had independently chosen a mode of transport. The authors publish the association but specify that the comparison alone does not demonstrate a causal effect of cycling.

People already in good health might find it easier to choose cycling; commuting distance and occupation might also affect both transport choice and reported well-being. The report proposes collecting this information in a second phase. It does not conclude that cycling is useless: it distinguishes the observed result from the explanation that can be assigned to it. It also limits the description to adult commuters who responded, without automatically extending it to children or people who do not travel for work.

Which summary preserves the scope of the survey?

- All adult commuters have greater well-being when cycling
- Adults who did not respond report greater well-being
- Cycling necessarily produces the respondents’ well-being
- Among all residents, cyclists have greater average well-being
- Among responding adult commuters, cyclists report greater average well-being

## Explanation

The summary retains the observed population, the self-reported nature of well-being and the average comparison. The alternatives change at least one of these features, extending the result to unobserved people or turning the association into a causal effect. The passage explicitly rejects automatic extension beyond the participants described.
