During restoration of a mosaic, some tiles are missing along its border. The team could reconstruct the design by perfectly imitating ancient materials, but chooses a slightly different shade for the additions. From a distance the pattern remains readable; close up, visitors can distinguish what has been added.

A note explains that the work should support understanding of the piece without presenting recent material as ancient. The different color does not signal a temporary repair: it makes the intervention recognizable. Where the original design cannot be reconstructed with sufficient confidence, the team leaves a neutral surface. It therefore does not treat every gap as an invitation to invent the missing pattern. The result combines visual continuity with transparency about the intervention, accepting that some parts of the work’s history remain unknown.

When is a neutral surface left?

- When the aim is to conceal a recognizable addition
- When confidence about the original design is insufficient
- When the design is documented with certainty
- In every part visible at close range
- Only in areas where every tile is still present

## Explanation

The neutral surface corresponds to a limit of supported reconstruction. The text links the choice to uncertainty about the original pattern, avoiding turning a gap into an invention. A certain reconstruction would not require this restraint; neither close observation nor the presence of intact tiles explains the choice of a neutral surface.
