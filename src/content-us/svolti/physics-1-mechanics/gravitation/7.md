## Exercise 1

A minor planet travels an orbit around the Sun with the period $T = 8.00\,\mathrm{yr}$. Compute the semi-major axis of its orbit in astronomical units and in meters, with $1\,\mathrm{AU} = 1.496 \times 10^{11}\,\mathrm{m}$.

$$
\frac{T^2}{a^3} = \frac{4\pi^2}{GM_S}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
T^2 = a^3
\ \text{ with } T \text{ in years and } a \text{ in } \mathrm{AU}
$$
$$
a = T^{2/3} = 8.00^{2/3}\,\mathrm{AU} = 4.00\,\mathrm{AU}
$$
$$
a = 4.00 \cdot 1.496 \times 10^{11}\,\mathrm{m} = 5.98 \times 10^{11}\,\mathrm{m}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Every body orbiting the Sun obeys **Kepler's third law** with the same constant $\dfrac{4\pi^2}{GM_S}$, therefore the ratio $\dfrac{T^2}{a^3}$ is the same for the minor planet and for the Earth. The Earth has $T = 1\,\mathrm{yr}$ and $a = 1\,\mathrm{AU}$ by the definition of those units, hence that common ratio equals $1$ in them and the law reads $T^2 = a^3$.

Solving for $a$ gives $a = T^{2/3} = 8^{2/3} = 4.00\,\mathrm{AU}$, since $8 = 2^3$ and therefore $8^{2/3} = 2^2$. Converting with $1\,\mathrm{AU} = 1.496 \times 10^{11}\,\mathrm{m}$ gives $5.98 \times 10^{11}\,\mathrm{m}$, a distance comparable with the orbit of Jupiter.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Titan orbits Saturn with a nearly circular orbit of radius $r = 1.222 \times 10^{9}\,\mathrm{m}$ and period $T = 15.95\,\mathrm{d}$. Compute the mass of Saturn.

$$
T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{r^3}{GM}}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
M = \frac{4\pi^2 r^3}{G\,T^2}
$$
$$
T = 15.95 \cdot 86400\,\mathrm{s} = 1.378 \times 10^{6}\,\mathrm{s}
$$
$$
M = \frac{4\pi^2 \cdot (1.222 \times 10^{9}\,\mathrm{m})^3}{6.674 \times 10^{-11}\,\mathrm{N\cdot m^2/kg^2} \cdot (1.378 \times 10^{6}\,\mathrm{s})^2}
= \frac{7.204 \times 10^{28}}{1.267 \times 10^{2}}\,\mathrm{kg}
= 5.68 \times 10^{26}\,\mathrm{kg}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The orbit is circular, therefore the period satisfies $T = 2\pi\sqrt{\dfrac{r^3}{GM}}$, where $M$ is the mass of the central body alone, since the mass of the orbiting body cancels in the equation of motion. Squaring and solving for $M$ gives the formula written above.

The period is converted to seconds before any substitution, because $G$ is expressed in SI units. The result is $5.68 \times 10^{26}\,\mathrm{kg}$, about $95$ times the mass of the Earth.

The method requires only the radius and the period of one satellite, both of which are measured by observation, and it is how the mass of every planet with a satellite is determined. A planet with no satellite requires the deflection it produces on a passing probe.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A comet travels an elliptical orbit around the Sun with perihelion $r_p = 0.600\,\mathrm{AU}$ and aphelion $r_a = 5.40\,\mathrm{AU}$. Compute the semi-major axis, the period, and the speeds at perihelion and at aphelion. Use $GM_S = 1.328 \times 10^{20}\,\mathrm{m^3/s^2}$ and $1\,\mathrm{AU} = 1.496 \times 10^{11}\,\mathrm{m}$.

$$
a = \frac{r_p + r_a}{2},
\qquad
T^2 = a^3 \ \text{(in years and AU)},
\qquad
\frac{1}{2}v^2 - \frac{GM_S}{r} = -\frac{GM_S}{2a}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
v = \sqrt{GM_S\left(\frac{2}{r} - \frac{1}{a}\right)}
$$
$$
a = \frac{0.600 + 5.40}{2}\,\mathrm{AU} = 3.00\,\mathrm{AU} = 4.488 \times 10^{11}\,\mathrm{m},
\qquad
T = 3.00^{3/2}\,\mathrm{yr} = 5.20\,\mathrm{yr}
$$
$$
v_p = \sqrt{1.328 \times 10^{20}\left(\frac{2}{8.976 \times 10^{10}} - \frac{1}{4.488 \times 10^{11}}\right)}\,\mathrm{m/s} = 5.16 \times 10^{4}\,\mathrm{m/s}
$$
$$
v_a = \sqrt{1.328 \times 10^{20}\left(\frac{2}{8.078 \times 10^{11}} - \frac{1}{4.488 \times 10^{11}}\right)}\,\mathrm{m/s} = 5.73 \times 10^{3}\,\mathrm{m/s}
$$
$$
r_p\,v_p = 4.63 \times 10^{15}\,\mathrm{m^2/s} = r_a\,v_a
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Perihelion and aphelion are the ends of the major axis, therefore their distances add up to $2a$ and the semi-major axis is their arithmetic mean, $3.00\,\mathrm{AU}$. The period follows from **Kepler's third law** in years and astronomical units, which gives $5.20\,\mathrm{yr}$.

The energy of an elliptical orbit is $E = -\dfrac{GM_Sm}{2a}$, a result that extends to the ellipse the formula proved for the circle. Writing the conservation of energy at a generic distance $r$ and isolating $v$ gives the expression used above, from which the two speeds follow.

The comet is nine times nearer at perihelion and moves nine times faster there, which is **Kepler's second law**: the velocity is perpendicular to the radius at both ends of the major axis, hence the angular momentum per unit mass reduces to $r v$ and the two products must be equal. The computed values confirm $r_pv_p = r_av_a = 4.63 \times 10^{15}\,\mathrm{m^2/s}$.

</div>

## Exercise 4

A satellite is to be transferred from the circular orbit of radius $r_1 = 6.77 \times 10^{6}\,\mathrm{m}$ to the geostationary orbit of radius $r_2 = 4.22 \times 10^{7}\,\mathrm{m}$ along an elliptical orbit tangent to both. Compute the semi-major axis of the transfer orbit and the duration of the transfer. Use $GM_E = 3.984 \times 10^{14}\,\mathrm{m^3/s^2}$.

::grafico{assi=0 uguale=1 curve="-1.7715+2.4485*cos(t),1.6903*sin(t),0,6.29;0.677*cos(t),0.677*sin(t),0,6.29;4.22*cos(t),4.22*sin(t),0,6.29" punti="0,0,pieno;0.677,0,pieno;-4.22,0,pieno" segmenti="-4.22,0,0.677,0" etichette="0.1,-1.15,Earth;0.72,0.32,P;-5.0,0.35,A" xmin=-5.4 xmax=5.4 ymin=-4.9 ymax=4.9 titolo="The transfer ellipse touches the inner orbit at the perigee $P$ and the outer orbit at the apogee $A$; its major axis is $r_1 + r_2$"}

$$
2a = r_1 + r_2
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
a = \frac{r_1 + r_2}{2},
\qquad
t = \frac{T}{2} = \pi\sqrt{\frac{a^3}{GM_E}}
$$
$$
a = \frac{6.77 \times 10^{6}\,\mathrm{m} + 4.22 \times 10^{7}\,\mathrm{m}}{2} = 2.449 \times 10^{7}\,\mathrm{m}
$$
$$
t = \pi\sqrt{\frac{(2.449 \times 10^{7}\,\mathrm{m})^3}{3.984 \times 10^{14}\,\mathrm{m^3/s^2}}}
= \pi\sqrt{3.684 \times 10^{7}\,\mathrm{s^2}} = 1.91 \times 10^{4}\,\mathrm{s} = 5.30\,\mathrm{h}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The transfer orbit touches the inner circle at its perigee and the outer circle at its apogee, therefore those two distances are $r_1$ and $r_2$ and their sum is the major axis. The semi-major axis is then $2.449 \times 10^{7}\,\mathrm{m}$.

Kepler's third law gives the period of the whole ellipse from $a$ alone, and the transfer covers exactly one half of it, from perigee to apogee. The duration is therefore $\dfrac{T}{2} = \pi\sqrt{\dfrac{a^3}{GM_E}} = 5.30\,\mathrm{h}$.

The shape of the ellipse does not enter the period, which depends on the semi-major axis only, hence the time of flight is the same for every transfer with the same $a$. Two changes of speed are needed, one at $P$ to leave the circular orbit and one at $A$ to enter the geostationary orbit, and in between the satellite is in free fall.

</div>
