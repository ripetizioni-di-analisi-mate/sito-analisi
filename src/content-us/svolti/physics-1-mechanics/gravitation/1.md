## Exercise 1

Two uniform spheres of masses $m_1 = 120\,\mathrm{kg}$ and $m_2 = 85\,\mathrm{kg}$ have their centers $d = 0.60\,\mathrm{m}$ apart. Compute the magnitude of the force they exert on each other, and the distance at which that force would be reduced to one quarter.

$$
F = G\,\frac{m_1 m_2}{d^2}
$$
$$
F = 6.674 \times 10^{-11}\,\mathrm{N\cdot m^2/kg^2} \cdot \frac{120\,\mathrm{kg} \cdot 85\,\mathrm{kg}}{(0.60\,\mathrm{m})^2}
= 1.89 \times 10^{-6}\,\mathrm{N}
$$
$$
\frac{F'}{F} = \frac{d^2}{d'^2} = \frac{1}{4}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
d' = 2d = 1.2\,\mathrm{m}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The two bodies are uniform spheres, therefore by the **shell theorem** each of them acts as a particle of the same mass placed at its center, and the distance to use in the law of universal gravitation is the distance between the centers. Substituting the data gives $F = 1.89 \times 10^{-6}\,\mathrm{N}$, a force roughly $5 \times 10^{8}$ times smaller than the weight of either sphere.

The force is inversely proportional to the square of the distance, hence dividing it by four requires multiplying the distance by two, which gives $d' = 1.2\,\mathrm{m}$. The masses do not enter this ratio, since they are unchanged.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Three small spheres lie on the same line: $m_1 = 20\,\mathrm{kg}$ at the origin, $m_2 = 5.0\,\mathrm{kg}$ at $x = 0.40\,\mathrm{m}$ and $m_3 = 10\,\mathrm{kg}$ at $x = 1.00\,\mathrm{m}$. Compute the resultant force on $m_2$, and find the position between $m_1$ and $m_3$ at which a body would feel no resultant force.

$$
F_{21} = G\,\frac{m_1 m_2}{x_2^2} \ \text{(toward } -x\text{)},
\qquad
F_{23} = G\,\frac{m_2 m_3}{(x_3 - x_2)^2} \ \text{(toward } +x\text{)}
$$
$$
F_{21} = 6.674 \times 10^{-11} \cdot \frac{20 \cdot 5.0}{(0.40)^2}\,\mathrm{N} = 4.171 \times 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{N}
$$
$$
F_{23} = 6.674 \times 10^{-11} \cdot \frac{5.0 \cdot 10}{(0.60)^2}\,\mathrm{N} = 9.269 \times 10^{-9}\,\mathrm{N}
$$
$$
F = F_{23} - F_{21} = -3.24 \times 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{N}
$$
$$
G\,\frac{m_1}{x^2} = G\,\frac{m_3}{(L-x)^2}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\frac{L-x}{x} = \sqrt{\frac{m_3}{m_1}} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{2}}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
x = \frac{L}{1 + \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}} = 0.586\,\mathrm{m}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Both forces act along the line of the three spheres, therefore the vector sum reduces to an algebraic sum with the $x$ axis as the positive direction. The sphere $m_1$ attracts $m_2$ toward the origin and the sphere $m_3$ attracts it in the opposite sense, hence the two contributions carry opposite signs by the **principle of superposition**.

The first force is larger, because $m_1$ is twice $m_3$ and is also closer, therefore the resultant has magnitude $3.24 \times 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{N}$ and points toward $m_1$.

A body placed at the distance $x$ from $m_1$ feels no resultant force when the two fields have equal magnitudes. Taking the square root of the equality of the two fields gives $\dfrac{L-x}{x} = \sqrt{\dfrac{m_3}{m_1}}$, where the positive root is chosen because $0 < x < L$, and solving for $x$ gives $0.586\,\mathrm{m}$. The point lies nearer to the lighter sphere, as the smaller field of $m_3$ requires.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Three small spheres are placed at the vertices of a right triangle: $m_1 = 6.0\,\mathrm{kg}$ at the origin, $m_2 = 8.0\,\mathrm{kg}$ at $x = 0.30\,\mathrm{m}$ on the $x$ axis, and $m_3 = 4.0\,\mathrm{kg}$ at $y = 0.40\,\mathrm{m}$ on the $y$ axis. Compute the magnitude and the direction of the resultant force on $m_1$.

::grafico{assi=0 uguale=1 punti="0,0,pieno" vettori="0,0,3.559,0,F₂;0,0,0,1.001,F₃;0,0,3.559,1.001,F" segmenti="3.559,0,3.559,1.001;0,1.001,3.559,1.001" etichette="-0.1,-0.35,m₁;1.0,0.08,θ" xmin=-0.7 xmax=4.4 ymin=-0.9 ymax=1.8 titolo="Composition of the two forces on $m_1$, drawn to scale ($1.0 \times 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{N}$ per unit): the resultant has magnitude $3.70 \times 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{N}$ and makes the angle $\theta = 15.7^\circ$ with the $x$ axis"}

$$
F_x = G\,\frac{m_1 m_2}{d_{12}^2},
\qquad
F_y = G\,\frac{m_1 m_3}{d_{13}^2},
\qquad
F = \sqrt{F_x^2 + F_y^2},
\qquad
\tan\theta = \frac{F_y}{F_x}
$$
$$
F_x = 6.674 \times 10^{-11} \cdot \frac{6.0 \cdot 8.0}{(0.30)^2}\,\mathrm{N} = 3.559 \times 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{N}
$$
$$
F_y = 6.674 \times 10^{-11} \cdot \frac{6.0 \cdot 4.0}{(0.40)^2}\,\mathrm{N} = 1.001 \times 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{N}
$$
$$
F = \sqrt{(3.559 \times 10^{-8})^2 + (1.001 \times 10^{-8})^2}\,\mathrm{N} = 3.70 \times 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{N}
$$
$$
\theta = \arctan\frac{1.001 \times 10^{-8}}{3.559 \times 10^{-8}} = 15.7^\circ
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Each of the two spheres attracts $m_1$ along the line joining the centers, therefore the force of $m_2$ lies along the $x$ axis and the force of $m_3$ lies along the $y$ axis. The two directions are perpendicular, hence each force coincides with one component of the resultant and no projection is needed.

The force of $m_2$ is larger although $m_2$ is only twice $m_3$, because the distance $0.30\,\mathrm{m}$ is smaller than $0.40\,\mathrm{m}$ and the square of the distance enters the denominator. The magnitude of the resultant follows from the Pythagorean theorem and equals $3.70 \times 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{N}$.

The angle is measured from the positive $x$ axis, and both components are positive, so the resultant lies in the first quadrant and the arctangent gives the angle directly, $\theta = 15.7^\circ$. The resultant points **toward the pair of spheres**, as every gravitational attraction does.

</div>

## Exercise 4

The centers of the Earth, of mass $M_E = 5.97 \times 10^{24}\,\mathrm{kg}$, and of the Moon, of mass $M_M = 7.35 \times 10^{22}\,\mathrm{kg}$, are $d = 3.84 \times 10^{8}\,\mathrm{m}$ apart. Find the point of the segment joining them at which the resultant gravitational field vanishes, and compute the common magnitude of the two fields at the neighboring points of that line.

$$
\frac{GM_E}{x^2} = \frac{GM_M}{(d-x)^2}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\frac{d-x}{x} = \sqrt{\frac{M_M}{M_E}}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
x = \frac{d}{1 + \sqrt{\dfrac{M_M}{M_E}}}
$$
$$
\sqrt{\frac{M_M}{M_E}} = \sqrt{\frac{7.35 \times 10^{22}}{5.97 \times 10^{24}}} = 0.1110
$$
$$
x = \frac{3.84 \times 10^{8}\,\mathrm{m}}{1.1110} = 3.456 \times 10^{8}\,\mathrm{m},
\qquad
d - x = 3.84 \times 10^{7}\,\mathrm{m}
$$
$$
g = \frac{GM_E}{x^2} = \frac{3.984 \times 10^{14}\,\mathrm{m^3/s^2}}{(3.456 \times 10^{8}\,\mathrm{m})^2} = 3.34 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m/s^2}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

On the segment joining the two centers the field of the Earth points toward the Earth and the field of the Moon points toward the Moon, therefore the two fields have opposite senses and their sum vanishes where their magnitudes are equal. Equating the magnitudes and taking the positive square root, which is legitimate because $0 < x < d$, gives the ratio $\dfrac{d-x}{x}$ and then the position.

The point lies at $3.456 \times 10^{8}\,\mathrm{m}$ from the center of the Earth, that is at $3.84 \times 10^{7}\,\mathrm{m}$ from the center of the Moon, hence at about one tenth of the way from the Moon to the Earth. The Earth is $81$ times more massive, and the distances stand in the ratio $\sqrt{81} = 9$, which is the square root required by the inverse-square law.

Substituting $x$ in either expression gives the same value $3.34 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$ for the two fields that cancel there. The resultant field is zero only at that single point, and a probe displaced from it is pulled back toward whichever body it has approached.

</div>
