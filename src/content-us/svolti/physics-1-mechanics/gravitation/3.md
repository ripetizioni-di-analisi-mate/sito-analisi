## Exercise 1

A satellite travels a circular orbit at the altitude $h = 600\,\mathrm{km}$ above the surface of the Earth, for which $GM_E = 3.984 \times 10^{14}\,\mathrm{m^3/s^2}$ and $R_E = 6.37 \times 10^{6}\,\mathrm{m}$. Compute its speed and its period.

$$
\frac{GM_E m}{r^2} = m\,\frac{v^2}{r}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
v = \sqrt{\frac{GM_E}{r}},
\qquad
T = \frac{2\pi r}{v},
\qquad
r = R_E + h
$$
$$
r = 6.37 \times 10^{6}\,\mathrm{m} + 6.00 \times 10^{5}\,\mathrm{m} = 6.97 \times 10^{6}\,\mathrm{m}
$$
$$
v = \sqrt{\frac{3.984 \times 10^{14}\,\mathrm{m^3/s^2}}{6.97 \times 10^{6}\,\mathrm{m}}} = 7.56 \times 10^{3}\,\mathrm{m/s}
$$
$$
T = \frac{2\pi \cdot 6.97 \times 10^{6}\,\mathrm{m}}{7.56 \times 10^{3}\,\mathrm{m/s}} = 5.79 \times 10^{3}\,\mathrm{s} = 96.5\,\mathrm{min}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

On a circular orbit the speed is constant, therefore the acceleration is purely centripetal and has magnitude $\dfrac{v^2}{r}$. Gravitation is the only force acting on the satellite, hence **Newton's second law** along the radius equates the gravitational attraction with $m\,\dfrac{v^2}{r}$, and the mass of the satellite cancels.

The radius of the orbit is measured from the center of the Earth, so the altitude is added to the radius of the Earth, which gives $r = 6.97 \times 10^{6}\,\mathrm{m}$. The speed follows as $7.56\,\mathrm{km/s}$ and the period as $5.79 \times 10^{3}\,\mathrm{s}$, a little over an hour and a half.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A satellite of the Earth has the period $T = 6.00\,\mathrm{h}$. Compute the radius of its orbit, the corresponding altitude and the orbital speed.

$$
T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{r^3}{GM_E}}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
r = \left(\frac{GM_E\,T^2}{4\pi^2}\right)^{\frac{1}{3}}
$$
$$
T = 6.00\,\mathrm{h} = 2.16 \times 10^{4}\,\mathrm{s}
$$
$$
r = \left(\frac{3.984 \times 10^{14}\,\mathrm{m^3/s^2} \cdot (2.16 \times 10^{4}\,\mathrm{s})^2}{4\pi^2}\right)^{\frac{1}{3}}
= \left(4.708 \times 10^{21}\,\mathrm{m^3}\right)^{\frac{1}{3}}
= 1.676 \times 10^{7}\,\mathrm{m}
$$
$$
h = r - R_E = 1.039 \times 10^{7}\,\mathrm{m},
\qquad
v = \sqrt{\frac{3.984 \times 10^{14}}{1.676 \times 10^{7}}}\,\mathrm{m/s} = 4.88 \times 10^{3}\,\mathrm{m/s}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The period and the radius of a circular orbit determine each other, hence the relation $T = 2\pi\sqrt{\dfrac{r^3}{GM_E}}$ can be inverted. Squaring both sides gives $T^2 = \dfrac{4\pi^2 r^3}{GM_E}$, and solving for $r$ gives the cube root written above.

The orbit has radius $1.676 \times 10^{7}\,\mathrm{m}$, that is an altitude of $1.039 \times 10^{7}\,\mathrm{m}$, roughly one and a half times the radius of the Earth. The speed on that orbit is $4.88\,\mathrm{km/s}$, smaller than the $7.56\,\mathrm{km/s}$ of the preceding exercise, since a satellite farther from the Earth moves **more slowly**.

The data are consistent with the geostationary orbit, whose period of $24\,\mathrm{h}$ is four times longer and whose radius, larger by the factor $4^{\frac{2}{3}} = 2.52$, is $4.22 \times 10^{7}\,\mathrm{m}$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A probe travels a circular orbit grazing the surface of the Moon, whose radius is $R = 1.74 \times 10^{6}\,\mathrm{m}$ and whose surface gravity is $g_0 = 1.62\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$. Obtain the speed and the period in terms of $g_0$ and $R$, and compute them.

$$
GM = g_0R^2
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
v = \sqrt{\frac{GM}{R}} = \sqrt{g_0 R},
\qquad
T = \frac{2\pi R}{v} = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{R}{g_0}}
$$
$$
v = \sqrt{1.62\,\mathrm{m/s^2} \cdot 1.74 \times 10^{6}\,\mathrm{m}} = 1.68 \times 10^{3}\,\mathrm{m/s}
$$
$$
T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{1.74 \times 10^{6}\,\mathrm{m}}{1.62\,\mathrm{m/s^2}}} = 6.51 \times 10^{3}\,\mathrm{s} = 108\,\mathrm{min}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The mass of the Moon is not given, therefore it is eliminated through the surface gravity. The relation $g_0 = \dfrac{GM}{R^2}$ gives $GM = g_0R^2$, and substituting this product in the formulas for a circular orbit of radius $R$ leaves expressions that contain only $g_0$ and $R$.

The grazing orbit is the fastest and the shortest circular orbit around a body, since every other orbit has a larger radius. For the Moon it gives $v = 1.68\,\mathrm{km/s}$ and $T = 108\,\mathrm{min}$.

The same formulas applied to the Earth give $v = \sqrt{9.82 \cdot 6.37 \times 10^{6}}\,\mathrm{m/s} = 7.91\,\mathrm{km/s}$ and $T = 84.4\,\mathrm{min}$, which are the limiting values that no satellite of the Earth can go below. A grazing orbit is of course not realizable, because the atmosphere would stop the satellite.

</div>

## Exercise 4

Two satellites travel circular orbits around the Earth with radii $r_A = 8.00 \times 10^{6}\,\mathrm{m}$ and $r_B = 2.00 \times 10^{7}\,\mathrm{m}$. Compute the two periods and the two speeds, and verify that the ratio of the periods obeys Kepler's third law.

::grafico{assi=0 uguale=1 curve="0.637*cos(t),0.637*sin(t),0,6.29;0.8*cos(t),0.8*sin(t),0,6.29;2*cos(t),2*sin(t),0,6.29" punti="0,0,pieno;0.8,0,pieno;0,2,pieno" vettori="0.8,0,0.8,0.86,vA;0,2,-0.86,2,vB" etichette="1.05,-0.28,A;0.3,2.12,B;-0.42,-0.22,Earth" xmin=-2.5 xmax=2.8 ymin=-2.5 ymax=2.9 titolo="The two circular orbits drawn to scale with the Earth; the velocity arrows are in the ratio $\dfrac{v_A}{v_B} = 1.58$"}

$$
T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{r^3}{GM_E}},
\qquad
v = \sqrt{\frac{GM_E}{r}},
\qquad
\frac{T_B}{T_A} = \left(\frac{r_B}{r_A}\right)^{\frac{3}{2}},
\qquad
\frac{v_A}{v_B} = \sqrt{\frac{r_B}{r_A}}
$$
$$
T_A = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{(8.00 \times 10^{6})^3}{3.984 \times 10^{14}}}\,\mathrm{s} = 7.12 \times 10^{3}\,\mathrm{s} = 1.98\,\mathrm{h}
$$
$$
T_B = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{(2.00 \times 10^{7})^3}{3.984 \times 10^{14}}}\,\mathrm{s} = 2.82 \times 10^{4}\,\mathrm{s} = 7.82\,\mathrm{h}
$$
$$
v_A = 7.06 \times 10^{3}\,\mathrm{m/s},
\qquad
v_B = 4.46 \times 10^{3}\,\mathrm{m/s}
$$
$$
\frac{T_B}{T_A} = \frac{2.82 \times 10^{4}}{7.12 \times 10^{3}} = 3.95,
\qquad
\left(\frac{r_B}{r_A}\right)^{\frac{3}{2}} = (2.50)^{\frac{3}{2}} = 3.95
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Both satellites orbit the same central body, therefore the same constant $GM_E$ appears in both periods, and the two can be compared directly. Dividing the two periods makes $GM_E$ and $2\pi$ disappear and leaves $\dfrac{T_B}{T_A} = \left(\dfrac{r_B}{r_A}\right)^{\frac{3}{2}}$, which is **Kepler's third law** written as a ratio.

The radii stand in the ratio $2.50$, hence the periods stand in the ratio $2.50^{\frac{3}{2}} = 3.95$, and the computed periods confirm it. The speeds instead stand in the ratio $\sqrt{2.50} = 1.58$, with the inner satellite faster.

The outer satellite therefore travels a path $2.50$ times longer at a speed $1.58$ times smaller, and $2.50 \cdot 1.58 = 3.95$ is exactly the ratio of the periods, which is the geometric meaning of the law.

</div>
