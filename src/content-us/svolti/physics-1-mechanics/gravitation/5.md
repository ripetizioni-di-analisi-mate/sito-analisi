## Exercise 1

Compute the escape speed from the surface of Mars, whose mass is $M = 6.42 \times 10^{23}\,\mathrm{kg}$ and whose radius is $R = 3.39 \times 10^{6}\,\mathrm{m}$, and verify the result with the surface gravity $g_0 = 3.73\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$.

$$
v_f = \sqrt{\frac{2GM}{R}} = \sqrt{2 g_0 R}
$$
$$
v_f = \sqrt{\frac{2 \cdot 6.674 \times 10^{-11}\,\mathrm{N\cdot m^2/kg^2} \cdot 6.42 \times 10^{23}\,\mathrm{kg}}{3.39 \times 10^{6}\,\mathrm{m}}}
= \sqrt{2.528 \times 10^{7}\,\mathrm{m^2/s^2}} = 5.03 \times 10^{3}\,\mathrm{m/s}
$$
$$
\sqrt{2 \cdot 3.73\,\mathrm{m/s^2} \cdot 3.39 \times 10^{6}\,\mathrm{m}} = \sqrt{2.529 \times 10^{7}\,\mathrm{m^2/s^2}} = 5.03 \times 10^{3}\,\mathrm{m/s}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

A body escapes when its mechanical energy is non-negative, because the potential energy tends to zero at large distances and the kinetic energy cannot be negative. Imposing $\dfrac{1}{2}mv^2 - \dfrac{GMm}{R} \ge 0$ and taking the smallest admissible speed gives $v_f = \sqrt{\dfrac{2GM}{R}}$, in which the mass of the body does not appear.

The two computations agree because $GM = g_0R^2$, hence $\dfrac{2GM}{R} = 2g_0R$. The second form is the one to use when the surface gravity is known and the mass is not.

The escape speed from Mars, $5.03\,\mathrm{km/s}$, is $45\%$ of the terrestrial value $11.2\,\mathrm{km/s}$, since Mars has both a smaller mass and a smaller radius.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A probe is launched from the surface of the Earth with the speed $v_0 = 1.50\,v_f$, where $v_f = 11.2\,\mathrm{km/s}$ is the escape speed. Compute the speed it retains at a distance so large that the potential energy is negligible.

$$
\frac{1}{2}mv_0^2 - \frac{GM_E m}{R_E} = \frac{1}{2}mv_\infty^2,
\qquad
\frac{GM_E}{R_E} = \frac{1}{2}v_f^2
$$
$$
v_\infty^2 = v_0^2 - v_f^2 = (1.50\,v_f)^2 - v_f^2 = 1.25\,v_f^2
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
v_\infty = v_f\sqrt{1.25}
$$
$$
v_\infty = 11.2 \times 10^{3}\,\mathrm{m/s} \cdot 1.118 = 1.25 \times 10^{4}\,\mathrm{m/s}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Mechanical energy is conserved along the flight. At arrival the potential energy is taken as zero, therefore the whole energy is kinetic, while at launch it is the sum of the initial kinetic energy and the potential energy at the surface.

The quantity $\dfrac{GM_E}{R_E}$ is replaced by $\dfrac{1}{2}v_f^2$, which is the definition of the escape speed, so the energy balance reduces to the relation $v_\infty^2 = v_0^2 - v_f^2$ between three speeds alone.

The probe retains $12.5\,\mathrm{km/s}$, which is less than the $16.8\,\mathrm{km/s}$ of the launch: the difference went into separating the probe from the Earth. A launch at exactly $v_f$ would give $v_\infty = 0$, and any launch speed **below** $v_f$ gives no real solution, because the probe does not reach large distances at all.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Compute the speed that a probe already at the distance $a = 1.496 \times 10^{11}\,\mathrm{m}$ from the Sun, the radius of the Earth's orbit, must have to escape the solar system, and compare it with the orbital speed of the Earth. Use $M_S = 1.99 \times 10^{30}\,\mathrm{kg}$ and neglect the attraction of the Earth.

$$
v_f = \sqrt{\frac{2GM_S}{a}},
\qquad
v_{\text{orb}} = \sqrt{\frac{GM_S}{a}}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
v_f = \sqrt{2}\,v_{\text{orb}}
$$
$$
GM_S = 6.674 \times 10^{-11} \cdot 1.99 \times 10^{30}\,\mathrm{m^3/s^2} = 1.328 \times 10^{20}\,\mathrm{m^3/s^2}
$$
$$
v_{\text{orb}} = \sqrt{\frac{1.328 \times 10^{20}}{1.496 \times 10^{11}}}\,\mathrm{m/s} = 2.98 \times 10^{4}\,\mathrm{m/s}
$$
$$
v_f = \sqrt{2} \cdot 2.98 \times 10^{4}\,\mathrm{m/s} = 4.21 \times 10^{4}\,\mathrm{m/s},
\qquad
v_f - v_{\text{orb}} = 1.23 \times 10^{4}\,\mathrm{m/s}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The condition for escape has the same form at any starting distance, with $a$ in place of the radius of the planet, therefore $v_f = \sqrt{\dfrac{2GM_S}{a}}$. The orbital speed on a circular orbit of the same radius is $\sqrt{\dfrac{GM_S}{a}}$, hence the escape speed is $\sqrt{2}$ times the orbital speed at every distance.

The Earth travels its orbit at $29.8\,\mathrm{km/s}$, and a probe at that distance escapes the Sun with $42.1\,\mathrm{km/s}$. A probe launched in the direction of the Earth's motion already carries the orbital speed of the Earth, hence it needs an additional $12.3\,\mathrm{km/s}$ relative to the Earth.

This is the reason interplanetary launches are made in the direction of the orbital motion of the Earth, and the saving of $29.8\,\mathrm{km/s}$ is what makes such missions possible.

</div>

## Exercise 4

A body is launched vertically from the surface of the Earth and reaches a maximum distance $r_{\max} = 4R_E$ from the center. Compute the launch speed and express it as a fraction of the escape speed. Use $GM_E = 3.984 \times 10^{14}\,\mathrm{m^3/s^2}$ and $R_E = 6.37 \times 10^{6}\,\mathrm{m}$.

::grafico{curve="t,-1/t,0.5,8" asintoti="y=-0.25" punti="1,-1,pieno;4,-0.25,pieno" segmenti="1,-1,1,0;4,-0.25,4,0" nomi="r,U" legenda=0 etichette="7.0,-0.40,E;1.12,0.24,R;4.12,0.24,4R" xmin=-0.8 xmax=8.8 ymin=-2.3 ymax=0.75 titolo="Energy diagram of the launch, with distances in units of $R_E$ and energies in units of $\dfrac{GM_Em}{R_E}$: the body stops where $U(r) = E$"}

$$
\frac{1}{2}mv_0^2 - \frac{GM_E m}{R_E} = -\frac{GM_E m}{r_{\max}},
\qquad r_{\max} = 4R_E
$$
$$
\frac{1}{2}v_0^2 = \frac{GM_E}{R_E} - \frac{GM_E}{4R_E} = \frac{3}{4}\,\frac{GM_E}{R_E}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
v_0 = \sqrt{\frac{3\,GM_E}{2\,R_E}}
$$
$$
v_0 = \sqrt{\frac{3 \cdot 3.984 \times 10^{14}\,\mathrm{m^3/s^2}}{2 \cdot 6.37 \times 10^{6}\,\mathrm{m}}}
= \sqrt{9.382 \times 10^{7}\,\mathrm{m^2/s^2}} = 9.69 \times 10^{3}\,\mathrm{m/s}
$$
$$
\frac{v_0}{v_f} = \frac{\sqrt{\dfrac{3\,GM_E}{2\,R_E}}}{\sqrt{\dfrac{2\,GM_E}{R_E}}} = \sqrt{\frac{3}{4}} = 0.866
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The body is momentarily at rest at the maximum distance, therefore its energy there is purely potential, and conservation of mechanical energy between the surface and that point gives the balance written above. Isolating $v_0^2$ leaves the difference of the two potential terms, equal to $\dfrac{3}{4}\dfrac{GM_E}{R_E}$.

The launch speed is $9.69\,\mathrm{km/s}$, that is $86.6\%$ of the escape speed. The ratio is obtained without any numbers, since both speeds contain the same factor $\sqrt{\dfrac{GM_E}{R_E}}$, and it shows how close to escape a body must already be in order to reach four radii.

The energy of the body is $E = -\dfrac{GM_Em}{4R_E}$, which is negative, hence the motion is bound and the diagram has a turning point. Raising the launch speed by a further $15\%$ to $11.2\,\mathrm{km/s}$ would make $E = 0$ and remove that turning point altogether.

</div>
