## Exercise 1

A simple pendulum has length $L = 0.80\,\mathrm{m}$ and swings with small amplitude. Determine its period, the length a pendulum must have for a period of $1.00\,\mathrm{s}$, and the period the first pendulum would have on the Moon, where $g_{\mathrm{M}} = 1.62\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$.

$$
T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{L}{g}}
$$
$$
T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{0.80\,\mathrm{m}}{9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2}}} = 2\pi \cdot 0.2856\,\mathrm{s} = 1.79\,\mathrm{s}
$$
$$
L = \frac{g\,T^2}{4\pi^2} = \frac{9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2} \cdot (1.00\,\mathrm{s})^2}{39.48} = 0.248\,\mathrm{m}
$$
$$
T_{\mathrm{M}} = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{0.80\,\mathrm{m}}{1.62\,\mathrm{m/s^2}}} = 2\pi \cdot 0.7027\,\mathrm{s} = 4.42\,\mathrm{s}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The amplitude is small, therefore $\sin\theta$ may be replaced by $\theta$ and the motion is simple harmonic with $T = 2\pi\sqrt{\dfrac{L}{g}}$. The mass of the bob does not appear, hence it need not be given.

The second question inverts the same relation, and the length comes out close to a quarter of a metre, since the period goes with the square root of the length and $1.00\,\mathrm{s}$ is about $0.56$ times $1.79\,\mathrm{s}$.

On the Moon the length is unchanged and $g$ is smaller, therefore the period is **longer**. The ratio of the two periods is $\sqrt{\dfrac{9.81}{1.62}} = 2.46$, and $2.46 \cdot 1.79\,\mathrm{s} = 4.42\,\mathrm{s}$ confirms the result.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A uniform rod of length $L = 1.20\,\mathrm{m}$ swings in a vertical plane about a horizontal axis through one of its ends, with negligible friction and small amplitude. Determine its period and compare it with the period of a simple pendulum of the same length.

$$
I = \frac{1}{12}mL^2 + m\left(\frac{L}{2}\right)^2 = \frac{1}{3}mL^2 ,
\qquad
d = \frac{L}{2}
$$
$$
T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{I}{m\,g\,d}} = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{\frac{1}{3}mL^2}{m\,g\,\frac{L}{2}}} = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{2L}{3g}}
$$
$$
T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{2 \cdot 1.20\,\mathrm{m}}{3 \cdot 9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2}}} = 2\pi\sqrt{0.08155\,\mathrm{s^2}} = 1.79\,\mathrm{s}
$$
$$
L_{\mathrm{eq}} = \frac{2L}{3} = 0.800\,\mathrm{m} ,
\qquad
T_{\mathrm{simple}} = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{1.20\,\mathrm{m}}{9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2}}} = 2.20\,\mathrm{s}
$$

::grafico{assi=0 uguale=1 linee="-1.6,0 1.6,0" poligoni="0.054,0.025 1.406,-2.875 1.298,-2.925 -0.054,-0.025" segmenti="0,0,0,-3.2" punti="0,0,pieno;0.676,-1.45,pieno" etichette="0.16,-1.05,θ;0.86,-1.36,G;1.2,-2.05,L" xmin=-1.9 xmax=2.6 ymin=-3.5 ymax=0.5 titolo="A uniform rod pivoted at one end: the weight acts at the centre $G$, at distance $d = \dfrac{L}{2}$ from the axis"}

<div class="spiegazione">

The rod is an extended body, therefore the simple-pendulum formula does not apply and the period is computed from $T = 2\pi\sqrt{\dfrac{I}{mgd}}$. The moment of inertia about the end follows from the value about the centre, $\dfrac{1}{12}mL^2$, and the **parallel-axis theorem** with $d = \dfrac{L}{2}$.

The mass cancels in the ratio $\dfrac{I}{md}$, hence the period depends on the length alone and equals $2\pi\sqrt{\dfrac{2L}{3g}}$. The equivalent length is $\dfrac{2L}{3} = 0.800\,\mathrm{m}$, therefore the rod swings exactly like a simple pendulum $0.800\,\mathrm{m}$ long.

A simple pendulum of the same length $1.20\,\mathrm{m}$ has period $2.20\,\mathrm{s}$, which is longer. The reason is that in the rod part of the mass is nearer the axis than the end, so the weight of the rod acts at a distance $\dfrac{L}{2}$ while the mass is spread out to $L$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A uniform disk of radius $R = 0.15\,\mathrm{m}$ hangs from a horizontal axis through a point on its rim and swings with small amplitude. Determine its period.

$$
I_{\mathrm{cm}} = \frac{1}{2}mR^2 ,
\qquad
d = R
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
I = \frac{1}{2}mR^2 + mR^2 = \frac{3}{2}mR^2
$$
$$
T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{\frac{3}{2}mR^2}{m\,g\,R}} = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{3R}{2g}}
$$
$$
T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{3 \cdot 0.15\,\mathrm{m}}{2 \cdot 9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2}}} = 2\pi\sqrt{0.02294\,\mathrm{s^2}} = 0.952\,\mathrm{s}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The axis passes through a point of the rim, therefore the distance from the centre of mass to the axis is the radius, $d = R$. The moment of inertia about the axis follows from the value about the centre, $\dfrac{1}{2}mR^2$, by the **parallel-axis theorem**.

The mass cancels again, hence the period depends on the radius alone, and the equivalent length is $\dfrac{3R}{2} = 0.225\,\mathrm{m}$. A simple pendulum of length $R = 0.15\,\mathrm{m}$ would have period $0.777\,\mathrm{s}$, therefore spreading the mass over the disk **lengthens** the period.

</div>

## Exercise 4

A body of mass $m = 2.0\,\mathrm{kg}$ and irregular shape is pivoted on a horizontal axis at distance $d = 0.25\,\mathrm{m}$ from its centre of mass, and the measured period of its small oscillations is $T = 1.40\,\mathrm{s}$. Determine its moment of inertia about that axis, and about a parallel axis through the centre of mass.

$$
T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{I}{m\,g\,d}}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
I = \frac{m\,g\,d\,T^2}{4\pi^2}
$$
$$
I = \frac{2.0\,\mathrm{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2} \cdot 0.25\,\mathrm{m} \cdot (1.40\,\mathrm{s})^2}{39.48} = \frac{9.614\,\mathrm{kg\cdot m^2}}{39.48} = 0.244\,\mathrm{kg\cdot m^2}
$$
$$
I_{\mathrm{cm}} = I - m\,d^2 = 0.244\,\mathrm{kg\cdot m^2} - 2.0\,\mathrm{kg} \cdot (0.25\,\mathrm{m})^2 = 0.119\,\mathrm{kg\cdot m^2}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The shape of the body is unknown, therefore the moment of inertia cannot be computed from its geometry and the period is used instead. Inverting the formula of the physical pendulum gives $I$ from quantities that are all measurable with a balance, a ruler and a stopwatch, which is the standard experimental method.

The parallel-axis theorem then transfers the result to the axis through the centre of mass, by subtracting $md^2 = 0.125\,\mathrm{kg\cdot m^2}$. The value obtained is **positive**, as it must be, and it is smaller than $I$, since among all parallel axes the one through the centre of mass gives the smallest moment of inertia.

</div>
