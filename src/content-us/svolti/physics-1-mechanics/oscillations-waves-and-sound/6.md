## Exercise 1

A transverse wave on a long string is described by
$$
y(x, t) = 0.020\sin(4.0\,x - 60\,t) ,
$$
with $x$ and $y$ in meters and $t$ in seconds. Determine the amplitude, the wavelength, the frequency, the period, the speed and the sense of travel, and the maximum transverse speed of a particle of the string.

$$
y(x,t) = A\sin(kx - \omega t)
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
A = 0.020\,\mathrm{m},
\qquad
k = 4.0\,\mathrm{rad/m},
\qquad
\omega = 60\,\mathrm{rad/s}
$$
$$
\lambda = \frac{2\pi}{k} = \frac{2\pi}{4.0\,\mathrm{rad/m}} = 1.6\,\mathrm{m}
$$
$$
f = \frac{\omega}{2\pi} = \frac{60\,\mathrm{rad/s}}{2\pi} = 9.5\,\mathrm{Hz} ,
\qquad
T = \frac{1}{f} = 0.105\,\mathrm{s}
$$
$$
v = \frac{\omega}{k} = \frac{60\,\mathrm{rad/s}}{4.0\,\mathrm{rad/m}} = 15\,\mathrm{m/s}
\quad\text{toward increasing } x
$$
$$
v_{y,\max} = \omega A = 60\,\mathrm{rad/s} \cdot 0.020\,\mathrm{m} = 1.2\,\mathrm{m/s}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The given function has the form $A\sin(kx - \omega t)$, therefore the wave number and the angular frequency are read off as the coefficients of $x$ and of $t$. The wavelength and the frequency follow from $k = \dfrac{2\pi}{\lambda}$ and $\omega = 2\pi f$.

The two terms inside the sine have opposite signs, hence the phase stays constant when $x$ grows together with $t$, and the wave travels toward increasing $x$ with speed $\dfrac{\omega}{k}$. The same value follows from $v = \lambda f = 1.571\,\mathrm{m} \cdot 9.549\,\mathrm{Hz} = 15\,\mathrm{m/s}$.

Each particle of the string performs simple harmonic motion in the transverse direction with amplitude $A$ and angular frequency $\omega$, therefore its maximum speed is $\omega A = 1.2\,\mathrm{m/s}$. That quantity is **different** from the speed of the wave, which here is more than ten times larger.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A string has linear mass density $\mu = 5.0\,\mathrm{g/m}$ and is kept under a tension of $80\,\mathrm{N}$. Determine the speed of a transverse wave on it, and the tension that would double that speed.

$$
v = \sqrt{\frac{F_T}{\mu}}
$$
$$
\mu = 5.0\,\frac{\mathrm{g}}{\mathrm{m}} = 5.0 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{kg/m}
$$
$$
v = \sqrt{\frac{80\,\mathrm{N}}{5.0 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{kg/m}}} = \sqrt{1.6 \cdot 10^{4}\,\mathrm{m^2/s^2}} = 1.3 \cdot 10^{2}\,\mathrm{m/s}
$$
$$
v' = 2v
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
F_T' = \mu\,v'^2 = 4\,\mu\,v^2 = 4\,F_T = 320\,\mathrm{N}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The linear mass density is converted to kilograms per meter before any substitution, since the formula is written in SI units. The unit of the result is correct, because $\dfrac{\mathrm{N}}{\mathrm{kg/m}} = \dfrac{\mathrm{kg\cdot m/s^2 \cdot m}}{\mathrm{kg}} = \mathrm{m^2/s^2}$.

The speed goes with the square root of the tension, therefore doubling the speed requires **four times** the tension. The same relation explains why the speed cannot be raised indefinitely: the tension would reach the breaking load of the string.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A string of length $L = 0.65\,\mathrm{m}$ and linear mass density $\mu = 1.2\,\mathrm{g/m}$ is fixed at both ends and kept under a tension of $90\,\mathrm{N}$. Determine the speed of the waves on it, the fundamental frequency, the frequencies of the second and third harmonics, and the wavelength of the third harmonic.

$$
v = \sqrt{\frac{F_T}{\mu}} = \sqrt{\frac{90\,\mathrm{N}}{1.2 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{kg/m}}} = \sqrt{7.5 \cdot 10^{4}\,\mathrm{m^2/s^2}} = 274\,\mathrm{m/s}
$$
$$
f_n = n\,\frac{v}{2L} ,
\qquad
\lambda_n = \frac{2L}{n}
$$
$$
f_1 = \frac{274\,\mathrm{m/s}}{2 \cdot 0.65\,\mathrm{m}} = 211\,\mathrm{Hz}
$$
$$
f_2 = 2f_1 = 421\,\mathrm{Hz} ,
\qquad
f_3 = 3f_1 = 632\,\mathrm{Hz}
$$
$$
\lambda_3 = \frac{2 \cdot 0.65\,\mathrm{m}}{3} = 0.433\,\mathrm{m}
$$

::grafico{curve="t,sin(pi*t/0.65),0,0.65;t,sin(2*pi*t/0.65),0,0.65;t,sin(3*pi*t/0.65),0,0.65" legenda="n = 1;n = 2;n = 3" nomi="x,y" punti="0,0,pieno;0.65,0,pieno" etichette="0.665,-0.22,L" xmin=-0.08 xmax=0.82 ymin=-1.35 ymax=1.5 titolo="The first three modes of the string, with $x$ in meters: the $n$-th mode has $n$ half-wavelengths between the two fixed ends"}

<div class="spiegazione">

The speed of the waves depends on the string and on its tension alone, therefore it is computed first and it is the same for every mode. The two ends are fixed, hence each of them must be a node, and only the wavelengths $\lambda_n = \dfrac{2L}{n}$ fit on the string.

The frequencies follow from $f_n = \dfrac{v}{\lambda_n} = n\dfrac{v}{2L}$, therefore they are integer multiples of the **fundamental** $f_1 = 211\,\mathrm{Hz}$. The third harmonic has three half-wavelengths between the ends, hence $\lambda_3 = \dfrac{2L}{3} = 0.433\,\mathrm{m}$, and $v = \lambda_3 f_3 = 0.433\,\mathrm{m} \cdot 632\,\mathrm{Hz} = 274\,\mathrm{m/s}$ confirms the speed.

</div>

## Exercise 4

A guitar string of length $L = 0.64\,\mathrm{m}$ and linear mass density $\mu = 0.40\,\mathrm{g/m}$ is to sound the fundamental frequency $f_1 = 330\,\mathrm{Hz}$. Determine the required tension, and the point at which the player must press the string, with the tension unchanged, to obtain the fundamental frequency $440\,\mathrm{Hz}$.

$$
f_1 = \frac{v}{2L}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
v = 2\,L\,f_1 = 2 \cdot 0.64\,\mathrm{m} \cdot 330\,\mathrm{Hz} = 422\,\mathrm{m/s}
$$
$$
v = \sqrt{\frac{F_T}{\mu}}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
F_T = \mu\,v^2 = 4.0 \cdot 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{kg/m} \cdot (422.4\,\mathrm{m/s})^2 = 71\,\mathrm{N}
$$
$$
L' = \frac{v}{2f_1'} = \frac{422.4\,\mathrm{m/s}}{2 \cdot 440\,\mathrm{Hz}} = 0.48\,\mathrm{m}
$$
$$
L - L' = 0.64\,\mathrm{m} - 0.48\,\mathrm{m} = 0.16\,\mathrm{m}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The required fundamental frequency fixes the wave speed, since the vibrating length is given and $\lambda_1 = 2L$. The speed then fixes the tension through $F_T = \mu v^2$, and the value obtained, $71\,\mathrm{N}$, is of the order of the tension carried by a single string of a real instrument.

Pressing the string shortens the vibrating part without changing the tension, therefore the speed stays at $422\,\mathrm{m/s}$ and the new fundamental frequency is $\dfrac{v}{2L'}$. Imposing $440\,\mathrm{Hz}$ gives $L' = 0.48\,\mathrm{m}$, hence the finger must be placed $0.16\,\mathrm{m}$ from the fixed end that is left free of the vibrating part.

The ratio of the two frequencies is $\dfrac{440}{330} = \dfrac{4}{3}$, and the ratio of the lengths is $\dfrac{0.64}{0.48} = \dfrac{4}{3}$, which confirms that frequency and vibrating length are **inversely proportional** at constant tension.

</div>
