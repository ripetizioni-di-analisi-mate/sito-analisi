## Exercise 1

A block of pine of density $\rho_b = 5.2 \times 10^{2}\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}$ and height $H = 0.12\,\mathrm{m}$ floats in fresh water, $\rho_f = 1.00 \times 10^{3}\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}$, with its faces horizontal and vertical. Compute the fraction of the volume below the surface and the depth of the submerged part.

::grafico{assi=0 uguale=1 poligoni="0,0 6,0 6,3 0,3" linee="0,4.4 0,0 6,0 6,4.4;2.2,2.06 3.8,2.06 3.8,3.86 2.2,3.86 2.2,2.06" segmenti="0.4,3,5.6,3;4.1,2.06,4.1,3;4.7,2.06,4.7,3.86" etichette="4.2,2.45,d;4.85,3.4,H;0.25,2.6,water" xmin=-0.4 xmax=6.6 ymin=-0.5 ymax=4.9 titolo="The block floats with the depth $d = \dfrac{\rho_b}{\rho_f}H$ below the free surface"}

$$
\rho_f\,g\,V_{\mathrm{sub}} = \rho_b\,g\,V
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
\frac{V_{\mathrm{sub}}}{V} = \frac{\rho_b}{\rho_f},
\qquad
d = \frac{\rho_b}{\rho_f}\,H
$$
$$
\frac{V_{\mathrm{sub}}}{V} = \frac{5.2 \times 10^{2}}{1.00 \times 10^{3}} = 0.52
$$
$$
d = 0.52 \cdot 0.12\,\mathrm{m} = 6.2 \times 10^{-2}\,\mathrm{m} = 6.2\,\mathrm{cm}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The block floats at rest, therefore the buoyant force balances the weight. Archimedes' principle gives the buoyant force as the weight of the displaced fluid, $\rho_f g V_{\mathrm{sub}}$, where $V_{\mathrm{sub}}$ is the volume of the part below the surface, and the weight is $\rho_b g V$. Equating the two gives the **submerged fraction** $\dfrac{\rho_b}{\rho_f} = 0.52$, which depends on the densities alone and not on the size of the block.

The block has a constant horizontal cross-section, hence the volume below the surface is proportional to the depth of the submerged part, and the submerged depth is the same fraction of the height, $d = 6.2\,\mathrm{cm}$. The remaining $5.8\,\mathrm{cm}$ stay above the water.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A raft is built of wood of density $\rho_b = 6.0 \times 10^{2}\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}$, with a horizontal area $A = 2.4\,\mathrm{m^2}$ and a thickness $t = 0.20\,\mathrm{m}$, and floats in fresh water. Compute the mass of the raft and the largest load it can carry before its upper face reaches the level of the water.

$$
V = A\,t,
\qquad
m_r = \rho_b\,V,
\qquad
(m_r + m_c)\,g = \rho_f\,g\,V
$$
$$
m_c = (\rho_f - \rho_b)\,A\,t
$$
$$
V = 2.4\,\mathrm{m^2} \cdot 0.20\,\mathrm{m} = 0.48\,\mathrm{m^3},
\qquad
m_r = 6.0 \times 10^{2}\,\mathrm{kg/m^3} \cdot 0.48\,\mathrm{m^3} = 2.9 \times 10^{2}\,\mathrm{kg}
$$
$$
m_c = (1.00 \times 10^{3} - 6.0 \times 10^{2})\,\mathrm{kg/m^3} \cdot 0.48\,\mathrm{m^3} = 1.9 \times 10^{2}\,\mathrm{kg}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The raft alone floats with the fraction $\dfrac{\rho_b}{\rho_f} = 0.60$ of its volume below the surface, hence $0.12\,\mathrm{m}$ of its thickness are in the water and $0.08\,\mathrm{m}$ stand above it. Adding a load pushes the raft down, and the limiting situation is the one in which the upper face is level with the water, because beyond it the displaced volume cannot increase.

In that situation the displaced volume is the whole volume of the raft, therefore the available buoyant force reaches its largest value $\rho_f g V = 4.71 \times 10^{3}\,\mathrm{N}$, the weight of $480\,\mathrm{kg}$ of water. The raft itself weighs the equivalent of $288\,\mathrm{kg}$, hence the load can weigh at most the equivalent of $192\,\mathrm{kg}$, that is $1.9 \times 10^{2}\,\mathrm{kg}$.

The result is read as the difference of the two densities multiplied by the volume, so a raft of the same size built of a lighter wood would carry a **larger** load.

</div>

## Exercise 3

An iceberg of ice of density $\rho_b = 9.17 \times 10^{2}\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}$ floats in sea water of density $\rho_f = 1.025 \times 10^{3}\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}$. Compute the percentage of its volume that stands above the surface, and the total volume of an iceberg whose emerged part measures $120\,\mathrm{m^3}$.

$$
\frac{V_{\mathrm{sub}}}{V} = \frac{\rho_b}{\rho_f},
\qquad
\frac{V_{\mathrm{em}}}{V} = 1 - \frac{\rho_b}{\rho_f}
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
V = \frac{V_{\mathrm{em}}}{1 - \dfrac{\rho_b}{\rho_f}}
$$
$$
\frac{V_{\mathrm{sub}}}{V} = \frac{9.17 \times 10^{2}}{1.025 \times 10^{3}} = 0.8946,
\qquad
\frac{V_{\mathrm{em}}}{V} = 0.1054 = 10.5\%
$$
$$
V = \frac{120\,\mathrm{m^3}}{0.1054} = 1.14 \times 10^{3}\,\mathrm{m^3}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The ice floats, hence the submerged fraction is the ratio of the two densities, $0.895$. Subtracting it from $1$ gives the emerged fraction, $0.105$, therefore only $10.5\%$ of an iceberg is in sight.

The emerged volume is a known fraction of the total, so the total follows by division, $V = 1.14 \times 10^{3}\,\mathrm{m^3}$, and the submerged volume is $1.02 \times 10^{3}\,\mathrm{m^3}$.

The two densities differ by about $10\%$, and the emerged fraction is close to the same $10\%$ for that reason. In fresh water, whose density is smaller, the same ice would float with $8.3\%$ above the surface, hence an iceberg sits **lower** in a lake than in the sea.

</div>

## Exercise 4

A hydrometer is a weighted tube of mass $m = 24\,\mathrm{g}$ with a uniform stem of cross-sectional area $A = 1.5\,\mathrm{cm^2}$. It floats upright in water, $\rho_1 = 1.00 \times 10^{3}\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}$, and in ethanol, $\rho_2 = 7.9 \times 10^{2}\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}$. Compute the submerged length in each liquid and the distance between the two marks on the stem.

$$
\rho\,g\,A\,L = m\,g
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
L = \frac{m}{\rho\,A}
$$
$$
L_1 = \frac{0.024\,\mathrm{kg}}{1.00 \times 10^{3}\,\mathrm{kg/m^3} \cdot 1.5 \times 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m^2}} = 0.16\,\mathrm{m}
$$
$$
L_2 = \frac{0.024\,\mathrm{kg}}{7.9 \times 10^{2}\,\mathrm{kg/m^3} \cdot 1.5 \times 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m^2}} = 0.203\,\mathrm{m}
$$
$$
\Delta L = L_2 - L_1 = 0.203\,\mathrm{m} - 0.160\,\mathrm{m} = 4.3 \times 10^{-2}\,\mathrm{m} = 4.3\,\mathrm{cm}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The instrument floats, therefore the weight of the displaced liquid equals its weight, and the submerged part is a cylinder of base $A$ and length $L$, hence $\rho g A L = mg$. The mass and the area are properties of the instrument, so the product $\rho L$ is the same in every liquid and the submerged length is **inversely proportional** to the density.

In water the submerged length is $16.0\,\mathrm{cm}$ and in ethanol, which is lighter, it is $20.3\,\mathrm{cm}$: the hydrometer sinks deeper in the less dense liquid. The two marks are $4.3\,\mathrm{cm}$ apart, and a scale engraved between them reads the density of any liquid in that range directly.

The spacing of the marks is set by $\dfrac{m}{A}$, therefore a slender stem gives a long scale and a fine reading, which is how the sensitivity of the instrument is chosen.

</div>
