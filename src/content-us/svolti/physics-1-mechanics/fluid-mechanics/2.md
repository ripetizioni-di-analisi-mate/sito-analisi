## Exercise 1

A tank of gas is connected to one arm of an open-tube mercury manometer. The mercury stands in the open arm at a height $h = 28\,\mathrm{cm}$ above its level in the arm connected to the tank. Taking $\rho_{\mathrm{Hg}} = 1.36 \times 10^{4}\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}$ and $p_{\mathrm{atm}} = 1.013 \times 10^{5}\,\mathrm{Pa}$, compute the gauge pressure and the absolute pressure of the gas.

::grafico{assi=0 uguale=1 poligoni="1,0 4,0 4,0.6 1,0.6;1,0.6 1.6,0.6 1.6,1.6 1,1.6;3.4,0.6 4,0.6 4,4.8 3.4,4.8" linee="1,5.6 1,0 4,0 4,5.6;1.6,5.6 1.6,0.6 3.4,0.6 3.4,5.6" segmenti="4.6,1.6,4.6,4.8;1.6,1.6,4.6,1.6" punti="1.3,1.6,pieno;3.7,4.8,pieno" etichette="0.15,5.95,to the tank;3.2,5.95,open;4.75,3.1,h" xmin=-0.4 xmax=6.6 ymin=-0.5 ymax=6.4 titolo="Open-tube manometer: the two levels differ by $h$, hence the gauge pressure of the gas is $\rho_{\mathrm{Hg}} g h$"}

$$
p_{\mathrm{gas}} = p_{\mathrm{atm}} + \rho_{\mathrm{Hg}}\,g\,h
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
p_{\mathrm{gauge}} = \rho_{\mathrm{Hg}}\,g\,h
$$
$$
p_{\mathrm{gauge}} = 1.36 \times 10^{4}\,\mathrm{kg/m^3} \cdot 9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2} \cdot 0.28\,\mathrm{m} = 3.74 \times 10^{4}\,\mathrm{Pa}
$$
$$
p_{\mathrm{gas}} = 1.013 \times 10^{5}\,\mathrm{Pa} + 3.736 \times 10^{4}\,\mathrm{Pa} = 1.39 \times 10^{5}\,\mathrm{Pa}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The mercury in the tube is at rest and forms one connected body, therefore the pressure at a given level is the same in the two arms. Take the level of the mercury surface in the arm connected to the tank. Computing the pressure at that level along the connected arm gives the pressure of the gas, since the gas column above it weighs a negligible amount. Computing it along the open arm gives the atmospheric pressure plus the weight per unit area of the mercury column of height $h$.

Equating the two expressions gives $p_{\mathrm{gas}} = p_{\mathrm{atm}} + \rho_{\mathrm{Hg}} g h$, hence the reading $h$ measures the **gauge pressure** of the gas directly, $3.74 \times 10^{4}\,\mathrm{Pa}$. Adding the atmospheric pressure gives the absolute pressure $1.39 \times 10^{5}\,\mathrm{Pa}$, which is the value to use in a gas law.

</div>

## Exercise 2

At a given place and time a mercury barometer shows a column of height $h = 752\,\mathrm{mm}$. Compute the atmospheric pressure, and the height of the column that the same instrument would show if it were filled with water instead of mercury.

$$
p_{\mathrm{atm}} = \rho_{\mathrm{Hg}}\,g\,h_{\mathrm{Hg}}
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
h_{\mathrm{w}} = \frac{p_{\mathrm{atm}}}{\rho_{\mathrm{w}}\,g} = \frac{\rho_{\mathrm{Hg}}}{\rho_{\mathrm{w}}}\,h_{\mathrm{Hg}}
$$
$$
p_{\mathrm{atm}} = 1.36 \times 10^{4}\,\mathrm{kg/m^3} \cdot 9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2} \cdot 0.752\,\mathrm{m} = 1.003 \times 10^{5}\,\mathrm{Pa}
$$
$$
h_{\mathrm{w}} = \frac{1.36 \times 10^{4}}{1.00 \times 10^{3}} \cdot 0.752\,\mathrm{m} = 10.2\,\mathrm{m}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The space above the mercury in the closed end of a barometer contains no gas, therefore the pressure at the top of the column is zero. Applying the law of pressure with depth along the mercury, from the top of the column down to the free surface of the basin, gives $p_{\mathrm{atm}} = \rho_{\mathrm{Hg}} g h$, so the column of $752\,\mathrm{mm}$ corresponds to $1.003 \times 10^{5}\,\mathrm{Pa}$, slightly below the standard value.

The same atmospheric pressure supports a column of any liquid, and the height of that column is inversely proportional to the density, since the product $\rho h$ is fixed by $\dfrac{p_{\mathrm{atm}}}{g}$. Water is less dense than mercury by the factor $13.6$, hence the water column is **taller** by the same factor and reaches $10.2\,\mathrm{m}$. A barometer of that height is impractical, which is why mercury is used.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A U-tube open at both ends contains water, $\rho_{\mathrm{w}} = 1.00 \times 10^{3}\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}$. An oil that does not mix with water is poured into the left arm, and at equilibrium it forms a column of height $h_1 = 24.0\,\mathrm{cm}$ above the oil-water interface, while in the right arm the water stands $h_2 = 19.6\,\mathrm{cm}$ above the level of that interface. Compute the density of the oil.

$$
p_{\mathrm{atm}} + \rho_1 g h_1 = p_{\mathrm{atm}} + \rho_{\mathrm{w}} g h_2
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
\rho_1 = \rho_{\mathrm{w}}\,\frac{h_2}{h_1}
$$
$$
\rho_1 = 1.00 \times 10^{3}\,\mathrm{kg/m^3} \cdot \frac{19.6\,\mathrm{cm}}{24.0\,\mathrm{cm}} = 8.17 \times 10^{2}\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The level of the oil-water interface is chosen as the reference level, because it is the lowest level at which the two arms contain the same liquid, and only there can the pressures be equated. In the right arm the fluid between that level and the free surface is water, hence the pressure at the reference level is $p_{\mathrm{atm}} + \rho_{\mathrm{w}} g h_2$. In the left arm the fluid above the interface is oil, hence the pressure is $p_{\mathrm{atm}} + \rho_1 g h_1$.

The water below the interface is at rest and connected, therefore the two values are equal, the atmospheric terms cancel and $\rho_1 h_1 = \rho_{\mathrm{w}} h_2$. Solving gives $\rho_1 = 8.17 \times 10^{2}\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}$. The oil is less dense than water, and consistently its column is **taller**, since the product of density and height is the same on the two sides.

</div>
