## Exercise 1

A small sphere of mass $m = 0.020\,\mathrm{kg}$ falls from rest in a liquid that exerts a drag force $F_D = b\,v$ with $b = 0.040\,\mathrm{kg/s}$. Compute the terminal speed, the time constant and the speed after $t = 1.0\,\mathrm{s}$.

$$
m\,\frac{dv}{dt} = m g - b v
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
v(t) = v_t\left(1 - e^{-\frac{t}{\tau}}\right),
\qquad
v_t = \frac{m g}{b},
\qquad
\tau = \frac{m}{b}
$$
$$
v_t = \frac{0.020\,\mathrm{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2}}{0.040\,\mathrm{kg/s}} = 4.91\,\mathrm{m/s}
$$
$$
\tau = \frac{0.020\,\mathrm{kg}}{0.040\,\mathrm{kg/s}} = 0.50\,\mathrm{s}
$$
$$
v(1.0\,\mathrm{s}) = 4.905\,\mathrm{m/s} \cdot \left(1 - e^{-2.0}\right) = 4.905\,\mathrm{m/s} \cdot 0.8647 = 4.24\,\mathrm{m/s}
$$

::grafico{f="4.905*(1-exp(-x/0.5))" asintoti="y=4.905" punti="1,4.2412,pieno" segmenti="1,0,1,4.2412;0,4.2412,1,4.2412" nomi="t,v" xmin=-0.15 xmax=3 ymin=-0.6 ymax=6.2 etichette="2.25,5.35,v = vₜ" titolo="Speed of the sphere against time, with $v_t = 4.91\,\mathrm{m/s}$ and $\tau = 0.50\,\mathrm{s}$: after $t = 2\tau$ the speed has reached $0.865\,v_t$"}

<div class="spiegazione">

Two forces act on the sphere while it falls, its weight and the drag force, and the drag grows in proportion to the speed. The $y$ axis is taken downwards, hence Newton's second law gives $m\dfrac{dv}{dt} = m g - b v$, a first-order separable equation.

The terminal speed is the speed at which the two forces balance, obtained by setting the acceleration to zero: $v_t = \dfrac{m g}{b} = 4.91\,\mathrm{m/s}$. The time constant $\tau = \dfrac{m}{b} = 0.50\,\mathrm{s}$ measures how fast that speed is approached.

Integrating the equation with the initial condition $v(0) = 0$ gives $v(t) = v_t\left(1 - e^{-t/\tau}\right)$. The instant $t = 1.0\,\mathrm{s}$ equals $2\tau$, therefore the exponential is $e^{-2} = 0.135$ and the speed is $0.865\,v_t = 4.24\,\mathrm{m/s}$.

The sphere never reaches the terminal speed exactly, since the exponential is positive for every finite $t$, yet after $3\tau = 1.5\,\mathrm{s}$ the difference is already below $5\%$ and the motion is **uniform** for practical purposes.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A skydiver of mass $m = 80\,\mathrm{kg}$ falls in air with a drag force $F_D = D\,v^2$, with $D = 0.30\,\mathrm{kg/m}$ before the parachute opens. Compute the terminal speed, and compute the drag force and the acceleration when the speed is $v = 30\,\mathrm{m/s}$.

$$
m g - D v^2 = m\,a
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
v_t = \sqrt{\frac{m g}{D}}
$$
$$
v_t = \sqrt{\frac{80\,\mathrm{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2}}{0.30\,\mathrm{kg/m}}} = \sqrt{2616\,\mathrm{m^2/s^2}} = 51.1\,\mathrm{m/s}
$$
$$
F_D = 0.30\,\mathrm{kg/m} \cdot (30\,\mathrm{m/s})^2 = 270\,\mathrm{N}
$$
$$
a = \frac{m g - F_D}{m} = \frac{784.8\,\mathrm{N} - 270\,\mathrm{N}}{80\,\mathrm{kg}} = 6.44\,\mathrm{m/s^2}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

At the speeds of a free fall in air the drag is proportional to the square of the speed, hence the equation of motion is $m g - D v^2 = m a$. Setting the acceleration to zero gives the terminal speed $v_t = \sqrt{\dfrac{m g}{D}} = 51.1\,\mathrm{m/s}$, that is about $184\,\mathrm{km/h}$.

At $30\,\mathrm{m/s}$ the drag is $270\,\mathrm{N}$, which is $34\%$ of the weight $785\,\mathrm{N}$, therefore the net force is still directed downwards and the acceleration is $6.44\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$, smaller than $g$. The quantity $\dfrac{v}{v_t} = 0.587$ gives the same result at once, since $a = g\left(1 - \dfrac{v^2}{v_t^2}\right)$.

The drag grows with the square of the speed, hence doubling the speed multiplies it by four. A parachute multiplies $D$ by about $30$, therefore it divides the terminal speed by about $\sqrt{30} = 5.5$ and brings it near $9\,\mathrm{m/s}$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A drop of mass $m = 5.0 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{kg}$ falls from rest with a drag force $F_D = b\,v$, with $b = 0.025\,\mathrm{kg/s}$. Compute the speed and the distance fallen after $t = 0.60\,\mathrm{s}$.

$$
v_t = \frac{m g}{b},
\qquad
\tau = \frac{m}{b},
\qquad
v(t) = v_t\left(1 - e^{-\frac{t}{\tau}}\right),
\qquad
y(t) = v_t\left[\,t - \tau\left(1 - e^{-\frac{t}{\tau}}\right)\right]
$$
$$
v_t = \frac{5.0 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2}}{0.025\,\mathrm{kg/s}} = 1.962\,\mathrm{m/s},
\qquad
\tau = \frac{5.0 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{kg}}{0.025\,\mathrm{kg/s}} = 0.20\,\mathrm{s}
$$
$$
\frac{t}{\tau} = 3.0,
\qquad
1 - e^{-3.0} = 0.9502
$$
$$
v(0.60\,\mathrm{s}) = 1.962\,\mathrm{m/s} \cdot 0.9502 = 1.86\,\mathrm{m/s}
$$
$$
y(0.60\,\mathrm{s}) = 1.962\,\mathrm{m/s} \cdot \left[\,0.60\,\mathrm{s} - 0.20\,\mathrm{s} \cdot 0.9502\,\right] = 1.962\,\mathrm{m/s} \cdot 0.4100\,\mathrm{s} = 0.804\,\mathrm{m}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The drag is proportional to the speed, hence the motion is the one obtained by integrating $m\dfrac{dv}{dt} = m g - b v$ from rest. The terminal speed is $1.962\,\mathrm{m/s}$ and the time constant is $0.20\,\mathrm{s}$, therefore the interval of $0.60\,\mathrm{s}$ equals three time constants.

The speed after three time constants is $0.950\,v_t = 1.86\,\mathrm{m/s}$, hence the drop is already close to its terminal speed.

The distance is the integral of the speed, which gives $y(t) = v_t\left[\,t - \tau\left(1 - e^{-t/\tau}\right)\right] = 0.804\,\mathrm{m}$. In free fall with no drag the same interval would give $\dfrac{1}{2}g t^2 = 1.77\,\mathrm{m}$ and a speed of $5.89\,\mathrm{m/s}$, therefore the drag has **more than halved** both quantities.

</div>
