## Exercise 1

A cyclist rides $1.80\,\mathrm{km}$ east along a straight road in $4.0\,\mathrm{min}$, then turns around and rides $0.60\,\mathrm{km}$ west in $2.0\,\mathrm{min}$. Taking the $x$ axis pointing east with the origin at the starting point, find the displacement, the distance travelled, the average velocity and the average speed for the whole ride.

$$
x_1 = 1800\,\mathrm{m},
\qquad
x_2 = 1800\,\mathrm{m} - 600\,\mathrm{m} = 1200\,\mathrm{m},
\qquad
\Delta t = 240\,\mathrm{s} + 120\,\mathrm{s} = 360\,\mathrm{s}
$$
$$
\Delta x = x_2 - x_0 = 1200\,\mathrm{m},
\qquad
s = 1800\,\mathrm{m} + 600\,\mathrm{m} = 2400\,\mathrm{m}
$$
$$
v_{\mathrm{av}} = \frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t} = \frac{1200\,\mathrm{m}}{360\,\mathrm{s}} = 3.3\,\mathrm{m/s}
$$
$$
\text{average speed} = \frac{s}{\Delta t} = \frac{2400\,\mathrm{m}}{360\,\mathrm{s}} = 6.7\,\mathrm{m/s}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The two stretches are covered in opposite senses, therefore the second one is counted with a negative sign in the position: the final coordinate is $1200\,\mathrm{m}$ and not $2400\,\mathrm{m}$. The **displacement** compares only the final position with the initial one, hence it is $1200\,\mathrm{m}$ east.

The **distance travelled** is the length of the path, so the two stretches are added with their lengths, which gives $2400\,\mathrm{m}$. The average velocity uses the displacement and the average speed uses the distance travelled, therefore the two numbers differ by the factor $2$ that separates $1200\,\mathrm{m}$ from $2400\,\mathrm{m}$. Both are computed over the same total time $360\,\mathrm{s}$, since the durations of the two stretches add.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A body moves along the $x$ axis with the law of motion
$$
x(t) = 2.0 + 4.0\,t - 0.50\,t^2 ,
$$
with $x$ in meters and $t$ in seconds. Find the average velocity between $t_1 = 1.0\,\mathrm{s}$ and $t_2 = 4.0\,\mathrm{s}$, and then the average velocity and the average speed between $t_1 = 1.0\,\mathrm{s}$ and $t_3 = 6.0\,\mathrm{s}$.

$$
x(1.0\,\mathrm{s}) = 5.5\,\mathrm{m},
\qquad
x(4.0\,\mathrm{s}) = 10.0\,\mathrm{m},
\qquad
x(6.0\,\mathrm{s}) = 8.0\,\mathrm{m}
$$
$$
v_{\mathrm{av}} = \frac{x(4.0\,\mathrm{s}) - x(1.0\,\mathrm{s})}{3.0\,\mathrm{s}} = \frac{4.5\,\mathrm{m}}{3.0\,\mathrm{s}} = 1.5\,\mathrm{m/s}
$$
$$
v(t) = \frac{dx}{dt} = 4.0 - t
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
v(t) = 0 \ \text{ for } \ t = 4.0\,\mathrm{s}
$$
$$
s = \big[x(4.0\,\mathrm{s}) - x(1.0\,\mathrm{s})\big] + \big[x(4.0\,\mathrm{s}) - x(6.0\,\mathrm{s})\big] = 4.5\,\mathrm{m} + 2.0\,\mathrm{m} = 6.5\,\mathrm{m}
$$
$$
v_{\mathrm{av}} = \frac{8.0\,\mathrm{m} - 5.5\,\mathrm{m}}{5.0\,\mathrm{s}} = 0.50\,\mathrm{m/s},
\qquad
\text{average speed} = \frac{6.5\,\mathrm{m}}{5.0\,\mathrm{s}} = 1.3\,\mathrm{m/s}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The average velocity requires only the two end positions, which are obtained by substituting the two instants into the law of motion. Between $1.0\,\mathrm{s}$ and $4.0\,\mathrm{s}$ the displacement is $4.5\,\mathrm{m}$ over $3.0\,\mathrm{s}$, hence $v_{\mathrm{av}} = 1.5\,\mathrm{m/s}$.

For the distance travelled the sense of the motion has to be checked, therefore the velocity is computed as the derivative of the law of motion. The velocity $4.0 - t$ is positive for $t < 4.0\,\mathrm{s}$ and negative afterwards, so the body advances up to $x = 10.0\,\mathrm{m}$ and then **reverses**. The path is made of two stretches, $4.5\,\mathrm{m}$ forward and $2.0\,\mathrm{m}$ backward, which add to $6.5\,\mathrm{m}$.

The displacement over the same interval is only $2.5\,\mathrm{m}$, since the backward stretch cancels part of the forward one. The average speed is therefore larger than the magnitude of the average velocity, as happens whenever the body reverses.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A car covers the first half of a straight route at the constant speed $v_1 = 90\,\mathrm{km/h}$ and the second half at $v_2 = 60\,\mathrm{km/h}$. Find the average speed over the whole route. Find then the average speed of a car that holds the same two speeds for equal *times*.

$$
t_1 = \frac{d}{v_1},
\qquad
t_2 = \frac{d}{v_2},
\qquad
\text{average speed} = \frac{2d}{t_1 + t_2} = \frac{2d}{\dfrac{d}{v_1} + \dfrac{d}{v_2}} = \frac{2\,v_1 v_2}{v_1 + v_2}
$$
$$
\text{average speed} = \frac{2 \cdot 90\,\mathrm{km/h} \cdot 60\,\mathrm{km/h}}{150\,\mathrm{km/h}} = 72\,\mathrm{km/h} = 20\,\mathrm{m/s}
$$
$$
\text{equal times:}
\qquad
\text{average speed} = \frac{v_1 \tau + v_2 \tau}{2\tau} = \frac{v_1 + v_2}{2} = 75\,\mathrm{km/h}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Each half has length $d$, therefore the time spent on it is $\dfrac{d}{v}$ with the speed of that half. The total time is the sum of the two times and the total distance is $2d$, hence the average speed is $\dfrac{2 v_1 v_2}{v_1 + v_2}$, in which the length $d$ cancels. The result $72\,\mathrm{km/h}$ is therefore the same for any route length.

The value is smaller than the arithmetic mean $75\,\mathrm{km/h}$ because the slower half takes longer, so the speed $60\,\mathrm{km/h}$ is held for a longer time than $90\,\mathrm{km/h}$ and weighs more in the average. When instead the two speeds are held for equal times $\tau$, the two distances are $v_1 \tau$ and $v_2 \tau$ and the average speed is exactly the **arithmetic mean** $75\,\mathrm{km/h}$.

The comparison shows what the definition already states: the average speed is the total distance divided by the total time, and the weights are the times.

</div>
