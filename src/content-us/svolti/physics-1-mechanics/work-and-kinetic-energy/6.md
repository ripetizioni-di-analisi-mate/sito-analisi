## Exercise 1

A constant force $\vec{F} = (3.0\,\hat{\imath} + 4.0\,\hat{\jmath})\,\mathrm{N}$ acts on a body that moves from the point $A = (0, 0)$ to the point $B = (2.0,\ 5.0)\,\mathrm{m}$. Compute the work done by the force, and state whether the result changes if the body reaches $B$ along a curved path.

$$
W = \int_{A}^{B} \vec{F} \cdot d\vec{r} = \int_{A}^{B} (F_x\,dx + F_y\,dy)
$$
$$
W = F_x \int_{0}^{2.0} dx + F_y \int_{0}^{5.0} dy = F_x\,\Delta x + F_y\,\Delta y = \vec{F} \cdot \Delta\vec{r}
$$
$$
W = 3.0\,\mathrm{N} \cdot 2.0\,\mathrm{m} + 4.0\,\mathrm{N} \cdot 5.0\,\mathrm{m} = 6.0\,\mathrm{J} + 20\,\mathrm{J} = 26\,\mathrm{J}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The components $F_x$ and $F_y$ are constant, therefore they can be taken out of the two integrals, and what remains are the integrals of $dx$ and of $dy$, which give the two components of the total displacement.

The work is thus $\vec{F} \cdot \Delta\vec{r} = 26\,\mathrm{J}$, and it depends on the endpoints alone. A curved path joining the same two points gives the same displacement $\Delta\vec{r}$, hence the **same work**.

The magnitude of the force is $5.0\,\mathrm{N}$ and the magnitude of the displacement is $\sqrt{29}\,\mathrm{m} = 5.39\,\mathrm{m}$, so the relation $W = F\,d\cos\varphi$ gives $\cos\varphi = \dfrac{26}{5.0 \cdot 5.39} = 0.966$ and $\varphi = 15.1^\circ$, which is the angle between the force and the displacement.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A bead of mass $m = 1.2\,\mathrm{kg}$ slides along a curved wire of any shape, going from a point at height $y_1 = 2.5\,\mathrm{m}$ to a point at height $y_2 = 0.40\,\mathrm{m}$ above the floor. Compute the work done by the weight, and compute the speed the bead reaches if the wire is smooth and the bead starts from rest.

$$
\vec{P} = -m g\,\hat{\jmath}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
W_P = \int_{y_1}^{y_2} (-m g)\,dy = -m g\,(y_2 - y_1) = m g\,(y_1 - y_2)
$$
$$
W_P = 1.2\,\mathrm{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2} \cdot (2.5 - 0.40)\,\mathrm{m} = 24.7\,\mathrm{J}
$$
$$
W_{\text{tot}} = W_P = \frac{1}{2}m v^2
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
v = \sqrt{2\,g\,(y_1 - y_2)} = \sqrt{2 \cdot 9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2} \cdot 2.1\,\mathrm{m}} = 6.42\,\mathrm{m/s}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The weight is a constant force directed downwards, therefore in the line integral only the term $-m g\,dy$ survives and the result is $m g\,(y_1 - y_2)$, which depends on the two heights alone. The shape of the wire does not enter the computation, as the **work done by the weight** states.

The wire is smooth, so the only other force is the reaction of the wire, which is perpendicular to the wire at every point and therefore perpendicular to the displacement, hence it does no work. The total work is the work of the weight.

The bead starts from rest, so the work-energy theorem gives $v = 6.42\,\mathrm{m/s}$, the same speed a body dropped from a height of $2.1\,\mathrm{m}$ would reach.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A body moves in the $xy$ plane from the origin to the point $B = (2.0,\ 3.0)\,\mathrm{m}$ along the parabola $y = \dfrac{3}{4}x^2$, under the force
$$
\vec{F} = (\alpha\,x^2)\,\hat{\imath} + (\beta\,y)\,\hat{\jmath},
\qquad
\alpha = 2.0\,\mathrm{N/m^2},
\qquad
\beta = 3.0\,\mathrm{N/m} .
$$
Compute the work done by the force along that path.

$$
W = \int_{A}^{B} (F_x\,dx + F_y\,dy) = \int_{0}^{2.0} \alpha\,x^2\,dx + \int_{0}^{3.0} \beta\,y\,dy
$$
$$
W = \frac{\alpha\,x_B^3}{3} + \frac{\beta\,y_B^2}{2}
$$
$$
W = \frac{2.0\,\mathrm{N/m^2} \cdot (2.0\,\mathrm{m})^3}{3} + \frac{3.0\,\mathrm{N/m} \cdot (3.0\,\mathrm{m})^2}{2} = 5.33\,\mathrm{J} + 13.5\,\mathrm{J} = 18.8\,\mathrm{J}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The component $F_x$ depends on $x$ alone and the component $F_y$ depends on $y$ alone, therefore the two terms of the line integral separate into an integral in $x$ and an integral in $y$, each with the limits given by the coordinates of the endpoints.

The equation of the parabola is not needed, since it would only be required to express $y$ in terms of $x$ inside the second integral, and that integral already contains $y$ alone. For the same reason the work is the same along any path joining the two points, so this force is conservative, as the chapter on potential energy and conservation of energy develops.

The two contributions are $5.33\,\mathrm{J}$ and $13.5\,\mathrm{J}$, and the total work is $18.8\,\mathrm{J}$.

</div>

## Exercise 4

A body moves along the quarter circle of radius $R = 1.5\,\mathrm{m}$ centred at the origin, from the point $A = (R, 0)$ to the point $B = (0, R)$, under the force
$$
\vec{F} = k\,(-y\,\hat{\imath} + x\,\hat{\jmath}),
\qquad
k = 4.0\,\mathrm{N/m} .
$$
Compute the work done by the force along that path.

::grafico{uguale=1 curve="1.5*cos(t),1.5*sin(t),0,1.5708" punti="1.5,0,pieno,A;0,1.5,pieno,B" vettori="1.5,0,1.5,0.8,F;1.0607,1.0607,0.4947,1.6267,F;0,1.5,-0.8,1.5,F" xmin=-1.7 xmax=2.3 ymin=-0.7 ymax=2.3 titolo="The force is tangent to the circle at every point and has the constant magnitude $k R$, hence the work is $k R$ times the length of the arc"}

$$
x = R\cos\theta,
\qquad
y = R\sin\theta,
\qquad
d\vec{r} = R\,(-\sin\theta\,\hat{\imath} + \cos\theta\,\hat{\jmath})\,d\theta
$$
$$
\vec{F} = k R\,(-\sin\theta\,\hat{\imath} + \cos\theta\,\hat{\jmath})
$$
$$
\vec{F} \cdot d\vec{r} = k R^2\,(\sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta)\,d\theta = k R^2\,d\theta
$$
$$
W = \int_{0}^{\pi/2} k R^2\,d\theta = \frac{\pi}{2}\,k R^2
$$
$$
W = \frac{\pi}{2} \cdot 4.0\,\mathrm{N/m} \cdot (1.5\,\mathrm{m})^2 = 14.1\,\mathrm{J}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The path is a circle, therefore it is described by the angle $\theta$ measured from the positive $x$ axis, and the element of displacement is obtained by differentiating the two coordinates with respect to $\theta$.

Substituting $x$ and $y$ in the components of the force shows that $\vec{F}$ is $k R$ times the unit vector tangent to the circle, hence the force is tangent to the path at every point and $\cos\varphi = 1$ in the formula $dW = F\cos\varphi\,ds$. The scalar product reduces to $k R^2\,d\theta$ by the identity $\sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta = 1$.

The integral over a quarter turn gives $W = \dfrac{\pi}{2}k R^2 = 14.1\,\mathrm{J}$. The same value follows from $W = F\,s$ with $F = k R = 6.0\,\mathrm{N}$ constant and $s = \dfrac{\pi R}{2} = 2.36\,\mathrm{m}$, the length of the arc.

</div>
