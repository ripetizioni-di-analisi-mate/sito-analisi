## Exercise 1

A car of mass $m = 1400\,\mathrm{kg}$ increases its speed from $v_1 = 12\,\mathrm{m/s}$ to $v_2 = 28\,\mathrm{m/s}$ along a straight horizontal road, covering the distance $d = 180\,\mathrm{m}$. Compute the change in kinetic energy and the average magnitude of the resultant force along the road.

$$
K = \frac{1}{2}m v^2,
\qquad
W_{\text{tot}} = \Delta K = K_2 - K_1,
\qquad
W_{\text{tot}} = R\,d
$$
$$
\Delta K = \frac{1}{2}m\,(v_2^2 - v_1^2),
\qquad
R = \frac{\Delta K}{d} = \frac{m\,(v_2^2 - v_1^2)}{2\,d}
$$
$$
K_1 = \frac{1}{2} \cdot 1400\,\mathrm{kg} \cdot (12\,\mathrm{m/s})^2 = 1.008 \times 10^{5}\,\mathrm{J},
\qquad
K_2 = \frac{1}{2} \cdot 1400\,\mathrm{kg} \cdot (28\,\mathrm{m/s})^2 = 5.488 \times 10^{5}\,\mathrm{J}
$$
$$
\Delta K = 5.488 \times 10^{5}\,\mathrm{J} - 1.008 \times 10^{5}\,\mathrm{J} = 4.48 \times 10^{5}\,\mathrm{J}
$$
$$
R = \frac{4.48 \times 10^{5}\,\mathrm{J}}{180\,\mathrm{m}} = 2.49 \times 10^{3}\,\mathrm{N}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The kinetic energy is computed from its definition at the two ends of the stretch, and the difference is the change $\Delta K = 4.48 \times 10^{5}\,\mathrm{J}$. The road is horizontal, so the weight and the normal force are perpendicular to the displacement and do no work, and the total work is the work of the resultant of the horizontal forces.

By the **work-energy theorem** the total work equals the change in kinetic energy. The resultant is taken constant along the road, therefore its work is $R\,d$ and the equality gives $R = 2.49 \times 10^{3}\,\mathrm{N}$.

The time taken is not needed. The data give a distance and two speeds, and the theorem relates exactly these quantities.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A bucket of mass $m = 12\,\mathrm{kg}$ hangs at rest from a cable of negligible mass, and the cable then raises it through the height $h = 4.0\,\mathrm{m}$ keeping the constant tension $T = 150\,\mathrm{N}$. Air resistance is negligible. Compute the speed of the bucket at the end of the rise.

$$
W_T = T\,h,
\qquad
W_P = -m g\,h
$$
$$
W_{\text{tot}} = (T - m g)\,h = \frac{1}{2}m v^2 - 0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
v = \sqrt{\frac{2\,(T - m g)\,h}{m}}
$$
$$
m g = 12\,\mathrm{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2} = 117.7\,\mathrm{N}
$$
$$
W_T = 150\,\mathrm{N} \cdot 4.0\,\mathrm{m} = 600\,\mathrm{J},
\qquad
W_P = -117.72\,\mathrm{N} \cdot 4.0\,\mathrm{m} = -471\,\mathrm{J}
$$
$$
W_{\text{tot}} = 600\,\mathrm{J} - 470.9\,\mathrm{J} = 129\,\mathrm{J}
$$
$$
v = \sqrt{\frac{2 \cdot 129.1\,\mathrm{J}}{12\,\mathrm{kg}}} = 4.64\,\mathrm{m/s}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Two forces act on the bucket, the tension of the cable and the weight, and both of them are vertical while the displacement is vertical and upward. The tension has the sense of the displacement, hence its work is $+T\,h = 600\,\mathrm{J}$; the weight has the opposite sense, hence by the **work done by the weight** its work is $-m g\,h = -471\,\mathrm{J}$.

The total work is the sum of the two, $129\,\mathrm{J}$, and it is positive because the tension exceeds the weight. The bucket starts from rest, so $K_1 = 0$ and the **work-energy theorem** reduces to $W_{\text{tot}} = \dfrac{1}{2}m v^2$, from which the final speed is $4.64\,\mathrm{m/s}$.

The same result follows from Newton's second law with $a = \dfrac{T - m g}{m} = 2.69\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$ and from $v^2 = 2\,a\,h$, which confirms the value.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A car of mass $m = 1500\,\mathrm{kg}$ traveling at $v_1 = 20\,\mathrm{m/s}$ is brought to rest by a constant braking force of magnitude $F = 6.0 \times 10^{3}\,\mathrm{N}$. Compute the stopping distance, and compute it again for the same car traveling at $30\,\mathrm{m/s}$.

$$
W_{\text{tot}} = -F\,d,
\qquad
\Delta K = 0 - \frac{1}{2}m v_1^2
$$
$$
-F\,d = -\frac{1}{2}m v_1^2
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
d = \frac{m\,v_1^2}{2\,F}
$$
$$
d_1 = \frac{1500\,\mathrm{kg} \cdot (20\,\mathrm{m/s})^2}{2 \cdot 6.0 \times 10^{3}\,\mathrm{N}} = 50\,\mathrm{m}
$$
$$
d_2 = \frac{1500\,\mathrm{kg} \cdot (30\,\mathrm{m/s})^2}{2 \cdot 6.0 \times 10^{3}\,\mathrm{N}} = 112.5\,\mathrm{m}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The braking force is opposite to the displacement, hence its work is $-F\,d$, and it is the only force doing work because the weight and the normal force are perpendicular to the road. The car stops, so the final kinetic energy is zero and the change in kinetic energy is $-\dfrac{1}{2}m v_1^2$.

The **work-energy theorem** then gives the stopping distance $d = \dfrac{m v_1^2}{2F}$, which is $50\,\mathrm{m}$ at $20\,\mathrm{m/s}$ and $112.5\,\mathrm{m}$ at $30\,\mathrm{m/s}$.

The distance is proportional to the square of the initial speed, therefore multiplying the speed by $\dfrac{3}{2}$ multiplies the stopping distance by $\dfrac{9}{4} = 2.25$, and the two values satisfy $\dfrac{112.5}{50} = 2.25$.

</div>

## Exercise 4

A block of mass $m = 4.0\,\mathrm{kg}$ is launched up a frictionless inclined plane that makes the angle $\theta = 30^\circ$ with the horizontal, with the initial speed $v_0 = 6.0\,\mathrm{m/s}$ directed along the plane. Compute the distance traveled along the plane before the block stops.

$$
W_N = 0,
\qquad
W_P = -m g L\sin\theta
$$
$$
W_{\text{tot}} = \Delta K = 0 - \frac{1}{2}m v_0^2
$$
$$
-m g L\sin\theta = -\frac{1}{2}m v_0^2
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
L = \frac{v_0^2}{2\,g\sin\theta}
$$
$$
L = \frac{(6.0\,\mathrm{m/s})^2}{2 \cdot 9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2} \cdot \sin 30^\circ} = \frac{36\,\mathrm{m^2/s^2}}{9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2}} = 3.67\,\mathrm{m}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The plane is frictionless, therefore the only forces are the weight and the normal force, and the normal force is perpendicular to the displacement at every point, so it does no work. The block climbs the height $h = L\sin\theta$, hence by the **work done by the weight** the work of the weight is $-m g L\sin\theta$, negative because the block rises.

The block stops, so the final kinetic energy is zero and the change is $-\dfrac{1}{2}m v_0^2$. Setting the total work equal to this change and simplifying the mass gives $L = \dfrac{v_0^2}{2 g\sin\theta} = 3.67\,\mathrm{m}$.

The mass disappears from the formula, therefore two blocks of different mass launched with the same speed travel the same distance along the plane. The rise in height is $h = L\sin 30^\circ = 1.83\,\mathrm{m}$, which is the same height a body thrown vertically upwards at $6.0\,\mathrm{m/s}$ would reach.

</div>
