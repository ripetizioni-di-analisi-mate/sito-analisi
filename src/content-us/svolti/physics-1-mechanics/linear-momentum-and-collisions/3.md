## Exercise 1

A sphere of mass $m_1 = 0.300\,\mathrm{kg}$ moving at $v_1 = 4.00\,\mathrm{m/s}$ on a horizontal frictionless track collides head-on and elastically with a sphere of mass $m_2 = 0.500\,\mathrm{kg}$ at rest. Determine the velocities after the collision and verify that the kinetic energy is conserved.

$$
v_1' = \frac{m_1 - m_2}{m_1 + m_2}\,v_1,
\qquad
v_2' = \frac{2m_1}{m_1 + m_2}\,v_1
\qquad\text{(target at rest)}
$$
$$
v_1' = \frac{0.300\,\mathrm{kg} - 0.500\,\mathrm{kg}}{0.800\,\mathrm{kg}} \cdot 4.00\,\mathrm{m/s} = -1.00\,\mathrm{m/s}
$$
$$
v_2' = \frac{2 \cdot 0.300\,\mathrm{kg}}{0.800\,\mathrm{kg}} \cdot 4.00\,\mathrm{m/s} = 3.00\,\mathrm{m/s}
$$
$$
K_i = \frac{1}{2} \cdot 0.300\,\mathrm{kg} \cdot (4.00\,\mathrm{m/s})^2 = 2.40\,\mathrm{J},
\qquad
K_f = \frac{1}{2} \cdot 0.300\,\mathrm{kg} \cdot (1.00\,\mathrm{m/s})^2 + \frac{1}{2} \cdot 0.500\,\mathrm{kg} \cdot (3.00\,\mathrm{m/s})^2 = 2.40\,\mathrm{J}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The track is horizontal and frictionless, therefore no external force acts along the line of motion and the total momentum is conserved. The collision is elastic by hypothesis, hence the total kinetic energy is conserved as well, and the two conditions give the pair of formulas proved for the one-dimensional elastic collision.

The projectile is lighter than the target, so the factor $\dfrac{m_1 - m_2}{m_1 + m_2}$ is negative and the first sphere **rebounds** at $1.00\,\mathrm{m/s}$, while the second moves forward at $3.00\,\mathrm{m/s}$.

Two checks confirm the result. The total momentum is $1.20\,\mathrm{kg\cdot m/s}$ before and $0.300 \cdot (-1.00) + 0.500 \cdot 3.00 = 1.20\,\mathrm{kg\cdot m/s}$ after. The total kinetic energy is $2.40\,\mathrm{J}$ in both states, as an elastic collision requires.

</div>

## Exercise 2

Two bodies move towards each other along a horizontal frictionless line: the first has mass $m_1 = 2.0\,\mathrm{kg}$ and velocity $v_1 = 5.0\,\mathrm{m/s}$, the second has mass $m_2 = 3.0\,\mathrm{kg}$ and velocity $v_2 = -2.0\,\mathrm{m/s}$. The collision is elastic. Determine the velocities after the collision and verify the reversal of the relative velocity.

$$
\begin{cases}
m_1 v_1 + m_2 v_2 = m_1 v_1' + m_2 v_2' \\[4pt]
v_1' - v_2' = -(v_1 - v_2)
\end{cases}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\begin{cases}
v_1' = \dfrac{m_1 - m_2}{m_1 + m_2}\,v_1 + \dfrac{2m_2}{m_1 + m_2}\,v_2 \\[8pt]
v_2' = \dfrac{m_2 - m_1}{m_1 + m_2}\,v_2 + \dfrac{2m_1}{m_1 + m_2}\,v_1
\end{cases}
$$
$$
v_1' = \frac{-1.0}{5.0} \cdot 5.0\,\mathrm{m/s} + \frac{6.0}{5.0} \cdot (-2.0\,\mathrm{m/s}) = -1.0\,\mathrm{m/s} - 2.4\,\mathrm{m/s} = -3.4\,\mathrm{m/s}
$$
$$
v_2' = \frac{1.0}{5.0} \cdot (-2.0\,\mathrm{m/s}) + \frac{4.0}{5.0} \cdot 5.0\,\mathrm{m/s} = -0.4\,\mathrm{m/s} + 4.0\,\mathrm{m/s} = +3.6\,\mathrm{m/s}
$$
$$
v_1 - v_2 = 7.0\,\mathrm{m/s},
\qquad
v_1' - v_2' = -3.4\,\mathrm{m/s} - 3.6\,\mathrm{m/s} = -7.0\,\mathrm{m/s}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The positive sense is that of the first body, therefore the velocity of the second enters the formulas as $-2.0\,\mathrm{m/s}$. Writing the signs at the start is what keeps a head-on collision from being treated as an overtaking one.

The energy equation has been replaced by the relation $v_1' - v_2' = -(v_1 - v_2)$, which is equivalent to it for a one-dimensional elastic collision and is linear, hence the system is solved by elimination rather than by substitution into a second-degree equation.

Both bodies reverse their motion, the first leaving at $3.4\,\mathrm{m/s}$ backwards and the second at $3.6\,\mathrm{m/s}$ forwards. The two bodies approached at $7.0\,\mathrm{m/s}$ and **separate at the same rate**, as the last line shows. The total momentum is $4.0\,\mathrm{kg\cdot m/s}$ before and after, and the total kinetic energy is $31\,\mathrm{J}$ in both states.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A ball of mass $m_1 = 0.150\,\mathrm{kg}$ moving at $v_1 = 6.00\,\mathrm{m/s}$ collides head-on and elastically with a ball at rest, and rebounds at $2.00\,\mathrm{m/s}$. Determine the mass of the second ball and its velocity after the collision.

$$
v_1' = \frac{m_1 - m_2}{m_1 + m_2}\,v_1
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\frac{v_1'}{v_1} = \frac{m_1 - m_2}{m_1 + m_2} = \frac{-2.00}{6.00} = -\frac{1}{3}
$$
$$
3\,(m_1 - m_2) = -(m_1 + m_2)
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
4m_1 = 2m_2
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
m_2 = 2m_1 = 0.300\,\mathrm{kg}
$$
$$
v_2' = \frac{2m_1}{m_1 + m_2}\,v_1 = \frac{0.300\,\mathrm{kg}}{0.450\,\mathrm{kg}} \cdot 6.00\,\mathrm{m/s} = 4.00\,\mathrm{m/s}
$$
$$
K_i = \frac{1}{2} \cdot 0.150\,\mathrm{kg} \cdot (6.00\,\mathrm{m/s})^2 = 2.70\,\mathrm{J},
\qquad
K_f = \frac{1}{2} \cdot 0.150\,\mathrm{kg} \cdot (2.00\,\mathrm{m/s})^2 + \frac{1}{2} \cdot 0.300\,\mathrm{kg} \cdot (4.00\,\mathrm{m/s})^2 = 2.70\,\mathrm{J}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The unknown is a mass and not a velocity, therefore the formula for the elastic collision with the target at rest is read as an equation in $m_2$. The ratio of the final to the initial velocity of the projectile depends on the masses alone, and here it equals $-\dfrac{1}{3}$.

Clearing the denominator and collecting the terms gives $m_2 = 2m_1 = 0.300\,\mathrm{kg}$. The negative ratio was already enough to state that the second ball is the heavier one, since the projectile rebounds only when $m_2 > m_1$.

With the mass known, the second formula gives $v_2' = 4.00\,\mathrm{m/s}$. The check on the kinetic energy returns $2.70\,\mathrm{J}$ in both states, and the relative velocity passes from $6.00\,\mathrm{m/s}$ to $-6.00\,\mathrm{m/s}$, as an elastic collision requires.

</div>

## Exercise 4

A neutron of mass $m$ and kinetic energy $K_0 = 2.0\,\mathrm{MeV}$ collides head-on and elastically with a carbon nucleus of mass $12m$ at rest. Determine the fraction of the kinetic energy transferred to the nucleus in one collision, and the number of such collisions needed to bring the neutron down to $0.025\,\mathrm{eV}$.

$$
v_1' = \frac{m - 12m}{13m}\,v_1 = -\frac{11}{13}\,v_1,
\qquad
v_2' = \frac{2m}{13m}\,v_1 = \frac{2}{13}\,v_1
$$
$$
\frac{K_2'}{K_0} = \frac{\dfrac{1}{2}(12m)v_2'^2}{\dfrac{1}{2}mv_1^2} = 12 \cdot \left(\frac{2}{13}\right)^2 = \frac{48}{169} = 0.284
$$
$$
\frac{K_1'}{K_0} = \left(\frac{11}{13}\right)^2 = \frac{121}{169} = 0.716
$$
$$
K_n = K_0\,(0.716)^n \le 0.025\,\mathrm{eV}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
n \ge \frac{\ln\left(\dfrac{0.025\,\mathrm{eV}}{2.0 \cdot 10^{6}\,\mathrm{eV}}\right)}{\ln 0.716} = 54.5
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
n = 55
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The ratio of the two masses is the only datum needed, since the formulas for the elastic collision with the target at rest contain the masses through that ratio alone. The neutron rebounds with $\dfrac{11}{13}$ of its speed and the nucleus moves forward with $\dfrac{2}{13}$ of it.

Kinetic energy is proportional to the mass and to the square of the speed, therefore the fraction transferred to the carbon nucleus is $12 \cdot \left(\dfrac{2}{13}\right)^2 = 0.284$, that is, $28.4\%$ in a single head-on collision. The general expression $\dfrac{4m_1m_2}{(m_1 + m_2)^2}$ gives the same value and reaches $1$ for equal masses, which is why hydrogen slows neutrons faster than carbon.

Each collision leaves the neutron with the fraction $0.716$ of the energy it had, so after $n$ collisions its energy is $K_0(0.716)^n$. Imposing that this be at most the thermal energy $0.025\,\mathrm{eV}$ and taking logarithms gives $n \ge 54.5$, hence **$55$ head-on collisions** suffice. Real collisions are not all head-on, therefore a moderator needs a larger number of them.

</div>
