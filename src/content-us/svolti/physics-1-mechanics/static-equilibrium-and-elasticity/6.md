## Exercise 1

A steel wire of diameter $d = 2.0\,\mathrm{mm}$ and length $L_0 = 3.0\,\mathrm{m}$ hangs vertically and supports a body of mass $m = 50\,\mathrm{kg}$. Young's modulus of steel is $Y = 2.0 \times 10^{11}\,\mathrm{Pa}$ and its proportional limit is $2.5 \times 10^{8}\,\mathrm{Pa}$. Determine the stress, the strain, the elongation of the wire and its stiffness, and check that the wire works below the proportional limit. The weight of the wire itself is neglected.

$$
A = \pi\left(\frac{d}{2}\right)^2 = \pi\,(1.0 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m})^2 = 3.14 \times 10^{-6}\,\mathrm{m^2},
\qquad
F = m g = 490.5\,\mathrm{N}
$$
$$
\sigma = \frac{F}{A},
\qquad
\varepsilon = \frac{\sigma}{Y},
\qquad
\Delta L = \varepsilon L_0,
\qquad
k = \frac{Y A}{L_0}
$$
$$
\sigma = \frac{490.5\,\mathrm{N}}{3.142 \times 10^{-6}\,\mathrm{m^2}} = 1.56 \times 10^{8}\,\mathrm{Pa}
$$
$$
\varepsilon = \frac{1.561 \times 10^{8}}{2.0 \times 10^{11}} = 7.81 \times 10^{-4},
\qquad
\Delta L = 7.806 \times 10^{-4} \cdot 3.0\,\mathrm{m} = 2.34\,\mathrm{mm}
$$
$$
k = \frac{2.0 \times 10^{11}\,\mathrm{Pa} \cdot 3.142 \times 10^{-6}\,\mathrm{m^2}}{3.0\,\mathrm{m}} = 2.09 \times 10^{5}\,\mathrm{N/m}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The wire is pulled by the weight of the body at its lower end and held at its upper end, hence every cross section carries the force $F = m g = 490.5\,\mathrm{N}$, perpendicular to itself. The **stress** is that force divided by the area of the cross section, and the area is computed from the diameter, with the radius equal to $1.0\,\mathrm{mm}$.

The stress is $1.56 \times 10^{8}\,\mathrm{Pa}$, smaller than the proportional limit $2.5 \times 10^{8}\,\mathrm{Pa}$, therefore **Hooke's law for solids** applies and the strain follows from the definition of Young's modulus, $\varepsilon = \dfrac{\sigma}{Y}$. The elongation is the strain times the initial length, $2.34\,\mathrm{mm}$ over three meters.

The stiffness $k = \dfrac{Y A}{L_0}$ gives the same result through $F = k\,\Delta L$, since $\dfrac{490.5\,\mathrm{N}}{2.094 \times 10^{5}\,\mathrm{N/m}} = 2.34 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m}$, which is a check on the two computations.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A steel wire of length $L_1 = 1.2\,\mathrm{m}$ and a copper wire of length $L_2 = 0.80\,\mathrm{m}$, both of cross section $A = 2.0\,\mathrm{mm^2}$, are joined end to end and hang vertically, with a body of mass $m = 20\,\mathrm{kg}$ attached at the lower end of the copper wire. Young's moduli are $Y_1 = 2.0 \times 10^{11}\,\mathrm{Pa}$ for steel and $Y_2 = 1.1 \times 10^{11}\,\mathrm{Pa}$ for copper. Determine the elongation of each wire and the total elongation, neglecting the weight of the wires.

$$
F = m g = 196.2\,\mathrm{N},
\qquad
\sigma = \frac{F}{A} = \frac{196.2\,\mathrm{N}}{2.0 \times 10^{-6}\,\mathrm{m^2}} = 9.81 \times 10^{7}\,\mathrm{Pa}
$$
$$
\Delta L_1 = \frac{\sigma L_1}{Y_1},
\qquad
\Delta L_2 = \frac{\sigma L_2}{Y_2},
\qquad
\Delta L = \Delta L_1 + \Delta L_2
$$
$$
\Delta L_1 = \frac{9.81 \times 10^{7}\,\mathrm{Pa} \cdot 1.2\,\mathrm{m}}{2.0 \times 10^{11}\,\mathrm{Pa}} = 5.89 \times 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m} = 0.589\,\mathrm{mm}
$$
$$
\Delta L_2 = \frac{9.81 \times 10^{7}\,\mathrm{Pa} \cdot 0.80\,\mathrm{m}}{1.1 \times 10^{11}\,\mathrm{Pa}} = 7.13 \times 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m} = 0.713\,\mathrm{mm}
$$
$$
\Delta L = 0.589\,\mathrm{mm} + 0.713\,\mathrm{mm} = 1.30\,\mathrm{mm}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The two wires are in series, hence each of them carries the same force $F$, equal to the weight of the hanging body, and they have the same cross section, therefore the stress is the same in both, $9.81 \times 10^{7}\,\mathrm{Pa}$. The strain is not the same, because the two materials have different Young's moduli.

The elongation of each wire is computed separately from $\Delta L = \dfrac{\sigma L_0}{Y}$. The lower end of the body descends by the sum of the two elongations, since the junction descends by $\Delta L_1$ and the copper wire adds $\Delta L_2$ below it.

The copper wire is shorter and still stretches more, because its Young's modulus is about half that of steel, and the ratio of the two elongations is $\dfrac{\Delta L_2}{\Delta L_1} = \dfrac{L_2 Y_1}{L_1 Y_2} = 1.21$. The whole arrangement behaves as two springs in series, of stiffnesses $k_1 = \dfrac{Y_1 A}{L_1} = 3.33 \times 10^{5}\,\mathrm{N/m}$ and $k_2 = \dfrac{Y_2 A}{L_2} = 2.75 \times 10^{5}\,\mathrm{N/m}$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A steel cable supports an elevator cabin of total mass $m = 1200\,\mathrm{kg}$, which starts upward with the acceleration $a = 1.5\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$. The tensile strength of the cable steel is $\sigma_u = 5.0 \times 10^{8}\,\mathrm{Pa}$ and the design uses the safety factor $n = 5$. Determine the smallest cross section and the corresponding diameter of the cable, and the elongation of a length $L_0 = 30\,\mathrm{m}$ of cable loaded at the allowable stress, with $Y = 2.0 \times 10^{11}\,\mathrm{Pa}$.

$$
T - m g = m a
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
T = m\,(g + a) = 1200\,\mathrm{kg} \cdot 11.31\,\mathrm{m/s^2} = 1.357 \times 10^{4}\,\mathrm{N}
$$
$$
\sigma_{\mathrm{all}} = \frac{\sigma_u}{n} = \frac{5.0 \times 10^{8}\,\mathrm{Pa}}{5} = 1.0 \times 10^{8}\,\mathrm{Pa}
$$
$$
A \ge \frac{T}{\sigma_{\mathrm{all}}} = \frac{1.357 \times 10^{4}\,\mathrm{N}}{1.0 \times 10^{8}\,\mathrm{Pa}} = 1.36 \times 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m^2} = 1.36\,\mathrm{cm^2}
$$
$$
d = \sqrt{\frac{4A}{\pi}} = \sqrt{\frac{4 \cdot 1.357 \times 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m^2}}{\pi}} = 1.31 \times 10^{-2}\,\mathrm{m} = 1.31\,\mathrm{cm}
$$
$$
\Delta L = \frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{all}} L_0}{Y} = \frac{1.0 \times 10^{8}\,\mathrm{Pa} \cdot 30\,\mathrm{m}}{2.0 \times 10^{11}\,\mathrm{Pa}} = 1.5 \times 10^{-2}\,\mathrm{m} = 1.5\,\mathrm{cm}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The cabin accelerates upward, hence the tension exceeds the weight. Newton's second law along the vertical, with the positive sense upward, gives $T = m(g + a) = 1.357 \times 10^{4}\,\mathrm{N}$, and the cable must be designed for this value and not for the weight alone.

The **allowable stress** is the tensile strength divided by the safety factor, $1.0 \times 10^{8}\,\mathrm{Pa}$. The condition $\sigma \le \sigma_{\mathrm{all}}$ becomes a condition on the area, $A \ge \dfrac{T}{\sigma_{\mathrm{all}}}$, which gives $1.36\,\mathrm{cm^2}$; a solid round cable of that area has the diameter $1.31\,\mathrm{cm}$.

At the allowable stress the strain is $\dfrac{\sigma_{\mathrm{all}}}{Y} = 5.0 \times 10^{-4}$, hence a cable of $30\,\mathrm{m}$ lengthens by $1.5\,\mathrm{cm}$. The value is small compared with the length, yet it is what makes a cabin at the top of a tall shaft sit slightly lower when it is loaded.

The allowable stress is far below the proportional limit of steel, therefore Hooke's law applies and the elongation computed in this way is legitimate.

</div>
