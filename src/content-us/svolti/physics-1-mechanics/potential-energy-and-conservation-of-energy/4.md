## Exercise 1

A block of mass $m = 4.5\,\mathrm{kg}$ slides along a horizontal floor with the initial speed $v_0 = 6.0\,\mathrm{m/s}$ and comes to rest. The coefficient of kinetic friction between the block and the floor is $\mu_k = 0.30$. Compute the distance travelled and the energy transformed into thermal energy.

$$
W_{\text{nc}} = \Delta K + \Delta U,
\qquad
W_{\text{nc}} = -f_k\,d = -\mu_k m g\,d,
\qquad
\Delta U = 0
$$
$$
-\mu_k m g\,d = 0 - \frac{1}{2}mv_0^2
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
d = \frac{v_0^2}{2\mu_k g}
$$
$$
d = \frac{(6.0\,\mathrm{m/s})^2}{2 \cdot 0.30 \cdot 9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2}} = \frac{36\,\mathrm{m^2/s^2}}{5.886\,\mathrm{m/s^2}} = 6.12\,\mathrm{m}
$$
$$
Q = \frac{1}{2}mv_0^2 = \frac{1}{2} \cdot 4.5\,\mathrm{kg} \cdot 36\,\mathrm{m^2/s^2} = 81\,\mathrm{J}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The floor is horizontal, hence the height of the block does not change and $\Delta U = 0$. The normal force is perpendicular to the displacement and does no work, so kinetic friction is the only force that changes the mechanical energy. Friction is **nonconservative**, therefore the conservation of mechanical energy does not apply and the energy equation $W_{\text{nc}} = \Delta K + \Delta U$ is used instead.

On a horizontal floor the normal force is $N = mg$, hence the friction force has the constant magnitude $f_k = \mu_k mg = 13.2\,\mathrm{N}$ and does the work $-f_k d$ over the distance $d$. The block ends at rest, so the whole initial kinetic energy is removed, which gives $d = 6.12\,\mathrm{m}$. The mass cancels in the stopping distance, while it does not cancel in the energy, and the $81\,\mathrm{J}$ appear as thermal energy of the block and of the floor.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A block of mass $m = 1.2\,\mathrm{kg}$ is released from rest on a plane inclined at $\alpha = 25^\circ$ and slides $d = 2.0\,\mathrm{m}$ along the plane. The coefficient of kinetic friction is $\mu_k = 0.30$. Compute the speed of the block at the end of the run and the energy dissipated by friction.

$$
\begin{cases}
N = m g \cos\alpha \\[4pt]
W_{\text{nc}} = -\mu_k m g \cos\alpha\,d
\end{cases}
$$
$$
-\mu_k m g \cos\alpha\,d = \frac{1}{2}mv^2 - m g \sin\alpha\,d
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
v = \sqrt{2 g d\,(\sin\alpha - \mu_k \cos\alpha)}
$$
$$
\sin 25^\circ - 0.30\cos 25^\circ = 0.4226 - 0.2719 = 0.1507
$$
$$
v = \sqrt{2 \cdot 9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2} \cdot 2.0\,\mathrm{m} \cdot 0.1507} = \sqrt{5.914\,\mathrm{m^2/s^2}} = 2.43\,\mathrm{m/s}
$$
$$
|W_{\text{nc}}| = 0.30 \cdot 1.2\,\mathrm{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2} \cdot 0.9063 \cdot 2.0\,\mathrm{m} = 6.40\,\mathrm{J}
$$

::grafico{assi=0 uguale=1 poligoni="0,0 6,0 6,2.8" linee="0,0 6.8,0" punti="3.207,1.739,pieno" vettori="3.207,1.739,3.207,0.539,w;3.207,1.739,2.699,2.827,N;3.207,1.739,4.458,2.322,f" riferimento="1.0,2.6,25,1.1,x,y" etichette="0.85,0.2,25°" xmin=-0.5 xmax=7.3 ymin=-0.9 ymax=3.9 titolo="Free-body diagram of the block: the normal force is perpendicular to the displacement and does no work, while the friction force $f_k = \mu_k m g \cos\alpha$ is directed up the plane"}

<div class="spiegazione">

The block slides down, hence the friction force points up the plane and does negative work. The normal force follows from the equilibrium in the direction perpendicular to the plane, where the block has no acceleration, therefore $N = mg\cos\alpha$ and $f_k = \mu_k mg\cos\alpha = 3.20\,\mathrm{N}$.

The block descends the height $d\sin\alpha$, so the gravitational potential energy decreases by $mgd\sin\alpha = 9.95\,\mathrm{J}$ while friction removes $6.40\,\mathrm{J}$. The difference, $3.55\,\mathrm{J}$, is the kinetic energy at the end of the run, and the speed is $2.43\,\mathrm{m/s}$. Without friction the same drop would give $4.07\,\mathrm{m/s}$, therefore friction has removed about $64\%$ of the energy released by the weight.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A block is launched up a plane inclined at $\alpha = 30^\circ$ with the initial speed $v_0 = 6.0\,\mathrm{m/s}$. The coefficients of friction are $\mu_s = \mu_k = 0.20$. Compute the distance travelled along the plane before the block stops, and the speed with which the block returns to the starting position.

$$
-\mu_k m g \cos\alpha\,d = 0 - \frac{1}{2}mv_0^2 + m g \sin\alpha\,d
$$
$$
d = \frac{v_0^2}{2g\,(\sin\alpha + \mu_k\cos\alpha)} = \frac{36\,\mathrm{m^2/s^2}}{2 \cdot 9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2} \cdot 0.6732} = 2.73\,\mathrm{m}
$$
$$
\tan 30^\circ = 0.577 > \mu_s = 0.20
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
\text{the block slides back down}
$$
$$
v_1 = \sqrt{2 g d\,(\sin\alpha - \mu_k\cos\alpha)} = \sqrt{2 \cdot 9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2} \cdot 2.73\,\mathrm{m} \cdot 0.3268} = 4.18\,\mathrm{m/s}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

While the block moves up, the weight and the friction force both oppose the motion, hence both remove kinetic energy and the two contributions add in the denominator, with $\sin\alpha + \mu_k\cos\alpha = 0.6732$. The block stops after $2.73\,\mathrm{m}$ along the plane, that is $1.36\,\mathrm{m}$ above the starting level.

The block stays at rest only if the static friction can balance the component of the weight along the plane, which requires $\tan\alpha \le \mu_s$. Here $\tan 30^\circ = 0.577$ exceeds $0.20$, therefore the block **slides back down**. On the way down friction reverses and now opposes the weight, so the two contributions subtract and $\sin\alpha - \mu_k\cos\alpha = 0.3268$. The block returns with $4.18\,\mathrm{m/s}$, which is less than the launch speed, because friction has removed energy on both legs of the trip.

</div>

## Exercise 4

A crate of mass $m = 20\,\mathrm{kg}$ is at rest on a horizontal floor and is pulled by a constant horizontal force $F = 80\,\mathrm{N}$ over the distance $s = 4.0\,\mathrm{m}$. The coefficient of kinetic friction is $\mu_k = 0.25$. Compute the final speed of the crate and the fraction of the work of $F$ that is dissipated by friction.

$$
W_{\text{nc}} = W_F + W_f = F s - \mu_k m g\,s,
\qquad
\Delta U = 0
$$
$$
W_F = 80\,\mathrm{N} \cdot 4.0\,\mathrm{m} = 320\,\mathrm{J},
\qquad
W_f = -0.25 \cdot 20\,\mathrm{kg} \cdot 9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2} \cdot 4.0\,\mathrm{m} = -196\,\mathrm{J}
$$
$$
\frac{1}{2}mv^2 = 320\,\mathrm{J} - 196\,\mathrm{J} = 124\,\mathrm{J}
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
v = \sqrt{\frac{2 \cdot 124\,\mathrm{J}}{20\,\mathrm{kg}}} = 3.52\,\mathrm{m/s}
$$
$$
\frac{|W_f|}{W_F} = \frac{196\,\mathrm{J}}{320\,\mathrm{J}} = 0.61
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The applied force and the friction force are both nonconservative, hence both belong to $W_{\text{nc}}$. The applied force is horizontal and agrees in sense with the displacement, therefore its work is $+Fs$; the friction force opposes the motion, therefore its work is $-\mu_k mg\,s$. The floor is horizontal, so $\Delta U = 0$ and the energy equation reduces to $W_{\text{nc}} = \Delta K$.

Of the $320\,\mathrm{J}$ supplied, $196\,\mathrm{J}$ are dissipated and $124\,\mathrm{J}$ remain as kinetic energy, which gives the final speed $3.52\,\mathrm{m/s}$. The dissipated fraction is $61\%$, and it depends on the ratio $\dfrac{\mu_k m g}{F}$ alone, hence a larger applied force would waste a smaller fraction over the same distance.

</div>
