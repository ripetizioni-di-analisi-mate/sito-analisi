## Exercise 1

A stone of mass $m = 0.40\,\mathrm{kg}$ is released from rest at the height $h = 12\,\mathrm{m}$ above the ground. Air resistance is negligible. Compute the speed with which the stone reaches the ground and its speed when it is $5.0\,\mathrm{m}$ above the ground.

$$
\frac{1}{2}mv_1^2 + mgy_1 = \frac{1}{2}mv_2^2 + mgy_2
$$
$$
v_1 = 0,
\quad
y_1 = h,
\quad
y_2 = y
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
v = \sqrt{2g\,(h - y)}
$$
$$
v_{\text{ground}} = \sqrt{2 \cdot 9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2} \cdot 12\,\mathrm{m}} = \sqrt{235.4\,\mathrm{m^2/s^2}} = 15.3\,\mathrm{m/s}
$$
$$
v_{5.0} = \sqrt{2 \cdot 9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2} \cdot (12 - 5.0)\,\mathrm{m}} = \sqrt{137.3\,\mathrm{m^2/s^2}} = 11.7\,\mathrm{m/s}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Air resistance is negligible, hence the weight is the only force that does work on the stone, and the weight is conservative. The hypotheses of the **conservation of mechanical energy** are therefore satisfied, and the sum $\dfrac{1}{2}mv^2 + mgy$ has the same value at every height. The ground is taken as the reference level, so that $U = mgy$ with $y$ measured upwards from it.

The stone starts from rest, hence its initial mechanical energy is the potential energy $mgh$ alone. At the height $y$ the potential energy is $mgy$ and the rest of the energy is kinetic, which gives $\dfrac{1}{2}mv^2 = mg(h-y)$ and the formula written above. The mass cancels, therefore the two speeds do not depend on the mass of the stone, and a body of $4.0\,\mathrm{kg}$ released from the same height arrives with the same $15.3\,\mathrm{m/s}$.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A ball is thrown vertically upwards with the speed $v_0 = 14\,\mathrm{m/s}$ from a window $y_0 = 1.6\,\mathrm{m}$ above the ground. Air resistance is negligible. Compute the maximum height reached above the ground and the speed with which the ball hits the ground.

$$
\frac{1}{2}mv_0^2 + mgy_0 = 0 + mgy_{\max}
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
y_{\max} = y_0 + \frac{v_0^2}{2g}
$$
$$
y_{\max} = 1.6\,\mathrm{m} + \frac{(14\,\mathrm{m/s})^2}{2 \cdot 9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2}} = 1.6\,\mathrm{m} + 9.99\,\mathrm{m} = 11.6\,\mathrm{m}
$$
$$
\frac{1}{2}mv_0^2 + mgy_0 = \frac{1}{2}mv_f^2
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
v_f = \sqrt{v_0^2 + 2gy_0}
$$
$$
v_f = \sqrt{196\,\mathrm{m^2/s^2} + 2 \cdot 9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2} \cdot 1.6\,\mathrm{m}} = \sqrt{227.4\,\mathrm{m^2/s^2}} = 15.1\,\mathrm{m/s}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

At the highest point the velocity of the ball is zero, since the motion is vertical and the ball reverses its sense there. The mechanical energy at the window is therefore equal to the potential energy at the highest point, which gives the rise $\dfrac{v_0^2}{2g} = 9.99\,\mathrm{m}$ above the window and the height $11.6\,\mathrm{m}$ above the ground.

The second question compares the window with the ground, where $U = 0$. The ball arrives with the kinetic energy it had at the window increased by $mgy_0$, hence $v_f = 15.1\,\mathrm{m/s}$, which exceeds the launch speed by the amount gained over the extra drop of $1.6\,\mathrm{m}$. The direction of the initial velocity never enters the computation, therefore a ball thrown **downwards** with $14\,\mathrm{m/s}$ from the same window arrives with the same $15.1\,\mathrm{m/s}$.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A child of mass $m = 28\,\mathrm{kg}$ starts at the top of a slide, at the height $h = 3.2\,\mathrm{m}$ above the ground, with the speed $v_0 = 1.5\,\mathrm{m/s}$. The slide is frictionless. Compute the speed at the bottom of the slide and the height at which the speed is $4.0\,\mathrm{m/s}$.

$$
\frac{1}{2}mv_0^2 + mgh = \frac{1}{2}mv^2 + mgy
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
v = \sqrt{v_0^2 + 2g\,(h - y)}
$$
$$
v_{\text{bottom}} = \sqrt{(1.5\,\mathrm{m/s})^2 + 2 \cdot 9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2} \cdot 3.2\,\mathrm{m}} = \sqrt{65.03\,\mathrm{m^2/s^2}} = 8.1\,\mathrm{m/s}
$$
$$
y = h + \frac{v_0^2 - v^2}{2g} = 3.2\,\mathrm{m} + \frac{(2.25 - 16)\,\mathrm{m^2/s^2}}{19.62\,\mathrm{m/s^2}} = 3.2\,\mathrm{m} - 0.70\,\mathrm{m} = 2.50\,\mathrm{m}
$$

::grafico{f="0.2*(4-x)^2" punti="0,3.2,pieno;4,0,pieno;0.46,2.5,pieno" asintoti="y=2.5" xmin=-0.4 xmax=4.8 ymin=-0.5 ymax=3.9 nomi="x,y" titolo="Profile of the slide: the child leaves the top at $y = 3.2\,\mathrm{m}$ with $1.5\,\mathrm{m/s}$, passes the level $y = 2.50\,\mathrm{m}$ with $4.0\,\mathrm{m/s}$ and reaches the bottom with $8.1\,\mathrm{m/s}$"}

<div class="spiegazione">

Two forces act on the child, the weight and the normal force of the slide. The normal force is perpendicular to the velocity at every instant, hence it does no work, and the slide is frictionless, hence no other force does work. The hypotheses of the conservation of mechanical energy are satisfied, and the **shape of the slide does not enter** the computation, since only the heights of the initial and of the final position matter.

The initial kinetic energy is not zero, therefore the term $\dfrac{1}{2}v_0^2$ stays in the formula and the speed at the bottom, $8.1\,\mathrm{m/s}$, is slightly larger than the $7.9\,\mathrm{m/s}$ obtained from rest. For the second question the relation is solved for $y$: the speed $4.0\,\mathrm{m/s}$ is reached after a drop of $0.70\,\mathrm{m}$, that is at the height $2.50\,\mathrm{m}$ above the ground.

</div>

## Exercise 4

A bead slides along a frictionless wire. It starts from rest at the height $h_A = 2.5\,\mathrm{m}$, descends to the bottom of the wire and then rises over a bump whose top is at the height $h_B = 1.8\,\mathrm{m}$. Compute the speed of the bead at the bottom and at the top of the bump, and the height from which the bead must start in order to reach the top of the bump with zero speed.

$$
\frac{1}{2}mv^2 + mgy = mgh_A
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
v = \sqrt{2g\,(h_A - y)}
$$
$$
v_{\text{bottom}} = \sqrt{2 \cdot 9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2} \cdot 2.5\,\mathrm{m}} = 7.0\,\mathrm{m/s}
$$
$$
v_B = \sqrt{2 \cdot 9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2} \cdot (2.5 - 1.8)\,\mathrm{m}} = \sqrt{13.73\,\mathrm{m^2/s^2}} = 3.7\,\mathrm{m/s}
$$
$$
v_B = 0
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
h_A = h_B = 1.8\,\mathrm{m}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The wire is frictionless and the reaction of the wire is perpendicular to the velocity, hence the mechanical energy keeps the value it has at the start, $mgh_A$ with the bottom of the wire taken as the reference level. The speed at a height $y$ follows from $\dfrac{1}{2}mv^2 = mg(h_A - y)$, which gives $7.0\,\mathrm{m/s}$ at the bottom and $3.7\,\mathrm{m/s}$ at the top of the bump.

The bead reaches the top of the bump with zero speed when its whole mechanical energy is potential energy at that point, that is when $h_A = h_B$. The limiting height is therefore $1.8\,\mathrm{m}$, the height of the bump itself, and a bead released from a lower position turns back before the top. The same conclusion for a body that must complete a vertical loop requires a further condition, since there the body also needs a centripetal acceleration at the top, as the chapter on applications of Newton's laws establishes.

</div>
