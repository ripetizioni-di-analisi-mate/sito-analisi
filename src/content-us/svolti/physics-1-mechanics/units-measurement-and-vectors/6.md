## Exercise 1

Given $\vec{A} = (4.0,\ -3.0)$ and $\vec{B} = (2.0,\ 6.0)$, compute the scalar product, the two magnitudes and the angle between the two vectors.

$$
\vec{A} \cdot \vec{B} = A_xB_x + A_yB_y,
\qquad
\cos\varphi = \frac{\vec{A} \cdot \vec{B}}{A\,B}
$$
$$
\vec{A} \cdot \vec{B} = 4.0 \cdot 2.0 + (-3.0) \cdot 6.0 = 8.0 - 18.0 = -10.0
$$
$$
A = \sqrt{(4.0)^2 + (-3.0)^2} = \sqrt{25} = 5.00,
\qquad
B = \sqrt{(2.0)^2 + (6.0)^2} = \sqrt{40} = 6.32
$$
$$
\cos\varphi = \frac{-10.0}{5.00 \cdot 6.32} = -0.316
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\varphi = 108.4^\circ
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

In components the scalar product is the sum of the products of corresponding components, and the result is a number, not a vector. Here one of the two products is negative and larger in absolute value than the other, hence the sum is negative.

The sign of the scalar product is the sign of $\cos\varphi$, because the two magnitudes are non-negative. A negative scalar product therefore means $\cos\varphi < 0$, hence an angle larger than $90^\circ$, and the two vectors point to opposite sides of the line perpendicular to either of them. The computed value $\varphi = 108.4^\circ$ **confirms** this.

The angle is obtained from the definition $\vec{A} \cdot \vec{B} = A\,B\cos\varphi$ solved for the cosine. Since $\cos\varphi$ is a ratio of a product of two quantities to the product of their magnitudes, it is a dimensionless number between $-1$ and $1$, which is a quick check that no arithmetic slip occurred. Here $|-0.316| < 1$, hence the value is admissible.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A crate is dragged $d = 12\,\mathrm{m}$ along a horizontal floor by a constant force of magnitude $F = 85\,\mathrm{N}$ directed at $32^\circ$ above the horizontal. A constant friction force of magnitude $f = 20\,\mathrm{N}$ opposes the motion. Compute the work done by the applied force, the work done by friction and the total work.

$$
W = \vec{F} \cdot \vec{d} = F\,d\cos\varphi
$$
$$
W_F = 85\,\mathrm{N} \cdot 12\,\mathrm{m} \cdot \cos 32^\circ = 1020\,\mathrm{J} \cdot 0.8480 = 865\,\mathrm{J}
$$
$$
W_f = 20\,\mathrm{N} \cdot 12\,\mathrm{m} \cdot \cos 180^\circ = -240\,\mathrm{J}
$$
$$
W_{\mathrm{tot}} = W_F + W_f = 865\,\mathrm{J} - 240\,\mathrm{J} = 625\,\mathrm{J}
$$

::grafico{assi=0 uguale=1 punti="0,0,pieno" vettori="0,0,3.604,2.252,F;0,0,4,0,d;0,0,-1,0,f" etichette="0.95,0.22,32°" xmin=-1.9 xmax=5.1 ymin=-1 ymax=3 titolo="The applied force $\vec{F}$ makes the angle $32^\circ$ with the displacement $\vec{d}$, while the friction force $\vec{f}$ is opposite to it; the force arrows are drawn to the scale of $20\,\mathrm{N}$ per unit"}

<div class="spiegazione">

The work of a constant force is the scalar product of the force and the displacement, hence it equals $F\,d\cos\varphi$, where $\varphi$ is the angle between the two vectors. The applied force makes the angle $32^\circ$ with the displacement, therefore only the component $F\cos 32^\circ = 72.1\,\mathrm{N}$ along the displacement contributes, and the work is $865\,\mathrm{J}$.

Friction opposes the motion, hence the angle between the friction force and the displacement is $180^\circ$ and $\cos 180^\circ = -1$. The work of friction is therefore negative and equals $-240\,\mathrm{J}$. A negative work means that the force removes kinetic energy from the body, as the chapter on work and kinetic energy develops.

The weight and the normal force are both perpendicular to the displacement, since the floor is horizontal and the crate stays on it, hence $\cos 90^\circ = 0$ and neither of them does any work. The total work is therefore the sum of the two contributions computed above, $625\,\mathrm{J}$, and by the work-energy theorem that is the gain in kinetic energy of the crate.

</div>

## Exercise 3

Let $\vec{A} = (3.0,\ k,\ -2.0)$ and $\vec{B} = (2.0,\ 4.0,\ 1.0)$. Determine the value of $k$ for which the two vectors are perpendicular. Then, for $k = 3.0$, compute the component of $\vec{A}$ along the direction of $\vec{B}$ and the angle between the two vectors.

$$
\vec{A} \perp \vec{B}
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\vec{A} \cdot \vec{B} = 0
$$
$$
\vec{A} \cdot \vec{B} = 3.0 \cdot 2.0 + k \cdot 4.0 + (-2.0) \cdot 1.0 = 4.0 + 4.0\,k = 0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
k = -1.0
$$
$$
k = 3.0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\vec{A} \cdot \vec{B} = 6.0 + 12.0 - 2.0 = 16.0
$$
$$
A_B = \vec{A} \cdot \hat{B} = \frac{\vec{A} \cdot \vec{B}}{B},
\qquad
B = \sqrt{(2.0)^2 + (4.0)^2 + (1.0)^2} = \sqrt{21} = 4.583
$$
$$
A_B = \frac{16.0}{4.583} = 3.49
$$
$$
A = \sqrt{(3.0)^2 + (3.0)^2 + (-2.0)^2} = \sqrt{22} = 4.690,
\qquad
\cos\varphi = \frac{16.0}{4.690 \cdot 4.583} = 0.7444
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\varphi = 41.9^\circ
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Two nonzero vectors are perpendicular exactly when their scalar product vanishes, therefore the condition on $k$ is an equation of the first degree. The two terms that do not contain $k$ give $6.0 - 2.0 = 4.0$, hence the equation is $4.0 + 4.0\,k = 0$ and the solution is $k = -1.0$. Substituting that value back gives $\vec{A} = (3.0,\ -1.0,\ -2.0)$ and $\vec{A} \cdot \vec{B} = 6.0 - 4.0 - 2.0 = 0$, which **verifies** the answer.

With $k = 3.0$ the scalar product is positive, hence the angle between the two vectors is acute. The component of $\vec{A}$ along the direction of $\vec{B}$ is the scalar product of $\vec{A}$ with the unit vector $\hat{B} = \dfrac{\vec{B}}{B}$, which is the same as dividing $\vec{A} \cdot \vec{B}$ by the magnitude of $\vec{B}$ alone. That component is $3.49$, and it is the length of the projection of $\vec{A}$ on the line of $\vec{B}$.

The projection is smaller than the magnitude $A = 4.690$, as it must be, since a projection cannot exceed the length of the vector projected. Their ratio is the cosine of the angle, $\dfrac{3.49}{4.690} = 0.744$, hence $\varphi = 41.9^\circ$, the same value obtained from the general formula.

</div>

## Exercise 4

A constant force $\vec{F} = (12\,\hat{\imath} - 5.0\,\hat{\jmath} + 8.0\,\hat{k})\,\mathrm{N}$ acts on a particle that moves in a straight line from the point $\vec{r}_1 = (1.0,\ 2.0,\ 0)\,\mathrm{m}$ to the point $\vec{r}_2 = (4.0,\ 0,\ 3.0)\,\mathrm{m}$. Compute the work done by the force and the angle between the force and the displacement.

$$
\vec{d} = \vec{r}_2 - \vec{r}_1,
\qquad
W = \vec{F} \cdot \vec{d}
$$
$$
\vec{d} = (4.0 - 1.0,\ 0 - 2.0,\ 3.0 - 0)\,\mathrm{m} = (3.0,\ -2.0,\ 3.0)\,\mathrm{m}
$$
$$
W = 12 \cdot 3.0 + (-5.0) \cdot (-2.0) + 8.0 \cdot 3.0 = 36 + 10 + 24 = 70\,\mathrm{J}
$$
$$
F = \sqrt{(12)^2 + (-5.0)^2 + (8.0)^2}\,\mathrm{N} = \sqrt{233}\,\mathrm{N} = 15.26\,\mathrm{N},
\qquad
d = \sqrt{22}\,\mathrm{m} = 4.690\,\mathrm{m}
$$
$$
\cos\varphi = \frac{70}{15.26 \cdot 4.690} = 0.9777
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\varphi = 12.1^\circ
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The displacement is the difference of the two position vectors, hence its components are the differences of corresponding coordinates, and it runs from the initial position to the final position. The force is constant and the path is a straight line, therefore the work is the single scalar product $\vec{F} \cdot \vec{d}$, with no integral required.

The three products are all positive, since the signs of corresponding components agree in each pair, hence every component of the force contributes positively and the total work is $70\,\mathrm{J}$. The unit is the product of the newton and the meter, which is the joule.

The angle between the force and the displacement comes out small, $12.1^\circ$, which agrees with the fact that the work is nearly as large as the product $F\,d = 71.6\,\mathrm{J}$ of the two magnitudes. That product is the greatest work the force could do over a displacement of this length, and it would be attained only if the force were parallel to the displacement.

</div>
