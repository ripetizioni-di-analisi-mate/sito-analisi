## Exercise 1

Count the significant figures of each measured value and rewrite it in scientific notation: $0.00480\,\mathrm{m}$, $25.30\,\mathrm{s}$, $6.0 \times 10^{4}\,\mathrm{N}$, $1200\,\mathrm{kg}$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
0.00480\,\mathrm{m} &= 4.80 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m} &&\text{three significant figures} \\[6pt]
25.30\,\mathrm{s} &= 2.530 \times 10^{1}\,\mathrm{s} &&\text{four significant figures} \\[6pt]
6.0 \times 10^{4}\,\mathrm{N} &= 60\,000\,\mathrm{N} &&\text{two significant figures} \\[6pt]
1200\,\mathrm{kg} &= 1.2 \times 10^{3}\,\mathrm{kg}
\ \text{ or }\ 1.200 \times 10^{3}\,\mathrm{kg} &&\text{two, three or four, undetermined}
\end{aligned}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

Significant figures are counted from the leftmost nonzero digit. In $0.00480\,\mathrm{m}$ the two zeros after the decimal point only locate the decimal point, hence they are not significant, while the final zero is a digit the instrument produced, hence it is significant and the value carries **three** significant figures.

In $25.30\,\mathrm{s}$ every digit is significant, including the final zero, which states that the hundredths place was read and found to be zero. The value therefore carries four significant figures, and writing $25.3\,\mathrm{s}$ would claim one digit less.

The value $6.0 \times 10^{4}\,\mathrm{N}$ is already in scientific notation, and only the digits of the factor $6.0$ are significant, hence there are two. Writing the same value as $60\,000\,\mathrm{N}$ hides that information, which is the ambiguity of the last line, because in $1200\,\mathrm{kg}$ the two final zeros may be measured digits or may only fix the order of magnitude, and nothing in the notation distinguishes the two cases. Scientific notation removes the ambiguity, since $1.2 \times 10^{3}\,\mathrm{kg}$ carries two significant figures and $1.200 \times 10^{3}\,\mathrm{kg}$ carries four.

</div>

## Exercise 2

A rectangular metal plate is measured with two different instruments and the sides come out as $a = 14.73\,\mathrm{cm}$ and $b = 3.2\,\mathrm{cm}$. Compute the area and the perimeter of the plate with the correct number of significant figures.

$$
A = a\,b,
\qquad
p = 2\,(a + b)
$$
$$
A = 14.73\,\mathrm{cm} \cdot 3.2\,\mathrm{cm} = 47.136\,\mathrm{cm^2}
\quad\longrightarrow\quad
A = 47\,\mathrm{cm^2}
$$
$$
p = 2\,(14.73 + 3.2)\,\mathrm{cm} = 2 \cdot 17.93\,\mathrm{cm} = 35.86\,\mathrm{cm}
\quad\longrightarrow\quad
p = 35.9\,\mathrm{cm}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The area is a product, therefore the rule for products applies and the result keeps as many significant figures as the factor with the fewest. The side $a$ carries four significant figures and the side $b$ carries two, hence the area keeps **two** significant figures and is written $47\,\mathrm{cm^2}$, even though the calculator displays five digits.

The perimeter contains a sum, therefore the rule for sums applies and the result keeps as many decimal places as the term with the fewest. The term $3.2\,\mathrm{cm}$ is known to the first decimal place, hence the sum $17.93\,\mathrm{cm}$ is reliable only to the first decimal place. The factor $2$ is exact and does not limit anything, so the perimeter is rounded to the first decimal place and comes out as $35.9\,\mathrm{cm}$.

The two rules give different answers because an error on $b$ of one hundredth of a centimeter changes the area by $14.73 \cdot 0.01 \approx 0.15\,\mathrm{cm^2}$, while it changes the perimeter by only $0.02\,\mathrm{cm}$. In a product the relative uncertainties of the two factors add, while in a sum the absolute uncertainties of the two terms add.

</div>

## Exercise 3

A solid cylinder has diameter $d = 2.50\,\mathrm{cm}$, height $h = 4.0\,\mathrm{cm}$ and mass $m = 68.4\,\mathrm{g}$. Compute its density in $\mathrm{g/cm^3}$ and in $\mathrm{kg/m^3}$.

$$
V = \pi \left(\frac{d}{2}\right)^{\!2} h,
\qquad
\rho = \frac{m}{V}
$$
$$
V = \pi \cdot (1.25\,\mathrm{cm})^2 \cdot 4.0\,\mathrm{cm} = 19.635\,\mathrm{cm^3}
$$
$$
\rho = \frac{68.4\,\mathrm{g}}{19.635\,\mathrm{cm^3}} = 3.4836\,\mathrm{g/cm^3}
\quad\longrightarrow\quad
\rho = 3.5\,\mathrm{g/cm^3}
$$
$$
\rho = 3.5\,\frac{\mathrm{g}}{\mathrm{cm^3}} = 3.5 \cdot \frac{10^{-3}\,\mathrm{kg}}{10^{-6}\,\mathrm{m^3}} = 3.5 \times 10^{3}\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The volume of a cylinder is the area of the base times the height, and the radius is half the diameter, hence $r = 1.25\,\mathrm{cm}$. The number $\pi$ is exact, so it does not limit the significant figures; among the data the height carries two significant figures and is the least precise, therefore the final density keeps **two**.

The intermediate volume is kept with all its digits, $19.635\,\mathrm{cm^3}$, and the rounding is applied once, at the end. Rounding the volume first to $20\,\mathrm{cm^3}$ would give $\dfrac{68.4}{20} = 3.42\,\mathrm{g/cm^3}$, hence $3.4\,\mathrm{g/cm^3}$, and the last digit of the answer would already be wrong.

The two routes differ by $0.1\,\mathrm{g/cm^3}$, which is about $3\%$ of the result, hence the whole second significant figure is affected by the intermediate rounding.

The conversion to $\mathrm{kg/m^3}$ multiplies the value by $10^{3}$, as in every passage from $\mathrm{g/cm^3}$, hence $\rho = 3.5 \times 10^{3}\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}$. The value lies between the density of aluminium and that of titanium, so it is a plausible density for a metal alloy.

</div>

## Exercise 4

The position of a body along a straight track is read as $x_1 = 145.2\,\mathrm{m}$ at the first instant and as $x_2 = 145.67\,\mathrm{m}$ at the second instant, the two readings being separated by $\Delta t = 0.250\,\mathrm{s}$. Compute the displacement and the average velocity with the correct number of significant figures.

$$
\Delta x = x_2 - x_1,
\qquad
v_{\mathrm{av}} = \frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t}
$$
$$
\Delta x = 145.67\,\mathrm{m} - 145.2\,\mathrm{m} = 0.47\,\mathrm{m}
\quad\longrightarrow\quad
\Delta x = 0.5\,\mathrm{m}
$$
$$
v_{\mathrm{av}} = \frac{0.47\,\mathrm{m}}{0.250\,\mathrm{s}} = 1.88\,\mathrm{m/s}
\quad\longrightarrow\quad
v_{\mathrm{av}} = 2\,\mathrm{m/s}
$$

<div class="spiegazione">

The two positions carry four and five significant figures, yet their difference carries only one. The rule for differences keeps as many decimal places as the term with the fewest, and $145.2\,\mathrm{m}$ is known to the first decimal place, therefore the displacement is reliable only to the first decimal place and is written $0.5\,\mathrm{m}$, with a single significant figure.

Subtracting two values that are close to each other destroys significant figures, since the leading digits cancel and only the uncertain tail remains. The average velocity is then a quotient, so it keeps the significant figures of the displacement, hence one, and it is written $2\,\mathrm{m/s}$.

The loss disappears when the two positions are read to the same number of decimal places. With $x_1 = 145.20\,\mathrm{m}$ both readings carry two decimal places, hence the displacement is $0.47\,\mathrm{m}$ with two significant figures and the average velocity is $1.9\,\mathrm{m/s}$.

</div>
