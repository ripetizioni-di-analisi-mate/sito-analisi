A vessel contains a layer of oil, less dense than water, floating on a layer of water, and the total depth of liquid is $H$. How does the gauge pressure on the bottom compare with the gauge pressure on the bottom of an identical vessel filled with water alone to the same depth $H$?

- It is larger.
- It is smaller.
- It is equal, since the total depth is the same.
- It depends on the area of the bottom.

## Explanation

Applying the law of pressure with depth layer by layer, the gauge pressure on the bottom is $\rho_{\mathrm{oil}}\,g\,h_{\mathrm{oil}} + \rho_{\mathrm{w}}\,g\,h_{\mathrm{w}}$, with $h_{\mathrm{oil}} + h_{\mathrm{w}} = H$. Since $\rho_{\mathrm{oil}} < \rho_{\mathrm{w}}$, this sum is smaller than $\rho_{\mathrm{w}}\,g\,H$. The relation $p = \rho g h$ with a single density holds within one fluid only, and the area of the bottom does not enter the pressure.
